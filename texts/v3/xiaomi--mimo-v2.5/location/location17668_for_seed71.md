# The Plateau of Unwinding

The land has no name that survives intact. The people who live here call it different things depending on where they stand, and none of the names agree with each other, and some of the names change when you say them twice, and this is considered normal.

---

## The Land Itself

The scale is the first thing that breaks you.

You stand at the edge of the high country — which is to say, you stand at the edge of everything — and the plateau extends in every direction until it meets a horizon that doesn't behave like a horizon should. It curves. Not like the curvature of a planet. It curves *upward*, gently, as though the world is a vast shallow bowl and you are standing on its rim, and the center, impossibly far away, is both higher than you and further than sight should allow. The high country is elevated not just in the way mountains are elevated, but in the way a thought is elevated above the ground it walks on — there is a sense, pervasive and unshakable, that this place exists at a *higher level of being* than whatever is below it, and that the air itself knows this.

The ground beneath your feet is stone, but it is stone that has been thinking about being stone for longer than most geological formations can account for. Eons — not the word. Longer than aeons. This is stone that was old when the first minerals cooled from the first magma of the first world, and it has spent every moment since then being slowly, patiently *unwound*. The erosion here is not destructive. It is *completion*. Every sharp edge has been released. Every fracture has been persuaded into smoothness. The plateau is not flat; it is *settled*, the way a body settles into stillness after a very long journey. There are low rises, gentle basins, shallow swales that channel water in times of rain — but everything is rounded, worn to a kind of terminal softness, as though the land itself has finished saying what it had to say and is now simply *being*.

The stone is pale — not white, not grey, but somewhere between, a color that suggests old bone or the underside of clouds. In places, it is dark, almost black, and these dark patches are where water pools or has pooled recently, leaving mineral stains that look like calligraphy written in a language the wind is still trying to read.

The rock is not uniform. It is layered in strata so fine and numerous that the cliffs and exposed faces look like the edges of enormous books, thousands of pages compressed into a vertical face, each page a different shade of grey or cream or faint blue. Some of these layers are only millimeters thick. The cumulative effect, over the vast distances the plateau extends, is of a landscape that is *textual* — as though the ground itself is a document written in slow time, and the settlement lives on top of an unreadable library.

---

## Water and Weather

The water here is shy.

It does not flow in rivers. There are no rivers. There are *seeps* — places where the ancient stone exhales moisture from somewhere deep within itself, where the surface is perpetually damp, where thin films of water slide along the rock with a patience that makes glaciers look hasty. These seeps appear unpredictably. One morning a seam of wetness will be weeping from a crack in the stone where it was dry the day before, and three days later it will be gone, and the stone around it will be stained a slightly different color, and no one will have noticed it move.

Rain comes in intervals that are not quite regular. It is always cold rain — the air is cold, the cloud-base is cold, the rain is the temperature of deep wells and underground streams. It falls not in storms but in *curtains*, broad sheets of fine droplets that sweep across the plateau like veils being drawn across an enormous window. When the rain passes, the stone steams. Not dramatically — there is no hiss, no eruption of vapor. The stone simply *warms* from its dampness the way a sleeping body warms from a breath, and a low mist rises and hangs and slowly dissipates, and the whole cycle takes hours.

In the basins and swales, water collects in shallow pools that have no visible source and no visible drainage. They simply *are*. Their surfaces are mirror-still when there is no wind, and in them you can see the sky reflected with a clarity that suggests the pools are deeper than they look — or that the sky reflected in them is not quite the same sky that is above you, and this is also considered normal.

The mist — the mist is constant. Not thick. Never thick. But always present, a gauze of moisture hanging a few meters above the ground, thinning as it rises, vanishing before it reaches any real height. It gives the distances a quality of *suggestion* rather than *fact*. Things far away are not hidden by the mist so much as *implied* by it.

Condensation is everywhere. Every surface above a certain height collects a fine rime of moisture — stones, walls, the wooden frames of structures. In the mornings, before the light strengthens, the settlement looks as though it has been dusted with something between frost and dew, something that catches the pale light and holds it, so that the whole place glimmers faintly, like a thought that hasn't yet decided whether to become a word.

## Vegetation

There are no animals here. This fact, which should be the most striking thing about this landscape, is somehow *not* — it is the kind of absence that the mind adjusts to faster than expected, the way you stop noticing a sound after a few minutes. But the silence it creates is fundamental. The silence is not an absence of noise. It is a *presence* of quiet — a thick, textured, almost *material* silence that fills the spaces between things and gives every other sound (and there are few) a weight and significance they would not carry elsewhere.

The vegetation, unburdened by herbivory, unchallenged by any grazing mouth or browsing antler, has done what vegetation does when nothing eats it: it has *filled in*.

But it fills in *sparsely*, because the space is vast and the cold is real and the water comes and goes in its shy, intermittent way. The result is a landscape of intense, startling pockets of green set within an enormous emptiness — like a painting where most of the canvas is pale stone and sky, but certain areas have been painted with extraordinary, almost obsessive detail and saturation.

The plants are low. Almost everything hugs the ground. There are mosses — dozens of species, hundreds, an almost incomprehensible diversity of moss, some so small they are visible only as a change in the texture of the stone, some forming thick cushions the size of sleeping mats, some trailing in long, elegant curtains from the edges of basins where the water seeps are strongest. The mosses are every shade of green that exists, plus several that probably shouldn't — a green so dark it is nearly black, a green so bright it seems to emit its own light, a green that shifts between warm and cool as you change your angle of view.

There are grasses, but they are not grasses as the lowlands understand grass. They grow in scattered tussocks, each one a dense knot of narrow, stiff blades that resist the cold with a waxy coating that makes them look slightly wet even when they are dry. The tussocks are separated by distances of several meters — sometimes more — so that the grass does not form meadows or fields but rather a kind of *punctuation*, a series of green marks on the pale stone, like beads on an abacus.

There are no trees. Nothing reaches higher than a person's waist. But what lacks in height it makes up for in *density of life* — within each pocket of growth, the vegetation is thick and layered and complex, with smaller things growing on top of larger things growing on top of the stone, and the whole assemblage humming with a slow, chlorophyll-powered intensity that is almost audible.

And here is where the weirdness begins.

Some of the vegetation is *not right*. A patch of moss in the eastern basin has started growing in a perfect Fibonacci spiral, each frond positioned with mathematical precision that no natural process should produce. It has been doing this for years. The people of the settlement harvest it carefully, because the moss does not seem to mind, and the spirals are useful — they can be dried and used as measuring tools, as templates for circular construction. But everyone knows this is not what moss does.

A species of ground-cover in the western flats has begun growing *through* the stone. Not around it. Not in the cracks of it. Through it. You can see the green emerging from the flat surface of the rock like a hair growing from skin, and if you dig down — and people have dug down — the root systems are embedded in the stone itself, threaded through it like veins, and the stone around the roots is *soft*, as though the plant has convinced it to become something else.

In the deep basins, where the mist hangs longest, there are plants that appear to be *listening*. They orient not toward light — the light here is diffuse enough that directionality barely matters — but toward *sound*, or toward something that occupies the same niche as sound but is not quite sound. When someone speaks near them, their leaves turn. When silence falls, they slowly, slowly turn away.

## The Settlement

The city — and it is a city, by the standards of what this place has produced — sits on a broad shelf of stone where three large basins converge and the water seeps are most reliable. It is called, depending on who you ask and what generation they belong to, something that translates roughly as *The Place Where We Stopped Walking* or *The Last Stain* or simply *Here*.

It is built from the stone of the plateau itself. Everything is stone and the few materials that can be worked with simple tools — wood from the tougher, more fibrous plants, cordage woven from dried moss-stems, a kind of crude mortar made fromiteite calcium deposits dissolved from the seeping water and reapplied. The buildings are low, as the vegetation is low — one story, sometimes two in the oldest structures, but never higher. They are rounded, following the aesthetic of the eroded landscape, their walls thick, their interiors small and warm-ish and dark. They look like natural formations. They look like the plateau's own slow geology has decided to arrange itself into shelter.

The streets — and they are streets, not paths, because they are *planned*, or were once planned, before the plan was forgotten and only the result remains — run in curves that echo the basins and swales of the surrounding land. There are no straight lines anywhere in the settlement. Straight lines are considered somewhere between foolish and dangerous — not for any practical reason anyone can articulate, but because the place itself resists straightness, and buildings that are built with straight walls develop cracks that follow no logic, and doors that are set in straight frames hang wrong, and there is a persistent, low-grade unease in any structure that has insisted on perpendicularity.

The settlement is *organized*. There are gathering places, storage areas, water-collection points, workshops. The workshops are where the primitive technology of this people manifests most clearly — stone tools, bone needles (but from what? there are no animals), wooden implements shaped with patience and the particular sharpness that comes from generations of refined technique rather than technological advancement. The people have not *invented* much. They have *refined* what they have until it is almost perfect, the way the plateau itself has been refined by erosion until it is almost nothing.

There are perhaps a thousand structures. The settlement is large enough to contain neighborhoods, districts, zones of different activity. But it is also sparse — buildings are separated by generous spaces, and the spaces between them are often left to the mosses and grasses, so that the city and the wild vegetation are not truly separate but interpenetrating, each one threading through the other like the roots through stone.

The people — I will describe them, though they are hard to describe — are tall and thin and pale, with the kind of angularity that comes from generations of living at altitude where the air is thin and the cold is constant. They move slowly, not from weakness but from *deliberation*. Everything they do, they do with a kind of considered intentionality, as though each action is being weighed against the vast, silent landscape around it and found either sufficient or wanting. They speak in low voices. They rarely raise them. The silence of the place has shaped their voices the way the erosion has shaped the stone.

They do not hunt. There is nothing to hunt. They do not trap. There is nothing to trap. Their diet is entirely botanical — the mosses, the grasses, the roots (some of which are enormous, thick, starchy, almost potato-like in their nutritiveness), the strange fruits that grow in the basins where the mist is thickest. These fruits are not recognizable as fruit. They look like small stones, or like drops of solidified mist, or like something the mind refuses to categorize. They are nourishing. That is all that matters.

## The Weirdness

Reality, on this plateau, is *old*, and it is *thin*, and in places it has worn through entirely.

This is not a metaphor. The erosion that has shaped the landscape for eons — that smooth stone, those rounded basins, that terminal softness — has not been limited to rock and soil and water. It has been eroding *reality itself*. The fundamental structures — the rules, the laws, the basic assumptions about what things are and how they relate to each other — have been wearing away, slowly, patiently, the way water wears away stone, and what remains is a place where the rules still *mostly* apply but where the exceptions are visible, audible, tangible.

The most obvious manifestation is the *folding*. In certain places — always near the basins, always where the mist is densest — the space folds. Not dramatically. Not in a way you would see from a distance. But if you walk through one of these zones, you will walk for what feels like several minutes through a distance that should take seconds, and when you emerge on the other side, the landscape will be *slightly different* — the same features, the same stone, the same mosses, but arranged in a subtly different configuration, as though someone has shuffled a deck of cards and put it back together in a different order. The people of the settlement know where these folding-zones are. They avoid most of them. Some of them are used deliberately, for travel, but only by those who have learned to read the subtle cues — a particular quality of light, a shift in the texture of the mist, a sound that is not quite the sound of wind.

There are places where *causality* is locally unreliable. In the northeastern quarter of the settlement, there is a building that was built before the current generation, and everyone remembers it being built, and the tools that built it are still in their workshop, and the marks of their use are still on the handles, and yet the building shows signs of weathering that should take centuries. The moss on its roof is old — older than anyone alive. The stone of its walls has been worn smooth in a way that the newer buildings have not. It is as though time passes differently there, faster, or in a different *pattern*, and no one has found an explanation for this, and the building is still used as a storehouse, because what else would you do with a building that is already there.

There are places where *memory* is external. Certain stones in the deep basins, when touched, produce — not images, not sounds, but *knowings*. You touch the stone and you *know* something you did not know before, something about the stone itself, about its age, about the strata beneath it, about the water that has passed over it, about the slow eons of its unmaking. These knowings are not words. They are not visual. They are a kind of *direct apprehension*, as though the stone is communicating in the only language it has — the language of *being what it is, for as long as it has been that* — and the human mind, by touching it, briefly *participates* in that being.

Some of these knowings are frightening. Some describe things that happened so long ago that the concepts required to understand them do not exist in any human language. Some describe things that are *still happening*, deep in the rock, in the slow movements of strata that have not yet finished settling, and the implication is that the plateau is still *forming*, still becoming what it is, that its ancient settledness is an illusion and that the deepest layers are still in motion, still thinking about what to be.

There are places where *color* is wrong. Not dramatically — you would not see it from a distance. But up close, certain stones are a shade that does not correspond to any known pigment or wavelength. It is not quite blue. It is not quite green. It is not quite any color that has a name. The eye sees it and the mind reaches for a label and finds nothing, and for a moment there is a small, peculiar vertigo, a feeling of *almost understanding something fundamental about the nature of light and perception*, and then it passes, and the stone is just a stone again, and the color is just a color, and you cannot quite remember what was so strange about it.

## Sound and Silence

The quiet is the loudest thing here.

It is not the quiet of an empty room. It is the quiet of a place where *listening is the primary mode of engagement*. The mist moves through the basins and it does not make a sound. The water seeps from the stone and it does not make a sound. The moss grows and it does not make a sound. But there is a texture to the silence — a *grain*, like the grain of wood, like the grain of stone — and if you listen long enough, you begin to hear it. Not hear *something in* the silence. Hear *the silence itself*. The particular frequency of *this* quiet, *this* plateau, *this* ancient and thinning place.

When the wind comes — and it comes rarely, and it comes softly — it makes a sound that is almost subsonic, a vibration felt in the chest more than heard in the ear. It is the sound of the plateau's vastness, its sheer *extent*, the sound that a continent-sized mass of ancient stone makes when air moves across it. It rises and falls with a pattern that is not quite random and not quite rhythmic, and the people say that the wind is the plateau *breathing*, and they say this in a way that suggests they do not entirely mean it as a metaphor.

The settlement itself is quiet. People move with soft steps on soft soles. They speak in low tones. The sound of stone being shaped — the *tap tap tap* of a chisel, the *scrape* of a smoothing tool — carries across the distances and sounds like something geological, like the plateau continuing its own refinement.

At night — and the nights are long, the nights are *enormous*, the sky above the plateau is a dome of black punctured by stars that seem closer and sharper and more numerous than they should be — at night, the silence deepens to a degree that becomes almost physical. It presses. Not unpleasantly. But with *weight*, with *substance*, as though the silence has mass and you are immersed in it. People sleep lightly here. Not because they are afraid. Because the silence is so full that sleep becomes a different kind of experience — not rest, but *communion*, a merging with the vast quiet of the plateau, a dissolution of the boundary between the sleeper and the stone.

And sometimes, in the deepest hours of the night, when the mist is thickest and the cold is most absolute, there is a sound.

No one agrees on what it is. It is not loud. It is not close. It comes from *everywhere* and *nowhere* — from the stone, from the mist, from the thin air, from the vast dark sky above. It is not a tone. It is not a voice. It is a *something* that the ear receives and the brain cannot process, a signal that arrives at the threshold of perception and then *stops*, hovering there, never quite resolving into anything recognizable. It has been described as the sound of the folding-zones opening. It has been described as the sound of reality's erosion reaching a new depth. It has been described, by the oldest and most respected residents, as the sound the plateau made when it was first born, still echoing, still arriving, after all this time.

They do not fear it. They do not seek it. They simply note it, in the way they note the rain and the cold and the direction of the mist, and they continue their work.

## The Ecology of Absence

With no animals, the ecosystem operates on a loop that is almost *circular* — growth, death, decay, mineralization, regrowth — with very few branches or side-exits. The vegetation grows. It dies. It decomposes. The decomposition feeds the soil, such as it is. The minerals feed the stone. The stone, eroding, releases minerals into the water. The water feeds the vegetation. The loop is tight, efficient, almost *closed*.

But it is not entirely closed. The weirdness opens small exits — places where the loop feeds into something else, where the energy of growth is redirected into the folding, into the thinning of reality, into whatever process is slowly wearing away the fundamental structures of this place. Some of the plants seem to *know* this. The Fibonacci moss, the stone-penetrating ground-cover, the listening plants of the deep basins — they are the ones growing near the exits of the loop, near the places where reality is thinnest, and they are being *changed* by it, shaped by it, recruited into a process that is larger than ecology, larger than biology, larger than anything the settlement's people have a framework to understand.

The people do not worry about this. They have lived here for generations — for *centuries*, though their primitive technology and the time-distortions of the folding-zones make precise chronology impossible. They have watched the moss spiral. They have watched the roots penetrate stone. They have watched the mist move in patterns that are almost, but not quite, intelligent. They have adapted, as the plateau has adapted, as the reality itself has adapted — by *yielding*, by *settling*, by becoming whatever this place is asking them to become.

They are not afraid of the weirdness. They are not comfortable with it. They are *adjacent* to it, the way the moss is adjacent to the stone, the way the mist is adjacent to the water, the way the silence is adjacent to the sound. They exist in relationship to something they cannot name or fully perceive, and they have built their city in the place where that relationship is most *livable*, and they tend their gardens and shape their stones and speak in their low voices, and the plateau erodes, and reality thins, and the mist moves through the basins, and the water seeps from the ancient stone, and nothing hurries, and nothing is wasted, and the vast, high, cold, quiet, ancient, strange, living, sparse, urban, dense with absence and meaning — the plateau *is*.

That is the most important thing. It *is*. Persistently, quietly, with great patience, in a way that has nothing to do with any individual human life and everything to do with the slow, deep, incomprehensible processes that are unwinding the world at its edges.

You could stand here for a long time and not be bored. You could stand here forever and not understand it. Both of these are the same statement.
