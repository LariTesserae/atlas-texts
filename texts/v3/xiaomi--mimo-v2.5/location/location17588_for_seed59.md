# The Weft

## The Setting

The settlement of Sowren clings to a high basin in the Vaelcum Range, a shelf of old stone barely a half-kilometer across, set at an altitude where the air thins and cools but never quite dries. The basin is not natural in the way that word usually means. It is carved — not by tools, but by centuries of root action, fungal networks, and the slow hydraulic leverage of ten thousand plant species competing for the same stone. The rocks are rounded, pocked, riddled with channels. Water weeps from every surface. It runs in braided threads across the basin floor, pools in depressions of moss and peat, and drips steadily from every overhang. The air tastes of mineral and chlorophyll. Cloud often sits directly on the settlement, not above it — *in* it — so that people walk through a grey cool mist that beads on every vine-strut and thatched face, and by midmorning the entire settlement glistens.

The cool is constant and close. Not freezing. Never freezing. A persistent damp chill that settles into joints and timber alike, that keeps the body moving, the blood working. You never get fully dry here. Clothes mildew in a day if not aired. Wood darkens. Metal — what little there is, a few copper knives, some nail-like pegs of hammered bronze — develops a pale green skin within weeks. So metal is rare, kept wrapped, used sparingly. Almost everything is plant.

## The Architecture

Sowren is built the way a coral reef is built: by accumulation, by secretion, by organisms growing faster than they die. The structures are not carved from living trees so much as *directed* — generations of growers coaxing fast-growing species into arches, walls, platforms, and bridges, then killing the outer layers to form hard bark-shells while the inner wood stays alive and feeds new growth. The result is a settlement that looks less like a town and more like a single enormous organism that has learned to include human-shaped spaces within itself.

The main avenue — if you can call it that — is a tunnel of woven liana, thick as a man's torso, grown into a tube perhaps two meters in diameter. It branches and rebranches. The tunnels are not fixed. A path that was passable last season might be choked now with new growth, and a new passage may have opened where a wall collapsed into rot. The people of Sowren navigate by smell as much as sight. The tunnels smell different depending on what's growing there: sharp and acidic where bromeliad-analogues dominate, sweet and heavy where something like a night-blooming jasmine has taken hold, acrid and fungal where decomposition is winning.

Above the tunnel network, the main living platforms are built on the broad flat crowns of a dozen great woody structures — part tree, part constructed scaffold — that rise five to eight meters above the basin floor. These are the oldest living things in Sowren, some of them three or four hundred years old, their trunks as thick as houses, their roots gripping the stone basin with a grip that has slowly *become* the basin. People live in the canopy. They sleep in hammocks of woven grass-fiber strung between secondary branches. They cook on stone-lined platforms where fire is carefully contained — fire is the most dangerous technology they possess, and its use is governed by strict custom. One uncontrolled burn could consume the entire settlement in an afternoon, given how much of it is dry outer bark and dead vine-mass.

Storage structures hang like fruit from the undersides of the great platform-trees: gourd-like containers, some natural, some shaped over generations by selective growth, filled with dried food, smoked meat, medicinal pastes. The smell of smoke — fish-smoke, herb-smoke — threads through everything. It's one of the few scents that can cut through the pervasive green smell of the living wood.

## The Water

Water is not a feature of Sowren. It is the medium. The basin collects it from three sources: rainfall (frequent, sometimes daily, a cool steady drizzle that can last for days), seepage from the slopes above (carrying dissolved calcium and iron, staining the stone in streaks of ochre and rust-red), and condensation — the constant fog that deposits a fine film on every surface each morning.

The water does not drain quickly. The basin floor is a mosaic of stone, peat, and the dense root-mats of semi-aquatic plants that hold water like a sponge. Walking off-path in Sowren means stepping into ankle-deep muck, cool and brown and alive with movement. The muck teems. You feel things shift under your feet — roots, larvae, the flat bodies of amphibians. The children learn to walk lightly, almost skimming the surface, placing feet where they see the telltale raised hump of a root-mat rather than the flat sheen of open water.

There are no wells. There is no need. Water comes to you. It drips from the ceiling of every tunnel. It beads on every vine. In the dry intervals between rains — which are brief, sometimes only hours — a strange tension builds in the air, a heaviness, and then the mist thickens and the dripping resumes, and everyone relaxes. The people of Sowren are uncomfortable in dry air. Some say they can feel it in their teeth.

## The Fauna

This is the part that outsiders find hardest to adjust to. The *sound* of Sowren. The *movement*.

The animal life in the basin is not merely abundant. It is excessive. It is aggressive in its fecundity, in its occupation of every conceivable niche. The wet, warm-ish, dense-vegetation environment has produced an explosion of life that operates at a scale and intensity that overwhelms human senses and, frankly, human ability to manage.

**Insects** are the foundation. Not one or two dominant species but hundreds, thousands, occupying every level. There are beetles that bore into the living tunnel-walls and must be regularly scraped out or the structures weaken. There are moths with wingspans the size of a human palm, pale and wet-winged, that cluster in the cool hours of dawn on the bark-shells and must be brushed aside to open a door. There are ants — always ants — in disciplined columns that traverse the settlement without pause, carrying fragments of fungus, dead insects, the tiny bones of birds. The ants are not hostile, exactly, but they do not stop, and they do not care what they walk over. You learn to check your hammock before sleeping. You learn to shake out your clothes.

**Amphibians** breed in every pool, every cup of collected water, every bromeliad basin. Their calls are continuous — a wall of sound that rises and falls with temperature and humidity but never ceases. At night, the chorus is so loud it vibrates in the chest. There are small bright species that live inside the tunnel walls themselves, their calls echoing through the hollow spaces, giving the passages an eerie resonant hum. There are larger species, the size of a human hand, that sit motionless on branches and watch with gold-rimmed eyes, and that eat the moths.

**Birds** are numerous and varied, filling every aerial niche. Small swifts dart through the tunnel network itself, using the passages as hunting corridors for insects, their wings whispering against the woven walls. Larger species nest on the platform-trees and must be actively discouraged from roosting near living areas — their droppings are caustic and carry parasites. A species of honeyeater has learned to raid the smoke-dried food stores, and its raids are one of the few things that reliably draws a Sowren citizen to violence.

**Larger fauna** exists but is kept at bay. There are snakes — small, mostly arboreal, mostly harmless, though a few species are venomous and deeply unwelcome. There are rodents that gnaw at dried stores and must be fought with constant vigilance. And there are the **root-crawlers**: a family of slow, heavy-bodied creatures, perhaps the size of a large dog, that live in the peat-muck of the basin floor, feeding on submerged root-mass. They are not dangerous to humans, but they are large enough to damage the foundations of ground-level structures, and their movements send vibrations through the muck that can be felt on the platforms above.

The overall effect is of a place where the boundary between the human settlement and the wild ecosystem has essentially dissolved. The people of Sowren do not live *in* nature. They live *as* nature, or as close to it as a thinking species can manage. Every surface is inhabited. Every space is contested. The air itself seems alive with spore and pollen and the wings of small creatures.

## The People and Their Ways

Sowren holds perhaps three hundred people, though the number fluctuates. People come and go. It is not a closed community, but it is not an easy one to reach — the basin sits high, the approaches are steep and root-tangled, and the constant moisture makes stone slippery and paths unreliable.

The people are **growers**. Their primary skill, their cultural obsession, is the manipulation of plant growth. They do not think of it as agriculture. It is more like conversation. They have identified, over centuries, which species respond to which stimuli — certain sounds (they tap rhythmically on bark to encourage branching), certain substances applied to root zones (fermented insect waste, certain mineral-rich clays), certain pruning patterns. They grow a thick-fleshed fruit that is their staple food, something like a breadfruit crossed with a melon, starchy and mild-flavored, that ripens in clusters of four or five on vines trained along the tunnel walls.

They eat animal protein too — smoked fish from the mountain streams above, insects roasted on flat stones (a crunchy, nutty food that visitors find revolting until hunger overcomes squeamishness), and occasionally larger game, though this is rarer and carries prestige.

Their tools are simple. Stone axes with wooden handles. Knives of copper or bronze, precious and carefully maintained. Fire — always fire, always dangerous, always necessary. They use it to cauterize cut ends of living wood (preventing rot), to smoke food, to drive away swarms of biting insects during peak hatches, and to warm themselves in the worst of the cold damp, which penetrates everything. Fire is kept in stone-lined hearths on the main platforms. Never in the tunnels. *Never* in the tunnels. This is the one absolute rule of Sowren, taught to children before they can walk.

## The Turbulence

What makes Sowren feel truly *alive* — and somewhat precarious — is the rate of change. The combination of water and aggressive vegetation means that the settlement is in a constant state of construction and collapse. A new passage might open in a week as a fast-growing vine matures and is trained into an arch. A section of tunnel wall might fall away in a single night when a colony of boring beetles reaches critical mass. The great platform-trees grow slowly but steadily, their branches shifting position over years, meaning that hammock-attachment points must be periodically relocated, that the geometry of lived space is always slightly different than memory insists.

The people have adapted to this. They travel light. They own little that cannot be replaced. They carry knowledge of plant-growth in their bodies — the angle of a cut, the rhythm of a tap, the smell of healthy versus diseased growth — and they share it constantly, verbally, because written records in this environment would rot in a season.

There is an energy to the place, a buzzing forward momentum, that mirrors the ecology. Things grow and things die and things grow from the dying, and the people are caught in that current, not fighting it but swimming with it, adjusting and adapting and moving and building and abandoning and building again. It is exhausting to witness. It is presumably exhausting to live. But the people of Sowren do not seem to want another life. The dry places — the inland valleys, the cities of stone that outsiders build — seem to them like cemeteries. Dead material. No conversation. No growth. How could anyone bear it?

## A Moment

Dawn in Sowren. The fog has settled during the night and now sits thick among the tunnels, diffusing the early light into a soft, directionless glow. Everything drips. The sound of water is everywhere — on bark, on leaf, on the stretched skins of the hammocks where people sleep. The amphibian chorus has quieted to a murmur as the temperature drops to its overnight low. A child emerges from a hammock on the third platform of the great central tree, yawning, scratching at mosquito bites, and peers down through the lattice of branches at the basin floor below, where the peat-muck steams faintly in the cool air and a root-crawler's back, slick and dark, moves slowly through the shallow water like a surfacing log.

Somewhere in the tunnel network, someone is tapping rhythmically on a wall — *tok tok tok tok tok* — encouraging a new branch to split. A moth the size of the child's hand lands on the railing nearby, wings opening and closing slowly, dusted with moisture. The child does not look at it. There are always moths. The child swings down to the next level, bare feet finding the familiar grooves in the bark, and heads toward the smell of smoke and the promise of breakfast — the warm, starchy cut of fruit and the sharp tang of roasted beetle, and tea made from a leaf that tastes like mint and copper, drunk from a vessel that was, a month ago, still growing.
