# The Underbelly Furnaces

You descend through a shaft that was once circular and is now something closer to an oval, its walls polished to a dull sheen by centuries of water and heat. The air thickens with each step down. Not the sharp dampness of a cave, but a heavy, mineral warmth — the breath of something geological that has never stopped exhaling. The walls are slick. Not with water exactly, but with a thin film of condensation that gathers and re-gathers in perpetual slow cycles, never quite dripping, never quite dry. You can feel it on your forearms. It tastes of iron and something faintly alkaline, something that pricks at the back of the throat.

The shaft opens.

What you step into is a chamber — though *chamber* is an understatement that collapses under the weight of what it actually is. It's the size of a cathedral nave, roughly, with a ceiling that rises to perhaps thirty meters at its highest arch and slopes down to low galleries on either side. The floor is not flat. It has been shaped — by water, by heat, and by hands you cannot see the prints of anymore — into a landscape of broad, shallow basins and low ridges, like a relief map of a delta rendered in stone and mineral crust. Some of the basins hold standing water, black and still as obsidian glass. Others are choked with growth.

And there is so much growth.

Everything is growing. The walls are a vertical garden of things you have no names for — thick, fleshy fronds the color of dark jade, cascading in layered curtains from every ledge and crevice. They pulse with a slow, rhythmic contraction, not quite a breathing, more like the movement of something digesting. Between them, thinner filaments thread in dense networks, hairlike and translucent, catching what little light there is and holding it in their bodies until they glow — not brightly, not with any color you'd call vivid, but with a dim, persistent amber that turns the whole space the color of an old lantern. The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. There are no lamps, no fixtures, no bioluminescent hotspot. It is simply *in* the walls, in the vegetation, in the mineral deposits themselves. A phosphorescence that has been building, slowly, across geological time.

The plants — if they are plants — grow so thickly on the lower surfaces that the ground itself is obscured. You walk on a living carpet, springy and warm underfoot, that rises in mounded clumps around the bases of larger structures. These structures are the remains of industry.

They are not ruins exactly. Ruins imply collapse, disorder, the entropy of abandonment. These are more like *fossils of purpose* — the shapes of machines and conduits and housing blocks preserved in mineral accretion, still standing, still holding their geometry against the slow tide of growth. You can make out the arch of a boiler housing, its riveted plates now studded with nodules ofite and creeping moss-analog. A pipe runs along the far wall for fifteen meters before dissolving into the vegetal mass, still attached at intervals by brackets that someone bolted in, someone who expected that pipe to carry something somewhere. Perhaps it still does. You can hear it.

The sounds.

The chamber is *loud*, and this is the strangest thing about it, because there is no animal life here. No insects humming, no bats clicking in the upper galleries, no rodents in the walls. You have looked. You have listened past the noise for any creature-sound and found none. What fills the space instead is a layered, persistent mechanical and botanical cacophony.

There is a deep, resonant hum — barely a sound, more a vibration in the sternum — that seems to come from below the floor itself. Geothermal. Water moving through heated rock, steam pressing against stone, pressure cycling in slow geological rhythm. On top of that, the machinery. Some of it still runs. You can see a flywheel turning in the middle distance, its motion so slow it might be optical illusion, but it is turning, has been turning, is still turning. Gears mesh with the patient inevitability of something that has forgotten it was ever set in motion. A piston — or what was once a piston, now more of a mineral column with an internal reciprocating core — pushes upward and draws downward in a cycle that takes nearly ten seconds per stroke, producing a groaning, basso sound that vibrates the water in the nearest basin into concentric ripples.

And then there is the vegetation itself.

The thick fronds, when they contract, emit a sound — a creaking, fibrous noise, like the hull of a wooden ship in rough water. Multiply that by thousands. By the tens of thousands. Every surface that is alive is *producing*, and the aggregate is a sound like a forest in a hurricane, except there is no wind, and the motion is slow, and the trees are warm and wet and growing in a cave. It fills the space. It echoes and re-echoes off the stone and the mineral crust and the old iron, building into a wash of noise that is not unpleasant but is absolutely relentless. You feel it in your teeth.

The heat is constant. Not the sharp, dry heat of a desert, but a saturated, wet heat — the inside of a living body. Temperature holds at a level that would be unbearable in open air but here, in this enclosed stone volume with its slow air movement and its mineral drafts, is merely oppressive. You sweat. Your sweat does not evaporate. The air takes it in and gives it back, and the cycle is as patient and endless as everything else.

There are structures here that were not made by geology or growth. You find them at intervals — low walls of cut stone, arranged in rooms that open to the central nave. They are small, each one the size of a modest study, and they are partially claimed. The vegetation has entered through doorways and window-holes, has filled corners, has crept across floors in thick mats. But the walls remain, and in one you find a desk — not a desk, exactly, but a stone ledge that was shaped to serve as one — and on it, corroded beyond recognition but still holding its form, a device of some kind. Gears within gears, too small to have been driven by the great flywheel. A mechanism for measurement, perhaps. Or for recording. The mineral crust has sealed it shut.

You do not find bodies. You do not find tools, or personal effects, or anything that suggests specific human presence. What you find is *infrastructure* — the scale-system, the large and impersonal architecture of production. This place was built to *do something* at volume: to pump, to heat, to process, to refine. The geological heat was the engine. The water, channelled from above and from below, was the medium. What was produced here is unclear. The conduits lead into walls and disappear. The basins hold dark water that does not evaporate despite the heat, which should not be possible, which suggests the water is not entirely water.

The strangeness accumulates slowly, the way the mineral crust accumulates on everything. You notice that the vegetation does not grow toward the light — there is no directional source to grow toward. It grows toward *heat*, and the heat gradients in the chamber are subtle and shifting, which means the growth patterns shift too, slowly, over seasons that have nothing to do with the surface world. You notice that the old machinery, the flywheel, the piston, the gears — none of them are connected to anything. They drive nothing. They produce nothing. They simply *move*, powered by the geothermal hum, in circuits of motion that went purposeless long ago. You notice that the loudness is not chaotic. If you listen long enough — and you will, because leaving this place requires walking back up the shaft, and your legs are heavy with heat — you begin to hear a pattern. A cycle. The creaking of the fronds rises and falls in a rhythm that repeats every forty minutes or so, and when it peaks, the flywheel speeds up — barely, a fraction of a rotation faster — and when it subsides, the piston slows. The vegetation and the machinery are synchronized. They have adapted to each other. Or they have become each other.

The slow dynamics of this place are the most disorienting thing. Everything here operates on timescales that make a human lifespan feel like a single beat. The mineral crust grows a millimeter per decade. The water cycles take years. The machinery will run, or something like it will run, until the heat source below exhausts itself, which may be millions of years from now. You are a brief, hot, sweating interruption in a process that does not require you and will not remember you. The plants do not react to your presence. The sounds do not change. The dim amber light does not brighten or dim. You are inside a mechanism that is also an ecosystem that is also a geological event, and all three of those things are the same thing, and they have been settling into equilibrium since before your species learned to make fire.

You should leave. The air is thin in a way that has nothing to do with oxygen and everything to do with *density* — the sheer volume of sound and moisture and heat and slow mineral exhalation pressing against you. But for a moment you stand at the edge of one of the black-water basins and look down into it, and it looks back up at you with a surface so still it might be solid, and in its depths — or in your reflection, you cannot tell — something moves with the same patient, purposeless, eternal motion of everything else in this place.

You climb back up the shaft. The air cools. The sounds fade. The light changes from amber to grey to the white of an overcast sky, and you emerge into a world that moves too fast, that changes too quickly, that has not yet found its equilibrium. You carry the taste of iron and alkaline water. You carry the hum in your sternum. It will take hours to fade. Some of it never does.
