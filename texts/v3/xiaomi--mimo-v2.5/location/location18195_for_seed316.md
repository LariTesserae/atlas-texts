# The Brine Flats of Eru An

You see it from very far away, if you come from any direction — or think you do. A dark line on the horizon that refuses to stay a line. It smears, thickens, becomes a bruise-coloured band, and then it is not a horizon anymore but a wall of hanging moisture that has no distinct edge. You walk toward it. The ground has been nearly flat for longer than most geological processes can account for. Every hill that ever existed here has been ground down to its last syllable. What remains is a surface like the palm of an impossibly old hand — creased, but only just. Barely perceptible undulations. Ridges no taller than your ankle that run for hundreds of kilometers in branching, dendritic patterns, the ghosts of river deltas that died before life had learned to crawl.

---

## The Ground

It is not really ground in the way you think of ground.

Beneath your feet — or above them, depending on which way the surface is behaving at the moment — is a mineral substrate so ancient and so thoroughly worked by erosion that it has reached a state almost like the end of a sentence. Everything that can be dissolved has been dissolved. Everything that can be ground to powder has been ground to powder. What remains is the residue of deep time: dense, crystalline, interlocking matrices of mineral that have no pore space between them. Not a gap. Not a void. Every cubic centimetre is occupied. The ground is *saturated with itself*.

This gives it properties that are hard to anticipate. It is warm — not from geothermal heat, though some of that too, but because the mineral mass holds thermal energy like a fist holds a stone. The warmth radiates upward constantly, and it is the warmth of something that has been absorbing sunlight and chemical energy for aeons without ever cooling. You can feel it through your soles if you are barefoot. It is the temperature of a body that has forgotten it has a body.

The colour of the surface shifts. Most of the time it is a deep, wet iron-grey, like the belly of a storm cloud seen from above. But there are veins and pockets where different mineral suites dominate — rust-reds that bleed into the grey like wounds, pale calcitic streaks that catch what little light there is and hold it for a moment too long, bands of something almost violet that seem to pulse if you stare at them. The surface is not smooth. It is covered in a fine crystalline texture, like frost, except it is warm, and it does not melt, and when you run your finger across it the crystals catch on your skin and leave a faint, mineral taste on your fingertip.

Some of these formations are clearly structured — not biological, but *arranged*. Stacked columns of crystallized mineral, two or three metres tall, growing in clusters of four or six like petrified organ pipes. They appear to be growing even as you watch, though so slowly that you cannot be certain. They hum. Not metaphorically. They produce a faint, grinding overtone, the sound of a crystal lattice adding a new atom to its face, multiplied across millions of simultaneous events.

## The Water

It is everywhere, but you would not call it a lake.

The water here exists in a state between surface water and atmosphere. The air is so warm and so dense with mineral vapour that water does not behave according to the rules you grew up with. It condenses on surfaces that are not there — you see curtains of moisture hanging in mid-air, shimmering like heat haze, five metres above the ground, defying any visible means of support. You walk through them. They are warm, mineral-heavy, and they leave a film on your skin that dries into a fine, glittering dust within minutes.

Where water does collect on the surface, it is shallow — rarely more than a hand's depth — and so loaded with dissolved minerals that it has the viscosity of light oil. It moves slowly, almost reluctantly, spreading into thin, branching sheets across the mineral flats and then being reabsorbed into the substrate or lifted back into the air. The movement is not random. There are patterns — spiral patterns, fractal branching, hexagonal cells that form and reform over the course of hours. You realise, with a feeling you cannot quite name, that the water is not flowing downhill. There is no downhill. The water is flowing toward the nearest concentration of mineral activity, which can be in any direction, including upward.

In some places the water has pooled into deeper basins — the only depressions in the landscape, carved not by erosion but by chemical dissolution from below. These pools are dark, almost black, and they steam gently. The surface of each pool vibrates at a frequency just below hearing, and when you kneel beside one and look down, you see your reflection, but it is not your reflection — it is a slightly different you, moving slightly out of sync, as though the pool contains a version of the world that is running a fraction of a second behind.

The atmosphere itself is saturated. The humidity is not a percentage you would recognise. The air is so thick with moisture and mineral aerosol that visibility is reduced to a few kilometres on a "clear" day, and on other days — most days — it contracts to a few hundred metres or less. Light does not scatter through this atmosphere the way it should. It arrives diffuse and directionless, a dim, warm glow that seems to emanate from everywhere and nowhere. There are no shadows. There have never been shadows, as far as the landscape is concerned. The light simply *is*, the way heat is — present, ambient, undifferentiated.

## The Sound

It is deafening.

This is difficult to reconcile with the apparent emptiness of the place — sparse life, no visible machinery, nothing you would recognise as a noise-making object. But the sound is everywhere, and it is enormous, and it comes from the landscape itself.

There is the mineral grinding — the sound of crystalline growth at planetary scale. Billions of crystals, across a continent, simultaneously adding atoms to their lattices. It should be inaudible. It is not. It produces a vast, droning, harmonic chord that you feel in your chest before you hear it in your ears. The pitch shifts slowly over hours, sometimes over minutes, as different mineral suites dominate different regions. It is the sound of the planet thinking about geometry.

There is the water — not flowing, but condensing and evaporating in cycles so rapid that the air itself oscillates. Each condensation event is tiny. Across this landscape, there are trillions per second. They produce a continuous, soft, percussive roar, like the sound of rain on a tin roof heard from inside a cathedral made of tin roofs. Underneath this, the deeper bass of evaporation — water molecules tearing themselves free from the mineral surface and punching into the thick air, each one producing a pressure wave so small it should be nothing, but together they create a subsonic rumble that never stops, that has never stopped, that is as fundamental to this place as gravity.

There are the fracture events. The mineral substrate is not entirely stable. Despite being ancient and settled and at its final form, the sheer density of material means that stress accumulates — thermal expansion, crystal growth pressure, the weight of saturated air. When the stress exceeds the local tolerance, the mineral fractures. Not violently. Not in earthquakes, exactly. In sharp, cracking, reports that carry for enormous distances across the flat landscape — *tak-tak-tak* — like a giant walking on marble. Sometimes a fracture will propagate for kilometres along a crystal boundary, producing a sound like tearing silk amplified through a tannoy system.

And then there are the other sounds. The ones you cannot attribute to any physical process. The harmonic that is too regular to be natural. The low, rhythmic pulse that sounds almost like breathing. The occasional sharp, tonal note — a single, clear frequency, sustained for three or four seconds, rising in pitch, then cutting off — that seems to come from inside your own skull.

## The Weirdness

It is not a single event. It is not a phenomenon you can point to. It is a *quality* of the place, like humidity or warmth, except it operates on the substrate of reality itself.

The simplest way to describe it: here, the rules that govern matter and space are *worn thin*.

This place is old. Not old the way mountains are old. Old the way a word is old when you have repeated it so many times it has lost its meaning. The laws of physics have been operating here for so long, on this particular configuration of matter, that they have developed... habits. Ruts. Places where they don't quite apply anymore, not because they have been violated, but because they have been *satisfied* so completely that they have become redundant.

The evidence is everywhere, once you learn to see it.

**Distance is unreliable.** You walk toward a mineral formation that appears to be a kilometre away. After ten minutes of walking, it appears to be exactly the same distance away. You are not moving toward it. The landscape between you and it is expanding at precisely the rate you are closing. You try walking in a different direction. The formation slides into your path. You turn again. It follows. You stop. After a long time — minutes? longer? — the formation simply *is* there, beside you, as though it had always been there, as though the concept of "approach" was a polite fiction the landscape had been maintaining for your benefit and has now abandoned.

**The surface duplicates.** You look down and see the mineral flat stretching away from you. You look up and see the same flat, from the same angle, extending in the opposite direction — but reflected, as though the sky were a mirror, except there is no mirror, there is only the same dark, wet, crystalline ground going on forever in a direction that should be sky. You wave your hand. The reflection does not wave. It continues doing whatever it was doing, which appears to be nothing, which appears to be waiting.

**Time loops locally.** You see a mineral fracture propagate across the surface — *tak-tak-tak* — and then you see it propagate again, along exactly the same path, with exactly the same sound. And again. And again. Each repetition is identical except that the sound is very slightly louder, as though it is approaching from a great distance and arriving here, in this spot, repeatedly. The fracture has not happened four times. It has happened once, but its sound is arriving from four different temporal positions simultaneously, because time, here, is not the smooth river you were promised. It is a lake with no outlets, and the stones you throw into it send ripples in every direction, including backward.

**Matter forgets its state.** There are moments — brief, disorienting — when the ground beneath you is simultaneously solid and liquid. Not in the sense of mud. In the sense that the crystalline matrix is *both*, that it is observing two contradictory physical states at once and the act of observation is what resolves it, and no one is observing it, so it doesn't bother resolving. You feel this. The surface yields under your foot like water and then catches you like stone and then releases you like water again, and the transition is not instantaneous but *gradual*, and during the gradual transition there is a third state that has no name, and in that state you can feel the mineral thinking.

**Geometry is optional.** The mineral columns — the stacked, organ-pipe formations — should obey Euclidean space. They do not. They are taller on the inside than the outside. They contain more volume than their external dimensions permit. You can look into a column from the top and see a space that descends far deeper than the column's height — a shaft of crystalline interior that goes down, and down, and down, and at the bottom there is light, and the light is warm, and it is the same light that is above you, diffused and directionless, as though the interior of the column has folded space to include the sky.

## The Life

Sparse, but present.

There are no animals. Not in any form you would recognise. The few living things here are at the boundary between chemistry and biology — mineral-silk organisms that grow along the crystal surfaces in thin, branching filaments, white against the dark grey, almost invisible unless you are close. They are not plants. They do not photosynthesize. They feed on the mineral gradients in the substrate, drawing energy from the differences between one crystal lattice and another, which, given the density and variety of mineral here, is a rich source. They grow slowly. They are very old individually, though their age is hard to measure because they bud and fragment and rejoin, and the concept of a single individual is a stretch.

There is one other thing.

You notice it the way you notice a draught — not by seeing it but by sensing the shape of its absence. Somewhere in this landscape, something large is moving. Not often. Not quickly. But with the unmistakable weight and intention of a living thing that has chosen, for reasons you will never know, to remain at the very edge of perception. You see disturbances in the mineral flat — smooth channels, carved not by water but by *passage*, curving away into the haze. They are warm to the touch, warmer than the surrounding mineral, and they are fresh, though fresh in a place where time loops and fractures arrive from four directions is a word that has lost some of its certainty.

Whatever is making these trails does not appear in the haze. You do not see it. You hear it, occasionally — a deep, infrasonic pulse, too low for your ears, felt in the marrow of your bones, the way you feel the approach of a storm before the wind arrives. It is not hostile. You have no evidence for this. You have only a feeling, which in a place where reality is worn thin and the ground thinks about geometry, may be the most reliable data available.

## The Remnants

There are structures here. Old ones.

They are not buildings. They were never buildings. They are arrangements — stones placed in patterns, but not by hands. The mineral substrate has, in a few locations, spontaneously organized into configurations that are too regular to be natural and too abstract to be useful. Concentric rings, fifty metres across, of precisely stacked mineral blocks, each block identical in size. Spirals of crystal that follow the Fibonacci sequence with mathematical exactness. A single, freestanding arch, fifteen metres tall, of a mineral that does not exist anywhere else in the landscape — pale, almost white, warm to the touch, and emitting a tone so high it is at the very edge of hearing, a sound like a finger on wet glass, continuous, unwavering.

These structures were not built by the organisms. They were not built by anything. They are what happens when matter reaches a certain density and age and degree of reality-thinning: it begins to *arrange itself*, not with purpose but with the deep, structural inevitability of a crystal forming in a supersaturated solution. The landscape is its own architect, and its blueprints are the laws of physics, which here have been running so long they have begun to loop, and in looping they have begun to repeat themselves, and in repeating they have begun to create — not life, not thought, but *pattern*, which is the thing that comes before both.

---

## The Whole of It

Stand here — if you can stand; the surface may be solid or it may be the third thing — and try to comprehend the scale. This is not a landscape in the way a valley or a plain is a landscape. This is a *condition*. It extends to every horizon, and the horizons curve, and beyond the curve there is more of the same, because this is not a place on a planet so much as a planet that has become a place. The erosion has stripped it to its essence. The age has worn the rules of reality thin. The density has packed every possible atom into every available space. The wetness has made the whole thing fluid, alive with phase changes, the mineral and the aqueous and the atmospheric all blurring into each other until the ground is the air is the water is the ground.

And through all of it, the sound. That vast, layered, impossible sound — the chord of crystallization, the roar of condensation, the crack of fracture, the pulse of something large and unseen moving through the mineral dark. The sound is the place. The place is the sound. You cannot separate them. You cannot stand outside it and observe it, because outside it does not exist, because this is scale-of-its-own-physics, continental, planet-wide, and you are a small, warm, temporary thing standing in the middle of a world that has been ending and beginning simultaneously for longer than the word "time" has meant anything.

The dim, ambient light catches on a wet mineral surface. The surface has six sides. The surface is thinking. The sound changes pitch. The arch hums. The water moves sideways. The ground is solid. The ground is liquid. The ground is the third thing. Something large passes beneath you, or behind you, or in a direction that has not been invented yet, and the mineral trail it leaves is warm, and it smells of ozone and deep stone, and it leads nowhere, and it leads everywhere, and it is the only path in a place where paths have no meaning, and you follow it because there is nothing else to do, because the landscape has decided that you are here, and here is the only direction it knows.
