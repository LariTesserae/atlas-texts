# The Packed Bathyal

---

You would not call it a city. You would not have the word for it yet, if you came upon it — a cluster of angular forms so tightly nested that the eye struggles to find where one structure ends and the next begins, like vertebrae of something that died mid-flexion. The whole of it occupies a space you could cross in twenty steps. That is part of the strangeness.

It sits on a shelf of volcanic glass — young basalt, still black and sharp-edged, not yet softened by any of the usual patient abrasions of the deep. The rock was born recently in geological terms, pushed up in a single violent event, and the settlement clings to it the way a barnacle clings to a whale that hasn't yet moved. The basalt's edges are knife-clean. They catch light they have no business catching.

Because there is light here. That is the first impossible thing.

At this depth — and it is considerable, hundreds of meters below any surface the sun still touches — there should be only the slow dark, the occasional spark of bioluminescence from something alive. But the structures radiate. Not warmly. The light they give off is pale, blue-white, almost clinical, and it emanates from the materials themselves. The walls are not lit by fixtures. The walls *are* the light. It pulses faintly, irregularly, as though the buildings are breathing or thinking or both, and in the gaps between pulses the darkness rushes in and then is pushed back again. The rhythm is not quite regular enough to be mechanical. It is not quite irregular enough to be organic. It lives in the uncanny interval between.

The water is everywhere, of course. It fills every alley, every crevice, presses against every surface with the patient, equal weight of a thing that has never learned to be gentle. There is no dome, no membrane, no bubble of air held artificially in place. The settlement is fully, completely submerged. Whatever inhabits it — or whatever *inhabited* it — breathes this water or does not breathe at all. The water is cool, perhaps eight or nine degrees, and it moves. Not in currents exactly. In something more deliberate. It flows through channels cut into the basalt and threaded between the structures in patterns that feel choreographed, as though the water itself is being told where to go. It murmurs and grates and hums as it does. The sound is constant and layered — a low foundational drone from somewhere deep in the rock, overlaid with higher tones that rise and fall like a language without pauses for breath. It is loud here. Not violent. But insistent. The kind of loud that fills your skull and becomes its own silence after a while.

The buildings — if they are buildings — are constructed from a material that does not look like concrete or metal or stone. It is dark and faintly translucent, like obsidian that has been melted and re-formed by something other than heat. It has a tightness to it, a density, as though every cubic centimeter of it was compressed until the atoms had nowhere left to move. There is no mortar between the structures. They are not joined. They simply occupy the same space, or nearly so — packed against one another with gaps measured in finger-widths, so narrow that the water passing between them sings a thin, high note. The architecture is angular but not geometric. The angles do not repeat. Each face, each edge, each corner seems to have been resolved individually, as though each structure is a solution to a different problem. And yet they all belong together. They form a coherent whole the way a reef forms a coherent whole — through proximity and pressure and time, though there has been very little time here.

There is vegetation. Sparse. A few fronds of something that is not quite algae and not quite fungal — pale, almost colorless, growing in the narrow interstices where structures meet rock. It moves with the water. It is the only thing in this place that moves *with* the water rather than *against* it. Otherwise, the absence of animal life is profound and eerie. No fish drift through the corridors. No invertebrate clings to the walls. No micro-swarm of copepod or amphipod fills the water column with tiny, frantic life. The water is clear — startlingly, unnaturally clear, as though it has been filtered of everything biological. The sparse vegetation is alone. It has no pollinator, no grazer, no predator. It grows in silence and silence grows around it.

The strangeness deepens if you look closely. The structures are not static. They shift. Not dramatically — you might watch for a full minute and see nothing — but over the span of several minutes, certain surfaces will have reoriented. An angle that pointed northwest now points north-northwest. A face that was flat now carries a slight concavity. The changes are so small they feel like hallucinations, but they are real, and they are continuous. The settlement is *adjusting*. To what pressure, what current, what signal, it is impossible to say. It moves the way a plant moves toward light, except there is no light here that it did not create itself, and the motion has no obvious purpose.

And here is the thing that unsettles most: the technology that made this place is not visible. There are no conduits, no control panels, no screens, no lights that are *lights* rather than *walls that happen to glow*. Whatever engineering holds this settlement together — whatever keeps the water flowing in its choreographed patterns, whatever generates the light, whatever compresses the building material into its impossible density — it operates at a scale or through a principle that leaves no artifact behind. It is as though the place was not built but *convinced* into existence. As though someone stood on this fresh basalt shelf and simply insisted, very firmly, that a city should be here, and the rock and the water and the physics of the deep complied.

It is active. Processes run constantly, though they produce no product you can identify. The light pulses. The water circulates. The structures shift. The drone persists. It is a place caught in the act of *being*, continuously, without rest, without audience. The noise never stops. It does not build to anything. It does not resolve. It simply continues — dense and complex and total, the way a body's metabolism continues, the way a heart's electrical rhythm continues, always, whether or not anyone is listening.

No one is listening. No one is here. The water is cool and clear and alive with sound and light, and the small, sharp, impossible city sits on its young black rock and breathes its blue-white breath and adjusts its angles by increments too small to name, and it does this for no one, and it has done it for no one, and it will continue to do it for no one, in the absolute dark of a place that has somehow made its own light.
