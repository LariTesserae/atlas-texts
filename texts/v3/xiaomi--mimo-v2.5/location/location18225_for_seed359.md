# The Made Basin

The ground is not ground.

You understand this immediately underfoot, though the understanding arrives sideways, the way you know a dream is a dream. The surface gives slightly — warm, dry, faintly granular — and it is *even*. Not flat the way earth is flat after rain, but even the way a table is even, the way something made by hands is even. It extends in every direction to about the distance of a shout, and then the walls begin.

The walls are young stone. Fresh stone — you can see the fracture planes still sharp as cuts, corners that have never been softened by weather because there has been no weather to soften them. They are pale, almost white, stacked in courses that are nearly regular. Nearly. Each block is slightly different from the one beside it, shaped with care but without templates. Some are rectangular, some are trapezoidal, some are just *fitted* — pressed into place until they held. Mortar of some kind fills the gaps:iteiteiteiteite — aiteiteiteite — aiteite — aite — aiteite — aite — a materialite that looksite likeite driedite pasteite,iteite a compoundite ofite baked clayite and something fibrous,ite something that was alive but is not now. The walls rise to about twice a man's height, and beyond them the sky is a flat, warm, pale grey, without cloud or feature, as though it too has been placed there by hand.

The basin is small. You could walk across it in two or three minutes. And yet it is *full*.

The vegetation is impossible and everywhere.

It grows from the made ground — and that is the first wrongness — in dense, layered profusion. Broad, flat fronds like the leaves of ferns, but they are not green. They are a deep, warm amber, the color of light through honey, and they are *thick* — not thick as in tall, but thick as in each individual leaf has substance, density, weight. They do not droop. They hold themselves rigid on stiff stalks that emerge directly from cracks in the paved surface, as though they have forced their way through the construction from beneath. There are hundreds of them. Thousands. They fill the basin in clusters and thickets, shoulder-high, hip-high, knee-high, and lower — a dense understory of smaller fronded things that carpet the ground between the larger growths. The colors range from that deep amber to a pale, almost golden yellow, with occasional specimens that are a startling copper-red, metallic in the warm, normal light.

There is no water. You look for it and it is not there. Not in the air — the air is dry and warm, the dryness of a kiln, of a sealed room where bread has been baked and left to cool. Not in the ground — the constructed surface shows no moisture, no dampness, no seepage. The walls hold no condensation. There is no stream, no basin of standing water, no dew on the fronds. And yet the vegetation is lush, vigorous, abundant. It *thrives*. This is the central contradiction of the place, and it does not resolve. You simply stand in it: thriving growth, no water. Both true. Neither explained.

The fauna lives among the fronds. You hear it before you see it — a rustling, dry and papery, like someone turning pages in another room. Small things move through the undergrowth. They are the size of mice or small birds, and they are difficult to see clearly. The basin is dim in the way that any dense thicket is dim — the amber fronds filter the flat sky-light into something golden and low. What you glimpse: shapes that are round and quick. Surfaces that might be smooth chitin or might be tightly furled skin. They are pale — cream-colored, or the same amber as the vegetation — and they move with a strange, patient deliberation, stopping often, holding still for long intervals, then continuing. There are many of them. The rustling comes from everywhere.

One lands on a frond near your hand. It is the size of a sparrow. It has a body that is almost spherical, six thin legs gripping the rigid leaf surface, and a face — if it is a face — that is a smooth, convex plane with two darker spots that might be eyes. It sits motionless, regarding you or not regarding you, and you feel the heat coming off its body, or off the frond beneath it, or off the made ground below. Everything in the basin is the same temperature: warm, dry, steady.

---

The builders are still here.

You find their tools first. A stone adze leans against the base of the wall, its blade a chipped semicircle of dark basalt, its handle a straight piece of the same pale stone as the walls, fitted into a bored hole with the same fibrous paste that serves as mortar. It is the only tool you can find. It might be the only tool there is. The walls were built with patience, not with equipment — each block shaped by chipping, by abrasion, by the slow application of hand-pressure and persistence. You can see the marks: fine, parallel scratches where something was rubbed back and forth, back and forth, wearing the stone down. Some of these scratch patterns are almost decorative. Someone worked slowly enough, carefully enough, to make their tool-marks beautiful.

There are no people. There are no skeletons, no remains, no signs of departure. The basin is simply occupied by everything *except* its builders: the walls they made, the ground they laid, the vegetation they somehow planted or encouraged, the creatures that came or were brought. The adze waits against the wall as though someone set it down moments ago and will return after a rest.

The absence is quiet. The whole basin is quiet. The rustling of the fauna is a sound that belongs to silence — it measures it, defines it, the way a single fish moving through still water makes the stillness more visible. There are no birds, no wind, no distant noise of any kind. The sky beyond the walls makes no sound. The ground, the stone, the dry fronds — all of it holds silence the way the walls hold heat: completely, as a property of the material itself.

---

And then the wrongness speaks.

You have been looking at the far wall. It is twelve paces away, maybe fifteen, and you have been looking at it without looking at it, the way you look at a wall in any room you've been standing in for a while. It is pale stone, stacked courses, sharp edges. You have seen it. You know it.

You look again and it is closer.

Not dramatically closer. Not lunging. But it is — you count the courses — three courses nearer than it was, and you did not hear it move, and there is no sound of grinding or shifting, and the fauna among the fronds has not reacted, and the dust on the ground at the base of the wall is undisturbed. It is simply closer. As though the space between you and it quietly, without fuss, decided to be less.

You turn. The wall behind you is the same distance it was. The walls on either side are the same. You look back. The far wall has not moved back. It has not compensated. It is where it is, and it is simply three courses closer than you remember, and there is no evidence that it was ever any farther away.

This is what *reality breakdown* means here, at 2.9. It is not violent. It is not dramatic. It is *architectural*. The basin is not dissolving or screaming or warping in any way that a sane person would call a crisis. It is simply... inaccurate. Distances are not reliable. You walk toward the wall and it does not get closer at the rate it should. You walk away and the basin does not get wider. At one point you are certain you have walked in a circle and arrive at the adze leaning against the wall, and then you walk the same circle again and do not arrive at the adze, and when you look for it it is behind you, leaning against a wall that you thought was on the far side of the basin, in a spot where you are now certain you stood moments ago and where it was not.

The vegetation does not notice. The fauna does not notice. The walls do not acknowledge the adjustments they make. The basin tolerates its own impossibility with the patience of something that has been tolerating it for a long time — since the beginning, perhaps, since the builders first laid the ground and set the first stone and planted the first amber frond in the dry, dry earth. Perhaps they built it this way. Perhaps the wrongness is a feature, a material property of the basin, like the sharpness of the stone or the warmth of the air. Perhaps the builders knew that the basin could not be made right and built it anyway, with their hand-tools and their fibrous paste, and left their adze against the wall and walked into the wrongness and did not come back, or came back different, or came back as one of the round pale things rustling through the fronds.

You cannot tell. The basin does not say. The basin is warm, and dry, and full, and quiet, and it changes slowly — so slowly that the changes are only visible in retrospect, when you notice that a frond has grown two inches, or that the wall is now five courses closer, or that there is a creature on your hand that was not there before and is not there now but left behind a circle of warmth on your skin, a small dry heat, like a thumbprint from a kiln.

You could stay here. The basin would let you. It has room — it is small, but the wrongness means it is also larger than it should be, and the denseness of the vegetation means there are always more fronds than you can see, always more rustling just beyond the edge of your vision. You could sit on the warm, made ground and watch the amber fronds filter the flat light and listen to the dry silence and let the walls move around you at their own pace, in their own direction, according to whatever geometry the basin has decided is true today.

The basin is young. The stone is sharp. The vegetation is vigorous. The creatures are numerous. Whatever this place is, whatever made it and whatever it is becoming, it is not old. It is not finished. The slow changes — the growing fronds, the shifting walls, the gradual accretion of dust on the adze — these are processes that are still underway, still accumulating, still building toward something that the basin has not yet decided to be. The builders set the terms. The basin is still reading them.

You pick up the adze. It is warm. The handle fits your hand the way it must have fit someone else's — imperfectly, because it was made for a different grip, but well enough to hold. The basalt blade catches the flat light and holds it, dark and smooth, with the scratch-patterns of its shaping visible along its edge like a language in a script you cannot read.

You set it back down. In the spot where it was. Against the wall where it was.

It is three courses farther away than you remember.
