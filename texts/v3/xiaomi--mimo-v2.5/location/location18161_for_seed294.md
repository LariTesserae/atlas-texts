# The Undercathedral

You are deep. The water is so far beneath any surface that the concept of "above" has become theoretical — a memory belonging to the geology itself, not to anything currently alive. The cold here is not the cold of winter or even the cold of a cellar. It is the cold of *depth*, of pressure, of a thermal reality that has never once, in the entire history of this place, known warmth. Your body would shut down in minutes. Your thoughts would slow. Even the idea of warmth feels like a foreign language.

And yet things live here.

---

The structures are enormous.

They are not ruins, exactly, because they were never finished — or rather, they were never *started* in any sense a human architect would recognize. They are grown. Every surface bears the texture of secretion, of accretion, of something laid down in layers over incomprehensible spans. Vast walls of calcium-mineral composite rise from ridgelines that may themselves be the tops of older structures, which in turn were secreted atop still older ones. The layering goes down into the sediment for hundreds of meters — you can feel it in the way the seafloor *hums* with density, compacted under its own accumulated history. Everything here has been settled into its final form, but that final form was never a form anyone chose. It is the form that results when something biological builds without intention, without blueprint, without end — and then stops.

The material is not stone, not coral, not bone. It is something closer toite — aite,ite,ite — aite, no. It is aite — *ite*. Mineralite. Aite. Calciumite. Let me say it plainly: it is a calcite-ceramic, grown biologically, with the structural density ofite boneite and the hardness ofiteiteiteiteiteiteite — 

I need to slow down.

The material is *ite*. Think of it this way: if a bone could become a mountain, and if that mountain could be polished smooth by currents for a billion years, you would begin to understand the surfaces here. They are pale in the dim light — not white, not grey, but something in between, the color of deep ocean snow, the color of light that has never been sunlight. Smooth. Rounded at edges where the current has worked them. But at their core, in the deep interior of each structure, the material is dense and layered like the rings of an impossibly ancient tree. You can read the accretion history in cross-sections where walls have cracked open. Each ring is a slightly different mineral composition, a slightly different color. Some rings are thicker than others. Some are so thin they suggest a season, or an event — a surge of nutrients, a shift in chemistry, a spawning cycle from whatever organisms built this place and are now gone from it.

Or not entirely gone.

---

The fauna is real and present, though scattered through the spaces with the sparseness of a city after a catastrophe. You will wait some time between encounters. But when one comes, it is unmistakable.

There are large things — creatures the size of horses, at least — that move slowly along the walls, their bodies pressed flat against the calcite surfaces. They feed on the surface layer, scraping it away with mouthparts you would rather not examine closely. They are the color of the walls themselves, pale and faintly luminous with a bioluminescence so dim it registers more as a warmth in the visual field than as actual light. They leave trails behind them — smooth grooves in the mineral surface, the thinnest possible erosion on top of erosion that has already been working for eons. The grooves are fresh. You can see the powdered mineral drifting away from them like smoke. These creatures are eating the architecture. They are eating the *history*.

Between the large grazers, smaller things dart. Quick, angular, built like arrowheads. They feed on — what? The grazers, sometimes, when one is injured or molting. The sediment. Each other. Each other mostly. There is no plant base to this ecosystem. There never was. The entire food web, from its deepest root to its highest predator, runs on *chemistry* — on the mineral gradients of the structures themselves, on the thermal micro-currents where slightly warmer water meets colder, on the slow radioactive decay of trace elements in the deepest walls. The creatures are not merely living *in* a mineral world. They are living *off* it, the way a forest lives off sunlight. The structures are both habitat and sun.

This is what the builders were doing. You understand it now, watching a grazer scrape pale dust from a wall that rises into darkness above. They were not building shelter or a city or a monument. They were *farming*. Growing the substrate that would feed everything that came after. Layer by layer, over eons, the organisms that came before laid down the food supply of everything alive now. And then they stopped, or died, or became something else, and the structures have been slowly, slowly, slowly being consumed ever since.

The erosion is ancient. The walls were once angular, perhaps. Sharp-edged. Defined. Now everything is smoothed, rounded, melted-looking. The corridors between structures — if you can call them corridors, they have no intentional geometry — curve and widen and narrow like the passages in a termite mound, like the chambers inside a bone. The current moves through them in patterns that are almost but not quite predictable. Almost. There is turbulence here that feels excessive for the apparent geometry. Water should flow more smoothly through spaces this large and this open. But it doesn't. It eddies and reverses and piles against itself in invisible standing waves. The pressure differentials are strange. Something about the architecture — the layered mineral, the varying densities, the microscopic crystalline structures within the walls — is doing something to the water itself, refracting its flow the way a lens refracts light.

---

The light is almost nothing.

There is bioluminescence from the fauna, but it is thin, tentative, the color of deep bruises — blue-violets and the faintest greens. It comes and goes as creatures pass through your field of vision and then leave it. Between their passages, the darkness is complete, and the only sense of the space comes from the current against your skin, from the pressure, from the way sound behaves here.

Sound. It is mostly quiet, yes. The water dampens almost everything into a low, omnipresent murmur — the sound of immense pressure doing what immense pressure does, which is pressing. But the turbulence creates interruptions. Every few seconds, a shift in current produces a tone — a low moan or a higher, keening whistle — as water moves through a crevice or over a ledge. The structures *sing*, in a way. Not melodiously. Not intentionally. But the layered mineral, with its varying densities and its ancient, carved channels, acts as an instrument played by the ocean itself. Each structure has its own resonant frequency. When the current hits it right, the entire mass vibrates at a pitch that is felt in the chest more than heard in the ear. Walk — drift — past a wall and you pass through a sequence of these frequencies, a chord progression of deep tones that shift as the current shifts, as your position shifts, as the water itself reshapes the conditions of hearing from moment to moment.

And beneath it all, beneath even the low groan of the walls, there is a sound that might not be sound at all. A rhythmic pulse. Felt rather than heard. Coming from somewhere below, from deep within the accumulated layers of grown architecture. It could be tectonic. It could be tidal. It could be something in the structures themselves — some remnant of the building process, some mechanism laid down in the deepest, oldest layers that has not yet stopped, that is still slowly, slowly growing, adding one more molecule of calcium to the base of a structure that already reaches kilometers into the dark water above.

---

The turbulence is the defining condition.

Everything here is in motion, and the motion is not gentle. The current reverses without warning. Water that was flowing northeast is suddenly flowing south, then east, then — for a disorienting moment — *upward*, as a thermal upwelling from somewhere in the deepest layers forces a column of slightly less cold water through a gap in the structures. The fauna has adapted to this. The grazers clamp down — literally, they press flat and lock against the wall with whatever gripping mechanism they possess — when the current shifts violently. The smaller creatures seem to thrive in it, using the eddies and reversals as highways, as shortcuts, as hunting grounds where prey is disoriented and slow.

You realize, watching a current reversal bend a cloud of mineral dust into a spiral that takes ten full seconds to unwind, that this place has been turbulent for its entire existence. The turbulence is not a storm. It is the *weather*. It is the climate. It is the permanent state of the deep water in this region, driven by some convection pattern rooted in the planet's internal heat, amplified by the architecture itself — by the canyons and chambers and passages that channel and redirect flow in ways that create perpetual chaos.

The structures survive it. They have to — they have been surviving it for longer than any surface civilization has existed. But they survive it by *yielding*. The surfaces are smooth because they are being continuously polished. The edges are rounded because sharp edges are erased within geological moments. The corridors are curved because straight corridors would create resonance patterns that would shatter the material. The architecture is, in a sense, designed — not by anyone, not by intention, but by the selection pressure of a billion years of violent water. What remains is what *works*. Everything else has been eroded away.

---

There is something unusual here, though. Something you notice after you have been immersed in the place for some time and your senses have adjusted to the near-darkness and the cold and the perpetual, disorienting motion.

The structures are not random.

You have been assuming they are — the product of biological accretion, shapeless, without plan. But as you drift through the larger passages and see how the walls align, how certain structures seem to *face* each other across gaps of exactly the right width to create specific current patterns, how the resonant frequencies of adjacent walls harmonize rather than clash — you begin to suspect that the builders, whoever and whatever they were, were doing something more than farming. The spacing is too regular. The harmonic relationships are too precise. The entire submerged landscape, stretching out in every direction into the dim blue-dark, is not a city and not a farm and not a natural formation.

It is an instrument.

The entire structure is built to be played by the water. Every wall, every chamber, every passage is a component of a device that converts the raw chaos of ocean currents into something — patterned, modulated, *structured* sound and pressure and flow. The turbulence is not incidental to the design. The turbulence is the *input*. The whole place takes chaos in and produces something else on the other side, something you cannot quite perceive because you are inside it, like a single cell trying to understand a symphony by hearing only the vibration of its own membrane.

What that something else is — what the output of this ancient, vast, submerged instrument might be — you cannot determine. Perhaps it was never meant to be perceived by anything living inside it. Perhaps it was meant to be perceived from somewhere else entirely. Perhaps the builders are out there, somewhere, in water so deep and so far away that they have never seen the structures they grew and left behind, and they are listening.

The fauna does not seem to know. The grazers scrape. The angular predators hunt. The current shifts and the walls sing their low, arrhythmic songs, and somewhere below, in the oldest and deepest layer, the pulse continues — patient, mechanical, purposeful, waiting for something that may have already happened or may never happen at all.

The dark water holds it all. The cold preserves it. The age of the place is beyond counting, and its future is the same as its present: turbulent, dim, abundant with strange life, full of sound that no one is quite hearing, built by something that no longer exists for a purpose that may no longer apply.

And still it grows. One molecule at a time, at the base. Still growing.
