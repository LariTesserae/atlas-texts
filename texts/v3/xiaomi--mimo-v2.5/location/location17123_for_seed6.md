# The Harrowed Gardens of Station Nuth

You are standing — if standing is what you do here — inside a constructed world that was never meant to be seen from outside.

The water is warm. Not bath-warm, but the warmth of something alive and working, the residual heat of industry bleeding into a liquid medium that holds it close and doesn't let go. It presses against every surface with the patient, omnidirectional weight of depth. You are not at the bottom of the ocean. You are somewhere higher — on a manufactured plateau, a constructed shelf of welded titanium plate and pressure-cast polymer that was raised up from the deep seafloor like an altar, and then the sea came and took it anyway. The elevation here is a matter of geometry, not geology. The ground was made to be high, and then the water came up to meet it, and now everything exists in a liminal condition: elevated terrain that is nonetheless thoroughly submerged, as though someone built a hilltop and then allowed the sky to become an ocean.

Everything here is made.

There is no rock. There is no sand. The substrate beneath you — and it *is* beneath you; you feel it through the soles of whatever you're standing on — is ribbed steel plating, bolted in overlapping panels that were once bright with anti-corrosion coating but have since gone to a deep, mottled bronze-green where marine films have taken hold. The walls, where there are walls, are the same: dense industrial fabrication, I-beams and riveted plate, pipes running in tight organized bundles along every surface. A canal-lock aesthetic, but smaller, tighter. Hamlet-sized, bounded. You can see the edges of this place. There are walls no more than fifty meters in any direction, and they curve inward slightly near the top, giving everything the feeling of the inside of a held breath.

But the walls are disappearing.

They are disappearing under the vegetation.

This is the strange part — or one of the strange parts, because strangeness here is not a single note but a chord. The plant life is extraordinary, and it is *everywhere*. It covers the steel surfaces in dense, suffocating layers — not the thin biofilm of ordinary marine growth but something cultivated, something *engineered*, something that was planted here with purpose and then allowed to run beyond all reason. Great curtains of kelp-analog hang from overhead pipe runs, their fronds broad as bedsheets, swaying in microcurrents so slow they seem more like breathing than movement. Encrusting organisms — if you can call them that; they are too regular, too geometrically precise — spread across the walls in fractal rosettes of deep viridian and electric teal, filling every crevice, bridging every gap, turning the angular industrial architecture into something organic and bulging and almost obscenely fertile. On the floor, or what serves as the floor, thick mats of filamentous growth have accumulated in soft, dense hummocks that make each step (each drift, each float, each slow negotiation of weight and buoyancy) a negotiation with living carpet.

There is no bare surface. Density 2.8 out of 3. The vegetation has taken every horizontal plane, every vertical plane, every diagonal, every underside. The only bare metal visible is on the newest growths — the recently installed fittings, the replacement panels, the freshly welded seams — and even those are already clouding over with the first pale fuzz of colonizing filaments. Everything is occupied. There is no room between presences. The plants grow over and through and around the machinery, and the machinery — which is everywhere, which constitutes the bones and organs of this place — hums on regardless, patient and unhurried, its vibrations traveling through the water as barely perceptible tremors that you feel in your sternum rather than hear.

Because the sound is almost nothing.

Underwater, sound travels, but here it seems to have been muffled, absorbed, swallowed by the dense vegetation and the thick water and the warm, close, saturated atmosphere of the place. There is a low, continuous tone — somewhere between a hum and a pulse — that comes from the machinery, from the pumps and the filtration units and the thermal exchangers that keep this place alive, or at least keep it *functioning*. But it doesn't echo. It doesn't carry. It dies within meters of its source, smothered by the living walls, and what reaches you is more a pressure in the bones than a sound in the ears. Occasionally something shifts — a valve cycling, a pipe contracting with temperature change, a slow mechanical adjustment — and the sound it makes is a brief, damp clunk that vanishes as quickly as it arrives. Silence here is not the absence of noise. It is the suppression of it. The place is loud, in the way all industrial sites are loud, but the noise goes nowhere. It stays close to its sources, trapped in the warm water like heat in a blanket.

No animals. None. Not a single fish, not a crustacean, not a worm, not a microbe that you can see. This is perhaps the strangest thing about the place — this oceanic fertility without oceanic life. The vegetation thrives in impossible abundance, thick enough to choke a diver, lush enough to suggest an entire ecosystem, but there is nothing that moves through it, nothing that grazes on it, nothing that shelters within it. The absence is conspicuous. It is *maintained*. You begin to notice the fixtures — small, dense nodes bolted to the pipe runs and wall panels at regular intervals, each one trailing a fine spray of something that disperses into the water and vanishes. Repellent? Sterilant? Whatever it is, it creates a perimeter of exclusion around each node, a tiny halo where even the aggressive vegetation thins back slightly, as though respecting a boundary. The nodes are old. Some are corroded nearly beyond recognition. But they still function. Something is still keeping the animals away.

The light is normal, which is to say it is *wrong*.

There is no sun here. You are deep underwater, on a constructed plateau, inside walls. The light should be dim, blue-shifted, the ghostly twilight of the photic zone's lower margins. Instead, it is — well, it is the light of a Tuesday afternoon. Even, diffuse, casting soft shadows that fall in the right direction and at the right angles. The source is overhead: a grid of sealed luminaires set into the ceiling structure, their housings caked with the same relentless vegetation but their output unimpeded, maintained by systems you cannot see but can feel in the faint electromagnetic buzz that accompanies them. The light is industrial in its quality — slightly too white, slightly too even — but it is, for all practical purposes, daylight. It falls on the dense green growth and the bronze-green metal and the slow, swaying curtains of kelp-analog and makes them look almost like a garden. A greenhouse. A place where things are grown.

The warmth makes sense now. The light makes sense now. The lush vegetation makes sense now. This is a farm. Or it was. Or it is becoming something else.

The technology is industrial in the broadest sense — not sleek, not refined, not beautiful. It is the technology of function without aesthetics, of systems designed to operate and to persist and to be repaired by people who do not care what they look like. Every surface is a work surface. Every space is a service corridor. The pipes carry fluids — nutrient solutions, coolant, filtered water, waste — in colors you can't quite identify through the greenish medium. The pumps are massive, overbuilt, designed to run for decades without maintenance, and they do. The filtration arrays are cathedral-scale, or they would be if they were above water; here, submerged, they look more like the ribcages of enormous animals, each rib a membrane filter, each chamber a processing stage, and they sit among the vegetation like ruins that are still, somehow, fully operational.

The strangeness accumulates. It is not one thing but many. It is the geometry of the vegetation — those fractal rosettes, too precise, too regular, suggesting a botanical intelligence or at least a botanical *algorithm* that has been released into the system and is now expressing itself in forms that are beautiful and not quite right. It is the way the water moves — not in currents but in slow, deliberate circulations that follow paths you can't quite see, as though the entire volume of liquid in this place is being choreographed by the machinery below. It is the temperature, which is not uniform: warmer in some places, cooler in others, and the boundaries between warm and cool are sharp, almost knife-edged, as though invisible membranes divide the water into zones. It is the young features — the sharp edges of newly installed components, the crisp corners of replacement panels, the raw brightness of recent welds — that tell you this place is still being built, still being modified, still being *added to*, even as the vegetation tries to swallow it whole.

And it is the growth rate.

Look closely at the vegetation. Look at a particular frond, a particular rosette, a particular filament mat. Hold it in your attention. And then — slowly, so slowly — watch it move. Not swaying. *Growing*. You can see it happening, in real time, at a speed that is just barely above the threshold of perception. The fronds extend. The rosettes unfurl. The filaments creep outward, millimeter by millimeter, filling space with the patient, relentless insistence of something that does not know how to stop. This is not normal growth. This is not the slow, seasonal progression of terrestrial plants. This is growth at the pace of watching a clock — perceptible, continuous, and deeply unsettling in a place where no animals exist to check it.

The vegetation is winning. Slowly, over years or decades, it is winning. The machinery still functions, but it is increasingly encased. The corridors are narrowing. The luminaires are dimming behind their caked housings. The filter membranes are loading up with growth that should have been removed but wasn't, because the maintenance corridors are too choked to walk through and the remotely operated service drones are — you notice, looking for them — absent. Gone. Perhaps repurposed. Perhaps consumed. Perhaps they simply stopped being replaced when they failed, one by one, and no one came to fix them.

The dynamic is slow. Patient. Minimal flux. The water doesn't rush. The plants don't surge. The machinery doesn't clatter. Everything here operates on a timescale that makes a human lifespan feel like a vibration — a brief tremor in a process that will continue long after you're gone. The place is changing, but it is changing the way a continent changes: continuously, imperceptibly, with an inevitability that is only visible when you compare two moments far apart.

Who built this? The question hangs in the warm water like a sediment that refuses to settle. The industrial scale suggests an organization, a purpose, a plan. The total construction — no natural substrate, everything made — suggests ambition and resources. The biological engineering — the precisely grown vegetation, the animal-exclusion systems — suggests a sophistication that goes beyond mere fabrication. And the silence, the slow decay, the relentless green growth, suggests that whatever the plan was, it has been left to run without supervision for a very long time.

You are inside a machine that has become a garden. You are inside a garden that was always a machine. The distinction stopped mattering somewhere in the past, and the place has been patiently, silently, greenly becoming itself ever since — a constructed ecosystem with no ecosystem, an industrial site with no industry, a garden with no gardener, growing in the warm water on its high plateau, small and bounded and utterly alone in the dark ocean that presses against its walls from every side, asking nothing, expecting nothing, simply — slowly, slowly — continuing.
