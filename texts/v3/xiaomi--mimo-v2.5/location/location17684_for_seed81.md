# The Steaming Quarters

The air is thick enough to chew. It rises from every surface — not steam, exactly, but something denser, heavier, a visible wavering that makes the distant structures ripple as if seen through flawed glass. The water here does not fall as rain. It condenses. It exhales. Every pipe, every wall, every slab of poured concrete weeps a slow mineral tear that leaves white residue in long vertical streaks, building up over decades into formations that have started to resemble stalactites hanging from balconies and highway overpasses and the undersides of industrial catwalks. The whole city is slowly calcifying from the outside in.

You are standing on a high plateau — or perhaps inside a basin carved from one. The horizon is difficult to read. The steam softens everything beyond a few hundred meters into silhouettes, and the sky above is a lid of brownish amber that seems close enough to touch, pressing heat downward like a hand on a chest. There is no sun visible. There has probably not been a sun visible here in a long time. The light comes from the sky itself, a dull and sourceless glow that is less illumination than the memory of it, and yet it is enough. Everything can be seen. Nothing can be seen clearly.

The buildings are enormous and they are few.

That is the first wrongness, or perhaps the second — the first being that the water is warm, always warm, almost hot, and smells faintly of copper and something beneath copper that has no name. The structures are vast industrial things, the kind of architecture that was designed for processes rather than people: long rectangular blocks with no windows on their lower floors, cylindrical towers with no apparent purpose, flat-roofed warehouses whose rooflines have been softened and rounded by decades of mineral accumulation until they look like the eroded mesas of some impossible desert. They are built from poured concrete, from riveted steel, from a pale ceramic material that no one outside this place would recognize — something between brick and bone. They are spaced far apart. Incredibly far apart. The distances between buildings are measured not in meters but in the time it takes to walk from one to the next, during which you pass through open ground of dark, wet, mineral-crusted stone that rings faintly under your boots like hollow metal. The ground is not solid. Or rather, it is solid, but it is *thin* — a crust over something else, something that breathes warmth upward through hairline fractures in the surface, fractures that the condensing water seeks and fills and deposits and widens in an endless patient negotiation.

Between the buildings there is nothing.

Not nothing as in emptiness, though it is that too. Nothing as in *absence*. No trees — there have never been trees here. No grass, no moss, no algae on the surfaces of the warm pools that collect in the low places. The water itself is too rich in dissolved minerals for anything biological to take hold in any way that would be recognized as life. There are, perhaps, bacterial mats in the hottest seeps — flat, slick, faintly iridescent films that coat the inside of drainage channels and the floors of collapsed structures. They may be alive. They may be something else entirely. They pulse very slightly, at a rhythm that might be thermal cycling and might be something with intention, and it is easier to think about the first explanation.

The fauna is rare but not absent. There are insects of a kind — hard-shelled, metallic-looking things the size of a human thumb that cluster in the dark spaces beneath stairwells and inside drainage pipes, drawn to the warmth. They make a sound when they move, a very faint clicking, like cooling metal contracting, and in the places where they congregate in large numbers this clicking becomes a continuous whisper that sounds almost like language if you let yourself listen too long. There are also birds — or objects that move like birds, dark shapes that appear in the upper levels of the steam, drifting between the tallest towers on thermals that rise from the cracked ground. They never land where they can be seen. Whether they are animals or machines or something that falls between those categories, no one who remains in the city seems to know or to want to discuss.

The city is called something. Whether that name is still used, and by whom, is unclear. It was built over a long period — you can see the layers in the walls, the different techniques of construction stacked on top of and against each other like geological strata. The lowest foundations are old in a way that makes oldness feel like a misnomer. They are massive, rough-hewn, set into the plateau with a permanence that suggests whoever built them never expected to move. Above that: industrial revolution construction, the kind of iron and rivet work that speaks of furnaces and foundries, of a time when the city was a place of making. The furnaces are still here. Some of them are still hot — not because anyone fires them, but because the geothermal energy that rises through the cracked ground feeds them in a way that was perhaps intentional. Perhaps the entire city was built here *because* of what lies beneath. The newer layers are concrete and steel and that pale ceramic material, and these are the buildings that feel most hollow, most unused, most like shells that something has left.

The industrial infrastructure is everywhere and mostly silent. Conveyor systems run between buildings on elevated rails, some of them still moving — slow, grinding, carrying nothing, their belts tracing the same empty loop they have traced for years because no one shut them off, or because they cannot be shut off, or because something about the system requires them to continue. The sound they make is the dominant sound of the city: a low, rhythmic groaning, like a sleeping animal breathing, punctuated by the occasional sharp *tink* of a metal contraction. Steam vents along the lower streets hiss continuously, and the mineral-rich water that pools around their bases has built up into elaborate terraced formations — tiny dams and basins and cascading shelves of white and pale green and rust-orange deposit, each one a small frozen waterfall that is still somehow always moving, always growing, always one molecule closer to closing the gap between buildings entirely.

In the deepest parts of the city, the buildings are close enough that their upper floors nearly touch, and the steam between them becomes a permanent fog, and the dim amber light filters down into something barely distinguishable from shadow. Here the mineral deposits have won. The spaces between structures are filled — not completely, not yet, but enough that you would have to climb or break through to pass from one street to the next. The walls are thick with accumulated crust, and if you press your hand against it, it is warm, and it vibrates very faintly, and you become aware that the vibration is not mechanical, not geological, not any vibration you have a name for, but it is regular and it is slow and it has a quality that feels less like rhythm and more like *patience*.

There are people here. Not many. They move through the upper levels of the city where the air is slightly thinner and the steam slightly less dense, and they maintain the conveyor systems and the water treatment infrastructure and the power grid that feeds whatever still needs feeding. They do not talk much. The quiet is not forced — it is simply that the environment does not reward sound. Voices are swallowed by the steam and the mineral-laden air. Conversations are short, functional, conducted in a kind of shorthand that developed out of long necessity. The city is not dying. It is not growing. It is *accreting*. Every surface is thicker than it was last year. Every gap is narrower. The conveyor belts run and run. The water weeps and deposits and builds. The dim amber light persists. The heat does not abate. And somewhere beneath the cracked and hollow ground, whatever the city was built to harness or contain or draw from continues its own slow process, patient as the mineral crust, active as the steam, strange as the clicking insects that might be speaking a language that no one here wants to hear.
