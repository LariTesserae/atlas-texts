# The Still Basin

The ground is new.

That is the first wrongness, though it takes time to understand *why* it feels wrong. The rocks have no history. Basalt fingers jut from the clay at angles that have no explanation — no folding, no faulting, no slow grinding of tectonic patience. They simply *are*: sharp-edged, black, glassy where the mineral hasn't yet decided what it wants to become. Crystals catch what little light there is and throw it back in wet, cold flashes. Some of the formations look less like geological features than like things that were *placed* — columns of vesicular stone rising from the muck in clusters, as though something enormous pressed its fingers into the earth and withdrew, leaving behind hollows that fill with hot water every few seconds. The rhythm is regular. The rhythm is always regular.

Everything here is saturated.

Water rises from below and falls from above in the same tepid breath. The sky — if you can call it a sky, this low vault of brownish vapor that never clears and never darkens — weeps constantly. Not rain exactly. Condensation so thick it accumulates on every surface and then releases in slow, fat droplets that strike the broad leaves below with a sound like fingers tapping a drum skin. The leaves never dry. They glisten with a permanent wet sheen, each one a shallow bowl of gathered moisture that trembles with the weight of things moving through the undergrowth. Water beads on the fur of creatures that don't seem to notice it. Water pools in the hollows of the raw stone and never drains, because the ground doesn't drain — the ground *holds*. The entire basin is a sponge of red-brown clay and crushed mineral, still settling into a shape it will never finish settling into, still warm from whatever heat source broods beneath it.

The heat is absolute.

Not the heat of a sun, though there is something above the vapor layer that functions as one — a diffuse, directionless warmth that presses down from every point of the dim sky simultaneously. It is the heat of the earth itself. The clay is warm to touch. The stone is warm. The water is warm — never cold, never refreshing, always the same tepid temperature that matches the air that matches the blood of everything living here. There is no relief. There is no cool pocket, no shaded respite. The air itself is a substance, thick enough to feel against the skin, carrying the mineral smell of wet basalt and the sweet, almost rotting perfume of vegetation so lush it seems to be dissolving even as it grows.

And it does grow. God, how it grows.

The plants here do not compete for light so much as *ignore* the concept of limits. Broad-leafed things with veins like green rivers spread across the ground in overlapping carpets, their edges serrated and glistening. Ferns — if ferns is even the right word for them — uncurl in spirals the size of a human fist, their fiddleheads coated in a fine black fuzz that steams faintly in the perpetual humidity. Vine-like growths climb the basalt columns in helical patterns so precise they look engineered, wrapping the raw stone in living sheaths of dark green, putting out aerial roots that dangle and sway in the still air. Fungi cluster at the bases of everything: pale, fleshy, fanning outward in shelf formations that layer on top of each other until the stone beneath disappears entirely. The color palette is limited but deep — greens in every shade from nearly black to a pale, sickly chartreuse, punctuated by the orange-brown of spore clusters and the occasional startling white of a flower that opens but never seems to attract anything. Some of the vegetation pulses. Not metaphorically. The broad ground-covering leaves expand and contract on a slow, respiratory rhythm, drawing in the wet air and pushing it back out. The sound of it is almost subliminal. Almost.

Everything living here is *close together*.

This is not a place where life occupies separate niches with comfortable distances between them. This is a place where life is stacked — vertically, horizontally, in every crevice and on every surface, layered until the concept of individual organism begins to blur. Insects coat the undersides of leaves in shimmering, restless blankets, their wings catching the dim light and reflecting it in dull metallic flashes. Some of them are as large as a hand, their carapaces iridescent green-black, moving in patterns that suggest coordination without suggesting thought. Beetles the size of small stones trundle along the basalt columns, mandibles working at the mineral surface, and the sound they make — a dry, continuous clicking — is one of the permanent fixtures of the basin's soundscape. Smaller things swarm in clouds above the water pools: gnats, mosquitoes, things with no common name, their wings a single sustained hum that never rises, never falls, never pauses.

The fauna is overwhelming.

It is everywhere, and it is *loud*, and it is permanent. Frogs — dense-bodied, bright-skinned, some of them nearly the color of fresh wounds — sit on every available surface and call in overlapping trills that merge into a wall of sound. The trills do not vary. The same pitch, the same cadence, the same duration, repeated without end. Birds — if birds is right — move through the upper vegetation in heavy, silent arcs, their wings too broad for their bodies, their eyes large and dark and reflective. They perch and stare. They do not forage. They do not fight. They stare with the patience of things that have never needed to hurry because time, here, is a closed loop. Below them, in the wet undergrowth, larger things move: sinuous shapes that press through the ferns with enough weight to flatten them, their passage marked by a brief parting of the green carpet and then an immediate closing behind them. The water pools ripple with the movement of tadpoles so large they create visible wakes. Eels — or eel-like things — surface in the warm, still water and breathe air with slow, deliberate mouths before sinking again. Their eyes catch the dim light and hold it.

There are things here that shouldn't work.

A beetle walks upside-down along the underside of a broad leaf, carrying on its back a smaller beetle, which carries on its back a smaller beetle, which carries — it goes on. The column hangs there, inverted, and does not fall. A vine has grown through the body of a resting frog — not around it, *through* it, entering the skin at one side and emerging at the other — and the frog sits placidly, its throat pulsing with its call, apparently untroubled. Water flows uphill in one place, along a groove in the raw basalt, moving against gravity for a distance of perhaps a meter before stopping. It has been stopping there, at the same point, for as long as the basin has existed. Which is not long. Which is forever.

This is the other wrongness, the deeper one. Nothing here has *changed*. Not once. Not in any way that can be measured. The basalt columns have not weathered. The clay has not compacted. The water level is exactly where it has always been, which is everywhere. The vegetation has not died back, has not browned, has not composted. The dead leaves that litter the ground are dead but not decaying — they lie in the same positions, curled at the same angles, preserving the exact color they had at the moment of their falling. If you moved one, it would return. Not immediately. Not violently. But by the next moment of attention, it would be where it was. The basin exists in a single state. A single, saturated, teeming, dripping, calling, crawling, growing instant that repeats without ever *being* a repetition because there is no before and no after. There is only this.

The light never changes. The vapor ceiling holds at the same height, the same opacity, filtering the source above into a dim, reddish-brown glow that illuminates everything equally and nothing well. Shadows exist but they are soft, indistinct, more like stains than shapes. It is always late afternoon, or early morning, or some hour that doesn't correspond to any clock. The air shimmers with heat distortion at ground level, making the basalt columns appear to waver slightly, as though the landscape is being viewed through water. In a sense, it is. The air *is* water, nearly. The humidity is so total that breathing feels like an act of partial drowning — each inhale carries moisture that condenses immediately in the airways, a warm, mineral-tasting wetness that the body adjusts to not because it is comfortable but because adjustment is all there is.

The sound is the most present thing.

It is not one sound but a single, continuous, composite roar — the frogs, the insects, the water dripping, the slow expansion of leaf surfaces, the clicking of the mineral-eating beetles, the deep, infrasonic hum of the geothermal warmth radiating through the clay — all of it blending into a wall of noise that is so constant it functions as silence. The ear stops processing it after a time. But the body doesn't. The chest resonates with it. The bones feel it. It is the sound of a place that is alive in every cell and will never die and never be born because it is already both, simultaneously, permanently.

There is no one here.

There are no structures, no traces of anything that has ever made tools or built shelters or shaped the land to suit its needs. The basin is untouched by anything that might want to change it. This is not an absence. This is a *condition*. The basin does not permit change. It exists in its own equilibrium — not a balanced one, not a peaceful one, but a *locked* one. A state of maximum intensity frozen at the moment of its own emergence. The geology is embryonic and will remain embryonic. The life is overwhelming and will remain overwhelming. The water will continue to weep from the vapor and the stone and the clay, pooling and steaming and never going anywhere, and the creatures will continue to call and crawl and stare with their large, dark, patient eyes, and the basalt will continue to glint wetly in the dim, directionless light, and none of it will ever become anything other than exactly what it already is.

The basin breathes. It has always been breathing. It will always be breathing.

You cannot stay here. Not because it is dangerous — though it is, in the way that all places of absolute saturation are dangerous, where the sheer *volume* of life presses against the boundaries of the body and asks, gently and insistently, to be let in. You cannot stay because there is no *here* to stay in. The basin is a single moment, and moments do not have space for visitors. You can pass through it, moving from one edge of its low, wet, teeming geography to another, feeling the heat and the humidity and the sound close around you like a warm, wet hand — but you will not change it. You will not mark it. When you leave, the basalt will look the same, the frogs will call the same note, the water will drip from the same leaf at the same angle.

The basin was just born. The basin has always been here.

Both of these are true, and neither will ever change.
