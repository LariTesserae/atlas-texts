# The Roaring Pocket

The sound hits first and never relents.

It is not a sound you hear so much as a condition you exist inside of — a wall of white noise that saturates every surface, every pooled drop, every pocket of air. It is the sound of water arriving from somewhere above and somewhere deep, funneled through a throat of fractured limestone into a chamber barely large enough to hold it. The roar is constant, unhurried, patient as geology, and it fills every cubic inch of this small dark place as though the space were built to contain nothing else.

**The chamber itself** is roughly ovoid — perhaps four meters at its widest, three at its tallest, though the ceiling vanishes into a darkness you cannot measure. The walls are smooth in the way that water makes smooth: polished by millennia of spray and seepage into forms that feel almost biological, like the inside of a throat. Limestone, old limestone, old enough that its layers are visible as faint banding — cream, ash, pale ochre — records of shallow seas that evaporated before anything with eyes existed to witness them. The erosion is complete. The chamber is not eroding; it *has eroded*. It is in its own settled state, each edge rounded, each surface wearing the micro-texture of water's long negotiation with stone. Nothing is sharp here. Even the broken places look healed.

The floor is uneven, sloping gently from a raised shelf at the back toward a central depression where the water gathers. This depression is a pool — not deep, perhaps a meter at its deepest point, dark and restless with the impact of a thin cascade that falls from a fissure in the upper wall. The water comes not as a single dramatic fall but as a broad curtain — a dozen threads, a sheet, splitting and rejoining as it descends along a stepped face of calcite before dropping the final meter into the pool with a concussion that vibrates in your sternum. The water is cold in a way that goes beyond temperature. It is cold as mineral, as deep earth, as stone itself.

The pool overflows through a narrow crack in the floor — the only obvious exit for the water — and the sound of that, too, adds to the roar: a gurgling undertone beneath the main percussion, the sound of a place draining perpetually into somewhere deeper and more hidden still.

## What Lives Here

**Darkness** is absolute except at the very margins of the water, where the falling curtain catches some faint quality — not light exactly, but a luminous suggestion, a pale phosphorescence that clings to the limestone spray-stone like a skin. It is biological. Chemoautotrophic organisms — bacteria, algae of a kind that needs no sun — colonize the wettest surfaces in faint stains of grey-green and ghost-white. They are the base of everything.

And everything here is built on them.

The walls above the splash zone — above where the spray reaches, but below where the ceiling dries to bare stone — are **lush** in a way that seems improbable in total darkness and near-freezing cold. Mosses. Dense, spongy, saturated mosses in deep emerald and near-black green, colonizing every ledge, every horizontal surface, every crack that holds even a film of moisture. They grow slowly — agonizingly slowly — but they have had a very long time, and the layers are thick, plush, almost cushion-like, soft enough to muffle the sound slightly if you pressed your hand against them, though nothing could truly muffle this sound. Small ferns — pale, translucent-veined, ghostly — unfurl from the moss in tight spirals, reaching for nothing, photosynthesizing nothing, fed entirely by the chemical richness of the water and the microbial world beneath them.

The vegetation is not spread evenly. It concentrates where the spray reaches its farthest — a band perhaps two meters above the pool level, a horizontal garden of wet green and pale frond ringing the chamber like a wreath. Above and below this band, bare stone.

**Fungi** supplement the moss. Not the colorful kind — nothing so dramatic. Instead: thin, ghostly mycelial threads that lace through the dampest moss, visible only as a faint whitening in the green, and occasionally a fruiting body: a tiny, pale stalk topped with a translucent cap no bigger than a fingertip, slick with moisture, almost invisible in the dark. These fruit rarely. The mycelium is the permanent thing — patient, slow, threading through the moss bed in networks that have been growing, undisturbed, for longer than any human structure on the surface has existed.

**Fauna is rare but not absent.** Small invertebrates — translucent, eyeless, pale — navigate the moss and the wet stone. Isopods, perhaps, or springtails, or something less easily named, something that has been evolving in this specific darkness for so long that it belongs to the chamber the way the limestone does. They move slowly. There is no urgency in them. The food web is thin: bacteria, the organic detritus washed in by the water, the slow death and decay of moss. Occasionally one of them — a larger thing, centipede-like, many-legged, white as bone — picks its way along the spray-line with a deliberate, mechanical gait, hunting the smaller creatures with senses tuned to vibration and chemical trace. It is the apex predator here, if such a term means anything at a pocket scale. It moves through the deafening sound as though the sound does not exist.

## What Was Left

On the raised shelf at the back of the chamber — dry enough to stand on, dry enough that the moss thins to a bare dusting — there are **marks**.

A cluster of stones, arranged with intention. Not a wall, not a shelter — too small for either. Something more like a cache, or an altar, or simply a place where someone once set things down and did not pick them up again. The stones are unworked river cobbles, the kind that arrive already rounded, already shaped by other water elsewhere. But among them: one with a deliberate flake removed, a crude edge, a tool. A hand-axe that never became anything more than its first strike. Beside it, a smaller stone with a pecked surface — a hammerstone, perhaps, worn concave at one end from repeated impact.

No bone. No ash that you can identify — though the shelf's surface has a faintly darker staining that might be ancient, might be nothing. No fabric, no metal, no ceramic. This is a place someone came to before any of those things were available to them, or before they reached this deep.

They came for the water, probably. The shelf is the only place in the chamber where you could crouch without being soaked. The sound would have been the same — deafening, constant, filling the skull — but the water here was drinkable, and in a world of darkness and cold, that mattered enough to come back to.

The tools have not moved. The moss has not quite reached them — the shelf is just dry enough, just removed from the spray — but it is encroaching, millimeter by millimeter, a green tide with no urgency whatsoever.

## The Sound

It is worth returning to, because it is the defining feature of this place.

The roar is not one sound but a composite: the broad-sheet impact of the cascade on the pool, the deeper boom where the pool's overflow plunges into the crack below, a resonant hum that vibrates through the limestone itself — the whole chamber singing at a frequency you feel in your teeth and behind your eyes. There are overtones. Harmonics. The stepped calcite face of the waterfall acts as a kind of natural pipe organ, and the water, in its constant variation, plays a chord that shifts imperceptibly over minutes and hours but never resolves, never pauses, never thins.

You cannot speak here. You cannot hear another person here. You can stand on the shelf, three meters from the cascade, and feel the mist on your face and the cold soaking through your clothing, and you can be in the presence of something that has been making this exact sound, at this exact volume, for longer than your species has existed, and the sound will not change when you leave. It will not notice your departure. It will not notice you were here.

The moss doesn't care. The blind isopods don't care. The limestone absorbs the vibration and gives nothing back.

## The Character of the Place

It is, in every sense, **established**. The erosion is finished — or rather, it has reached the pace at which it will continue for the next hundred thousand years, which is the same as saying it has stopped. The water finds its path and holds it. The moss grows at its rate, which is not a rate you could measure in a human lifetime. The creatures in the walls live their slow, eyeless lives in patterns that barely shift from generation to generation. Even the human artifacts have reached a kind of equilibrium — present, unmoving, slowly being approached by green.

And the scale is small. This is not a cathedral cave. This is a pocket — a single room inside a larger darkness that may or may not contain other rooms, other cascades, other hidden shelves with other forgotten things. But this room, this specific one, is self-contained. It has its water, its green ring, its shelf of stones, its deafening voice. It asks nothing. It is not waiting. It is simply ongoing.

The cold is the cold of deep stone, constant, unchanging with season or surface weather. If the world above is in summer, this chamber does not know. If the world above is buried in ice, this chamber does not know. It exists in its own time — geological time, bacterial time, moss time — and that time moves at the speed of mineral dissolving into water, one molecule at a time, into the roar.
