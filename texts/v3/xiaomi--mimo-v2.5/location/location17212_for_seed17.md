# The Floor of Everything

---

There is no shore. There has never been a shore.

The ocean is the world, and the world is ocean, and if you were to descend through its upper layers — through the cold blue gradient where light still reaches, where the water is thin enough to be called merely water — you would pass through zone after zone of deepening silence and cold, and eventually arrive at the floor. But you would not arrive all at once. You would descend for a very long time.

The seabed here is young. That is the first thing that strikes you, if you could see it: how *new* everything looks. The terrain is a jagged cathedral of black basalt and pale mineral crust, ridgelines sharp enough to split current, spires still wearing the rough texture of their own formation. There has been no rain to soften them. No wind. No glaciers to grind them down. They erupted from the mantle and stopped, and they have been waiting like that ever since, their edges as precise as the day they cooled — angular fins of volcanic glass standing in rows, vast shield structures with the smooth geometry of something that barely had time to exist before the world decided not to change anymore.

And between them — nothing.

That is the second thing. The emptiness.

You must understand the scale of it. These ridges and peaks and volcanic plateaus are enormous, some of them mountainous, their summits reaching high enough into the water column that their tips sit in a layer where light still penetrates in a diffuse, cold, bluish haze — normal light, the same spectral quality as a sunlit afternoon on a world that has no afternoons. But the ridges are separated by distances that are obscene. Hundreds of kilometers of featureless abyssal plain between one formation and the next, and the plain itself is not rich sediment or soft silt. It is mineral. Crystalline dust andite particulate and cold, cold rock, spread so thin across the basin floor that the darkness underneath it is almost visible — a darkness that goes down further, into the crust, into the mantle, into whatever the planet is made of below the thin frozen shell of its surface.

The density of *anything* here is startlingly low. You could swim for days and pass nothing. No coral. No kelp. No plant life of any kind — the temperature is too low, the chemistry too mineral, the light too feeless by the time it reaches these depths to support any photosynthetic process. This is a world without a single blade of anything green, without a single frond, without moss or lichen or algae on any surface. The rock is bare. The mineral crusts are bare. Everything is bare, and clean, and cold, and it has always been this way.

What there *is* — and this is where the emptiness reveals its paradox — is fauna.

Abundant fauna.

They don't look like animals you would recognize. They are slow, and pale, and many of them are large. Things like enormous translucent worms, meters long, trailing through the water with the unhurried patience of geology. Crustacean-like bodies, jointed and armored in plates of calcium and silica, crawling along the volcanic glass with careful, deliberate movements, picking at the mineral surfaces with mouthparts designed for scraping. Filter-feeders suspended in the water column, vast frondless structures — almost like jellyfish, almost like sponges, almost like nothing — that pulse rhythmically and strain the water for the microscopic particles of organic chemistry that drift here from some source you cannot see. They are fed by the vents. They have always been fed by the vents.

Because the vents are everywhere.

Hydrothermal chimneys rise from the young terrain like the organs of the planet itself, and they do not stop. Black smokers and white smokers, some of them enormous, cathedral-scale columns of deposited mineral, belching superheated water into the cold ocean in a perpetual, pressurized roar. This is the source of the sound. Not a sound you hear with your ears — a sound you feel in your chest, in your bones, in the fillings of your teeth. A deep, tectonic, roaring vibration that is constant and total and *deafening*, a sound that does not come from any one direction but from everywhere at once, because there are thousands of these vents across the visible terrain and the water carries their output for kilometers, a pressure wave that never subsides, never modulates, never pauses. The whole floor of the world is a throat, and it is always screaming, and the fauna have long since gone deaf to it or perhaps were never hearing to begin with.

The temperature is just above freezing. Not far below — just enough that the water does not solidify, just enough that exposed skin would go numb in minutes and then beyond numb into something permanent. The cold is not dramatic. It is not the cold of a blizzard or a glacier. It is the cold of deep water, ambient and total, a temperature that does not fluctuate because there is nothing to fluctuate it. There are no seasons. There are no currents to bring warmer water down from shallower zones. The ocean is stratified into layers that have not mixed in longer than you could measure, and the bottom layer — this layer, where the ridges are sharp and the vents roar — sits at a temperature that has never changed.

It will never change.

---

And here is the strangeness that you would find, if you followed one of those mineral ridges along its crest for long enough, past the vents and the pale slow creatures and the bare volcanic glass, until you came to a place where the rock was wrong.

Not wrong in the way that geology is sometimes wrong — a dike intrusion, an unexpected stratum, a fold that shouldn't be there. Wrong in a way that implies *purpose*.

A structure.

It is small compared to the ridge it sits on — small compared to the mountain of basalt it is bolted to — but it is unmistakably built. Steel, or something like steel. Corroded and crusted with mineral deposit after centuries — after millennia — of continuous hydrothermal exposure, but still standing, still rigid, still holding its shape against the pressure of the deep. It is a building. A factory, perhaps, or a processing station, or something that has no equivalent on a land-based world. Its walls are thick. Its joints are heavy. It was designed to survive down here, and it has survived, and it will survive long after whatever made it is gone.

Because whatever made it *is* gone.

The structure is empty. Its doors — massive, pressure-rated, sealed against the ocean — are open. Not opened. Just... open. As if whoever left simply did not bother to close them, or as if the mechanisms that held them shut failed at some point and no one was left to repair them. Inside, the machinery is still. Conveyors, processors, what might have been smelting apparatus or mineral extraction rigs, all of it frozen in place by corrosion and sediment. A thin layer of crystalline mineral dust covers every horizontal surface. The fauna have not moved in — the interior is too geometric, too unnatural, too *shaped* for the slow pale creatures to find it hospitable. They slide past the open doorways on their way to the vents and do not look inside.

You will find these structures scattered across the terrain. Not many. A few per ridge, perhaps. Always the same heavy construction, always the same thick corrosion, always the same open doors and dead machinery. They are spaced far enough apart that finding one does not mean you are near another — the distances between them are the same obscene distances between everything else on this floor, and the emptiness between them is the same emptiness, and you could pass from one structure to the next and see nothing but bare rock and vent-smoke and the pale drifting fauna for hours, for days, for as long as your light held out.

There are no other marks. No roads. No cables connecting the structures. No smaller buildings, no dwellings, no infrastructure of habitation. Just the factories, alone, bolted to the ridges like barnacles on the spine of a sleeping animal.

---

And the light.

The light is normal. This is perhaps the most disorienting thing about the place, after you have adjusted to the deafening sound and the crushing emptiness and the young, sharp, impossible terrain. The light is *normal*. It is not bioluminescent. It is not the flickering green-blue of deep-sea photography. It is the light of a sun — diffuse, yes, and cold at the edges, but full-spectrum and steady, falling on the basalt and the mineral crusts and the pale fauna with the even quality of an overcast day. There are shadows. There are shadows cast by the ridges onto the abyssal plains, and shadows cast by the chimneys onto the water, and if you look up — if you look up through kilometer after kilometer of clear, cold, darkening water — you will see it. The surface. A bright disc, far away, motionless, pouring light down into the deep with a constancy that does not vary.

The surface never ripples. There is no wind above it. There is no weather. The ocean extends in every direction to a horizon that curves gently with the planet's own enormous circumference, and it is flat, and it has always been flat, and the light comes through it as if through glass.

---

Everything here is in equilibrium. That is the final thing you would understand, if you stayed long enough, if the cold and the sound and the emptiness did not drive you to leave before understanding arrived.

The vents vent. The fauna feed. The factories stand. The light falls. Nothing increases and nothing decreases. The mineral crusts grow on the chimneys at the same rate the pressure dissolves them. The pale creatures live and die and are replaced at a rate that keeps their numbers exactly constant. The factories corrode at a pace so slow that in a hundred thousand years they will still be standing, still open, still empty, still bolted to ridges that are still sharp.

The world has found its level. It found it a long time ago, and it will never find another.

There is no erosion. There is no deposition that is not balanced by removal. There is no current that strengthens or weakens. The temperature does not rise or fall. The light does not dim. The sound does not change pitch or volume — a constant, world-spanning, pressurized roar, as steady as a heartbeat that never speeds up and never slows down, because this world has no heart and no blood and no reason to change.

You could set a clock by this place, but the clock would never move.
