# The Vat at the Throat of Making

---

You are inside something. That is the first thing to understand.

Not inside a building — though everything around you was built. Not inside a cave — though the walls weep and the air is wet enough to drown a slow breath. You are inside a *procedure*. The space knows it is unfinished. It tells you so constantly, in the sound it makes: a grinding, thick, syllabic noise, like a language being spoken for the first time by a throat that has no practice. The walls are not stone. They are not metal, not wood, not fired clay. They are something before all of those. A material that has not decided what it is yet. It looks likeiteite it looks like wet limestone packed withiteite — no. It looks likeite — it *keeps changing what it looks like* while remaining exactly the same substance. Smooth in one moment. Then the eye catches it at a different angle and the same surface has texture — fibrous, almost like sinew — and then smooth again, and you understand that your perception is not failing. The material is. It has not committed to a grain. It is still choosing.

The chamber is small. That is the second thing. You could touch both walls if you stretched your arms. The ceiling is low enough that a tall person would need to crouch. Perhaps three meters long, two wide, a meter and a half at its highest. It is packed. Every surface is *used* — there is no emptiness here, no void, no relief. Shelves have been carved or grown or pressed into the walls (the distinction has no meaning here), and every shelf holds objects. Small things. Hand-sized. Some are cups — or cups before cups, concavities pressed into the material with thumbs, their edges still rough with the ridges of fingerprints left by whoever made them. Others are dense, heavy little shapes that seem to serve no purpose. Cubes. Spheres. Polyhedra with too many faces. They are warm to the touch even though nothing heats them visibly, and when you pick one up it is heavier than it should be, as though its mass is not entirely committed to staying inside its boundaries. Some of them are slightly larger in your hand than they were on the shelf. They are still forming.

This is the elevation. You must understand this to understand the vertigo that lives at the edges of the room, not in your body but in the room itself — the space feels *high*, the way a dream feels high, as though the walls understand they are thousands of meters above whatever is below and are not comfortable with it. There are no windows. There does not need to be. The altitude presses inward through the material, and the material relays it to you as a feeling: the stomach-dropping, ear-popping awareness of being impossibly high up, on a column or a spire or a needle of this same uncommitted substance, rising out of — what? You don't know. The making hasn't decided. Below this chamber there may be kilometers of the same packed, wet, still-forming material, stretching downward through cloud layers and thermal updrafts, and below that, ground that hasn't been invented yet. Or there may be nothing. The spire may hang in air. Reality, at this scale and this altitude, is not obligated to provide a foundation.

---

The water is the most real thing here.

It runs in thin, constant streams down every surface. It gathers in the thumb-printed cups. It drips from the low ceiling in a rhythm so steady it functions as a clock — *tick, tick, tick* — each drop landing in a different vessel, a different frequency, so the room is layered with overlapping drips in a polyrhythm that never resolves. The air is hot. Not the dry, clean heat of a forge — this is swamp heat, body heat, the heat of a fever, thick and wet and close. Your skin becomes slick within moments. The water on the walls is not condensation from the air; the air is not warm enough on its own to produce this much moisture. The walls themselves are exuding it. The material is *weeping* — the whole structure is producing water the way a living thing produces sweat, and you realize the building is exerting itself. The making is hard work. The structure is straining to become itself, and the effort is generating heat and water the way a body generates both during labor.

And it is lush, in its way. In the crevices between the shelves and the walls, in the seams where surfaces meet, things grow. Not plants — not exactly. Green filaments, fine as spider silk, densely packed, filling every crack and gap with a saturated emerald mass that pulses faintly. They pulse in time with the drips. They are feeding on the water, and they are feeding on something else — something in the material itself, some nutrient that the unfinished substance is bleeding out as it cures. These filaments are the only living things you can see, and they are everywhere, so thickly that the green has become a structural element, a mortar, holding the shelves and walls together in the places where the material has not fully bonded. They hum. Not audibly — you feel the hum in your teeth, in the bones of your face. The packing of the room, the density of it, means there is no space that the filaments have not found and filled. Every gap is a garden.

---

The sound is enormous for such a small space.

It is not the drip, though the drip is constant. It is the *grinding* — the deep, wet, syllabic sound the room makes as it continues to form. It comes from everywhere at once. It has rhythm but no pattern. It sounds like speech in a language that has too many consonants, or like the sound of teeth being ground together by a sleeping giant, or like tectonic plates — except tectonic plates take millions of years, and this is happening now, in real time, audible and immediate. The room is *building itself around you while you stand in it.* The walls are microscopically thicker than they were a moment ago. The shelves have subtly new objects on them — not appearing, but *becoming*, coalescing out of the material like crystals forming in a saturated solution, except the solution is the air itself, which is so thick with particulate matter, so saturated with the products of the making, that solid objects are precipitating out of it. When you breathe, you can taste the forming. It tastes like wet clay and copper and the ozone-pink flavor you get when you hold a battery to your tongue. It tastes like the future.

And the weirdness.

You need to be told about the weirdness, because your mind will try to normalize it and it must not be allowed to.

The room is larger than it is. This is not a trick of perception. You can measure it. Place your hand on the left wall. Walk to the right wall. The distance is two meters. Now turn around. The distance is three. Now look at the ceiling and it is where it was — low, close, weeping — and then you blink and it is a half-meter higher, and the walls have subtly shifted, and the arrangement of shelves is different, and there is a new object on a shelf that was not there, and it is *warm*, and it was *always there*, you are *sure* it was always there, but the room is rearranging its memory. Not its geometry — its *memory*. It is editing what it has already done. The erosion parameter is zero because the room does not allow its own past to settle. Everything is pre-formation. Everything is embryonic. Even the things that look finished are not — they are moments away from being undone and reformed into something slightly different, and the room is going back and choosing again, and again, and the grinding sound is the sound of revision.

The objects on the shelves — you pick one up, a cup, and it is a cup. Simple, functional, heavy in the hand, the thumb-print edges warm. You set it down. You look away. You look back. It is a cup. But it is a different cup. The proportions are different. The thumb prints are in different positions, pressed by a different hand — or by the same hand at a different moment in a process that is not linear. The room is not changing the cup. The room is *showing you a different point in the cup's becoming*, and the line between before and after has come loose, and the cup exists in a superposition of all the moments of its making, and you can see this. You can *see* it, if you let your eyes soften, if you stop trying to see one thing and instead let them hold all of it — and for a moment the cup is every cup it could be, shimmering, rotating through its own possible forms like a living thing dreaming of itself.

This is the reality breakdown. It is not violent. It is not catastrophic. It is *gentle*, which is worse. The world here is not collapsing. It is *uncertain*. It has not yet committed to what is real, because the making is not finished, and until the making is finished, nothing is fixed, nothing is permanent, nothing has agreed to be exactly one thing and no other thing. The room is a draft. You are standing inside a draft. And a draft can be revised.

---

There are creatures here. Rare.

Not in the room itself. The room is too packed, too full, too occupied with its own becoming. But in the structure — in the kilometers of material below and above and around this tiny chamber, in the vast, wet, hot, still-forming body of the spire — there are things. You hear them sometimes through the walls. A sound like a fingertip dragged across wet ceramic. A sound like a throat clearing. They are small, whatever they are. The filaments are disturbed in their presence — they pulse faster, retract slightly, then settle. The creatures are probably eating the filaments. Or the creatures *are* the filaments, in some other phase of their lifecycle — a phase that involves mobility, and appetite, and the occasional scraping transit through the interior channels of a structure that is simultaneously their body and their home and something they are helping to build.

You do not see them. You hear them. You feel the walls vibrate when one passes nearby, and the vibration has a direction, a velocity, a *weight* — something the size of a hand, moving through the material like a fish through silt, displacing the still-forming substance and leaving a healed trail behind it that the grinding, syllabic sound rushes in to fill. The material heals fast. Everything here heals. The whole structure is an act of perpetual self-repair, because the whole structure is an act of perpetual self-formation, and the two processes are the same process, and the process is what makes the sound, and the sound is what holds the room together, and the room is what holds you.

---

The tools are everywhere.

Simple things. Bone levers. Rope made from the green filaments, braided thick, hanging from pegs driven into the still-wet walls. A flat stone — or something like stone, something that has committed to being hard and smooth and is the only object in the room that has fully made up its mind about what it is. It is used as a surface for pressing, for shaping. There are marks on it. Hundreds of marks. Thousands. Every mark is a thumbprint, a knuckle-drag, a tool-score, and they overlap so densely that the surface of the stone has become a texture unto itself, a Braille history of every act of making that has occurred in this room. You press your own thumb into it and it fits, effortlessly, into a depression that was made for a thumb exactly yours, and you understand that this is not coincidence — the stone collects thumbprints the way the room collects drips. It is building a record. It is becoming an archive. And in ten thousand years, if the making ever finishes, the stone will be a document, and the document will say: *Someone was here. They made things. Their hands were this size. They pressed this hard.*

But the making will not finish. You understand this now, standing in the hot, wet, grinding dark, surrounded by shelves that are rearranging themselves and cups that are dreaming of their own forms. The making does not intend to finish. The making is the point. The structure is not a place. It is an *act*. It is the act of building, extended across space and frozen in mid-gesture, and everything inside it — the water, the filaments, the creatures, the objects, the sound — is a byproduct of that gesture, a side effect of the effort of *becoming* that will never, because it must never, resolve into *being*.

You are standing in a verb.

The room is still conjugating.
