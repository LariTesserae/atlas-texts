# The Combing

You come down into it from higher ground that isn't much higher — a gentle slope of compactedite andite, a mineral grit the color of old teeth. The ground is firm here, has been firm, has always been firm. The slope levels into a broad, low basin, and in the basin sits the settlement, and the settlement is alive, and the settlement is loud, and the settlement has never changed.

---

## The Floor

The ground is not dirt. It is not rock exactly. It is a paleite — compacted mineral substrate, somewhere betweenite andite andite andite,ite compressed over deep time into a surface that yields slightly underfoot, like walking onite very old leather. It is cool always. The temperature does not fluctuate. There is no reason for it to fluctuate, and so it does not.

The surface is damp in patches. Not muddy — there is no soil to become mud — but slick with a fine condensation that beads on the mineral grain and gives every flat surface a faint, greasy gleam under the light. The light is normal. That is the strange thing about it. The light is the plain light of an overcast day that never clears and never darkens, a uniform illumination with no visible source and no shadow-play, as though the sky itself were a flat white panel suspended just out of sight. There are no suns here, or perhaps there are, but they have reached an agreement.

Water collects in the low places. Not streams — there is nothing to channel them. Just shallow, motionless pockets, two or three inches deep, sitting in shallow depressions in the paleite like mercury in a cupped palm. They reflect the white light. They have always reflected the white light. Nothing flows into them and nothing flows out. They exist in the same equilibrium as everything else, and their surfaces are perfectly, impossibly still.

The air smells ofite mineral chalk and wet copper and something underneath that is organic in the old sense — not biological exactly, but thick, mineral-adjacent, like the smell of a cave that has been sealed for geological time.

---

## The Structure

The settlement — and it is a settlement, not a ruin, not a site, but a functioning and occupied place — occupies perhaps two hundred meters across. It could be smaller. The boundaries are ambiguous, not because they are hidden but because the place simply *is* this size and has always been this size and does not think about boundaries the way living things do.

The buildings are industrial. This is the dominant character of the built environment. They are low structures, mostly single-story, with high roofs and heavy walls, constructed from the same paleite substrate as the ground but cut and fitted with a precision that speaks of machinery and time. The walls are thick — half a meter or more — and smooth on their outer faces, eroded to a soft matte finish by whatever passes for weather here. The erosion is complete. These buildings have been eroded to their final state. They will not erode further. They have reached the shape that the world has decided they should have.

Pipes run between the buildings at ground level and overhead, suspended on short pylons of the same pale material. They are large — some a meter across — and they hum. They all hum. The hum is constant, unchanging, a vibration you feel in your teeth before you hear it with your ears. Some of the pipes are damp. Condensation runs down their undersides in thin, steady trickles that never vary, pooling at the joints and dripping onto the pale ground with a metronomic regularity — *tik, tik, tik* — that never speeds up or slows down.

There are vents. Square and rectangular openings in the walls and roofs of the buildings, from which warmish air exhales steadily, carrying that mineral-copper smell and something else — a dry, enzymatic warmth, like the breath of a kiln that has been banked low and left alone for centuries.

The structures are sparse. There is much space between them — twenty meters, thirty meters, wide gaps of bare pale ground and standing water. The settlement is urban in its organization but not in its density. It is a town that was laid out for a population that does not need to cluster, that has spread itself comfortably across the basin floor with the unhurried logic of something that has always been here and will always be here.

Between the buildings, on the pale ground, there are marks. Not footprints — not exactly. The ground is covered in a fine tracery of grooves and scratches and shallow channels, some old, some newer, all of them geometric in a way that is not quite architectural and not quite biological. They look like something was dragged. Many things were dragged. The marks overlap in layers that go back further than you can see.

## The Fauna

This is the part that is difficult.

The fauna is overwhelming. The basin, the settlement, the buildings, the pipes, the pools of still water — everything is covered in and surrounded by and inhabited by *organisms*, and there are so many of them that the first impression is not of individual creatures but of a texture, a surface variation, a way that the pale ground and the pale walls are never quite smooth, never quite still, always shifting and clicking and breathing with a million small lives.

They are not animals. Not in any way you would recognize the term. They are the size of fists and the size of heads and the size of small dogs, and they move across the paleite on legs that are too thin and too many, their bodies encased in mineral shells that match the color of the ground so precisely that a stationary creature is nearly invisible. They are everywhere. They cluster on the damp pipes, on the walls of buildings, around the edges of the water pools. They are on the ground beneath your feet. You cannot take a step without displacing them, and they move aside with a soft, collective rustle, like dry leaves — but there are no leaves here, there have never been leaves here — and then close behind you again.

Their shells are paleite-colored, but not uniformly. Some carry a blue-green tinge, a patina of copper salts drawn up from whatever they eat. They eat the mineral. They eat the ground. They eat the walls. They process the paleite through bodies that are more geology than biology, excreting behind them a finer powder, aite chalk dust that settles into the grooves and marks on the ground and fills them slowly and then is walked over and packed down and becomes part of the substrate again.

They are loud.

Not individually. Individually they are nearly silent — a soft clicking of mandible-structures, a faint chitinous rasp. But there are so many. The cumulative sound is a constant, unchanging roar that sits beneath every other noise like a foundation. It is the sound of ten thousand small mineral jaws working on paleite, ten thousand shells scraping against pipes and walls, ten thousand thin legs clicking on the hard ground. It does not rise or fall. It does not pulse. It is simply *there*, the way gravity is there, a permanent acoustic floor that you stop noticing and then, suddenly, notice again, and the noticing is vertiginous.

Some of them are larger. The size of a man's torso, with shells that are ridged and plated, moving more slowly along the pipes with a purposeful deliberation, their mouths — if they are mouths — working at the mineral deposits that form along the pipe joints. These larger ones leave shallow tracks. They have always left these tracks. The tracks are deep.

And some are very small — too small to see individually — and they fill the air in fine clouds that hang motionless in the still atmosphere, drifting only when the warm exhale from a vent pushes them aside, reforming immediately behind the current. They are reproductive stages, or dormant stages, or something for which you do not have a word. They settle on every surface and add to the fine mineral film that coats everything.

None of them pay you any attention. None of them are afraid. This is a place that has not known predation or disruption or change of any kind for longer than can be reckoned, and the fauna exists in the same frozen equilibrium as the water pools and the hum of the pipes and the uniform white light. They have their place. They have always had their place.

---

## The Sound, Again

Because the sound is important and the sound does not stop.

The fauna. The pipes. The dripping water — *tik, tik, tik* — in every pool and at every joint, ten thousand metronomes keeping perfect, unchanging time. The deep vibration of whatever machinery is housed in the low buildings, running at a frequency you feel in the bones of your chest, never cycling up or down, never pausing for maintenance or startup or shutdown, simply *running*, the way it has always run, the way it will always run.

And beneath all of this, a sound you cannot identify. A low, vast, subsonic presence that is felt more than heard, as though the paleite ground itself were resonating at the very bottom of perception. It has no source. It has no cause. It is simply the sound of this place existing, this small, low, damp, mineral basin with its humming pipes and its chitinous fauna and its still water and its white light, this place that has been exactly this for longer than memory, that will be exactly this for longer than prophecy.

You stand on the pale ground. The creatures shift around your feet. The pipes hum. The water drips. The fauna clicks and rasps. The air smells of copper and chalk and dry enzyme-warmth. Nothing changes. Nothing has ever changed. You could stay here for an hour or a century and it would be the same — the same light, the same sound, the same dampness, the same slow mineral appetite of a million small hard bodies working the same ground, the same walls, the same pipes, in the same arrangement, under the same sky.

The settlement is complete. The settlement is in balance. The settlement is waiting for nothing, because nothing is coming, because nothing comes here, because this is a place at the end of all change, a small industrial basin in a low landscape of pale mineral, inhabited by creatures that eat stone and excrete chalk, fed by pipes that carry what they carry from nowhere to nowhere, lit by a light that has no source, filled with a sound that has no silence.

This is the Combing. It has always been the Combing. You cannot change it. You can only stand in it, among the clicking and the dripping and the hum, and feel the wet mineral ground under your feet, and watch the pale creatures move their pale bodies across the pale walls, and know that this moment is every moment, and every moment is this moment, and the basin is small, and the basin is full, and the basin does not end.
