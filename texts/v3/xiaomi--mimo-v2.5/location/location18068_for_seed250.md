# The Becoming Sea

The water has no bottom — or if it does, it is so far below that distance itself becomes theoretical. What you see, looking in any direction, is volume. Immense, cool, luminous volume, lit from above by a light source that behaves correctly, as sunlight should, yet whose origin no one below could name. The light penetrates cleanly, as though the water were filtered through a lens the size of a hemisphere, and it holds its brightness to shocking depth. This is not the sunken twilight of any ocean you could find elsewhere. This is an interior day.

The scale is continental. You could swim — if swimming were possible here, which it is not, not in any way a body could manage — for weeks and find the same texture of emptiness. The water is clear almost to the point of absurdity. There are no particles, no drift, no marine snow. It is as though someone filled a basin the size of a tectonic plate with distilled water and then placed a single slow fire above it. The emptiness between things is not merely large; it is the dominant feature. The ocean here is defined more by what it does not contain than by anything it does.

And what it contains — slowly, violently, in the way that geology is violent on timescales the eye can barely register — is *forming*.

Mountains are arriving.

They are not growing from the seafloor in any conventional sense, because there is no seafloor to speak of, only more water, deeper and deeper, the pressure increasing in ways that should crush stone but instead seem to encourage it. What emerges are pillars of raw mineral, naked and unweathered, their surfaces faceted like something grown rather than eroded. They are young in a way that has nothing to do with age and everything to do with incompleteness. Edges are sharp as crystal. Surfaces show the grain structure of rock that has not yet decided what kind of rock it will be — basalt and quartz and things that don't have names flickering through the same formation like channels on a screen. The tallest of these pillars, still ascending, has broken the surface somewhere far to the north, and you can feel the shockwave of that breach transmitted through the water: a standing turbulence, a permanent roar that has become the background noise of this place. The sound is enormous. It is the sound of a continent pushing itself into existence.

Between these rising monoliths, the water moves in ways that should not be possible at this scale. Currents curl through the empty space with the behavior of weather systems — cyclonic, shearing, folding back on themselves — but they carry no sediment, no debris, no life. They are currents of *force* alone, visible only by the way they bend the light and create transient lenses, magnifying and distorting the pillars beyond. Every few minutes, one of these currents collides with a formation and the impact resonates through the stone with a sound like a bell the size of a city. The pillars ring. The water shudders. New fractures appear in the rock — not damage, but *revision*, as though the formation is editing itself in response to the collision.

There is vegetation. Sparse, tentative, clinging to the mid-slopes of the older pillars — the ones that have been rising longest, whose surfaces have begun to stabilize into something recognizable as stone. It is not plant life in any familiar sense. These are filaments, hair-thin, luminous faintly in the ambient light, anchored to the rock by structures that look like they were designed rather than grown. They sway in the currents but do not bend; they resist the flow and in resisting, they generate their own turbulence, small eddies that spiral outward and merge with the larger chaos. They seem to be *mining* the rock — at the point of attachment, the stone is always slightly newer, slightly less resolved, as though the filaments are feeding on the geology's uncertainty, living on its incompleteness. Where the vegetation is thickest, the pillars are slowest to solidify. A parasitic relationship, or perhaps a symbiotic one. The filaments may be buying the rock time.

No animal moves through any of this. The absence is conspicuous, not because you expect fish or leviathans but because everything else here is so active that the stillness of fauna feels like a deliberate omission. The space that would be occupied by swimming things — the mid-water, the corridors between pillars, the thermoclines and pressure gradients where life traditionally gathers — is empty. Perfectly, aggressively empty. The turbulence passes through these zones without resistance, and the light falls through them unbroken, and the only sound is the roaring of stone being born and water being displaced.

What is strangest — what edges toward the unusual without quite tipping over — is the *precision* of it all. The pillars do not erode. They do not accumulate sediment or biofilm or any of the patient cruft that defines underwater geology in the ordinary world. They remain raw. They remain at the moment of their emergence, frozen in the act of formation, and the water around them acts as a kind of medium that preserves this state. The temperature holds cool and constant, never warming, never cooling, maintained by some equilibrium that has nothing to do with depth or surface conditions. The light does not attenuate with depth as it should. The pressure does not increase as it should. The physics of the place are *cooperative* — they have agreed to support this process, whatever it is, and they do not enforce their usual rules with their usual conviction.

There is a sound beneath the roaring. If you listen past the collisions and the resonance, past the hissing of the currents, there is a tone. Low. Continuous. It is the frequency of the water itself, vibrating in sympathy with the ascending stone, and it does something to the sense of distance. When you hear it — when it gets past the noise and arrives in whatever serves as your awareness — the vastness of the place does not shrink, but it *organizes*. The emptiness suddenly has structure. The distance between pillars is no longer void; it is interval, spacing, the deliberate gaps in a composition that is still being played.

The whole ocean is an instrument, and the mountains are its notes, and it is very, very loud, and it is not finished.
