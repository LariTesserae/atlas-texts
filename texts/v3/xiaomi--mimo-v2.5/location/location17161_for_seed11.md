# The Hollowlands

You are standing inside the throat of the world.

The terrain is stone — but not the stone of mountains still being born. This is stone that finished becoming itself a very long time ago, and has been doing nothing but breathing ever since. The rock is a deep ochre-red, veined with pale mineral seams that catch the light like healed scars. It has been eroded not into crags but into *chambers*: vast, open-mouthed amphitheaters hundreds of meters across, connected by winding corridors and sudden vertical drops, all of it worn smooth as river-polished bone. The edges are soft everywhere. There are no sharp angles left on earth. Everything has been sanded, scoured, and breathed upon until it arrived at its final, irreducible shape. The landscape looks like the inside of an instrument.

And it is an instrument.

**The sound is the first thing that annihilates you.**

Wind moves through the Hollowlands at velocities that seem impossible for air alone. It doesn't blow *across* this landscape — it blows *through* it, channeled by the ancient corridors into sustained, roaring harmonic frequencies. Stand in one of the great open basins and the sound is not one noise but a stack of them: a deep subsonic rumble beneath your feet (the wind compressing in lower tunnels), a middle-register howl that fills your chest, and above it a keening whistle where air shears across the lip of a high ridge. The resonances shift depending on where you stand. Move fifty meters and the dominant pitch changes. Certain narrow passages between basins produce a sound like an enormous bowed string — a single, sustained note that vibrates the fillings in your teeth and makes the loose pebbles on the ground dance in slow, dreamlike orbits.

The wind never stops. It shifts direction — sometimes two opposing currents meet in a basin and spiral upward in a vortex — but it never diminishes. There is no quiet place here. Even sleep becomes an act of surrender.

Between the roaring passages, the basins are staggeringly open. You can see across one for half a kilometer before the far wall rises, smooth and curved, like the inside of a bowl made for giants. The sky above is enormous, visible in every direction — there are no peaks to hem you in, because the peaks were eroded away long before anyone could name them. What remains is elevated terrain of extreme height — you are high, perhaps two or three thousand meters up — but it reads as a plateau, a high desert of sculpted hollows. The horizon is distant and wavy with heat.

---

**And yet it is alive.**

The warmth is steady and generous — not the heat of a low-latitude sun but a pervasive, mineral warmth that rises from below. The rock holds heat. The basins trap it. And where the wind-driven moisture — a fine, persistent mist that the air carries in from somewhere beyond the plateau's edge — collects in the hollows, vegetation has staged an improbable invasion.

It is *lush* in defiance of every other thing about this place. In the basin floors, where the rock has been ground down to a gritty, mineral-rich substrate, plants have taken root with a ferocity that seems almost aggressive. Broad-leafed ground cover spreads in mats of deep green and blue-green, threaded with pale root structures that grip the stone. Ferns — enormous, prehistoric-looking things — unfurl in the sheltered lees of rock walls. In places where the wind eddies have deposited soil over millennia, small trees have established: gnarled, wind-shaped, but *leafy*, their canopies dense and dark. The moisture condenses on every surface in the early hours — the rock "sweats," is the only way to describe it — and the plants drink. There are mosses on every surface that faces away from the dominant wind, thick as felt, in greens so vivid they look painted.

The ecology is strange, though. Sparse fauna — a few species of hard-shelled insects that cling to the undersides of rocks, a ground-dwelling lizard that seems to navigate by sound rather than sight, and birds — a small number of birds that have learned to ride the wind corridors like thermal highways, swooping through the passages at dizzying speed, their calls instantly swallowed by the roar. You see them more than you hear them. They are dark shapes against the ochre walls, impossibly fast, banking off updrafts with a precision that suggests this chaos has a grammar they've learned to read.

---

**The strangeness accumulates if you stay.**

The vegetation, on closer inspection, grows in patterns. Not random. In the basin floors, the broad-leafed ground cover radiates outward from certain points in near-perfect concentric rings, as though responding to a buried source — a warm spring, perhaps, or something else. The rings are too regular. They pulse slightly in the wind, and in the shifting light they create a moiré pattern across the basin floor that is genuinely disorienting.

Then there is the way the wind behaves at the thresholds between basins. The sound is continuous, yes, but at certain passages the air itself seems to *organize*. You feel it — a sudden coherence in the turbulence, as though the wind has momentarily remembered what it was trying to say. It lasts a few seconds. Then it dissolves back into roaring chaos. It happens again, at a different passage, a few hours later. You begin to wonder if it's a cycle.

The condensation, too, is uneven in ways that don't follow the obvious rules of topography. Some basins are slick with moisture while adjacent ones, apparently identical, are dry. The mist collects preferentially near certain rock formations — pale, cylindrical pillars that stand isolated in the basins like fingers of bone. These pillars are geological features, clearly: harder strata that resisted erosion while the softer rock around them was carved away. But they are *warm* to the touch, warmer than the surrounding stone, and the air near them shimmers.

---

**Human traces are faint and old.**

You find them by accident, because you are looking at the ground to avoid being knocked over by a gust. A flat stone, deliberately placed, its edges shaped with simple tools — a hand-axe, probably, something hard struck against soft stone. A cairn in a basin corner, three or four rocks stacked, its purpose unknowable. A shallow depression that might have been a fire pit, lined with heat-cracked pebbles arranged in a ring. The marks of habitation are so minimal they could almost be natural. But they aren't.

Someone came here. Primitive tool-users — early, careful, deliberate people who carried stone implements and built nothing permanent. Perhaps they sheltered in the deeper corridors where the wind was muffled to a tolerable hum. Perhaps they collected water from the condensation that dripped from the pillar formations. Perhaps they came for reasons that weren't practical at all — because the sound had meaning, or because the concentric rings in the vegetation pointed to something buried that they understood and we don't.

There is a single artifact that feels different from the rest: a stone set into a crevice in one of the corridor walls, at a height where a standing adult could rest their hand on it. It has been smoothed. It has been gripped so many times, over so many years, that the natural grain of the rock has been worn into a concave surface that fits a palm perfectly. It is the most intimate object in a landscape of enormous indifference. Someone stood here, in the roaring dark of a wind-corridor, and held this stone while something happened that they needed to be held through.

---

**At night, the Hollowlands become another country.**

The warmth persists — the stone releases its stored heat slowly, and the basins glow with a residual warmth that keeps the temperature even and soft. The condensation intensifies. Every surface beads with water that catches starlight. The wind drops, slightly, in the deepest basins — not to silence, never to silence, but to a low and continuous moan that is almost bearable. The bioluminescent mosses — and there are bioluminescent mosses, pale blue-green, growing in the dampest crevices — light up in patches that map the moisture distribution across the landscape like a living contour chart.

The stars overhead are extraordinary. At this elevation, with this clarity, with this thin and constantly-renewed air, the sky is not dark but *deep* — a three-dimensional space filled with light at every distance. The Milky Way is not a smear but a structure, visibly layered, and beneath it the hollowed basins of the ancient rock lie like cupped hands waiting to catch whatever falls.

Nothing falls. The landscape has been waiting for a very long time, and it is very good at waiting. It has finished its business with geology. It is doing something else now, something slower, something the wind is trying to articulate in a language that has too many frequencies to parse.

You will not understand it tonight. You may not understand it ever. But you will feel it in your teeth, and in the hollow of your chest, and in the way your hand finds that worn stone in the corridor wall without being told where it is.
