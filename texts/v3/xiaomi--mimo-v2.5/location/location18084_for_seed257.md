# The Kiln-Seat

You are deep. The water knows no surface — has not known one in a time that preceded any living memory. The pressure is constant and unremarkable, like gravity. You have always been here, or it has always been you.

The place is small. It could be walked around in minutes, if walking were possible, if there were ground in the usual sense. What there is, is *built*. Everything. The walls rising on all sides like the interior of a cup — or a kiln, which is what the creatures who made it called it, insofar as they called anything. Hand-formed basalt and calcium. Mortar ofite andite and ground bone, so ancient now that the joints have fused into the material itself. The builders are gone. The building remains. The building is all there is.

It sits high. A raised plateau on the floor of something that has no name, a platform of constructed stone rising from a seabed that descends in every direction into absolute black. The water around and above is warm — not hot, not cold, but the warmth of depth, of geothermal patience, of a world that has not been stirred from below or cooled from above in epochs. The warmth is uniform. There are no currents. The slow dynamic of this place is measured in centuries: the imperceptible thinning of a wall, the generational drift of a colony of things from one chamber to the next.

There is no light. There has never been light here. The builders did not build for light. They navigated — if they navigated — by pressure, by mineral taste, by the electric whisper of proximity. Now the fauna that has inherited the Kiln-Seat navigates the same way, and knows nothing of light as a concept, the way a stone knows nothing of flight.

And the fauna is *dense*.

They fill the chambers the way water fills a vessel, which is to say completely, which is to say you must move through them. Soft-bodied things with cartilaginous scaffolds, no bones, no shells — the builders ate anything with a shell, long ago, and the evolutionary memory lingers. They are pale, translucent in patches, veined with bioluminescence so faint it registers not as light but as a feeling — a sense that the water immediately around you is slightly less dark than the water a meter away. You would not call it seeing. You would call it knowing where things are.

There are swarms of them. Tiny, eyeless, with fringed mouths that comb the water for particulate — the steady rain of marine snow that is the only food, the only energy entering the system from above. They move in slow ribbons along the walls, along the floors of the chambers, following whatever invisible gradients of chemistry mark the best feeding lanes. Denser aggregations cluster in the corners and recesses of the architecture, where the ancient hand-formed channels still funnel the faint particulate drift into concentrated streams. The builders designed these channels. The builders are gone. The channels still work. This is the deepest kind of architecture — one that serves its inhabitants after the inhabitants who made it have been forgotten.

Larger things move through the open spaces. Slow, deliberate. They are almost flat — wide-bodied, laterally compressed, with undulating margins that ripple in frequencies too low to see but that you feel in your chest. They are grazers, though they graze on nothing vegetal. They crop the dense aggregations of smaller fauna from the walls with fringed, rasping mouthparts. Their passage leaves pale tracks in the dark mineral surfaces — not marks, exactly, but brief absences where the swarms scatter and do not re-coalesce for minutes.

In the deepest chambers, where the constructed walls are thickest and the water is most still, there are the keepers. Or what the keepers became. They are sessile now — attached to the walls by root-like extensions of tissue, not rooted in any botanical sense but rooted in the way that a thing roots when it has stopped expecting to move. They pulse. Slowly. A cycle of expansion and contraction that takes — you would guess, if you could measure — somewhere between four and six minutes. Each pulse sends a low-frequency pressure wave through the water that the smaller fauna seem to respond to: tightening their aggregations, pressing closer to the walls, becoming, for a moment, more dense and less dispersed. The keepers do not eat. The keepers do not reproduce. The keepers do something else, something that has no analog in the world above, if there is a world above. They maintain the mineral structure. Not actively — not by any action you could observe. But in their presence, the calcium walls do not dissolve. In the deep chambers where the keepers cluster, the ancient mortar holds. In the outer chambers where the keepers are absent, the walls have thinned, have softened, have begun their long return to unstructured sediment.

This is the rule of the place: the building maintains the keepers; the keepers maintain the building. A loop with no origin, no author. A circuit that has been running for longer than any of its components have existed in their current form.

The sound — or near-absence of sound — is the most striking thing to a visitor, if visitors existed, which they do not. The fauna produce no calls, no songs, no clicks. Communication, insofar as it occurs, is chemical and pressure-based. The water carries taste. The water carries push. There is no need for sound when the medium itself is information. The only noise is the faint, continuous creak of the structure settling under its own weight, a sound so low and so constant that it registers not as sound but as presence — the way you do not hear your own heartbeat until it stops.

There is the strangeness, too. Small. The kind of strangeness that does not announce itself but accumulates as you look. The walls in certain chambers bear marks — not natural erosion, not biological scarring. Grooves. Patterns. Repeated, deliberate, spaced at intervals that suggest intention without suggesting meaning. They are too old to read. They were possibly too old to read when the builders still lived. They may have been records, or prayers, or structural reinforcements disguised as decoration, or decoration disguised as something else entirely. They are part of the wall now. They are the wall's final face — the face it has worn into after ages of patient dissolution, the face that the keepers maintain and the fauna crawl across without comprehension.

The warmth does not change. The darkness does not change. The fauna drift and crop and pulse in their cycles, and the cycles repeat with a fidelity that makes time itself feel optional. You could stay here for years and observe nothing that could be called an event. You could stay for centuries and observe exactly the same nothing, slightly rearranged.

This is what remains when everything natural has been consumed, when everything built has been worn smooth, when the only processes left are the slow ones: dissolution, maintenance, grazing, pulsing. A hamlet-sized pocket of warmth and life and ancient handiwork, sitting high on a dark seabed, in water that has not seen the sky since before the sky was a concept.

The Kiln-Seat endures. It will continue to endure until the keepers die or the walls finally give or the water itself changes, none of which appears likely on any timescale that has meaning here. It is small. It is dense. It is dark and warm and nearly silent and full of things that have no name because there is no one to name them.

It is, in its way, complete.
