# The Pavement

There is no horizon in the usual sense. The land goes out and simply keeps going, flat as a fact, low enough that standing upright you can see the curvature of nothing at all — just a grey dimness where the stone meets the sky, and the sky, which is always overcast, a ceiling of water-laden cloud so constant it might be mistaken for solid stone at a greater height than the stone you're standing on.

The ground is old. That is the first and most inescapable impression. Not old the way a forest is old or a canyon is old — old the way the concept of ground itself is old. This is the bedrock reduced to its final argument. Billions of years of water have scoured it to a surface so low, so nearly flat, that the landscape reads more like a floor than a country. There are rises — barely rises, swells of stone no taller than a person, their surfaces worn into smooth asymmetries like the backs of sleeping animals. There are shallow basins where the stone dips and holds water in discs of grey-brown stillness. There are fracture-lines, ancient beyond measure, that run in long straight cuts across the surface, their edges so rounded by time they look poured rather than broken. These are the main topographic features. This is the mountain range of this world.

## Water

Water is the medium in which this place exists. It does not fall from the sky so much as *arrive* — a perpetual fine mist that is not quite rain, that thickens at certain hours into something heavier but never quite commits to being weather. The atmosphere is saturated. You can feel it on your skin, a cool dampness that never lifts, that settles into everything. The stone weeps. Every surface has a thin film of water moving across it so slowly that you can only tell by watching the mineral dust it carries, a barely perceptible creep of particles that will, over centuries, rearrange the face of the land by fractions.

In the basins, the water sits shallow — ankle-deep at most — and is not clear but turbid, milky with dissolved calcium and iron, stained faintly ochre or blue-grey depending on what stone it sits on. Where fracture-lines run deep, the water fills them like veins, flowing with a sluggish persistence, finding its way to lower basins, filling and overflowing and filling again in cycles that take hours, not days. Condensation beads on every vertical surface. The rocks are perpetually slick. There is no dry stone here. Even the highest swells carry moisture in their pores, and if you press your hand against one you can feel the stone sweat against your palm.

## The Mineral World

There are no plants. This must be said clearly, because the eye, arriving here, expects green the way it expects a floor to be flat. There is no green. There is no brown of dead growth, no tawny grass, no moss, no lichen — or what appears to be lichen. The stone surfaces are coated in mineral crusts: botryoidal formations in shades of bone-white, rust, and pale celadon that grow in cauliflower-heads along fracture edges. Crystalline efflorescence — delicate, branching, almost feathery — blooms across sheltered surfaces where evaporation leaves its residue. In the basins, mineral precipitates build terraced rims, millimeters at a time, the way a hot spring builds travertine but slower, so slow that the builders have been working since before the concept of living things existed in any recognizable sense.

Some of these formations blur the line. In the shallower pools, certain mineral growths — call them concretions, call them colonies, call them whatever you like — exhibit a branching pattern that seems too deliberate for pure chemistry. They are pale, translucent, vaguely tubular, rising from the pool floor in tangled stands a hand's breadth tall. They are not alive in any way a biologist would confirm, but they are not inert either. They grow. They respond to vibration by contracting. They reproduce by fragmenting. They exist in the taxonomic no-man's-land between mineral precipitation and something else, something the world has not yet named or has long since forgotten. They are the closest thing to vegetation this place has, and they are not vegetation.

## Fauna

Despite all of this — despite the cold, the wet, the mineral poverty, the absence of any food chain as traditionally understood — the place teems.

The fauna is abundant and strange and it is everywhere, and it is the reason this place is so loud.

In the shallow basins, the water is thick with life. Small, pale, soft-bodied things — not quite mollusks, not quite worms, not quite crustaceans — move through the mineral turbidity in dense aggregations, filter-feeding on precipitated particles, on the mineral colonies, on each other when the aggregations grow thin. They are translucent, ranging from the size of a grain of rice to the length of a finger, and they move with a slow undulation that barely disturbs the water's surface. There are millions of them. In some basins the water itself seems to pulse with their collective motion. They make a sound — a faint, continuous clicking, individually inaudible but collectively a soft static that underlies everything, like the noise a television makes between channels, heard from another room.

Above them, in the air, are the fliers. These are the ones that scream.

They are not insects. They have no wings in the insect sense — instead, a membranous sail of tissue stretched over a cartilaginous frame, and they navigate the low, still air with a flight that is less flying than controlled falling, tilting and gliding and dropping in long spiraling descents before catching an updraft off the wet stone and rising again. They are bone-colored, eyeless, with flat discs where a head should be — sensory organs that read vibration and chemical gradient. They range from the size of a thumbnail to the span of a human hand, and they are present in the hundreds of thousands, wheeling over the basins and fracture-lines in loose flocks that shift and reform like smoke.

And they are deafening.

The sound they produce is not a call, not exactly. It is a resonance — a vibration produced by the rapid flexion of their flight membranes, amplified by the flat stone below, by the water, by the saturated air itself. A single flier produces a thin, high keen, barely noticeable. A flock produces a drone. But flocks do not exist in isolation here. They exist in numbers that defy comprehension, and their resonant frequencies compound and interfere and build on each other until the air itself seems to vibrate. The sound has a physical presence. You feel it in your sternum, in the fillings of your teeth, in the wet surface of your eyes. It rises and falls in waves as flocks cross each other's paths, creating interference patterns that swell to a roar and then fall back to a shriek and then swell again. There are moments — often, moments — when the sound is so total that thought becomes difficult, when the landscape is reduced to a vibration in which you are a slightly denser concentration of matter.

Beneath all of this, on the stone surfaces themselves, there are the crawlers. These are larger, slower, armored in mineral-encrusted plates that make them look like moving pieces of the landscape. They are the grazers of this world, scraping the mineral crusts and crystalline growths from the stone with mouthparts that work like chisels. They leave trails — clean stone exposed beneath the film of mineral deposit, paths that fork and merge and persist for weeks before the crusts grow back. They are the size of a human fist at their smallest and the size of a human torso at their largest, and they move with a patience that borders on geological, but they are alive, undeniably, and they are many.

## Sound

You have already heard about the sound. But it must be emphasized, because it is the defining sensory experience of this place, more than the wetness, more than the grey light, more than the ancient flatness.

The sound never stops. It modulates — the fliers' resonance rises and falls, the crawlers' chiseling adds a sporadic staccato like stones being struck together, the basin fauna add their subliminal clicking — but it never ceases. At night (and it does grow dimmer, though it never becomes truly dark, the cloud cover diffusing what little light exists into a uniform grey glow), the fliers settle and the sound drops to something almost bearable, a deep hum, a resonance in the stone. But the basins keep their clicking, and the crawlers keep their chiseling, and the water keeps its slow movement through the fracture-lines, a sound like breathing. Then, as the grey light strengthens again, the fliers rise, and the sound builds, and by what passes for midday, the air is a wall of vibration.

There are places — fracture-lines deep enough to act as resonating chambers — where the sound becomes so concentrated that the stone itself vibrates visibly, where small mineral formations topple and the crawlers avoid the edges. There are places — sheltered hollows on the lee side of the stone swells — where the sound drops enough to think clearly, and these are the only places where the scattered evidence of habitation makes sense.

## The Structures

They are few. They are old. They are simple.

Along one of the deeper fracture-lines, where the walls rise to a person's height and the sound drops to something like manageable, someone — or something — built a wall. It is a dry-stone wall, fitted without mortar, the stones chosen for their shape and placed with a deliberation that reads as intelligence. It does not enclose anything. It runs for perhaps twenty meters and then stops. Its purpose is unclear. It may have been a windbreak. It may have been a marker. It may have been the beginning of something that was never finished. The stones are coated in the same mineral crusts as everything else, and in places the crusts have filled the gaps between them so completely that the wall and the natural stone have begun to merge. It is old enough to be returning to the landscape.

Elsewhere, there are cairns — small, rough piles of stone atop the larger swells, placed at intervals that suggest deliberate spacing. Some have been arranged in a pattern: a central pile surrounded by a ring of smaller ones. Some have been arranged around what appear to be the large crawlers' resting sites, though whether as offerings or markers or traps is impossible to say. The crawlers move through and over them without apparent awareness.

In one place, on a swell whose summit is just high enough to escape the worst of the basin water, there is what might be a shelter. Three walls of fitted stone, open on one side, the interior space large enough for a person to crouch in. The floor is covered in a thin layer of mineral deposit, suggesting it has been unoccupied for a very long time. But the fitting of the stones is precise, deliberate, skilled. Whoever built this knew stone. Whoever built this could make something that would last. And it has lasted, though what it was meant to last *for* has long since passed.

Scattered across the landscape, less organized: single worked stones. A flat stone with a groove cut along its length, half-buried in mineral crust. A rounded stone with a grip worn smooth. A fragment of what might have been a vessel, its rim shaped with care, its interior filled with the same turbid water that fills every basin. These are the artifacts. They are few and far between and each one sits in the landscape like a question.

## Atmosphere and Light

The light is dim and even and sourceless. The cloud cover is absolute, a seamless grey vault that admits light without permitting shadow. Colors are muted — the world exists in a narrow palette of grey, brown, pale blue, bone-white, and the dull rust of iron deposits. Everything is slightly blurred at the edges, the moisture in the air softening outlines, so that distant features — and you can see far, because the land is flat — appear as suggestions rather than shapes, smudges of slightly different grey against grey.

The temperature is cool — not cold, not freezing, but the kind of cool that seeps in and stays, the cool of a deep cellar, of a stone room in autumn. The water on the surfaces is never warm. The air is never warm. There is no sun to provide warmth, only the diffuse grey glow that is more illumination than heat. You dress for it or you suffer for it, and there is no way to warm yourself that does not involve movement. The crawlers seem unbothered. The fliers generate heat through their constant flight. The basin fauna are cold-blooded, or whatever the equivalent is here, thermally indifferent, shaped by a world that has never offered them anything warmer.

## Ecology, or What Passes For It

There is a system here, though it does not resemble any textbook food web. The mineral precipitation is the base — the slow chemical work of water on ancient stone, producing deposits of calcium carbonate, iron oxides, silica, a thin but persistent harvest of material that would, in another world, be called soil. The mineral colonies in the pools process this material into structures that are not quite food but serve the function of food for the basin fauna, who graze and filter and, in dying, contribute their own mass back to the water, where it dissolves and re-precipitates and becomes mineral again. The crawlers eat the crusts directly, scraping them from the stone, and their waste — chalky,ite,ite-rich — feeds the colony growth in turn. The fliers eat the crawlers' larvae and the basin fauna when they venture to the surface, and their own dead bodies, falling into the water or onto the stone, dissolve into the same mineral cycle.

It is a closed system. It has been closed for a very long time. It does not need the sun, does not need photosynthesis, does not need the green world. It runs on chemistry, on the energy stored in the ancient stone, on the slow exothermic reactions of mineral dissolution and precipitation, on the cool wetness that mediates every exchange. It is sparse — the energy budget is tight, and the landscape can support many creatures but not *dense* creatures, not creatures with high metabolisms, not creatures that burn hot. Everything here moves slowly or moves efficiently or moves in enormous numbers to compensate for individual insignificance.

And it is active. For all its ancient stillness, for all its settled-into-final-form quality, things are happening. Water moves. Minerals precipitate. The fauna eat and reproduce and die and are recycled. The fliers rise and fall. The crawlers leave their trails. The fracture-lines slowly widen as water freezes in them — not often, not dramatically, but enough, over enough time, to pry the stone apart by fractions of millimeters per year. The basins slowly deepen. The swells slowly flatten. The landscape is dying, if you can call it that — it is reaching a state of such perfect equilibrium that eventually nothing will change at all, and it may already be there, and the changes you see may be the last tremors of a system that has already achieved its final form.

## The Unusual

There is something else.

It is hard to name. It is a quality of the place rather than a feature of it. The fliers, in their flocks, occasionally align — thousands of them wheeling into a formation that holds for seconds or minutes, a shape in the air, a geometry that is too regular to be random and too complex to be mere flocking instinct. The shape is different each time. It is never repeated. It is never explained. The sound changes when the formation holds — the interference patterns shift, and for a moment the deafening chaos resolves into something almost harmonic, a chord, a structure in the noise, before the flock breaks apart and the chaos returns.

The mineral colonies in the basins sometimes grow in patterns that mirror the fracture-lines of the bedrock — not approximately, but precisely, branching at the same angles, following the same geometry, as though the stone is dictating their growth at a level deeper than chemistry. If you photograph them from above, the colony networks and the fracture networks are indistinguishable.

The crawlers sometimes stop. They simply stop, mid-trail, their chisel-mouthparts still against the stone, and they remain motionless for hours. When they resume, they have changed direction. They do not appear to have been disturbed. They do not appear to have decided. They simply stopped and then went a different way, as though something in the stone told them to.

These things are not dramatic. They are not frightening. They are simply *there*, persistent and unexplained, woven into the fabric of a place so old and so settled that perhaps explanation is no longer relevant — perhaps the place has existed long enough that it has begun to mean things without needing to mean them, to pattern itself for reasons that have nothing to do with function, to *be* in a way that exceeds the categories available to describe it.

## Standing Here

You stand on ancient stone that sweats beneath your feet. The air is cool and thick with moisture, so wet it has a taste — mineral, faintly metallic, like licking a stone pulled from a river. The light is grey and sourceless and will not change. The sound is everywhere, a pressure, a presence, a thing that occupies the air as surely as the moisture does, rising and falling in waves that make the distinction between hearing and feeling meaningless.

Around you, the landscape extends in every direction — flat, stone, wet, scoured to its essence. The fliers wheel overhead in their vast, screaming flocks. The basin water clicks and stirs. The crawlers move their slow trails. The fracture-lines run out toward a horizon that does not quite exist, dark lines on grey stone, carrying water to basins you cannot see.

There are no trees. There is no grass. There is no birdsong, no rustling, no sound of leaves. There is only the stone and the water and the creatures that have learned to live between them, and the structures left by hands that may no longer exist, and the sound — the immense, unrelenting, deafening sound of a world that is alive in ways you were not built to understand, alive and old and wet and dim and full of something that, if you stayed long enough, you might begin to hear beneath the noise.

You would not stay long enough. Almost no one does.
