# The Seamount Cities

The rock is new. That is the first thing anyone notices, even before the water, even before the heat. It is *new* — black and glassy where it meets the waves, still glassy, still remembering the melt. In some places you can see where one flow met another and did not quite become one thing. The joints are visible, the seams. The stone has not yet decided what kind of stone it is.

Everything below the peaks is underwater. The valleys, the plains, whatever substrate existed before — all of it is gone beneath warm salt water that stretches in every direction to a horizon you cannot clearly see, because the air itself is thick with moisture and a low grey haze that may be cloud or may be steam or may be both. The water is not deep everywhere near the summits — in some places you can see the flanks of the mountain dropping away through green-brown shallows, the rock still raw, not yet colonized, not yet softened. But further out it deepens fast. The seamounts were steep when they were born and nothing has yet had time to erode them into gentleness.

The people — and there are people — live on the summits and upper slopes, wherever the rock breaks the surface long enough to stand on. They have been here long enough to build.

Their construction is dense and vertical, because the habitable ground is narrow. Stone stacked on stone with no mortar, fitted by hand and eye, rising in rough towers and terraces that cling to the ridgelines. The blocks are cut from the living rock beneath them — you can see where quarry-faces scar the slopes, pale against the black basalt. The tool-marks are everywhere: the patient gouge of stone-on-stone, the chip and fracture of hand-shaped basalt. They have copper, a little. Enough for chisels and awls and needles. Not enough for anything structural. Everything load-bearing is the mountain itself, shaped and stacked.

The terraces are where the lushness lives. Soil here is thin — more composted plant matter and volcanic ash than true earth — but what grows in it grows ferociously. Broad-leafed things with waxy surfaces, dense mats of some creeping plant that the people cultivate for fiber, root vegetables with pale tubers. Everything is green and fleshy and slightly too alive, as if the warmth and the mineral richness of the young rock have given the vegetation a kind of urgency. Ferns crowd the shaded faces of buildings. Moss fills every crack. The stone is slowly being consumed.

There are no trees. Not yet. The tallest woody plants are knee-high, bright green, with thick stems. It has not been long enough.

Below the waterline — and the people dive frequently, for food, for shellfish, for the things that grow on the submerged flanks — the rock is coated in a living film: algae and something else, something that is not quite coral, that builds structures of a kind no one on land would recognize, branching and porous and faintly luminous in the murk. The water is warm. The deeper you go, the warmer it gets, which is wrong, and the people know it is wrong, and they do not dive below a certain depth because of this.

The strangeness is not only in the warmth below. It is in the rock itself. In certain places — not many, but enough — the stone has a geometry that does not feel accidental. Hexagonal columns that are too regular. Cavities in the flow that open into chambers almost too symmetrical to be natural. One slope on the largest seamount has a surface texture that looks, in certain light, like script. No one can read it because it is not writing. Probably. The elders say it is just cooling patterns, just the way basalt fractures under tension, and the younger people nod and then go look at it again when no one is watching.

The fauna is sparse. Birds exist — seabirds, mostly, nesting on the few ledges that offer purchase, thin-legged and patient. In the water, there are schools of small fish that move through the shallows like clouds of metallic thought, and larger solitary things at depth that the people glimpse and do not name. On land, almost nothing. A few species of beetle. A small lizard, brown, fast. The ecosystem is young, the way the rock is young. It is still assembling itself.

Sound carries strangely across the warm water and the humid air. The settlements are quiet places — the drip of condensation from overhangs, the scrape of a stone tool on stone, the muffled collapse of a small wave against the base of a terrace. Voices do not carry far. The haze swallows them. There is a low, almost sub-audible vibration that the people have learned to feel through the rock under their feet, steady as a heartbeat, which they say is the mountain breathing. They may be right about this.

The cycle of days is slow and indistinct. The haze never fully clears. Dawn and dusk are distinguished only by a shift from grey to pale amber to grey again. Time here is measured by tides, by the growth-rate of the green film on the submerged rock, by the slow cooling of stone that is still, in places, too warm to touch. The people measure time in the length of a fiber crop — the creeping plant takes a fixed number of days to reach harvest — and in the deepening of the quarry-faces as they cut further into the mountain, and in the patient outward creep of terraces as each generation adds a few more courses of stone to the settlement's edge.

They are not afraid of the water. They are not afraid of the strangeness. They are afraid, if they are afraid, of the slow warmth climbing up from below, of the not-coral growing in geometries it should not know, of the fact that the mountain is still young and the water is still rising, very slowly, almost imperceptibly, and that one day the highest peak may be only a reef. But that day is far away. The processes here are slow. Everything is slow. The cooling, the growing, the building, the drowning. You could live a whole life here and notice nothing changing at all.
