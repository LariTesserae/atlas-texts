# The Pore

You are inside something.

Not inside a cave — that would imply a world outside to escape into. This is more like the interior of a bone you didn't know you'd entered. The space is small. Perhaps the volume of a large wardrobe, though its proportions are difficult to fix: the ceiling sometimes seems high and sometimes seems to press close, not because it moves but because you cannot keep it in your mind at a single distance. The walls are stone, or something like stone — pale, faintly translucent, with the grain and texture of calcium deposit orite. Light comes through them. Not from behind them, not from a source beyond them, but *through* them, as though the mineral itself is a membrane and illumination is one of its fluids. The brightness is steady and sourceless, without warmth, giving everything the clean, shadowless look of an operating theatre or the inside of an egg.

The floor is raw — not worn smooth but *new*, or rather, perpetually arriving. It is a surface still deciding what it is. In one corner, a lattice of tiny crystals, no larger than grains of rice, bristles upward like a crop just sprouted. By the time you look again, a few have lengthened. Others, elsewhere, have dissolved back into a faint mineral dust. The ground is a negotiation between solid and solution, between becoming and undoing. Thin films of water glaze many of the surfaces — not flowing, just *present*, as if the air itself sweated. Occasionally a droplet gathers at the lip of some protrusion and falls. The sound it makes — a single, small, wet note — is enormous in this silence. It is perhaps the only sound that exists here with any conviction.

## What Lives Here

The fauna is startling in its abundance, given the smallness of the world. The walls host a dense and industrious population of organisms that don't correspond neatly to anything named. Most are arthropod in their general logic — jointed, exoskeletal, many-legged — but wrong in specifics. Some are the size of mites and move in coordinated trails along the crystal faces, pausing to manipulate the mineral with mouthparts that seem designed for *shaping* rather than eating. They are builders, though what they build is unclear: small architectural accumulations of chewed orite, tiny columns and buttresses, little arches no wider than a fingenuity. These structures appear in the spaces between crystal clusters, filling gaps, reinforcing corners. They are scattered throughout the space like the remnants of a civilization at miniature scale, some clearly abandoned — crumbling, colonized by pale fungal threads — and others freshly made, still attended by their makers.

Other creatures are less industrious and more ghostly. Translucent worms, barely thicker than a hair, loop through the films of moisture on the walls, leaving no visible trail. Something with too many legs and no eyes drifts across the ceiling — or what functions as the ceiling — in slow, deliberate passes, pausing occasionally to insert a leg-tip into the mineral and hold, as though listening to the stone. There are things that move in the light itself: tiny, darting presences, less like organisms and more like decisions the light makes about where to be. They leave faint trails. They vanish. The sheer variety is disorienting. For every creature you can focus on, two more resolve at the edge of your attention, and the sense is not of a population but of a *community* — interconnected, layered, operating according to rules you can glimpse but not read.

## What Doesn't Work

The geometry is not trustworthy. This is the primary wrongness, the one that underlies everything else. The space, which should be a simple hollow — a few meters across at most, enclosed, finite — resists being measured. If you look at one wall, it is the correct distance away, perhaps an arm's reach. If you look away and look back, it is slightly farther. Not dramatically, not enough to call a trick — just enough to know that the stone does not believe in fixed distances. The corners connect oddly. There is a point, roughly at the center of the far wall, where two surfaces meet at an angle that isn't an angle. It looks like a corner, behaves like a corner, casts a shadow (the only visible shadow) like a corner — but the angle is not Euclidean. You cannot name it. If you try to trace it with your eye, your gaze slides off into something adjacent, a surface you were sure was elsewhere.

The water exhibits this too. A small pool on the floor — no more than a palm's breadth across — reflects something that is not the ceiling. It shows a shape, pale and luminous, like the interior of a much larger cavity, vaulted and distant. When you look up, nothing matches. When you look down again, the reflection has shifted: now it shows the ceiling directly above, but *from above*, looking down — the correct reflection of a surface that, from your vantage, you cannot see. It is always showing you a place that is not this place, using the visual vocabulary of this place. The water does not ripple when a drop falls into it. The drops fall in and are simply absorbed without event, as though the pool is not a surface but a depth.

Crystals appear in midair. Not often — once every few minutes, perhaps — but undeniably. A tiny, perfect hexagonal form, no larger than a freckle, solidifies in the bright space between two walls, hangs for a moment as though deciding whether to exist, then either drifts downward and joins the floor or vanishes. They are the same mineral as the walls. They suggest that the walls are not the boundaries of this place but merely the places where the mineral has accumulated most densely — that the entire volume of the pocket is slowly, invisibly, mineralizing. That this room is a cavity inside a process, and the process is still running.

The fauna seem aware of these impossibilities and untroubled by them. The builders work around the fluctuating geometry, adjusting their structures with what looks like practiced ease, as though the space's refusal to be fixed is a known condition, a weather. The eyeless ceiling-drifter pauses at the non-corner, routes around it, continues. Nothing here treats the breakdown as a breakdown. For the organisms, this is simply what the world does.

## What Remains

The scattered built elements suggest history, or something like it. Some of the tiny structures are clearly very old — eroded, calcified into the growing crystal faces, indistinguishable from geology. Others are fresh. A few appear to be *in between*: built, then deliberately dismantled, then rebuilt in a slightly different configuration, as though their builders were iterating on a design. The marks on the walls — scratch-patterns, arranged with a regularity that suggests intention rather than biology — extend in long, wandering lines across several meters of surface. They are shallow. They might be maps, or records, or territory lines. They are partially overgrown by new crystal, half-erased by the space's own ongoing formation, which gives them the quality of a palimpsest — text written over text, meaning layered on meaning, all of it slowly being swallowed by the medium that bore it.

There is no technology. No metal. No fire. The intelligence here is entirely biological and mineral — the knowing of organisms that build and the knowing of stone that grows. Between them, something like an ecosystem of *construction* operates, where the living and the geological are not separate processes but different speeds of the same one.

## The Silence

The near-total quiet is the strangest element, given how *active* the space is. Crystals grow without sound. Creatures move without friction. The air is still — there is no circulation, no draft, no convection. Moisture condenses and drips, and these are the only punctuation. It is not the silence of emptiness but of density: a silence like the one inside a living body, where processes are happening constantly but none of them produce sound for the outside. You feel, if you stand in this space long enough, that you are inside something that is not a room but an organ — a place where work is being done in quiet, and you are not the worker, and the work does not require you, and the fact that you can see it is incidental to the fact that it continues.

The bright, sourceless light does not waver. It does not dim. It does not come in cycles. The space does not know day or night. It knows only the present tense of its own slow crystallization, and the tiny lives that thread through it, and the geometry that declines to hold still, and the silence, which is not empty but full — full the way a held breath is full — waiting for something that may have already begun.
