# The Resonance Vaults

It is warm down here, and utterly dry. The air tastes ofite andite — ofite andite — no. The air tastes ofite. Powderedite. You feel it between your teeth.

A network of chambers. Not large — the biggest is perhaps forty paces across, vaulted to a height where the ceiling disappears into a dim amber haze that you never quite see source of. The walls are young. You can tell because the cuts are clean and sharp, almost surgical, the stone faceted in flat planes that haven't had time to soften. Some of the walls are natural — fractured limestone, pale as bone, split open along fault lines that hadn't existed a thousand years ago, and you sense it might not have existed a thousand years ago even in the geological sense, as though the rock here is *newer* than it should be. Fresher. Still settling into the idea of being stone.

The light comes from everywhere and nowhere. A dim amber wash that seems to emanate from the stone itself — the walls exude a faint chemical luminescence, a slow exothermic glow, the product of whatever industrial process once ran through here. Pipes still line the upper walls in tangled arterial clusters, thick iron and ceramic conduits some of which still hum with a low, subsonic vibration you feel in your sternum more than hear. A few have burst or corroded open, and from their ruptured mouths spillsite —ite — aite. From their ruptured mouths spillsite —ite — a fineite dust. Orange. Mineral. Dead.

None of this has changed. None of this will change. The pipes will not corrode further. The dust will not settle more than it has. There is a quality to the stillness that goes beyond mere calm — it is the stillness of a thing that has already finished changing. A photograph. A held breath that forgot to exhale.

And yet it is *full.*

Dense with life. Dense with presence. The fauna here is extraordinary in its abundance and strange in its form. Thick-bodied, six-limbed creatures the size of a man's fist cluster on every surface, their carapaces matte andite-colored, blending with the dusted stone. They do not move. You watch them for a long time, and they do not move, but you are certain they are alive — their abdomens pulse faintly, a rhythmic distension, and their mouthparts work in slow, grinding circles, processing the mineral dust that coats everything. There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. They layer the walls in living crusts, cling to the pipes in dense curtains, carpet the floor in a rustling, clicking mosaic that your feet cannot avoid stepping on. They do not flee from you. They do not respond at all.

Above them, larger things. Winged, or something like winged — broad membranes stretched between elongated forelimbs, gliding between the vaulted chambers in slow, blind circuits. They navigate by echolocation, and this is where the sound comes from. A constant, overlapping chorus of clicks, whistles, and tonal sweeps that fills the space with a dense acoustic fog. Every chamber echoes into every other. The sound is not unpleasant but it is *relentless*, a wall of navigational chatter from hundreds of gliders mapping a space that hasn't changed in — you don't know how long, but the answer the place gives you is *irrelevant.* The space will not change. They will not stop mapping it. The sound will not stop.

Their droppings, dry andite-colored — everything is this same ochre, the same palette, the same dusty amber — accumulate in small cairns on every horizontal surface. They are not feces. They areite. Mineral excretions. The gliders process the walls, somehow, extracting what they need, depositing the rest in these neat, conical pellets. The pellets do not decay. There is nothing here to decay them. No moisture. No bacteria that have not themselves become mineral, become stone, become part of the frozen circuit.

Sparse vegetation — if it can be called that. Pale, wire-thin stalks, no thicker than thread, emerge from cracks in the floor in isolated clusters. They are warm to the touch. They photosynthesize nothing; there is no light to photosynthesize. Instead they are chemosynthetic, feeding on the faint exothermic reactions in the walls, threading their root systems into the stone itself and drawing warmth and chemical energy directly from the rock. They glow very faintly — a slightly different amber from the walls, almost gold. Where they grow, the stone is polished smooth, worn by the slow, patient vibration of their growth. They are the most alive thing here, and they are almost nothing. A few hundred thin stalks in a complex of rooms that stretches across — how far? You try to walk the extent of it and find you can in perhaps twenty minutes, if you don't stop. Hamlet-sized. A pocket. A wound in the earth that something made, once, for purposes that the place has outlived.

Because there are structures, too. Scattered. The industrial infrastructure is more than pipes. In the central chamber, something that was a machine still stands — a tower of riveted iron plate, eight or nine feet tall, bristling with conduits and pressure gauges whose needles are all frozen at various readings. It produces a sound. A deep, repetitive thrum, like a heart, like a piston cycling, though you cannot see anything moving. It may be the machine itself that generates the warmth. It may be the machine that generates the glow. It has not stopped. It will not stop. Nothing asks it to stop. Nothing here asks anything.

Around its base, the six-limbed things cluster thickest — a living collar of clicking, grinding, patient mouths. They are feeding on the vibrations, perhaps. Or on the heat. Or on the fine metallic dust that sheds from the machine's corroding flanges. They have built up a small hillock of their bodies, a mound of chitin and soft tissue, and at its peak the nearest ones are almost touching the machine's surface, and the surface where they touch is worn to a mirror shine, and the carapaces of the ones that touch it are worn to a mirror shine, and this, too, has been happening for exactly as long as it needs to have been happening and will continue for exactly as long as it needs to continue, which is to say: until it doesn't, and it won't, so it won't.

In a side chamber, a single structure of different provenance — a small shelter, human-scaled, built from salvaged metal sheeting and what looks likeite —ite —ite —ite —ite —ite — aite — a fine fibrousite material, almost likeite. Woven. Fibrous. Organic-looking but stiff, dried to the color of old bone. A shelter built by hands, or things like hands, from materials scavenged and repurposed. Inside it, nothing. Or nothing that matters — a flat surface, a bench, an arrangement of small stone fragments in a pattern that might be functional or might be the first and last gesture of an intelligence that visited this place once and left it to the machines and the things that fed on the machines and the silence that is not silence because of the sound.

The strangeness. You feel it but you cannot locate it precisely. It is in the fact that nothing here has ended. The machines run, the creatures feed, the walls glow, the sound fills the space, and none of it is going anywhere. It is not dead — the fauna are alive, the plants are growing, the machine functions. But it is not alive in any direction. There is no trajectory. No season. No succession. No growth toward something. The place exists in a state of absolute, warm, loud, crowded, luminous equilibrium. Every process is perfectly balanced by its counter-process. Energy in equals energy out. Population equals carrying capacity exactly. The sound fills the space exactly to the space's capacity. Nothing overflows. Nothing falls short. Nothing ever has.

You realize, standing in the central chamber with the six-limbed things clicking at your feet and the gliders' echolocation washing over you in warm, dry waves and the machine thudding its patient heartbeat into the stone, that this place may not be natural. That it may have been *made* to reach this state. That someone, once, designed a closed system — a sealed world — and set it running, and walked away, and it has been running ever since, perfectly, without variation, without progress, without end. A terrarium. A clock. A prayer in mechanical form, addressed to nothing, answered by everything being exactly and forever what it already is.

The dust coats your boots. You will carry it out with you. It is the only thing here that moves in a direction.
