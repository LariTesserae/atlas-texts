# The Wet Plateau of Solsk

The first thing anyone notices is the sound.

Not the water — though there is always water, always — but the *sound* of ten billion living things filling the air from ground to the low cloud ceiling, a roaring, chittering, clicking, rasping wall of biological noise that makes conversation a matter of proximity and gesture. The plateau of Solsk is warm and wet and alive in a way that has no green anywhere in it, and the sheer *density* of fauna — layer upon layer of it, occupying every cubic meter from the mineral crust upward — is the thing you carry with you long after you leave.

---

## The Ground

There is no soil. There never has been. The surface of Solsk is high limestone and basalt, ancient tectonic uplift that pushed a continental plate upward into warm, wet air millions of years ago and has held it there ever since. The stone is old. It has been in its current shape for longer than anyone can reckon — smoothed, scalloped, honeycombed by water that percolates through fissures so deep they reach the mantle's edge. The rock weeps constantly. Every surface is slick. Every depression holds a pool of mineral-bright water — copper-green, rust-orange, pale with dissolved calcium — and the atmosphere itself carries a perpetual condensation, a warm fog that never quite lifts, never quite becomes rain. It is simply *wet*. The air is wet. The stone is wet. The factories are wet.

The erosion here is complete and patient. There are no sharp edges anywhere. The entire landscape looks like it was sculpted by a hand that had centuries to smooth every contour. Caves open and close across the plateaus in vast networks — not the dark caves of cold mountains, but warm, fog-filled galleries where the temperature barely changes across a thousand meters of depth. The stone is pocked and dimpled at every scale: from hand-sized erosion pockets that hold fistfuls of bristling microfauna to basin-scale depressions kilometers across, filled with warm standing water and the low mechanical hum of infrastructure built into their rims.

## The Water

Water is the medium of Solsk. Not a feature of the landscape but the landscape's primary substance, always present, always moving but never quickly. The dynamic register here is slow: water takes the long path. It enters the stone as fog, condenses in pores the width of fingers, travels for decades through limestone capillaries, and emerges as springs that pour warm mineral water across surfaces already saturated. Runoff moves in braided, shallow sheets across the plateau floors rather than carving channels — the stone is too established, too settled in its form, for rivers to cut new paths. Instead, water follows paths worn smooth over geological ages, and the fauna has learned to move with it, ride it, breed in it, filter it.

There are marshes — but mineral marshes, not the reed-choked wetlands of green countries. Where water pools on flat stone, thick biofilms of chemoautotrophic microorganisms form rubbery crusts in ochre and black, and the larger fauna grazes on these. The smell is sharp and metallic: iron, sulfur, wet stone, and underneath it all the organic musk of staggering animal density.

## The Fauna

This is the heart of Solsk and its defining feature.

There are no plants. There have never been plants. The food web here is built from the bottom up by mineral-feeding organisms — bacteria and archaea that oxidize iron, metabolize sulfur compounds, derive energy from the chemical gradients in the weeping stone. They form the base. Above them, layered in dense, interlocking communities that fill every volume of the landscape, is everything else.

The word "overwhelming" is used by everyone who visits, and it does not capture the experience. Fauna occupies *space* here the way water occupies a vessel. It is not a scattering of animals across a landscape; it is a three-dimensional saturation of living matter pressing against the stone, against each other, against the infrastructure of the city.

Near the ground: crustacean-like organisms the size of human hands, pale and blind, scraping biofilm from wet rock with mouthparts that click in unison across vast populations. They move in coordinated waves across the plateau surfaces, thousands per square meter, their shells clacking against stone in a sound like continuous rain on tin. They are the grazers, the base-tier megafauna, and they are everywhere.

Above them: a dense stratum of flying and gliding organisms, some insectoid, some more like small vertebrates — warm-blooded, feathered or furred, with the metabolic urgency of animals that must fuel themselves in a world without fruit or seed. They hunt the grazers. They swarm. They nest in the erosion-pockets of the cliff faces and the ventilation shafts of the factories, in such numbers that the stone itself is shaped by their roosting — guano deposits and nesting material accumulating in formations as thick as the rock they cling to, built up over generations into their own geological strata.

In the water: pale, eel-like swimmers, some nearly transparent, others armored with mineral encrustations they've absorbed from the calcium-rich springs. Amphibious things — not amphibians as a green world would recognize them, but convergent forms, warm-skinned, with lungs and gills both, living in the boundary between the weeping rock and the open pools. They are fat and slow and numerous.

And above all of it, in the warm fog: larger things. Not predators in the dramatic sense — Solsk does not produce drama — but patient, massive filter-feeders that drift through the aerial fauna, straining flying organisms from the air like baleen whales straining krill. Some are meters across. They move with the slowness of everything here, changing direction over minutes, their translucent bodies catching the normal, diffused light of the overcast sky like living lanterns. They are beautiful. They are the closest thing this landscape has to grace.

The fauna changes slowly. Populations ebb and swell across decades. A species of grazer might dominate a plateau section for a century, shaping the erosion patterns with its feeding, before declining and being replaced by a competitor better suited to the altered surface texture. The ecology is old, established, in deep equilibrium — a machine running on geological time, every part fitted to every other part by ages of mutual adjustment.

## The City

It was not built *on* the plateau. It was built *from* it.

The urban structure of Solsk is limestone and basalt, carved and shaped in the same slow patient way that water shaped the caves — but with intent. The city is old. Its oldest structures are indistinguishable from natural rock formations, worn smooth by the same wet erosion that sculpted the cliffs. Newer construction is distinguishable only by its slightly sharper geometry, and even that is softening. The city weeps. Its walls drip. Its foundations are porous and let water pass through in controlled weirs and channels that double as the plumbing and the cooling systems for the factories.

The factories are the city's core. Solsk is industrial in the fullest sense: enormous, low-slung complexes of carved stone and corroded iron, their walls stained orange and black by mineral deposits and biological growth. They process the fauna. Not with cruelty — or rather, not with any more cruelty than a beehive processes its dead — but with systematic, mechanized efficiency. The grazers are harvested in vast quantities, their mineral-rich shells ground for industrial feedstock, their soft bodies rendered for the chemical compounds that power the city's economy. The filter-feeders that drift above are left mostly alone — they are too large, too diffuse, too slow to be worth the effort — but the flying swarms are netted and processed in towers whose ventilation systems channel the perpetual fog to cool the machinery.

The engines are steam-driven and water-driven — there is no shortage of water, and the geothermal heat that warms the plateau provides an inexhaustible energy source. The machinery is heavy, slow, and built to last centuries, because everything on Solsk is built to last centuries. The rhythm of the factories matches the rhythm of the fauna: slow, cyclical, patient. Shifts change across days. Production cycles run across seasons that measure in years rather than months. The whole city operates on a tempo that outsiders find maddening and residents find natural.

Infrastructure is dense and layered — centuries of construction built atop centuries of construction, in a place where erosion wears everything smooth but does not destroy it. Pipes run through carved channels in the rock, carrying warm mineral water to processes that need it, carrying processed biological slurry back to the waterways to feed the biofilms that feed the grazers that feed the factories. It is a closed system, or nearly so. The city metabolizes the landscape the way the fauna metabolizes the stone.

## Sound and Light

The light is normal — diffused through perpetual fog, soft and even, without harsh shadows or dramatic contrast. It is the light of an overcast afternoon, sustained indefinitely. Everything is visible. Nothing is hidden. The landscape has no dark corners because the landscape has no corners at all — every edge is rounded, every surface curves gently into the next, and the fog softens distance into layered veils of pale grey and warm amber.

The sound is constant and loud. It is the fauna. It is the engines. It is the water. It is all three layered together into a single sustained roar that varies in pitch and texture across the landscape but never, ever stops. In the city center, the machinery dominates: the deep thrum of waterwheels turning in carved channels, the rhythmic percussion of piston-driven grinders, the hiss of steam venting through biological filter membranes. At the plateau edges, the fauna dominates: the click-and-rustle of crustacean grazers, the high-pitched shrieking of flying swarms, the subsonic vibration of the filter-feeders moving through fog. In the caves beneath, only water speaks — dripping, seeping, flowing through darkness with the unhurried patience of deep time.

## The Rules of the Place

Solsk operates on patience. Everything here is old, or builds on something old, or erodes into something that will become old. The dynamic register is low: change happens, but it happens across timescales that make human lifetimes seem like single frames in a very slow film. The fauna shifts its populations over centuries. The city reshapes itself over millennia. The water carves new paths through stone at rates measurable in centimeters per century.

There is no winter. The temperature is warm and stable — the geothermal input and the thermal mass of the wet stone regulate everything. There are cycles, but they are hydrological and biological rather than seasonal: wet years and slightly-wetter years, population booms and gradual declines, factory maintenance cycles that take a decade to complete.

The weirdness is low, but present, in the way that any ecosystem this dense and this alien carries a faint charge of the uncanny. The fauna does not fear the city. It inhabits it. It breeds in the machinery, nests in the walls, feeds on the runoff, dies in the processing vats and is recycled into the water that feeds the biofilms that feed the grazers. The boundary between the built environment and the biological environment has long since dissolved. The city is not *in* the landscape. The city *is* the landscape, weathered and smoothed and weeping just like everything else, running on the same slow cycles, subject to the same patient erosion, filled with the same overwhelming, deafening, magnificent density of life.

---

You stand on a ledge overlooking one of the basin depressions — maybe three kilometers across, maybe four, it's hard to tell in the fog. The basin is filled with warm water, copper-green and opaque. On its rim, a factory complex is carved into the cliff face, its iron buttresses orange with rust and biological encrustation, steam rising from vents where hot stone meets cool fog. The sound is enormous. The grazers on the ledge beside you — dozens of them, pale and clicking — are moving toward the water's edge in their slow, synchronized wave. Above, the filter-feeders drift, translucent and vast, their shadows passing over you like clouds. The water drips from the stone beneath your hands. It is always dripping. It will always be dripping. This is Solsk.
