# The Crust Plain

There is nothing here that was not made. That is the first thing to understand, and the hardest, because everything looks like landscape — looks like ground, like horizon, like sky pressing down on earth. But the ground is not earth. There is no earth here. There was never earth.

The crust stretches in every direction, flat and pale, a continental expanse of accreted mineral — calcium, silica, precipitated salts — laid down in microscopic laminae by trillions of years of microbial activity. Not deposited in the way a river deposits silt. *Built*. Every millimeter of it was extruded, secreted, crystallized out of living chemistry by organisms too small to see, working in colonies so vast they have no edges. The substrate is biological. It always was. The floor of this place is the corpse and cathedral of an incomprehensibly ancient living surface, still active at its margins, still growing where moisture permits.

Walk across it — if walking is the word for navigating a landscape so flat and featureless that distance itself seems to lose meaning — and you are standing on a construct. Your weight presses down on millions of layers. If you knelt and pressed your face to the surface, you could see, without any lens, the fine banding: white, cream, faintly amber, white again. Annual bands, perhaps. Or tidal bands, though there are no tides. Bands of growth and bands of dormancy, of wet years and dry years, compressed into a centimeter of hard crust. The surface is warm even at night. It holds heat the way a kiln holds heat — a thermal mass built by chemistry rather than fire.

The flatness is not the flatness of a desert or a frozen sea. Those are surfaces carved by forces that removed material. This flatness was *imposed from below*. The crust grew upward with such uniformity — no hills, no ridges, no favored elevations — that the horizon becomes a problem for the eye. It insists on a geometry that the human face was not built to process. The sky is a dome. The ground is a plane. They meet at a line so clean and so distant that you begin to doubt whether it is real. On very hot days, when the air shimmers, the line dissolves into a smear of light, and the whole world becomes two enormous fields of brightness pressing against each other.

Scale is the operative word. This is not a plain. A plain implies a bounded space, something you might walk across in a lifetime. This is vaster than that. The crust plain extends — by what measurements are possible, which are few and unreliable — across an area comparable to a subcontinent. It has its own weather. Not metaphorically. The surface temperature differential between the crust at midday and the air at night generates convective columns so large they produce localized wind systems, dry cyclones that march across the flat expanse carrying nothing, moving air in vast slow spirals that are visible only by the way they bend the light. Rain falls here — rarely, and in patches. It arrives in isolated cells that drench a square kilometer and leave the next dry. The water is warm when it falls and does not last. It pools in shallow depressions in the crust, thin films of brine that glint for a few hours, then sink, then vanish. But the depressions remain. The water has done its work. Each cycle of wetting and drying has dissolved and re-precipitated the surface of the crust, leaving behind a pattern of shallow basins, some no larger than a palm, some the size of rooms, ringed by white mineral edges where the evaporation was most intense. From above — but there is no above, there is no one to be above — the plain would look like a vast stained sheet, mottled with pale circles and ellipses.

The crust cracks. This is the primary geological event of the place, the only form of erosion, and it is embryonic — it is happening now, it is the landscape's sole act of self-destruction. Thermal expansion. The surface heats during the day, expands, and contracts at night, and over sufficient time — decades? centuries? — the stresses propagate downward into the crust and find weaknesses. A crack opens. It is slow. It is nearly silent. The crack may be millimeters wide and meters long, a pale line in a pale surface, and it will widen over years until a slab of crust, perhaps a meter across, separates from its neighbor. Then the edges of the crack weather. Moisture — that intermittent, reluctant moisture — seeps in, dissolves the inner faces, rounds the edges. The crack becomes a shallow channel. Dust and precipitated minerals settle in the channel. In time, perhaps, something will grow there. But for now it is just a wound in the built surface, raw and new, the white interior of the crust exposed for the first time since it was laid down. This is what passes for erosion here: the slow cracking of an old construction, the emergence of seams in a surface that was never meant to be broken.

The silence is remarkable. Not the silence of a forest at rest, which hums with insect life and the friction of leaves. This is a silence that has no biological undertone at all. There are no animals. There have never been animals, as far as can be determined. The microbial communities that built the crust do not vocalize. The sparse vegetation — scattered, reluctant plants that have established themselves in the deeper cracks and moisture basins — does not rustle because there is almost never wind at ground level. The air is still. The heat presses down. The only sounds are occasional: the tick of cooling crust at night, a faint mineral percussion, like distant pottery cracking; the whisper of brine settling into a newly formed basin; and, very rarely, a low moan of air moving through a crack deep enough to produce resonance, a sound like a finger drawn slowly across the lip of a glass, except the glass is a thousand kilometers wide and made of bone.

The plants. They deserve a word, though they are few. They are not what you would recognize as vegetation. They are not grasses, not shrubs, not trees. They are encrusting organisms — lichens, perhaps, or something that does not map cleanly onto any familiar category — that grow in thin, hard rinds over the crust surface, usually in and around the moisture basins where they have intermittent access to water. They are pale green, almost grey, and they photosynthesize in the brutal light with an economy that suggests they have been doing this for a very long time and expect to continue for a very long time more. They do not compete. They are too sparse and too far apart to compete. Each colony occupies its basin and tolerates the emptiness around it. Some of the basins are old enough that the plant growth has contributed its own mineral layers to the crust — a faintly greenish tint in the banding, a slightly different texture, more porous. The crust is recording the presence of its sparse inhabitants in its own body, in the way a tree records fire in its rings.

No one made this place. That is perhaps the most disorienting fact of all, given that everything here was *made*. There is no builder. There is no blueprint. The crust plain is a total construction — a surface without a single square meter of natural, unworked substrate — and it was assembled by processes that have no intention, no design, no awareness. It is an engineering project executed by chemistry over deep time, and it is still underway. The active processes are slow but relentless: the microbial accretion, the mineral precipitation, the thermal cracking, the intermittent dissolution and re-deposition of the surface. The plain is changing. It is being built and un-built simultaneously, the way a coral reef is built and un-built, but across a continental scale and without a single visible organism larger than a grain of sand doing the work.

At night, the heat radiates upward and the sky is enormous. Stars are visible in numbers that suggest a very old universe. The crust cools slowly, ticking. The moisture basins reflect the sky in thin, imperfect mirrors — a shattered firmament, a sky broken into a thousand shallow fragments across the dark plain. And the plain goes on. It goes on in every direction, featureless, pale, made, quiet, and hot. It was made by life, and it is alive, and it is empty, and it is the size of a world.
