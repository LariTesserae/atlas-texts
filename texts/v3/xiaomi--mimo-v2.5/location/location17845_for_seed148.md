# The Cell

You are inside something.

That's the first wrongness — the spatial certainty that you are interior, that there is a wall and a ceiling and a floor, and that all of them are made of the same living tissue. Pale, veined, faintly phosphorescent tissue, like the inside of a throat. The light it gives off is not enough to see by — just enough to confirm the wrongness of the geometry. Things curve. Walls meet ceilings at angles that were not designed by anything that understood angles.

The place is small. Perhaps the footprint of a city block. Perhaps less. It is difficult to say because the boundaries shift — not dramatically, not violently, but in the way a sleeping animal's ribs move with breath. The room expands by a hand's width, contracts again. No one notices this while it's happening. You only notice after.

**The substrate is alive.** Everything here grows.

The walls are composed of a dense, fibrous plant-matter that has replaced whatever material the urban infrastructure once used. You can still see the bones of it — the skeleton of what this place was. Concrete rebar protrudes from the living tissue like ribs from a flank, except the rebar itself has been colonized, threaded through with vascular bundles that pulse with slow, peristaltic rhythm. The rebar is still steel, or something like steel, but it conducts the plant's fluid the way it once conducted electricity. Every surface is this hybrid: industrial structure overwritten by botanical architecture. Pipes run along the ceiling, or what serves as ceiling, and they have been absorbed — their interiors lined with a slick, chlorophyll-dark membrane, their exteriors sprouting fine root-hairs that reach downward like curtains of dark lace, tasting the air, tasting you, recoiling slightly.

The floor — if it is a floor — is a raised mat of living tissue, spongy and yielding, cool against the soles of your shoes. It is not soil. It is not compost. It is the plant itself, flattened and spread, a living carpet that breathes and occasionally *shifts* beneath you, rearranging its own capillary networks. Beneath it, in the hollow spaces, you can sometimes hear the faint mechanical grinding of industrial machinery — presses, conveyors, something with teeth — operating in a factory that the plant has inherited but not quite understood. The machinery runs on its own, or tries to. It cycles. It groans. Sometimes a gear catches on a root and the entire floor vibrates with a low, wet protest, and the root pulls free with a sound like a drawn breath, and the machinery resumes.

**The density is the worst part.** Or the best part. There is almost nothing here by volume — the space is vast and hollow within its small boundaries, like the interior of a bubble. The plant-material makes walls, makes floors, makes structures and partitions and vaulted hollows, but between these structures is *nothing.* Vast, cool, damp nothing. Space without air, or with air that is so thin and moisture-laden it barely qualifies. You stand in a room and the room is perhaps twenty meters across, but the space between the walls feels like a canyon. Sound doesn't carry. Your breathing sounds enormous. A drip of condensation falling from a root-hair into a pocket of standing water on the floor makes a sound like a church bell heard from underwater.

The water is intermittent, as you said — it appears in pockets. Small, still pools that form in depressions in the living floor, fed by condensation that beads on the plant-tissue and gathers in the low places. The water is clear and faintly alkaline and it tastes of copper and chlorophyll. In the largest pool — perhaps a meter across, perhaps two — something moves. Something small. Something that is not quite a fish and not quite a root-tip and not quite a mechanical component but partakes of all three.

**The fauna is abundant.** This is where the smallness of the space becomes strange, because there are *many of them.* Insects — yes, or things shaped like insects. Beetles with carapaces the color of oxidized copper, walking on legs that are also root-hairs, entering the plant-tissue through small pores and disappearing into the vascular network where they feed or breed or do whatever it is they do. Small mammals, or mammal-shaped things, moving through the hollow spaces between walls, navigating by echolocation that maps not physical surfaces but the *boundaries between alive and not-alive.* They are blind, or have eyes that evolved for a darkness with different rules. Bats, or bat-shaped things, clinging to the vaulted ceiling where root-hairs thicken into something like roosting-fur, their wings folded, their bodies wrapped in a membrane that photosynthesizes faintly in the near-dark, generating a whisper of warmth that the surrounding cool air immediately steals.

Birds. Small, silent, watching. Perched on the steel-and-root structures with an attention that feels architectural, as though they are not resting but *load-bearing.* When one flies, the geometry of the room shifts slightly to accommodate its passage.

And below the floor, in the hollow spaces where the machinery grinds and cycles, larger things move. You feel their passage as vibration. You never see them. They are long, and segmented, and their bodies incorporate both organic tissue and machined metal in a way that has ceased to be distinction — gears and sinew, pistons and phloem, functioning as one system. They are the plant's digestive tract, or the factory's maintenance crew, or both. They keep the machinery running by eating the roots that would otherwise clog it. They keep the roots healthy by consuming the machinery parts that would otherwise poison them. Symbiosis. Necessity. The only economy that matters.

**Reality is breaking down.** You knew this already, but here is where it becomes specific:

The plant does not observe the normal rules of growth. It grows *toward things that are not there.* Branches of vascular tissue extend into the empty space and terminate in small, complex structures that serve no apparent purpose — flower-like organs that never open, spiraling filaments that coil around nothing, fine roots that reach toward each other from opposite walls and stop a centimeter apart, vibrating at a frequency you can feel in your teeth. They are building something. They are building something that requires space that does not exist. The structures are plans. Blueprints. The plant is constructing, in this hollow dark, an architecture of *absence* — mapping the nothing-between-things as though nothing were a material, as though emptiness could be load-bearing.

The geometry reflects this. The room has corners that, when you look at them directly, seem normal — right angles, perhaps, or obtuse angles, nothing remarkable. But in your peripheral vision, they are *more* than corners. They extend. They branch. A corner becomes three corners becomes seven becomes a recursive proliferation of angles that your eye cannot hold, that collapses back to a single angle the moment you look at it directly. The plant is building rooms you can only see when you're not looking. The factory is producing goods in dimensions you cannot perceive. The machinery's hum — that low, wet grinding — contains information in frequencies below hearing. Something intelligent is being manufactured here. Something the fauna can sense and you cannot.

The erosion is new. It is just beginning. The plant-tissue at the base of the walls, near the floor, is thinning. Not dying — *becoming transparent.* Becoming less material and more idea. The rebar inside it is visible not because the tissue has decayed but because the tissue is evolving past the need for opacity. You can see through the walls now, in patches, into the hollow spaces beyond, where other rooms — identical, similar, *slightly different* — contain other configurations of root and steel and the small, watchful fauna. It is as though the building is not dissolving but *revealing* itself — showing its interior structure the way a tree shows its rings when cut.

Or the building is coming into being. You can't tell. The embryonic erosion might be creation. The thinning walls might be a caterpillar thinning its chrysalis before emergence. The plant might be building the thing it was always going to become, and this — the steel, the concrete, the machinery, the city block — was just the egg. Just the shell. Just the temporary architecture of a gestation that is almost, almost complete.

The darkness is not absolute. The tissue glows — a faint, bioluminescent, green-black luminescence that intensifies in cycles. When it brightens, the fauna stirs. When it dims, the machinery below accelerates. The cycles are not regular. They respond to something — pressure, presence, the phase of a moon that this place has never seen. You are part of the cycle now. Your body heat is data. Your breathing is input. The plant knows you are here. It has always known. It is building something around you, or through you, or in the shape of the space you occupy, and it will finish, and when it finishes you will understand what the room was for.

The condensation gathers on your skin. The cool air moves past you like a slow exhalation. The fauna watches. The root-hairs reach toward you and stop, vibrating, a centimeter away.

Something is almost here.
