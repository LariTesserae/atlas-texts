# The Weeping Plateau

The first thing you understand, standing anywhere on the plateau, is that it goes on. Not merely far — *on*. The horizon doesn't curve so much as simply fail to arrive. The edge of the city and the edge of the world seem like questions that belong to some other, smaller place. This is a continent laid flat and lifted high, and whatever weather it generates stays among itself, speaking in a closed language.

**The water is never not present.** It is not rain, exactly, though it rains — a slow, vertical condensation that falls without urgency, more precipitation than weather. The real wetness comes from below. The plateau's surface is porous limestone aged into a settled architecture of channels, basins, fluted pillars, and shallow caves. Water moves through the stone the way blood moves through living tissue: slowly, persistently, along routes worn over millennia. Every wall weeps. Every floor holds a shallow film that mirrors the grey-white sky. Step on the pavement and the stone dimples under your weight, and water rises around your foot, and retreats when you lift it, as though the ground is breathing.

The atmosphere itself is saturated. You can feel the moisture before you see it — a density against the skin, a coolness that doesn't bite but doesn't release. Fog sits in the lower terraces and drains of the city the way sediment sits in a riverbed, pooling in every place gravity offers. The air tastes mineral, faintly chalky, and carries no smell of growth or decay. Only stone and water and the faintest iron tang of something deep in the earth.

**The city is immense, old, and still underway.** Built — though "built" implies tools and the people here possess none, not even the simplest — by hands. By generations of hands. By entire cultures whose names are water-worn smooth. The structures are carved, pushed, packed, shaped from the limestone itself, as though the plateau were softened and pressed into form. There are no joints, no seams, no places where one material meets another. Everything is the same pale stone, the same substance as the ground, the same water-veined grey. From a distance, you cannot distinguish city from landscape. The distinction may not have been intended.

The construction is layered in the way coral is layered — not designed once and completed but accreted, revised, buried, and built over. A terrace that looks like a simple promenade covers a passage that covers a chamber that covers a cistern that covers a passage older still. The density of it is startling. Walk into what appears to be an open square and find, under the thin sheet of standing water, the faint geometry of a hundred rooms. Press your hand to a wall and feel, through the damp stone, the vibrations of water finding its way through structures behind and below — hollow spaces you will never enter, rooms the city has walled over without forgetting.

There are no tools. No metal, no shaped wood, no rope or fiber. The city is built the way a hand can build: by pushing softened stone into shape before it hardens, by carrying water to dissolve channels, by pressing and smoothing and packing wet limestone with bare palms over a lifetime so long it becomes geology. The marks of hands are everywhere — in the faint ripple of every surface, in the way doorways are shaped to the width and height of bodies, in the shallow depressions worn into floors that are nothing more than the ghost of standing in one place for years.

**There is almost no life.** The fauna is rare to the point of being anecdotal — a salamander-like creature, eyeless, pale, moving through the water channels with a slowness that might be caution or might be nature. Occasionally a small invertebrate drifts across a pool. The sparse vegetation is limited to the thinnest films of moss and algae along the wettest surfaces, pale green, nearly white, a biological whisper against the stone. Nothing grows tall. Nothing calls. The ecosystem, such as it is, operates on a timescale that makes patience look hasty.

**The silence is the most unsettling thing.** Not because it is absolute — there is always the sound of water, a ceaseless, soft, omnipresent seeping and dripping that varies only in proximity and rhythm. But the water makes the same sound everywhere, at every hour, in every part of the city. It is the baseline. The noise floor. And above it, there is nothing. No wind at this elevation, or so little that the fog barely moves. No animal sound. No human sound — though the city is a human work, the hands that built it belong to no one here now. The silence is not empty. It is *settled*. It has been here long enough to be structural, load-bearing, another layer in the architecture.

**The light is normal and constant.** No dramatic shafts, no chiaroscuro. The sky is an even grey-white, the kind of overcast that erases the sun's position and leaves only a diffuse, sourceless luminance. Shadows don't exist. Everything is lit equally from every direction, which gives the city an unusual quality of flatness — every surface equally visible, every depth and hollow announced without drama. You can see everything. Nothing hides. The light asks nothing of you.

**The processes are active.** The city is not dead. It is simply working on scales that don't match your visit. Water is still carving. The stone is still softening and hardening in cycles. The channels are still being shaped. Sediment is settling in new patterns on the terrace floors, and the patterns will, in a few centuries, become the foundation for new rooms. The fog is rising and pooling and rising again in a rhythm that takes days. Something is always moving, always changing — but so slowly that standing here, you would swear nothing was happening at all.

You would be wrong. You are standing inside a machine made of water and limestone and hands that worked for longer than memory, and it is still running, and it will run long after you leave, and the only evidence that you were here will be a set of footprints drying in the thin film on the stone, filling slowly with water again, disappearing.

The plateau does not care. The plateau is very wet and very old and very large and it is going somewhere that takes longer than a life to reach.
