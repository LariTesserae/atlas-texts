# The Kessarrean Tablelands

You feel the elevation before you understand it. Not as thin air — the atmosphere here is thick, almost syrupy, pressing against skin with the weight of a wet cloth held over your mouth. It is the *verticality* that disorients. The ground drops away in every direction from wherever you stand, not into valleys exactly but into vast cantilevered shelves of dark stone that step down like the broken tiers of some impossible cathedral, each shelf hundreds of meters across, their edges scalloped and fluted by time into forms that look almost organic — as though the rock had once been liquid and cooled in the shape of enormous fungal caps or the unfurling petals of something mineral.

And it is *hot*. Not the dry heat of a desert but a pressurized, saturating warmth, like the inside of a mouth. The air carries more water than it should be able to hold. You can see it: a fine perpetual haze drifting between the stone shelves, thickening in the shadows, pooling in the low basins where the rock has been hollowed into shallow, warm lakes of brown-green water that never quite drain and never quite overflow. Every surface weeps. The stone itself is slick with a film of condensation that catches the normal, unremarkable daylight and gives every surface a dim, wet sheen. You could wring moisture from the air with your hands.

---

**The stone.** It is old. The erosion parameter here is not chaos but *settlement* — the landscape has been worn into itself over durations that make human civilization feel like a weather event. Every surface is smoothed, rounded, patinated. The rock is a deep umber where it's bare, streaked with veins of pale mineral deposit — calcium or something like it — left behind by millennia of water running over and through it. In places, the erosion has carved enormous alcoves into the cliff faces, some of them hundred-meter vaults with ceilings dripping in curtains of moss and trailing root systems. The sound of water is everywhere: not dramatic waterfalls but the constant, omnipresent whisper and trickle of condensation finding its way downward through every crack, every pore of the stone. The rock is porous. It breathes water. Put your palm against a cliff face and you feel the dampness seeping through from the interior, as though the mountain itself is sweating.

The geological forms have a quality that resists quick categorization. They are not quite mesas, not quite towers, not quite canyon walls. They are *platforms* — vast, flat-topped, steep-sided — arranged in overlapping, slightly tilted layers, like a hand of cards dealt across a table and then half-buried in sediment. From any given shelf you can see others below and above, extending outward into the haze for kilometers. The scale is regional. You are standing on one unit of a system. The system goes on.

---

**The life.** Lush is the word, but it doesn't capture the suffocating density of it. Everything that can grow, grows. Every horizontal surface that isn't actively being scoured by running water is upholstered in vegetation — not the sparse, tenacious green of a highland, but a jungle thickness, a tropical excess, as though the altitude means nothing to the biology here. The plants are not quite recognizable as any terrestrial family, though they echo. Broad-leafed things with waxy, dripping surfaces crowd the tops of the stone platforms. Fern-like forms — but with fronds the size of doors, dark green verging on black — cascade over the cliff edges, their roots gripping the wet rock with an almost muscular tenacity. Epiphytes cluster on every vertical surface, forming dense collars of growth around the mouths of the alcoves, their aerial roots dangling into the condensation haze below like the beards of old gods.

The vegetation is layered. Canopy plants — tall, straight-trunked, with spreading crowns of narrow leaves that shed water constantly — create a broken ceiling over the platform surfaces. Beneath them, a dense understory of shrub-things and ground covers. And beneath that, in the perpetual dampness at the stone level, a carpet of bryophyte analogs so thick it muffles footfalls and turns every step into a soft, spongy compression. The colors are deep — saturated greens, brown-greens, blue-greens — with occasional startling punctuations: a flower-analog the size of a dinner plate, pale and waxy, glowing faintly with its own bioluminescence even in the daylight. Or a vine that has gone vivid orange, its bark peeling in translucent strips.

The ecology here is dense in a more-than-spatial sense. There are *many presences*. The vegetation isn't just covering the rock; it's interpenetrating it, breaking it apart slowly, its roots threading through cracks and widening them over centuries. The boundary between stone and soil and plant matter is indistinct. Everything is in contact with everything else. You reach into the undergrowth and your hand touches wet stone, wet roots, wet leaves, and something moving — a small fauna-analog, smooth-skinned, retreating from your fingers with a wet, clicking sound.

---

**The fauna.** Abundant. Noisy. The sound parameter is not satisfied by water alone. The biological noise here is extraordinary — a layered, continuous wall of sound that you stop hearing as individual calls and start experiencing as a single, pressurized atmosphere of its own. Insects — or their functional equivalent: six-legged, exoskeletal, ranging from tiny to the size of your fist — produce a drone that is felt in the sternum as much as heard. Larger creatures call from the canopy and the cliff faces: long, undulating vocalizations that echo through the alcoves and bounce between the stone shelves until the origin is impossible to locate. Something that might be a bird, or might be a flying reptile, or might be something with no terrestrial analogue, launches from a ledge above you and passes overhead with a sound like tearing fabric, its wings wide and membranous and wet-looking.

The fauna has adapted to verticality. Things cling to cliff faces with adhesive pads or hooked claws. Things glide between shelves on membrane-wings or parachute membranes. Things live in the shallow warm lakes — you see disturbances on the water surfaces, concentric ripples expanding outward from points where something has surfaced and submerged again. The density of animal life is startling. Every surface is occupied. Every volume of air contains something in flight or transit. You are not alone here in any sense. You are outnumbered by orders of magnitude, and the things outnumbering you are loud about it.

And there is strangeness. The fauna does not always conform to expectation. You see a creature — quadrupedal, roughly the size of a large dog — walking along the edge of a stone shelf with a gait that is *wrong*. Not injured-wrong but *geometry*-wrong, as though its legs are jointed in ways that shouldn't support locomotion but do. It pauses, turns a head that rotates too far, regards you with eyes that are too large and too reflective, and then continues on its path with that unsettling, efficient wrongness. Elsewhere, a cluster of small creatures on a rock face are arranged in a pattern that looks almost deliberate — a spiral, almost — and when you look away and look back, the pattern has changed. The strangeness is not aggressive. It does not threaten. It simply *is*, woven into the fabric of the place as casually as the moss.

---

**The remains.** Scattered. Faint. You almost miss them.

On the third stone shelf you traverse, you notice that one of the alcoves has a ceiling that is too smooth. Not weathered-smooth but *manufactured*-smooth — a gentle, mathematically precise curve that no natural erosion would produce. The stone within this alcove is a slightly different color: cooler, less stained, as though it has resisted the millennia of biological and chemical weathering that has shaped everything around it. Near the back wall, at the base of the curve, there is a small structure — a block of material that is not stone, about a meter tall, with a surface texture like ceramic but warmer to the touch. It has no visible seams. It has no visible purpose. It is simply *there*, old beyond reckoning, slowly being overgrown by the same bryophyte carpet that covers everything else. A root has found its way across its surface. Condensation beads on it in the same way it beads on the surrounding rock.

You find others. On another shelf, partially buried in sediment: a long, thin object, slightly curved, protruding from the ground at an angle. It might be a beam. It might be a pipe. It might be something for which there is no word. Its material is similar — warm to the touch, resistant to weathering, faintly iridescent in certain angles of light. On yet another shelf, set into the cliff face itself, partially obscured by a curtain of trailing vegetation: a rectangular indentation, a meter across, its edges too precise to be natural. Inside it, darkness. The air that reaches you from it is slightly warmer than the ambient warmth, and carries a sound — very low, felt more than heard, a vibration that seems to originate not from the indentation itself but from the mountain behind it. A hum. A pulse. Something still operating, deep in the rock, on timescales that make the geological patience of the erosion seem impulsive by comparison.

These artifacts are not ruins in any recognizable sense. There are no walls, no floors, no doorways shaped for bodies. They are *components* — fragments of a technology so far beyond current understanding that they read as geology. They are being absorbed by the landscape, and they are in no hurry to resist. The dynamic is slow. A root grows across a surface. A film of mineral deposit builds up, fraction of a millimeter per century. The hum deepens. The shape of things changes imperceptibly, and then imperceptibly again.

---

**The atmosphere, finally — not the air, but the *feeling*.** The place is settled. That is the word the erosion parameter keeps pointing toward, and it applies to everything. This landscape has been here for an incomprehensible span of time, and it has achieved a kind of equilibrium — not stasis, but a state where change occurs on its own schedule, at its own pace, in response to forces that operate over millennia rather than moments. The heat is constant. The moisture is constant. The noise is constant. The vegetation grows slowly. The water drips slowly. The technology hums slowly. The fauna moves through it all with the ease of things that have always been here and expect always to be.

You are temporary. Everything about this place tells you so. The wet stone, the ancient forms, the technology that has outlasted whatever built it and will outlast whatever you are — all of it communicates a deep, unbothered patience. The landscape is not hostile. It is not welcoming. It is simply *ongoing*, and your presence within it is a brief local fluctuation in a system that has no interest in your timescale.

The light remains normal throughout. That, at least, is familiar — a diffused daylight filtered through the perpetual moisture haze, casting soft shadows, illuminating everything evenly. No drama from the sky. The sky is a pale, washed-out white-gray, visible only in fragments between the edges of the stone shelves above. You rarely see more than a sliver of it at a time. The verticality of the terrain closes you in even as the openness of the shelf-system extends the landscape outward in every horizontal direction. You are at once enclosed and exposed, standing on a platform of ancient wet stone in a world that is dense with life, dense with moisture, dense with the faint, residual presence of something you cannot name, and very, very loud with the sound of everything that lives here going about the business of continuing.
