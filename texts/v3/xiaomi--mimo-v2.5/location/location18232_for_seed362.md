# The Loom

Everything is plant. Not trees, not grass, not any species that exists in any natural record. The material is fibrous, dense, warm to the touch despite the cool air, and it constitutes *everything* — the floor beneath you is a root-mat compressed under such pressure and duration that it has become structural, nearly mineral, approaching the hardness of bone. The walls, where you can distinguish walls from ceilings, are woven bundles of vascular tissue the diameter of sewer pipes, braided and rebraided into load-bearing columns and arches. The ceilings are vast plates of interlocking leaf-matter, each plate the size of a city block, their edges fitted so precisely that no gap remains between them.

The whole structure glows. Every surface emits its own bioluminescence — a cool blue-white that makes shadows soft and unreliable. There is no sky. There is no exterior. The world has no visible source of illumination other than itself, and the illumination is constant: bright enough to read by, bright enough to see fine fiber-grain at fifty meters, bright enough that the shadows beneath arching columns seem blue-black and absolute, compressed into those small spaces by the light pressing in from all sides.

---

The air is dry. Strangely, impossibly dry for a world made of living tissue. No moisture condenses on any surface. The plants — if they can still be called plants — operate on chemistry that requires no water. A solvent of some other kind moves through their vascular systems: something that carries nutrients but does not wet, does not pool, does not behave like any fluid you have encountered. The whole world smells faintly of ozone and crushed herbs — a sharp green scent without the sweetness of chlorophyll, more like industrial solvent married to something almost organic. Almost.

And the scale is what breaks you. It is planet-scale. The world is a shallow disc — perhaps a few hundred meters thick — but its diameter stretches beyond any distance you can conceive of measuring on foot. You are inside it. There is no bedrock beneath you, no ocean at the margins, no horizon punctuated by mountains. Constructed plant-matter extends in every direction. If you walked, you would pass through biome after biome of different engineered tissue: regions of pale, rigid growth that hums at a frequency just below hearing; fields of soft, cushiony material that swallows your footsteps and erases your tracks; labyrinths of hollow stems large enough to drive trucks through, arranged in patterns that seem random until you realize they form a computational network — the world thinking to itself in slow, vegetative logic.

---

It is old. Not ancient in the way of geology. Old in the way of something that has been *running*. The erosion is settled — surfaces worn smooth by whatever passes through, then regrown, then worn smooth again, over cycles that have accumulated into strata. Where the root-mat floor has been cut or broken at the margins, you can see distinct layers of compressed fiber, each a different shade of pale green or cream or ochre, each representing an era of growth. The world has its own geology, entirely biological, and it extends down as far as the disc extends, all the way to the underside, whatever the underside is.

It is dense. Not just with material but with *presence*. Every cubic meter is occupied — structural fiber, living tissue, embedded machinery, vascular networks, luminous membranes. There is no emptiness. Even the air feels thick, not with humidity but with the faint pressure of so much organized matter pressing inward from all sides. The open spaces — they are not rooms, more like clearings left deliberately in a solid mass — feel intimate even when they stretch for kilometers. You are always aware of the material around you.

---

Here is what you hear, pressing your ear against the fibrous wall: the slow pulse of machinery. Not mechanical pumps. Biological ones. The vascular bundles are engineered to move fluid at industrial pressures — thousands of PSI — through networks that map onto a circulatory system but carry the regularity and purpose of a factory floor. Every few hundred meters, a thicker column rises from floor to ceiling and vibrates faintly — a processing node, where plant-matter is broken down and reconstituted, where raw material is converted into structural fiber, where the world rebuilds itself molecule by molecule.

But the world is almost silent. The dense plant-matter absorbs every sound. Your footsteps produce no echo. Your voice dies within a meter of your lips. The machinery hums at frequencies that exist more as vibration than as noise — you feel them in your sternum, in your teeth, but you cannot point to a source. The world is loud with process and mute with evidence. Everything is happening. Nothing is heard.

---

The fauna is nearly absent. Once, perhaps, there were engineered insects — pollinators, maintenance creatures, things that moved through the plant-world and served it. Now they are rare. You might encounter one every few days of walking: a small, six-legged thing made of chitin and wire, clinging to a wall, its compound eyes dark. It does not fly. It does not feed. It seems to be waiting, or perhaps it is simply a leftover — a function no longer called. The world does not appear to need them but has not removed them. They persist in the margins, small and silent and almost invisible against the dense textures of their surroundings.

---

And then there is the active-ness. The ecosystem — if that word holds any meaning here — is in motion. The processing nodes cycle. The vascular networks pulse. The walls grow and are trimmed and grow again. New regions are synthesized at the margins, raw material fed into outermost columns and converted, over weeks, into finished architecture. Old regions are consumed at the heart, their structure broken down, their matter recycled. It is a managed forest in which the forest and the manager and the factory and the product are the same thing. A closed loop. An industrial ecology with no input and no output, running on whatever strange chemistry powers it, for reasons that may no longer be remembered by anything or anyone within it.

The floor is growing. Not metaphorically. The root-mat is visibly, slowly rising — millimeters per hour — and the ceiling is lowering at the same rate. The world is compressing itself, eating its own interior space, and this has been happening for a very long time. The clearings are not permanent features but temporary ones, slowly closing. Like mouths.

---

The weirdness does not arrive dramatically. There are no rips in the air, no impossible colors, no visions of other places. It accumulates slowly, like sediment.

You notice that the corridor you have been walking through has not changed in three hours, and yet your legs ache, and you are certain you have been moving. You notice that the bioluminescent ceiling, when you stare at it long enough, displays patterns that are not biological — geometric, recursive, fractal, folding inward in ways that hurt the backs of your eyes. You notice that one of the processing columns is pumping fluid upward on one face and downward on the other, but the fluid going up is the same fluid coming down, unchanged, unprocessed — a loop, a pointless cycle, running and running for no purpose you can identify.

You notice that the air tastes faintly of the word *remembered*.

You begin to suspect that the plant-matter is not merely alive but aware, and that its awareness is not like any animal's — something vaster and slower and more diffuse. A planetary consciousness built of fiber and fluid and light, thinking in centuries, not distinguishing between its body and its thoughts. You feel it the way you feel a presence in a dark room: not seen, not heard, but *there*, filling the space with a weight that has intention in it, even if you cannot name the intention.

You realize, with a clarity that is itself a little wrong, that you are not a visitor. You are a disruption. A foreign body. An error the world has not yet corrected because it has not yet noticed you, and that when it does, the correction will be gentle and total and you will not feel it happening.

The floor rose another millimeter while you were thinking this. The ceiling dropped to match. The clearings are narrowing. The world continues.

The bright, dry, silent, vast, dense, constructed, plant-built, actively-growing, nearly faunaless, industrially-humming, reality-softening world continues, and it is very patient, and it has always been patient, and it has all the time its slow and endless chemistry can give it.

You are very small inside it. The walls are warm. The light does not change.
