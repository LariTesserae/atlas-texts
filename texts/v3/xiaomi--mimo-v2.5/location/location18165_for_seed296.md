# The Throat of Old Thaleng

You are standing on a ridge that has no right to be this high and this old at the same time.

The elevation is extreme — not the sharp, knife-edge extremity of young mountains, but the impossible high-altitude flatness of a place that was once *higher still* and has been worn down over an inconceivable span of time. The stone underfoot is nearly horizontal, tilted at only a few degrees, its surface planed smooth as a tabletop by whatever processes operated here before anything alive existed. This is rock from the planet's deep time — gneiss and something older than gneiss, banded in dark minerals that catch the low light with a faint, oily sheen. If you knelt and pressed your palm flat against it, you'd feel a coolness that doesn't come from temperature alone. The stone remembers pressure. It remembers being buried beneath a weight that would have liquefied granite, and it carried that memory upward as the world restructured itself around it, and it has never fully let go.

The ridge extends east and west in a long, slightly convex line — the remnant of some immense anticline, eroded until only this one spine remained, and then eroded further until even the spine was blunted and broadened. It is perhaps forty meters wide at your feet, dropping away on both sides into cloud. The drop is not vertical. It is *stepped* — the ancient rock has fractured along parallel joints into great platforms and ledges, each one a few meters lower than the last, descending in a slow geometric staircase until the eye loses count and finds only white vapor. The overall effect is of standing on the prow of an impossibly vast ship, motionless, its hull half-submerged in a sea of cloud.

But the cloud is not empty.

---

The vegetation is dense and lush in a way that seems almost *insistent*, as if every surface that has existed for more than a few thousand years has been claimed. Mosses first — they carpet the flat stone in a continuous layer that ranges from deep emerald to near-black where the moisture pools. Not thin moss. This is moss with *body*, spongy and thick enough to step into ankle-deep, saturated with condensation that weeps from it when compressed. The water content is extraordinary. The moss is less a plant than a reservoir, a living sponge that breathes the fog and holds it.

From the moss rise ferns — not the delicate woodland kind but a high-altitude variety with thick, almost succulent fronds, dark green verging on purple, their surfaces waxy to resist the cold wind that occasionally slides up from the cloud valleys. They grow in tight rosettes, overlapping, and where they cluster thickly they create a secondary canopy barely a meter above the ground — a dwarf forest with its own interior climate, warmer and stiller than the open ridge. Beneath the ferns, in the permanent twilight of that understory, there are things growing that don't have common names. Liverworts with fringed, almost feathery edges. A creeping plant with dark red stems that branches with mathematical precision. Fungi — some bright, most not — erupting from the moss in shelf-like formations that cling to exposed rock faces, their pale undersides catching what little light filters down.

The light is dim. Not dark — not the darkness of caves or deep forest — but a *gray* dimness, the light of a world permanently overcast, where the sun is a brighter patch in a ceiling of cloud rather than a visible object. At this elevation the cloud should be below you, and it is, mostly — but it breathes. It rises and falls with thermal cycles, and in the mornings it spills over the ridge in silent, cold rivers of vapor that drench everything and then recede by midday, leaving the stone glistening. The light shifts through the day from pale gray to slightly-less-pale gray, with rare moments in the afternoon when the cloud thins enough to admit a diffuse, directionless luminescence that casts no shadows. These moments are unsettling. Without shadows, distances become uncertain, and the landscape — already flat and featureless — seems to lose a dimension.

You become aware, standing here, that the silence is not total but *constructed*. It is the silence of high altitude, where the air is thin enough to muffle, where sound doesn't carry the way it should. But underneath it, there are sounds.

---

The industrial presence is pervasive but quiet in the way that very old things are quiet — not silent, but operating below the threshold of attention until you deliberately listen. Along the eastern edge of the ridge, perhaps two hundred meters away, a row of structures sits flush against the stone. They are not buildings in the conventional sense. They are *encrustations* — low, broad forms, half-embedded in the moss and fern cover, their walls made of a dark metal that has corroded to the same color as the surrounding rock. Smoke or steam rises from several of them in thin, vertical columns that dissolve quickly in the cold air. The structures are connected by pipes — heavy, riveted iron pipes, some running along the surface, some disappearing into the stone through bored holes lined with what looks like ceramic. The pipes are warm to the touch. Not hot. Warm. They carry heat from deep within the ancient rock, tapping geological gradients that have been stable for longer than any civilization could have existed.

This is not new industry. This is industry that was *inherited* — built on top of processes already underway, using heat that was already rising, channeling moisture that was already condensing. The city — because it is a city, of a sort — grew up around the ridge's natural resources the way the moss grew up around the stone: slowly, opportunistically, with no single moment of construction but rather a long accumulation of *habitation*.

The buildings become denser as you move west along the ridge. They are low — almost none taller than you — but they are tightly packed, sharing walls, sharing foundations, growing into each other until individual structures become indistinguishable from the collective mass. The streets between them are narrow, paved with the same ancient gneiss, worn smooth by generations of feet. There are no vehicles. The ridge is too narrow and the industry too old-fashioned for that. Everything is moved by hand, by cart, by the small rail systems that run along the pipe networks, their narrow gauge and rusted rails blending into the infrastructure like veins into a limb.

And there are people. Fewer than you'd expect for a city this dense, but present — moving through the narrow streets with the unhurried pace of those who have learned that altitude punishes haste. They wear layered clothing in dark colors, hooded against the perpetual damp. They do not look up. There is nothing to look up at except cloud.

---

The strangeness is subtle and cumulative.

It begins with the erosion — or rather, with what erosion *hasn't* done. At 2.7 elevation and 2.6 ancient erosion, you'd expect this place to be worn to nothing, a flat remnant of a remnant. Instead, the rock retains specific forms that seem *resistant* to the processes that leveled everything around them. There are vertical pillars — not many, but scattered along the ridge's spine — that rise five or ten meters above the flat surface. They are too regular to be natural joints. Too smooth to be fracture remnants. They look like they were *left standing* on purpose, as if the erosion itself had instructions. The moss doesn't grow on them. The ferns don't climb them. They stand bare and dark against the gray sky, and when the cloud rises high enough to envelop them, they seem to move — not physically, but *conceptually*, as if the fog is showing you a different version of each pillar, one that doesn't quite match the version you saw a moment before.

Then there is the water. The moisture at 1.4 isn't heavy rain or flowing rivers — it's *condensation*, a place where the air itself is perpetually on the verge of precipitation. Every surface weeps. The pipes collect it. The moss absorbs it. The ferns channel it down their fronds into the root mat below. But there are also *pools* — perfectly circular, three or four meters across, scattered across the flat ridge surface at apparently random intervals. They are shallow, no more than a hand's depth, and the water in them is perfectly still and perfectly clear, reflecting the gray sky with mirror precision. They should be evaporating in the wind, or overflowing with condensation input, or filling with debris. They do none of these things. Their surfaces are undisturbed. Their edges are clean — the moss stops precisely at the rim, as if held back by an agreement. The pools are the strangest part of this place, and the residents do not discuss them.

The fauna is rare, as specified, but not absent. There are birds — small, dark, with the compact bodies of high-altitude species, silent in flight, calling rarely in voices that sound more like stone grinding than song. They nest in the crevices of the ancient pillars. There are insects in the moss — tiny, slow-moving, adapted to the cool and the damp. There may be something larger. Shadows move sometimes in the spaces between buildings where the light doesn't reach, but when you turn to look, there is only the sound of a pipe contracting as it cools, or the soft impact of a condensation droplet falling from a fern frond.

---

The active processes are everywhere once you learn to see them.

The steam rising from the eastern structures is the most visible, but there are others. The ground is warm in specific places — not uniformly, but in patches that correspond to nothing on the surface. The moss grows differently there, taller, more vigorous, its color shifting from emerald to something almost golden. The pipes hum. Not loudly — a vibration felt in the soles of the feet rather than heard in the ears — but the hum is constant, and it carries information. It rises and falls in patterns that might be cyclical or might be responsive to the condensation cycles overhead. The pools change their reflectivity in ways that correlate with the hum's frequency. The pillars, in the deepest fog, occasionally vibrate sympathetically, producing a tone so low it registers as nausea rather than sound.

The city breathes. The condensation cycles are its respiration — cloud rising in the morning like an exhalation, falling back in the evening like an inhalation. The industrial structures vent and intake in counterpoint. The moss swells with moisture and slowly releases it through the dry hours. Everything is wet, and everything is in the process of becoming wetter or drier in a rhythm so slow and so vast that it takes days to perceive a single cycle.

And at the center of the ridge, where the buildings are densest and the pipes converge, there is a structure that is not a building at all but a *depression* — a broad, shallow bowl carved into the ancient stone, perhaps thirty meters across and five meters deep at its center. Its walls are polished smooth, darker than the surrounding rock, warm to the touch. It collects the condensation from a wide area, fed by channels cut into the stone and lined with the dark metal of the pipes. The water gathers here and does not drain. It sits in the bowl in a thin, shimmering layer, and at its edges, where the depth is less than a finger's width, it evaporates into mist that rises and joins the cloud above.

The people call this place the Throat. They have always called it the Throat. It was the Throat before they arrived, and it will be the Throat after they leave, and it is not clear whether they came here because of the Throat or whether the Throat attracted them the way the moss attracted the ferns — slowly, passively, by simply being a place where accumulation was possible.

You stand on the ridge and look down into the bowl and the water is perfectly still and the cloud is rising and the pipes are humming and the moss is breathing and somewhere in the mist between the pillars, something that is not a bird and not an insect and not a person moves through the dimness with the patience of deep time, and the stone under your feet remembers pressures that haven't existed for eons, and the whole place — the city, the ridge, the cloud, the water, the strange unmoving pools and the warm pipes and the silent pillars — feels like a single organism, ancient and industrial and alive in a way that doesn't require a heartbeat.

It is mostly quiet. It is always damp. It is very, very old. It is still, somehow, *becoming* — and you cannot tell, standing here at the edge of the Throat, whether what it is becoming is something new or simply a more complete version of what it has always been.
