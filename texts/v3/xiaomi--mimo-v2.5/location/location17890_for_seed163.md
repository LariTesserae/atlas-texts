# The High Seeps

**The rock is new.** You understand this before anything else. It underlies everything — dark, basalt-grey, barely weathered — and it shows. The surfaces are still glassy where they broke open, smooth where pressure once held them. There are no sharp ravines, no sculpted gorges, no elegant undercutting. Water has only just begun to find this place, and the stone hasn't learned yet how to surrender to it. The first channels are shallow, tentative — finger-wide grooves running across flat expanses of exposed rock, more stain than scar. They look like the beginnings of something, the earliest pencil-sketches of a canyon system that won't exist for ten thousand years.

**But there is water everywhere.** It comes from below as much as from above. The rock is porous in places, riddled with vesicles and cooling fractures, and groundwater — heated somewhere deep, somewhere recent — pushes up through every weakness. Seeps. Weeping walls. Sheets of mineral-laden water flowing so slowly across flat stone that they barely shimmer. In other places it's faster: small cascades pouring off low shelves of rock into shallow basins, the water white with aeration, steaming faintly in the cool air. The atmosphere itself seems to carry water. It doesn't rain so much as *condense* — a perpetual mist that lifts and settles, lifts and settles, drawn up by the bright sun and dropped again by the cool elevation. The air tastes of wet mineral, of calcium and iron, of the deep earth's breath.

**Everything that can be covered, is covered.** The vegetation here has had no competition — no trees, no woody shrubs, nothing that needs years to establish. It arrived as spores and seeds on the wind and it found exactly what it wanted: wet, mineral-rich, bright, and open. The result is a landscape of total plant coverage that somehow remains low and vast. Sphagnum moss in six or seven shades of green, thick and saturated, building up slowly into hummocks and hummock-fields that will become small bogs within decades. Liverworts carpeting the rock in flat rosettes, dark and wetly shining. Ferns — not the tall tree-ferns of old forests, but low, spreading bracken and delicate maidenhair species — emerging from every crack and crevice where a little soil has begun to collect. Grasses in thin, bright green blades rising from the moss. Everything glistens. Everything is almost unreasonably alive.

From above — from the rare ridgeline or the roof of one of the scattered structures — the view is of a vast, luminous green-and-grey quilt stretching to the haze in every direction. The horizon is high and bright. The sky is enormous and almost white with light. The plateau extends for kilometers in every direction, a nearly level expanse broken only by low rises of exposed rock, shallow depressions where water pools into temporary ponds, and the slow suggestion of terrain beginning to differentiate — here a slightly steeper slope, there a place where two sheets of rock overlap and create a lip. But it is all mid-scale, all *open*. You could walk in a straight line for an hour and change elevation by perhaps thirty meters.

**The sound is the thing that disorients.** It is constant, layered, and enormous — not the single roar of a waterfall or the rhythmic crash of surf, but a *blanket* of water-sound coming from everywhere at once. Thousands of small sources: seeps whispering over stone, trickles finding their way through moss, small falls splashing into shallow pools, the collective hiss of mist condensing on every surface and running down. Stand still and try to isolate one source and you cannot. The sound has no center. It is the auditory equivalent of the landscape itself — diffuse, total, inescapable. Your body adjusts to it after a while, the way eyes adjust to brightness, but the adjustment itself is disorienting: the silence that follows when you put your hands over your ears is somehow louder than the sound was.

**The structures are few and far between.** You might walk twenty minutes across the spongy moss-plain before reaching one. They are industrial in character — utilitarian, concrete and rusted steel, built to a purpose that has nothing to do with beauty. Pumping stations with thick pipes disappearing into the rock, their motors humming beneath the water-sound. A processing facility with a corrugated metal roof gone orange with oxidation, its equipment partially submerged in moss that has climbed the walls and is working its way through broken windows. Sampling rigs — steel tripods with instruments attached, cables running to solar panels that are already clouded with algal growth. Everything here is being slowly consumed. The moss doesn't care about function. It grows over bolts and cables and the edges of concrete foundations with the same patient, thorough saturation it applies to everything. The structures feel temporary, already historical, though some still have power, still hum, still collect data on water chemistry and rock temperature and subsidence rates that will matter more in fifty years than they do now.

**There are almost no animals.** The landscape is too new, too wet, too exposed, and — perhaps — too loud for most creatures. Insects are present in the moss: tiny springtails and mites in impossible numbers, invisible unless you kneel and part the green with your fingers. A few hardy fly species patrol the pools. Occasionally a bird — a pipit or a wheatear — appears on a rock, bobs, listens, and leaves. There is no predator here, no prey worth chasing, no shelter tall enough to hide in. The ecosystem is plant-and-water, and the animals are incidental, barely present, like guests at a party they weren't quite invited to.

**The change is slow.** Not glacially slow — faster than that, because the water is persistent and the rock is new and vulnerable. But you don't see it happen. You see its evidence. A channel slightly deeper than it was last season. A patch of moss slightly thicker. A spring that has found a new path through the basalt. The erosion rate is small but measurable, and it is accelerating. In a century, the flat plateau will begin to show real topography — shallow valleys following the developing channels, the first low waterfalls where harder layers resist longer, the beginning of a drainage network that will, in geological time, carve this place into something unrecognizable. Right now it is the embryonic version of all of that. A sketch. A promise. The landscape hasn't decided yet what it wants to become.

**At night** — and the nights are long at this latitude and season — the mist drops close and the seeps glow faintly with bioluminescence from the microbial mats that thrive in the mineral-rich water. The industrial structures are dark except for the occasional blinking indicator light, red or green, reflected in the wet moss around them. The sound of water continues, unchanged by the absence of light. The plateau breathes in the dark, wetly and patiently, and in the morning the light returns enormous and bright and everything glistens again, exactly as before, but not quite — a millimeter more erosion here, a centimeter more growth there — and the work of becoming continues.
