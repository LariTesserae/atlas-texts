# The Worn Throat

You would pass this place a thousand times and never find it.

The plateau above is nothing — a vast, wind-scoured table of stone so old it has forgotten its own name, its surface worn to a smoothness that feels less like erosion and more like something *polished* across deep time. Everything on the upper surface is bleached and silent. The stone is the color of old bone. The sky presses close and featureless. You would think there was nothing here worth seeing.

But the rock has a throat.

---

A fissure, narrow as a man's shoulders, descends at an angle into the plateau's eastern face. Not a crack — too deliberate in its geometry, though no geometry that obeys current engineering. The walls are smooth in a way that erosion alone does not explain: not polished like river石, but *finished*, like something melted and re-solidified into a perfect curve. The stone here darkens, warms under your hands if you touch it. It vibrates faintly — not with seismic activity but with something almost electrical, a frequency just below the threshold of hearing that makes your teeth ache.

Twenty meters down, the throat opens.

---

**The pocket.** It is perhaps sixty meters across and half that deep — a hidden bowl worn into the heart of the ancient plateau, sheltered from every wind, lit normally by an opening high above where the fissure thins to a slot. Afternoon light falls in a single column that moves across the pocket's floor like a slow hand. The air inside is warm — noticeably, almost uncomfortably warm — and thick with moisture that has nowhere to go. The humidity clings. Your skin becomes a surface for condensation within minutes.

The stone of the pocket's interior is impossibly old and impossibly smooth. Every surface has been rounded to its final resting curvature: no sharp edges remain anywhere, as though the entire space has been sitting in the mouth of some vast patient process for longer than mountains last. The floor is a series of shallow basins — worn hollows in the stone, each one a few meters across, their shapes organic, almost biological, like the sockets of eyes in an enormous skull. Some are dry. Others hold water.

---

**The water** does not behave conventionally. It is not fed by rain or stream or spring in any obvious sense. Instead, it *condenses* — gathers in the basins through a process you can watch but not fully explain. A fine mist forms along the walls in the cooler hours, migrates downward along the stone's smooth contours, and pools in the lowest hollows. By midday the warm air has thinned it again, drawing it back into vapor that clings to the walls and waits. The water is faintly warm, faintly luminous in certain angles, and carries a mineral sweetness that is not unpleasant and not quite natural. You can hear it: a quiet, continuous trickle-and-settle, the sound of a thousand tiny movements, of liquid finding its level in smooth stone. It is one of the dominant sounds of the place.

---

**The vegetation** is lush but scattered — concentrated in and around the water basins, erupting in dense clusters of broad-leafed growth, trailing mosses, and thick-rooted plants that grip the smooth stone with a determination that suggests long competition for limited moisture. The leaves are dark, almost black-green, their surfaces waxy and adapted to catching every mote of condensation. Some species have evolved to channel water down their stems into the basins below — they are, in a sense, part of the hydrology. Ferns crowd the deeper shadows. In the warm, humid air, everything grows with an urgency that belies the sparseness of the larger space. Between the basins, the stone is bare. Between the green islands, nothing. The effect is of a handful of oases scattered across an alien moonscape — pockets of fierce life in an expanse of ancient, polished silence.

---

**The fauna** is not silent.

This is the first thing you notice after the warmth, and it is impossible to ignore: this pocket is *loud*. The sound is layered — a constant chitinous clicking and scraping from things that move along the walls, a low thrumming hum from somewhere beneath the floor (from the stone itself, or from something housed within it), sharp repetitive calls from creatures in the vegetation that sound almost like language and are not. The air above the water basins shimmers with flying things — dense, slow-winged, iridescent in the slanting light. They are not insects, not quite. Their bodies are segmented but the segments are too regular, almost *machined*, and they move with a coordination that suggests something more than instinct.

On the bare stone between basins, you can see larger presences. Lizard-like things, but long — two, three meters — their bodies low and sinuous, their coloration a perfect match for the warm grey stone. They are nearly invisible until they move, and they move often: flicking between patches of shadow with a speed that makes your eyes blur. Their mouths are open as they rest. You can hear a faint rasping — their breathing, or something else.

In the vegetation, things rustle constantly. Something small and fast is always moving through the undergrowth. Sometimes something not small. The dark leaves shake and settle, shake and settle, in patterns that seem too deliberate to be random. You catch glimpses — a limb, a flash of pale underbelly, an eye that reflects the overhead light with a geometric precision that no natural iris should possess.

The fauna here is not merely abundant. It is *architectural*. The creatures seem to occupy specific roles in a system that has been running for longer than any ecosystem on the surface — a closed economy of predation and symbiosis that has reached a density and complexity that feels almost engineered. The overwhelming impression is not of wilderness but of a machine that happens to be alive.

---

**The built remnants** are few and subtle, and they take time to recognize.

The first is the throat itself — the entrance fissure, with its impossible smoothness, its too-perfect curvature. It was shaped. Not carved, not eroded — *formed*, the way a ceramic is formed, by heat and intention applied simultaneously.

The second is a structure — if it can be called that — embedded in the pocket's northern wall, nearly consumed by moss and root growth. It is a smooth, rounded protrusion of a material that is not stone: darker, denser, with a faintly metallic sheen beneath the biological growth. It is shaped like a half-sphere, roughly two meters in diameter, and it is warm to the touch — warmer than the surrounding air. It produces the thrumming. If you press your hand against it, the vibration intensifies, and for a moment the flying things above the basins all orient toward you and then away, in unison, like a single organism responding to a stimulus.

There are smaller things, too. In one of the dry basins, half-buried in mineral sediment: a rod of the same dark material, perfectly straight, about a meter long, too heavy for its size. It does not corrode. It does not weather. The sediment that has settled around it is layered in annual or near-annual strata that suggest thousands of years of accumulation. It was placed here and it has not moved.

None of this technology is explicable in current terms. The materials do not match any known alloy or ceramic or composite. The thrumming structure's energy output — if it *is* output — cannot be accounted for by any power source that fits inside its volume. The engineering is beyond modern, not in the sense of being more advanced but in the sense of being *different* — operating on principles that current physics has not yet formalized. You can feel that it works. You cannot explain how.

---

**The strangeness** accumulates quietly. It is not a single dramatic wrongness but a slow saturation of the familiar with the *slightly off*. The fauna's geometric eyes. The water's too-deliberate movement. The way sound behaves in the pocket — your voice does not echo, it *decays*, as though the smooth stone is absorbing not just the vibration but the information in it. The light moves normally across the floor, but the shadows it casts are not quite the right shape — longer in some directions, shorter in others, as though the geometry of the space is very slightly non-Euclidean. Standing in the pocket's center, you feel watched, but not by any single thing. By the system. By the whole arranged machine of it.

---

**The atmosphere** is one of deep, settled, living patience. This place has been in its current state — not unchanged, but settled into its final *kind* of change — for longer than most things last on a planet's surface. The erosion has reached its end. The technology has reached its end. The fauna has reached its peak density and complexity. Everything is in motion — the creatures hunt and are hunted, the water condenses and evaporates, the thrumming continues — but the motion is circular, self-sustaining, locked into a groove worn as deep as the stone basins themselves.

It is a terrarium. An ancient terrarium, built by something that understood material science as an art form and left it running long after they departed — or dissolved, or became something else. The thermostat still works. The water cycle still runs. The organisms still fill their roles. It is warm, and loud, and alive, and it has been exactly this for longer than you can comfortably think about.

If you stay long enough, the flying things will land on you. They are curious or confused or simply indifferent to the distinction between you and any other surface in their world. They are warm and light and their tiny machined bodies vibrate at a frequency that is almost — but not quite — the same as the thrumming from the embedded structure.

You understand, then, that you are in something's idea of a garden. And the garden is still being tended, though by whom or what has been answered only in the grammar of basins and wings and smooth, dark, humming stone.
