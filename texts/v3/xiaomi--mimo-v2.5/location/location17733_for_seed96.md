# The Terraces of Unnamed Hands

You emerge onto a plateau that should not exist and know immediately that none of it is natural. Not a single surface was left to chance. Every face, every edge, every basin was shaped — and shaped with simple tools. The marks of that work are everywhere: the regular grooves of cord-wrapped stamps pressed into still-wet clay before it hardened; the shallow, crescent-shaped scoops left by antler blades or sharpened stones dragged across surfaces that were once pliable and are now rigid, permanent, mineral-white.

The scale is regional. You can see it stretch — the constructed landscape rolling outward in every direction, terraced and tiered and stacked, not in the gentle agricultural way of hillside fields but in something closer to architecture. Entire hillsides were built up from nothing, layer by layer, basin by basin, wall by wall, all of it packed so densely that there is no gap, no negative space that wasn't also considered. Every surface that could hold water does. Every depression was designed as a pool or a channel or a trough. The whole world functions as a kind of plumbing.

---

**The water is the most alive thing here, and it is not alive.**

It falls constantly — not rain, exactly, but a slow, bright condensation that assembles out of the thick, cool atmosphere and settles on every surface. The air itself seems to weep. Droplets form on the underside of carved lintels, on the lips of stone basins, along the razor-edges of recently-emerged walls. They fall with soft, individual sounds — a tapping here, a dripping there — but so many of them, across so many surfaces, that the aggregate is something between silence and a whisper. A texture of moisture. You hear it the way you hear your own blood.

There is no wind. Or rather: there is almost no wind. Occasionally the dense atmosphere shifts, a slow lateral movement like a curtain being drawn across a room, and the droplets on one face of a wall all release at once, creating a brief sheet of falling water, a silver curtain that lasts three seconds and then is gone. The sound of it is startling in the quiet: a bright, slapping hiss. Then silence resumes. Then dripping.

The water collects in the basins and runs along the channels, and it is always moving, always cycling. The terraces are engineered for this — you can see the deliberate gradients, the spillways, the overflow ports carved into the walls at precise heights. Water rises in the upper pools, spills over, descends through a sequence of catchments, and eventually reaches the lower basins where it pools and sits and slowly, very slowly, works its way back up. How? You're not sure. There may be capillary channels carved into the stone so fine you can barely see them — a wicking system, primitive but ingenious, drawing water upward through thousands of tiny grooves against gravity. It would take forever. It does take forever. The system is patient on a timescale that makes human patience look like an impulse.

---

**There is no life here.** Not a single organism. No moss, no lichen, no fungal bloom in the damp corners. The water is sterile. The surfaces are mineral and clean and, in their way, pristine — as though everything was built yesterday, though the marks of the tools suggest otherwise. The world has been like this for a very long time. It is a built environment that was never inhabited, or was inhabited once and emptied, or was built for no inhabitant at all.

The light is bright and diffuse and sourceless, or rather: the source is the atmosphere itself. The thick, wet air catches and scatters light from somewhere above — a sun behind a permanent veil of condensation — and returns it in every direction, so that there are no hard shadows. Everything is luminous. The wet surfaces catch the light and double it, triple it, pass it along in reflections that hop from basin to basin, pool to pool, wall to wall. Standing in the middle of a terrace, you are lit from every angle. It is difficult to see depth. Distances blur. The packed density of the constructed forms — walls buttressing walls, channels weaving between channels, basins nested inside basins — becomes a single, shimmering, complicated surface, wet and bright and disorienting.

Your shadow, when you manage to find it, is soft-edged and grey and seems to lag slightly behind you. This may be a trick of the refracted light. It may not be.

---

**The sharpness of the edges is striking.** This is a young world, geologically — not in the sense that it was made recently, but in the sense that erosion has barely touched it. The walls are still angular. The carved grooves are still defined. The spillway lips still have their original profile. Water is everywhere, but it is managed water — directed water — water that runs along paths designed for it and rarely wanders free to undercut or dissolve. The builders understood erosion. They accounted for it. Every surface that contacts flowing water is coated in a thin, hard, glassy slip — a fired or chemically-set clay lining that resists dissolution. Some of it has worn through after uncounted seasons, and where it has, the raw material beneath is eroding at last, softening, rounding, blurring — but slowly. The process is still young. The world still looks new, even if it isn't.

---

**The strangeness.**

It creeps in at the edges of your understanding. The proportions are wrong. Not dramatically — not in a way you could photograph and point to — but the spaces between walls, the height of the channels, the depth of the basins: they don't match the scale of a human body. They're too large for hands that would use antler tools. The tool marks say *primitive, manual, simple.* But the distances say *something taller, or longer, or shaped differently.* The grip that held the blade was not a human grip. The back that bent to carve a channel at ground level was not a human back.

And there is the question of purpose. This is not agriculture — there is nothing to grow. This is not a city — there is nothing to shelter. It is not a temple — there is nothing to worship. It is a water system. An elaborate, region-spanning, hand-carved water system that cycles sterile water through an empty landscape in perpetual slow motion, for no reason that anything present can identify.

Unless the system is the point. Unless the cycling itself — the patient, mechanical, endless movement of water upward and over and down and back up — is the only thing that was ever intended. A machine for moving water. A sculpture made of process. The builders made a world that does one thing, and the thing it does is continue.

---

You walk through it and your footsteps are the loudest sound. The water drips. The light refuses to dim. The terraces extend to every horizon, bright and wet and packed and empty, built by hands that left their marks and nothing else, for a purpose that was either forgotten or was never more than the motion itself — the slow, quiet, endless transit of water through a world that someone, once, with simple tools and impossible patience, decided should exist.
