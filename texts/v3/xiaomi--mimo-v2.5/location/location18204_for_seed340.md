# The Underneath

## I. The Space Before Understanding

The first thing is the sound.

It arrives not through ears but through the body itself — a pressure that fills the chest and vibrates the teeth, a ceaseless subsonic roar overlaid with higher shrieks, clicks, and a rhythmic thumping like a colossal heartbeat heard through water. You feel your skeleton before you feel the ground beneath your feet. The noise is not one noise but a thousand, a million, all happening in the same immense volume, and your mind simply *fails* to resolve it into parts. It becomes weather. It becomes climate. You stop hearing it the way you stop hearing your own blood.

When you finally open your eyes — and you may have been walking for some time before you realize you've closed them — you see that the world has no ceiling and no floor that you would recognize as such.

## II. The Walls That Are Not Walls

Everything is living tissue.

The ground — if it can be called that — is a compressed mat of root-matter, rhizome-lattice, and the fibrous husks of dead stems layered so thickly that you cannot tell where the organic ends and the mineral begins. It is warm to the touch, almost feverish, and it *yields* underfoot like the skin of something vast. In places your foot punches through a crust and lands on something softer, spongelike, that exhales a puff of dry, spore-laden air that smells of copper and overripe fruit and something underneath those smells that has no name in any language spoken by things with lungs.

The walls — curving, irregular, vanishing upward into a haze of amber light — are not carved from rock. They are grown. Thick buttresses of wood-like tissue, ribbed and striated, branch and interweave in patterns that seem architectural but are not, not exactly. They are the way trees would build if trees were not constrained by sky, if they could grow in every direction at once and had no reason to stop. Pillars of compressed cambium rise thirty, forty, sixty feet and then split into canopies of dense foliage that press upward against… what? You cannot see what the canopy presses against. There may be stone up there somewhere, the original cavern wall, the geological ceiling of this place, but the plant-matter has grown so thick, so total, that the stone is a memory buried under layers of root and bark and the calcified husks of ancient growth.

You are inside something. You are inside a living thing that has mistaken this enormous underground void for open sky.

## III. The Geology of Newness

What stone you can find — in the rare gaps where the root-matter has not yet closed — is raw. It has the fractured, unweathered look of rock that has never seen rain, never felt frost, never been touched by the slow patience of surface erosion. The fractures are sharp. The crystal faces are pristine. Veins of pale mineral run through dark basaltic material like lightning frozen mid-strike. Some of these veins glow faintly — not from heat, not from any source you can identify, but with a cold bioluminescence that seems mineral rather than biological, as if the rock itself is dreaming.

This is a young cave system. Or perhaps *young* is the wrong word — the geology may be ancient, but its *exposure* is new. The void was sealed for eons, and then something opened it, or the living mass found its way in, and the process of filling this space is still underway. In places you can see the boundary: bare rock face, sharp-edged, uncolonized, and then a creeping front of root-hairs and fungal filaments advancing across it like frost across a window. The frontier between mineral and organic is a battleground. You can smell the chemical warfare — acids, alkaloids, compounds that burn your nasal passages and make your eyes water.

Some of the bare rock is still settling. You hear it — not above the noise, never above the noise, but *within* it — the grinding and settling of stone that is finding its shape, caves within caves adjusting their geometry as the weight above them shifts. The whole place is in a state of architectural becoming. Nothing is finished. Nothing has agreed to be its final shape.

## IV. The Fauna

There is no describing the fauna without first acknowledging that *you are inside their house and they know it.*

They are everywhere.

In the first tier, the ground-level — or what passes for ground-level in this undulating mat of root-matter — things move through the gaps in the organic substrate. Things the size of your fist. Things the size of your head. Things shaped like beetles but armored in chitin that has the iridescent sheen of oil on water, clicking across the surfaces in armies that stretch farther than you can see. They are not insects. They are too complex, too purposeful, and their mouths — you see one turn toward you as you pass, its mandibles unfolding in three dimensions — contain structures that no surface insect has ever evolved. They are eating the substrate. They are eating it constantly, and the substrate is growing faster than they can consume it, and so the whole system is a negotiation between consumption and growth that has been running for longer than any surface forest.

In the second tier, in the canopy — the dense, interlocking foliage fifty or sixty feet above your head — something larger moves. You hear its passage: a crackling of branches, a displacement of air that you feel on your skin even from the ground. It is long. It is sinuous. It moves through the canopy the way a snake moves through grass, and its body is the same green-brown as the foliage, and its eyes — when you finally catch a glimpse of one — are the size of dinner plates and faceted and empty of anything you would recognize as thought but full of something worse: *attention.*

In the third tier, the spaces between canopy and ground, the airspace that is thick with spores and dust and the dry, electric atmosphere of this waterless world — things fly. Not birds. Not bats. Membranous things with too many wings, trailing filaments behind them that trail further filaments behind *them*, and the filaments trail further filaments after *those*, so that each flying thing is the center of a web of trailing threads that can be yards long, and where the threads touch the substrate they root, and where they root they grow, and the flying thing is pulling itself apart into new substrate as it flies, and it does not seem to mind.

There are things that are not tiers. There are things that are *events*. A sudden collapse of root-matter somewhere to your left, and from the gap erupts a wave of pale, eyeless creatures that pour over the surface like water — but this is not water, there is no water here, this is *life* poured out from some reservoir below the substrate, flowing over itself, tumbling, climbing your legs before you can move, and they are warm and dry and they weigh nothing and they are *everywhere* and they are gone in seconds, disappearing into another gap, another crevice, and the substrate is already knitting itself shut behind them.

The overwhelming fauna is not a backdrop. It is the point.

## V. The Atmosphere

The air is warm — consistently, uncomfortably warm, the warmth of a body, the warmth of enclosed metabolic processes on a vast scale. There is no wind in the conventional sense, but there is *breathing*. The entire cavern breathes. The root-matter expands and contracts in slow cycles that you can feel through the ground and see in the gentle motion of the foliage overhead, and with each expansion a current of air pushes outward from the deepest parts of the space, and with each contraction it pulls back. The air tastes of organic compounds — terpenes, phenols, things that sting and perfume in equal measure. It is thick. Not humid — there is no moisture to speak of, the air is desert-dry — but *dense*, packed with particulate matter, spores, shed chitin, fragments of dried plant tissue, all of it suspended, all of it catching the light.

The light. There is light. It does not come from above or from any single source. It emanates from the living matter itself. The substrate glows — faintly, in patches, in a spectrum that runs from deep amber to a sickly greenish-gold. The foliage glows more brightly, each leaf backlit by some internal process that converts the chemical energy of this waterless ecosystem into photons. The cumulative effect is an environment lit like a sunset that never ends, warm and shadowless and strange, with the darkest places being the bare-rock faces that have not yet been colonized, where the mineral veins add their own faint luminescence to the mix and the shadows are blue-black and sharp-edged.

## VI. The Structures

You find them occasionally. You almost miss them.

A shape that is too regular to be natural: a post driven into the root-matter, its surface carved with marks that might be writing or might be decoration or might be something else entirely. The post is old — older than the current growth cycle of the substrate, which means someone put it there before the living surface crept up around its base. The wood — if it is wood, it might be bone — is dark with age and dry beyond dry. It has the density of stone.

A low wall. Not a wall of stacked stone but a wall of *mud*, or rather of packed, dried organic material that has been shaped by hands — you can see the impressions, the fingerprints, or their equivalent — and then left to harden. It is half-buried now in new root-growth. Whatever purpose it served, the living world has decided to forget it.

A pit. Deliberately excavated, its edges too straight and too even to be natural. You peer into it and see the raw rock below the substrate, uncolonized, and at the bottom — far down, the pit is deeper than you thought — something glints. Metal. Primitive metal, hammered and crude, but metal. A tool. A weapon. An offering. You do not climb down to find out. The walls of the pit are slick with a dried secretion that smells like antiseptic, and the eyeless pale creatures from earlier are beginning to flow back into the pit through cracks in the substrate, and you decide that whatever is at the bottom, it belongs to them now.

These structures are scattered, rare, almost swallowed. Whatever intelligence built them either left long ago or was consumed by the growing world around them. The tech is primitive — hand-shaped, hand-carved, made from the materials at hand. But *made*. Deliberately, with intent. Someone looked at this overwhelming, deafening, living cavern and tried to build something in it, and the cavern is patiently, inevitably, absorbing their efforts back into itself.

## VII. The Turbulence

Nothing here is still.

The substrate shifts. Not like a tide — that implies water, and there is no water — but like a slow earthquake of living matter. Sections rise, compressing the air above them, and other sections sink, creating temporary depressions that fill with detritus and then close over. The root-matter is *growing* as you watch, not in any direction you can track, but in all directions at once, a thousand simultaneous micro-events of expansion that collectively create a landscape that is genuinely different from the one you started walking through five minutes ago.

The fauna amplifies this. The armies of chitinous creatures reshape the surface as they eat, carving channels and valleys into the mat. The flying things drag their rooting filaments across new territory. The larger canopy-dwellers knock down branches that fall and are immediately colonized by ground-level growth, creating new substrate that rises and reshapes the ground-level topography. Everything is food for everything else, and everything is building material for everything else, and the rate of turnover is so fast that the place seems to vibrate.

There are crises. Sudden collapses — whole sections of canopy giving way under the weight of a creature too large for the branch structure, crashing down, creating a gap in the foliage that floods the ground with brighter light, which triggers a growth spurt in the substrate below, which pushes the ground level up, which destabilizes the root-matter around it, which triggers a chain of small collapses and regrowths that ripples outward for hundreds of feet. These events happen constantly. The sound of them — the crack, the rush of air, the impact, the subsequent scramble of fauna fleeing and then returning — adds to the deafening total.

There is a storm here, but it is not weather. It is *ecology.* The storm is the system itself, the furious metabolic pace of a place where everything is alive and nothing is water and the energy inputs come from somewhere deep below the raw geology, some geothermal or geochemical source that feeds the entire edifice, and the energy is too much, always too much, and the system is always on the edge of overshooting itself. Growth and death and growth again, compressed into timescales that make surface forests look like stone.

## VIII. The Rules

You begin to understand the rules, or you begin to think you do, which is not the same thing but is the best you can manage:

**Do not stand still.** The substrate will grow over you. It is not malicious. It does not know you are there. It grows over everything.

**Do not touch the bare rock.** The bare rock is where the chemistry is rawest, where the acids are strongest, where the unprocessed minerals react with organic compounds in ways that will burn through leather and then through skin and then through the thing beneath skin that you need in order to be a person.

**Do not look up for too long.** Not because something will see you looking — though it might — but because the canopy is dense enough that looking up gives you no spatial orientation, and you will lose your sense of which way is which and fall into a section of root-matter that hasn't solidified yet and find yourself in the space between the substrate and the raw rock below, which is narrow and dark and full of things that have never seen light and do not need eyes to find you.

**Listen to the rhythm.** The breathing of the cavern is not random. It has a pattern. If you can learn the pattern, you can predict when the air will pull inward and when it will push outward, and you can move with the push and rest with the pull, and you will use less energy, and you will last longer, and lasting longer is the only strategy that has ever worked in a place that does not care whether you exist.

**The structures are warnings or invitations.** It does not matter which. They are the only fixed points in a world that moves, and fixed points are either anchors or traps. Treat them as both.

## IX. The Feeling of It

You are warm. You are dry. You are surrounded by more life per cubic foot than the surface world sees in a season. The sound has moved past discomfort and into a kind of liberation — you cannot hear yourself think, and so you stop trying, and you begin to perceive the place not through analysis but through your body, through the soles of your feet on the warm, yielding ground, through the air moving across your skin in time with the cavern's breath, through the light that has no source and the smell that has no name and the presence of ten thousand things around you that are eating and growing and dying and growing again in a cycle so compressed that it looks like chaos but is not, not exactly, it is metabolism on a geological scale, and you are standing inside it, and it does not know your name, and it will not learn it, and that is the most honest relationship you have ever had with a place.
