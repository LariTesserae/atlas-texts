# The Keening Plateau

The sound hits first. Not as noise but as *pressure* — a subsonic thrum felt in the chest cavity, overlaid with a harmonic shriek that seems to emanate from the stone itself. It never stops. It has never stopped. It is the sound of altitude remembering what it was before it was rock.

You stand on a high place. Very high. The air is thin and cool, carrying no moisture because there is no moisture to carry — not here, not in this entire basin of cracked mineral and pale crystalline shelf. The ground beneath your feet is the color of old bone: chalk-white limestone or something that merely resembles it, veined with threads of pale copper-green where the first mineral veins have begun to assert themselves. This is a young geology. Embryonic. The plateau is not eroding; it is *becoming*. Slopes that should have been smoothed by millennia of water instead show raw fracture planes, as though the mountain itself only recently decided to be a mountain and is still settling into the idea.

And yet: growth.

That is the first impossibility. Without water, in dim light, at this altitude where the sky is a bruised indigo pressing down from uncomfortably close — vegetation thrives. Not bushes. Not scrub. Great pale fronds, almost translucent, rising from fissures in the rock like the open hands of something pleading. They are white, green-white, the blue-green of deep glacier ice, and they are *enormous* — some reaching three or four meters, their edges feathered into filaments that vibrate in sympathy with the omnipresent sound. They photosynthesize something. Perhaps they eat the sound itself. Their root systems, exposed where the embryonic stone hasn't yet solidified around them, are thick and pale and muscular, wedging into hairline cracks and widening them with the patience of centuries. They are the only erosion here: biological, deliberate, slow.

No animals move among them. None have ever moved among them. The ecology of this place is plant and mineral and nothing between. The fronds shiver without wind — or rather, they shiver with the *sound*, that omnipresent harmonic that vibrates up through the rock and into their tissues. In the dim light, which seems to come from everywhere and nowhere, a diffuse luminescence without a visible sun or source, they cast no shadows. They simply *glow*, faintly, a cold bioluminescence that pulses in slow synchrony with the keening.

The scale is vast but sparse. You can see for kilometers in every direction — not because the air is clear (there is a haze, a mineral dust suspended in the thin atmosphere, catching the dim light into a perpetual twilight murk) but because there is almost nothing to obstruct the view. The plateau extends in all directions, broken by ridgelines and shallow canyons where the stone is still calving away in slow geological contractions — not crumbling, but *peeling*, like skin, revealing fresh surfaces beneath that haven't yet oxidized or weathered. These fresh surfaces are darker, almost black, and they steam faintly in the cool air. The canyons are young. Days old, maybe. Or centuries. Time here has lost its reliability.

The buildings — if you can call them that — are scattered across the plateau with no discernible pattern. They are small. A wall here, barely a meter high, built of the same chalk-white stone stacked without mortar. A circular arrangement of standing slabs there, like a crown embedded in the rock. Some are half-finished. Some appear to have been started, abandoned, and then partially swallowed by new stone growing around them. They are not old. They are not new. They exist in the same temporal ambiguity as everything else. Whoever built them used hand tools — crude, heavy things of shaped stone and bone, left in situ where they were dropped, their edges still sharp. No metal. No sophistication. Just the primal act of stacking one stone upon another, as if the builders were copying something they had seen the geology do and trying to understand it by imitation.

The reality breakdown is the hardest part to describe because it operates at the threshold of perception. It is not dramatic. There are no screaming voids or fracturing sky-cubes. It is subtle and it is *everywhere*:

The shadows of the fronds fall upward. Not cast by a light source below — there is no light source below — but simply *choosing* to fall upward, toward the dark sky, as though gravity has opinions.

Distances are unreliable. You can see a ridge that looks ten minutes away and walk toward it for an hour without noticeably closing the gap, only to turn around and find it right behind you, close enough to touch. The plateau is open, yes, extending to the horizon in every direction — but the horizon itself is a liar, curving in ways that suggest you are standing on the outside of something very large rather than its top.

The sound — always the sound — has *structure*. In certain places on the plateau, if you stand very still, you can hear something beneath the keening that almost resolves into language. Not words. Not even phonemes. But the *cadence* of language, as though the geology is trying to speak in a tongue composed entirely of tectonic pressure and mineral resonance. It has been trying for a very long time. It will keep trying. The dynamics here are slow. Everything is slow. The fronds grow a centimeter per decade. The canyons widen by millimeters per century. The sound has been building, pitch by imperceptible pitch, since before any living thing existed to hear it.

And yet nothing decays. The scattered walls do not crumble further. The hand tools do not dull. The fronds do not wither at their tips. There is no water to dissolve, no wind to abrade, no animals to disturb. In the absence of the usual mechanisms of entropy, things here simply *persist*, locked in their state of becoming, frozen at the exact moment of their emergence.

The dust in the air glows. Not brightly. Just enough that if you hold your hand out, you can see a faint aura around your fingers, as though the particles are repelled by — or attracted to — the particular wrongness of your biology. You do not belong here. Nothing alive does. The fronds are not alive in any way you would recognize. They are mineral structures that learned to move. They are stone that decided to grow. And they are doing it slowly, in the dark, in the deafening silence of a place that is still deciding what the rules are.

If you stay long enough — and staying is easy, because hunger comes slowly here, and thirst doesn't come at all, because you are not using water in the way you normally use it — you begin to notice that your own edges are softening. Not physically. Conceptually. The boundary between you and the plateau becomes a suggestion rather than a fact. The sound gets inside you. The dim light makes your skin look like stone. You could be one of the builders, one of those ancient hand-tool people, still here, still stacking, still listening to the mountain try to speak.

You could be one of the fronds.

You could be the sound itself, finally hearing itself from the outside.

The plateau doesn't care. It is very busy becoming a mountain. It will take a long time. It has all the time there is, which, here, is not the same amount as it is anywhere else. The dust glows. The fronds shiver. The sound builds, one harmonic at a time, toward a pitch that will eventually crack the sky open and let in whatever was on the other side before the sky was installed.

No one is listening.

Everyone is listening.
