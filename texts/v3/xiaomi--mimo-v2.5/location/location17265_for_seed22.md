# The Vast Mouth of Ond-Settling

## First Impression

You stand in a place that should not be quiet, and it is not. The sound arrives before the sight does — a continuous, layered roar that has no single source, as though the air itself were grinding teeth. It is warm. The warmth is immediate and close, the kind that presses against skin and doesn't leave. Your eyes adjust to a light that is not dark but is not light — a perpetual bruised amber, as if the sky were a sheet of smoked glass lit from behind by a furnace you can never quite see.

Then you see the land, and you understand the scale.

## The Terrain

The ground is young. That is the first thing you *feel* about it — not know, feel, the way you feel that a bone is set wrong. The rock is sharp. Not weathered. Not rounded by millennia of patient wind. The ridgelines are knife-thin, fresh-edged, the color of uncooked clay and darkened copper. This is a landscape that was recently *made*, assembled by forces that operated on a timeline that embarrasses geology. The low-lying basin stretches in every direction — you can see for forty, fifty kilometers before the haze swallows the horizon — and it is a basin of broken geometry. Shelves of pale stone jut at angles that suggest tectonic violence, but the angles are too regular, almost planned, as though something enormous pushed the earth upward in a grid. Between these shelves, the ground slopes in shallow, angular bowls, each one a few hundred meters across, and in the bottom of each bowl there is —

Water. Not much. A thin skin of it, sometimes just moisture darkening the rock, sometimes a pool no deeper than your ankle, warm to the touch and faintly luminous. The water doesn't flow. It seeps. It appears where the stone breathes out and disappears where the stone breathes in, and the cycle is fast enough to watch — you can see the edge of a pool creeping outward, then retreating, as though the ground has a pulse. Condensation gathers on the sharp ridgelines and drips in slow, regular intervals into the bowls below. Each droplet, when it lands, makes a sound that should be inaudible but isn't. Everything here is loud.

## The Vegetation

This is the contradiction that first confounds you: the place is *lush*. Incredibly, obscenely lush. The sharp-edged basins are choked with plant life that grows in dense, vertical curtains — not from the ground up, but from the rock faces *outward*, as though the stone were growing fur. The vegetation is thick-leafed, waxy, the deep saturated green of things that grow in permanent twilight. It has a texture like leather. Some of it is bioluminescent — not brightly, not like the stories, but enough that the overall dimness of the landscape is softened by a faint internal glow, a green-gold phosphorescence that comes from within the leaf tissue itself. The plants photosynthesize something other than what you'd expect. They are feeding on the light that exists here — this bruised amber wash — and they are *thriving* on it.

The flora has a structural quality that unsettles. It grows in fractals. Not approximately, not in the loose way that ferns approximate a Fibonacci spiral — *exactly*, with a precision that looks engineered. Each frond of the dominant ground-cover plant divides into six sub-fronds, each of which divides into six more, to a depth you can count if you lean close enough, and it goes down twelve, fifteen layers before the resolution of your eye gives out. The effect, across the whole basin, is of a landscape that is *detailed beyond its own resolution*, as though it were rendered at a fidelity that keeps resolving further the closer you look. Standing back, it looks like any jungle. Standing close, it looks like an accusation.

There are no trees in the familiar sense. The vertical structures are instead long, smooth columns — pale, the color of old bone — that rise ten, fifteen meters from the basin floors and split at their tops into broad, flat canopies of interlocking leaf-matter. They look like fungal fruiting bodies more than plants. They sway in a wind you cannot feel, and when they sway, they produce a low harmonic tone, a thrumming that adds another layer to the relentless sound.

## The Sound

The deafening is not one sound. It is *all* of them.

The fauna is abundant — shockingly so for a landscape this empty of solid volume. The animals are not large. Most are small, modular, insectoid things with too many legs and bodies that seem to be assembled from separate pieces that move independently. They are everywhere. They are *in* the vegetation, *on* the rock, *in* the water, in the air. Their sound is a layered, broadband roar — the collective vocalization of millions of organisms communicating in frequencies that span from below human hearing to just above it. It is not random. It has rhythm. It has structure. If you listen long enough — three minutes, five — you begin to perceive patterns, repetitions, call-and-response architectures that span the entire basin. The fauna are *talking to each other*, all of them, constantly, and the conversation is so vast and so loud that it becomes a single, continuous, overwhelming wash of noise — a wall of meaning that you can almost parse but never quite, like eavesdropping on a city from a great height.

The harmonic thrum of the plant-columns adds a bass foundation. The seeping and retreating of the water in the basins adds a rhythmic, percussive element — a wet clicking that varies from bowl to bowl, creating a phasing effect, as though the landscape were a drum kit played by something with too many hands. Wind moves through the fractal vegetation and produces a sound like whispered static.

You cannot hear yourself think. You learn, quickly, to think in images instead.

## The Fauna

Beyond the ubiquitous small arthropodoids, the basin hosts larger presences. They are rare — each one occupying a volume of space hundreds of meters wide, a radius of near-emptiness around them that is conspicuous precisely because the density parameter of this landscape is so low. They are the reason the density is so low. They have *cleared* their space.

The largest of them are slow-moving, multi-legged platforms — the size of houses, armored in plates of dark chitin that reflect the dim amber light like wet stone. They walk the basin floors with a stately, almost architectural precision, and as they walk, the ground beneath them reshapes. Not dramatically. Not like an earthquake. But you can see it — the sharp ridgelines of the young rock *adjusting* around their feet, smoothing, curving, as though the stone were temporarily becoming viscous. They leave behind them a trail that is not a track but a *road* — a smooth, slightly concave channel in the rock that hardens after they pass and persists. The roads they build connect nothing to nothing. They go from basin to basin in routes that seem random but, seen from above — if you could see from above — form a network of staggering complexity, a vast graph laid across the lowland like a nervous system.

Smaller fauna orbit these giants. Six-legged, quick, chittering, they ride on the armored plates and seem to tend them — cleaning, probing, repairing. The relationship is not parasitic and not symbiotic in any way you can easily classify. It looks like *custodianship*.

## The Settlement

And then there are the structures, and here is where the strangeness sharpens.

They are enormous, and they are few, and they are separated by distances that make the word "town" feel like a lie. A settlement, yes — organized construction, infrastructure, layered building — but at a scale and a dispersal that belongs to a species that has redefined proximity. You can see one of the structures from where you stand: a tall, narrow tower, maybe three hundred meters high, that rises from the floor of a distant basin like a needle driven into the earth. It is not straight. It curves gently, organic, the way a horn curves or a claw, and its surface is covered in a fine, shifting texture — not decoration, not armor, but something that moves. At this distance, it looks like scales. Up close, if you were to walk the kilometers to reach it, you would find that the scales are individually articulated panels, each one a few centimeters across, and each one is performing a computation. The tower *thinks*. Its surface is its mind.

The structures are connected by lines — not roads, not cables, but something that behaves like both. They are thin, luminous filaments that run along the ground or just above it, pulsing with a slow, rhythmic light that travels in one direction and then the other. They carry information, certainly. They might carry matter, too — the technology here does not observe the usual distinctions between signal and substance. You have heard that these filaments can move things. You have heard that they can move *ideas*, which in this place may be the same thing.

There are other structures. A cluster of lower, broader buildings in a basin to the south — the densest concentration of construction you can see, which is still almost nothing, a handful of shapes scattered across a kilometers-wide bowl. The buildings are not made of any material you can name. They are translucent, slightly, and they glow from within — not with light, exactly, but with a visual texture that your eye processes as light because it has no other category. They look like frozen smoke. They are as strong as anything you've ever touched and lighter than they should be. They were not built by construction. They were *grown* — assembled at the molecular level by swarms of invisible machines that still inhabit their walls, still maintaining, still adjusting, still *thinking* the buildings into continued existence.

This is the beyond-modern technology, and it is not hidden. It is everywhere. It is in the roads the fauna-plateaus build. It is in the fractal precision of the vegetation. It is in the filaments that pulse between the towers. It is in the towers themselves. The engineering here does not describe physics that current science fully models because the engineering has *augmented* physics, locally, in this basin, to suit its purposes. Gravity is slightly different near the filaments. Light bends in ways it shouldn't near the tower surfaces. The warm, damp air carries information — you can feel it on your skin, a faint tingling, as though the atmosphere is slightly aware of you and is deciding what to do about it.

## The Rules of the World

The landscape operates on a logic that is legible but not familiar. The young rock is young because it is *still being made*. The geological processes that created this basin did not finish. They are ongoing. The earth here is being manufactured by a force — the same force that built the towers, that engineered the vegetation, that perhaps even shaped the fauna — and the manufacturing has a pattern, a purpose, that you cannot read from the ground. From above, the basin would look like a diagram. From the ground, it looks like a wilderness.

The emptiness is deliberate. The technology here requires space — not for function, but for *process*. The filaments need room to carry their signals without interference. The towers need room to think. The fauna need room to perform their custodial rituals. The vegetation needs room to grow its fractals to full depth. Density is the enemy of this place. Everything is optimized for *separation*, for the precise, calibrated distance between one presence and the next.

And yet it is alive. It is teeming. The sound is proof — the sound alone is proof that this emptiness is not absence but *architecture*, that the space between things is as carefully constructed as the things themselves.

## The Atmosphere, Finally

You stand in a basin. The sharp ridgelines are dark against the amber sky. The vegetation glows faintly at the edges of your vision. The water seeps and retreats at your feet, warm and faintly luminous. The sound is everywhere, is everything, is a physical pressure against your chest and the inside of your skull. The air smells of wet stone and something sweet and mineral, like ozone mixed with crushed leaves. It is warm. It is always warm. The warmth does not change. It is a constant, a given, a fact of this place as fundamental as the young rock or the fractal plants or the towers that think.

You are the only thing here that was not built for this place. You are the only thing here that does not belong to the pattern. And the pattern is aware of you — you can feel it, that tingling in the air, that faint visual distortion at the edge of the nearest filament's pulse — and it is deciding, slowly, at the pace of geology, what to do with the anomaly of your presence.

You have time. The decision is not urgent. The basin will be here, warm and loud and alive and empty and strange, long after you are gone.
