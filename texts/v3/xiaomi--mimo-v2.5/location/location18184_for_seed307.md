# The Cleft of Glass Teeth

You have to know it's there to find it — a fissure in a basalt shelf no wider than a man's shoulders, hidden behind a curtain of mineral steam where hot water seeps from the living rock. The entrance is almost invisible: a dark lip of obsidian, still sharp enough to split skin, framing a crack that drops and bends and opens, barely, into the space below.

And the space *opens*. That is the first wrongness. The cleft should not be as large as it is. From outside, the fissure suggests a crack no deeper than a few body-lengths. Inside, the walls recede in geometries that don't resolve. A vertical shaft of black glass descends — or curves — into a hollow perhaps the size of a small room, perhaps the size of a much larger room, depending on where you try to measure from. The ceiling drips. The floor is not flat. There are tiers and shelves and sudden drops, all of them fresh-cut, every surface still carrying the conchoidal fracture of stone that broke recently on a geological scale — decades, perhaps. Centuries at most. The rock hasn't even begun to soften at its edges. Everything here is a knife.

**The water** is everywhere and it is hot. Not boiling — not quite — but hot enough that the air shimmers and every breath comes damp and heavy. It weeps from a hundred hairline fractures in the ceiling, condenses on walls that are cooler in some places and hotter in others, collects in shallow basins carved into the glassy floor where it sits milky with dissolved minerals and gives off thin spirals of vapor. Some of the pools are warm. Some are scalding. The rock between them is wet and slick and almost frictionless. The humidity is total. You cannot see clearly more than a few body-lengths in any direction — the air itself is a veil of suspended droplets, catching what little light reaches down here, turning it into a dim, opal haze.

**The light** is normal in the sense that it is not supernatural — it is daylight, pushed down through the fissure above, filtered through steam, refracted through water running down the walls. It arrives soft and sourceless, the color of weak tea. In some corners of the cleft it pools brighter where the geometry of the walls focuses it. In others it fails entirely, and those places are dark in a way that feels deliberate.

**The temperature** is punishing. The rock itself radiates — a deep, stored heat, volcanic in origin, not yet spent. The water is warm from below. The air is thick with moisture and warmth together, the kind of heat that presses on the skin from all directions and makes the lungs work harder. You sweat immediately and continuously. Your clothes, if you have any, cling to you within moments. The heat does not feel like summer. It feels like the breath of something still forming.

**The fauna is overwhelming.**

This is the thing that makes the cleft unbearable to stay in, and impossible to leave. The walls, the floors, the surfaces of every pool, the undersides of every ledge — they are *occupied*. Not by plants. Not by moss or lichen or anything with chlorophyll or roots. There is nothing green here. There is nothing that grows toward light.

Instead:

There are organisms — if that is what they are — that coat the wet rock in layers so dense the stone beneath is invisible. The lowest layer is a kind of living film, grey-white, somewhere between fungal and colonial-animal, spreading across the glass surfaces in fractal patterns that never quite repeat. It gives the walls a texture like wet felt. On top of that, and sometimes emerging *from* it, are things with shapes — small, hard to categorize, ranging from the size of a fingernail to the length of a forearm. They have shells, or plates, or exoskeletons of a mineralized material that gleams like the rock they cling to. Some are symmetrical. Many are not. They move, but slowly, and with a deliberateness that suggests they are not fleeing you so much as *acknowledging* you and choosing, for now, not to react.

Above them — higher on the walls, on the ceiling, in the darker crevices — the larger things live. They are the ones that breathe. You can hear them not with sound but with *pressure*: a rhythmic displacement of the hot, wet air, like the cleft itself is inhaling. Their shapes are wrong. They have too many joints, or too few. Some of them hang from the ceiling by appendages that could be limbs or could be roots or could be something else entirely — something that is not either, that fills the conceptual space between animal and mineral that we assume is empty. Their bodies are the color of wet basalt. Their undersides, where visible, are the color of raw meat. They do not have eyes that you can identify, but they orient toward you with a certainty that suggests sight is not the only way to see.

There are hundreds of them. In this small space, this pocket barely large enough to stand in, there are hundreds of individual organisms, layered on top of one another, occupying every surface, filling the volume of the air itself with hanging bodies and slow, patient movement. The density is obscene. The cleft is *full* — stuffed with life the way a wound is stuffed with maggots, except this was not a wound first. This is how the place has always been. The rock grew around them, or they grew into the rock, and the distinction between those two sentences has no meaning here.

**The reality breakdown.** This is the thing you notice after the heat and the wet and the creatures and the wrong geometry: the cleft does not behave consistently with itself. A ledge you climbed down to reach a pool is now above you. Not higher on the wall — *above you*, in the sense that it is the ceiling, which it was not before. The water in the deepest basin flows upward along one wall in a thin, steady sheet, adhering to the glass surface against gravity with no visible cause. Some of the creatures exist in places that are too small for them — a body the size of a cat wedged into a crack no wider than a finger, and yet visible, undistorted, simply *there*, as if the crack were the right size and your perception is what's wrong. Distances shift. You reach for a wall that is an arm's length away and your hand touches something that is closer and warmer than rock, and you pull your hand back, and the wall is where it was. One of the pools reflects a ceiling that has too many surfaces. Another pool reflects nothing at all — it is black and flat and shows you only the dim haze above it, but something is wrong with what it shows you, some detail is absent or added, and you cannot identify which.

The geometry of the cleft is not Euclidean. It is not non-Euclidean in any clean, mathematical way. It is *approximate* — as if space here has not fully committed to its own rules and is still negotiating.

**The sound.** Despite everything — the creatures, the dripping, the movement, the heat — the cleft is nearly silent. The water makes no noise when it falls. The organisms make no noise when they move. The breathing pressure of the larger things produces no audible sound, only that subcutaneous push against the eardrum. The silence is not peaceful. It is the silence of a place where sound should exist and does not — where the physics of vibration have been quietly subtracted from the local rules. You can hear your own heartbeat. You can hear the wet sound of your own breathing. Outside of that, nothing. The silence is so complete that it takes on texture. It becomes another layer of the humidity, another weight pressing against the skin.

**There is no plant life.** Nothing here photosynthesizes. Nothing here derives energy from the sun. The entire ecosystem — if it is an ecosystem — runs on heat and chemistry, on the mineral wealth of the volcanic rock, on whatever it is that the deepest water carries up from below. The organisms are consumers and converters, miners of stone, eaters of heat. They have no need for light. The light that reaches them is incidental, a curiosity of the fissure's geometry, and they orient toward it no more than you orient toward ultraviolet.

**The tech.** Somewhere on a ledge near the entrance, half-hidden by the living film that coats every surface, there is a stone. It has been shaped — struck, chipped, its edge refined into something that could cut. A simple hand tool, left behind by someone who came here before, who looked at this place with primate eyes and primate hands and made something to interact with it. The tool is half-absorbed now. The grey-white film has begun to grow over it. In a year, or ten, it will be indistinguishable from the wall. Whoever made it is not here. Whoever made it may not exist anymore, above, on the surface, wherever and whenever that is. The tool is the only evidence that anything with hands has ever stood where you are standing.

You can take it, if you like. It is warm from the rock. Its edge is still sharp. It fits the hand well, as if it were made for yours.

It probably was not.
