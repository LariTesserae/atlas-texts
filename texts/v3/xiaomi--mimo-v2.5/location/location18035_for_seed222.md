# The Kora Plateau

The light here is relentless. Not cruel, exactly — the warmth has a patient, dry quality to it — but undimmed. The sky sits thin and pale above the high country, and what filters through strikes the earth with a clarity that makes every edge sharp, every distance deceptive. Shadows are short and hard-edged. The plateau stretches in all directions, a broad elevated shelf of pale stone — aite and chalky limestone — worn into gentle, almost organic curves by millennia of wind and the intermittent work of water. Rounded ridges. Shallow basins. Channels that were carved by rivers long since redirected or evaporated, now holding only memory and the occasional flash of runoff after the rains that come and go without warning.

The rain, when it falls, is brief and scattered — a warm misting, a sudden gray curtain that sweeps across one section of the plateau while another remains bone-dry under the same pale sky. It fills the basins in hours, and the basins hold the water for days, sometimes weeks, fed also by condensation that gathers on certain stone surfaces in the early mornings. There is a pattern to the condensation that does not match the pattern of the dew point. The rock in certain places seems to *attract* moisture, beading with it on mornings when the air should be too warm and too dry for it. Those patches of stone are faintly darker, smoother, almost glassy to the touch, and the water that gathers on them has a faintly mineral taste that isn't quite right for limestone.

The vegetation is sparse but persistent. Tough, leathery shrubs cling to cracks in the stone, their roots driven deep into fissures where water lingers longest. Grasses — pale, straw-colored even when they're alive — grow in the basins and along the old river channels, forming soft, dry meadows that rustle faintly in the warm wind. Here and there, a larger plant: something like a candelabra tree, its branches reaching upward in stark, angular angles, its leaves small and waxy and dark green, almost black in this light. These trees are old. Their trunks are pale and smooth-barked, almost bone-colored, and they cast sharp, thin shadows. They tend to stand alone or in loose clusters of three or four, never forming anything like a forest. Between them, the ground is bare stone or thin, gravelly soil, warm underfoot even through boots.

And the sound is wrong.

This should be a noisy place. The wind should move through the grasses and the branches. There are insects — dragonflies with iridescent bodies that patrol the basins, beetles in the gravel, moths that come to the pools at dusk. There are lizards, dozens of species, their colors ranging from stone-gray to vivid blue. There are small mammals — a kind of hopping rodent with large eyes, a burrowing creature that leaves neat spirals of displaced soil around its entrances. There are birds — ground-dwelling things with heavy bodies and quick, darting flight patterns, raptors that ride the thermals above the ridge lines in slow, circling patience.

They are all here. The fauna is rich, abundant, thriving in this sparse and bright country.

But the sound doesn't carry.

It is as though the air itself is thick with something that isn't atmosphere — not humidity, not dust, but some quality that swallows vibration. A lizard clicks its throat pouch three feet away and the sound reaches you muffled, flattened, as though it traveled from much farther. Wind passes through the grass and the rustle is there but diminished, half a step behind the movement. When one of the raptors cries out from high above, the sound arrives thin and ghostly, already dying, as though the air were slowly drinking it. You can hear yourself breathe. Your footsteps are muted, as though walking on felt. It is not silence — nothing so absolute. It is a suppression. A *gathering-in* of sound, as though the plateau were holding its breath, or as though the vibrations were being collected, redirected, stored somewhere below the stone.

There are structures.

Not many. Scattered. Small, mostly — a few are larger, enough to shelter in. They are made of something that was not quarried from this plateau, or if it was, it has been changed. The material is smooth and warm, warmer than the surrounding rock even at midday, and it has a faint, shifting luster — not quite metallic, not quite crystalline, not quite ceramic. It seems to have depth. You can look at a surface and sense that there is more *in* it than its dimensions suggest, as though each slab or pillar or wall contains a compressed interior space, a fold, a pocket of dimension that the eye can almost resolve but never does. Some of the structures are partially buried, the pale local stone accumulated around them over centuries, and they protrude from the ground like bones through skin. Others stand freely — a single pillar here, a low curved wall there, a geometric form that might have been a shelter or might have been something else entirely, something whose purpose is not recoverable by looking at it.

They do nothing. Or they seem to. But the condensation gathers on them preferentially, and the stone immediately around them is sometimes cooler, sometimes warmer than it should be, and the pools that form in their depressions sometimes shimmer with a faint iridescence that has no source.

The dynamic of the place is slow but constant. The weather cycles through dry and brief-wet with a rhythm that feels almost biological — breathing in, breathing out. The fauna moves along ancient paths between the water sources, following the basins in a loose circuit that shifts with the seasons. Insects breed in the pools and their larvae feed the fish — yes, there are fish, small, pale, blind, living in the permanent pools fed by underground seepage that the old channels connect. The raptors feed on the rodents and the lizards. The shrubs drop leaves that break down slowly in the dry heat, feeding thin soil. Everything is connected at a distance, with long intervals between one thing and the next.

The weirdness is quiet. It lives in the way sound behaves, in the condensation that shouldn't be, in the structures that seem to contain more space than they occupy, in the fish that should not have evolved in pools this far above sea level and this far from any continuous water system. It lives in the way the light, already fierce, seems to intensify near the structures — not brighter, exactly, but *thicker*, more present, as though photons were accumulating in the space around them like pollen around a flower.

You could spend a week on the Kora Plateau and think it was merely a high, bright, dry, ancient country of pale stone and quiet fauna and strange ruins. You could spend a month and begin to notice the patterns. You could spend a year and start to understand that the place is doing something — slowly, patiently, in the timescales of geology and weather — and that the structures are not remnants of something that was, but components of something that is.

The plateau is working. It has always been working. The sound that vanishes goes somewhere. The water that condenses on stone that should not be wet feeds something that has not yet surfaced. The structures are not dead. They are patient.

The silence is not absence. It is accumulation.
