# The Dark Thermals

The plateau has no name because no one who has seen it has ever returned with language adequate to the task. It is not that the place resists description — it is that the scale of it exceeds the architecture of a single mind. Think of a landscape the size of a subcontinent, lifted high above whatever sea level means on this world, and then think of it in absolute darkness. Not the darkness of night, which implies a sun that will return. This is the permanent, structural darkness of a sky so choked with mineral-laden cloud that light has never, in any measurable epoch, reached the surface.

The warmth comes from below.



This is the essential fact of the place. Beneath the weathered granite and the old, old basalt, something still runs hot. The planet's crust is thin here — or perhaps the mantle is eager — and geothermal gradients bend upward like the roots of some enormous tree seeking a sun it will never touch. The rock underfoot is warm. Not scalding, not volcanic in any dramatic sense, but *warm*, the way a sleeping animal is warm. You feel it through your boots, through your bones, if you stand still long enough. The heat rises in slow, even pulses, a respiration with a period measured in centuries.

The terrain is ancient and has made its peace with itself.

Millions of years of chemical weathering — because there is no frost here to pry at cracks, only warm acid rain and the patient work of mineral acids leaching upward from below — have sculpted the highland into forms that feel almost anatomical. The ridgelines are not jagged. They are *worn smooth*, rounded at their crests like the exposed vertebrae of something long dead and turned to stone, then turned again to something softer by the unceasing touch of warm mist. The valleys between them are broad and shallow, floored with deposits of calcium sulfate and iron oxide that give the ground a pale, rusty quality — you would not see the color in this dark, but your hands would tell you. Everything here is tinted the color of old blood and chalk.

The scale means weather is generated locally, and the weather is peculiar.

Warm air rises from the geothermal ground in columns so broad they are less like updrafts and more like the slow circulation of the atmosphere's own bloodstream. This air carries moisture — not much, never much, but enough. It rises, it cools slightly (though "cool" here is relative; the upper reaches of the plateau's thin atmosphere never drop below what most worlds would call a warm afternoon), and it condenses into a low, persistent haze that never fully forms into clouds and never fully dissipates. The result is a permanent state of suspended dampness. The air tastes of minerals and warmth. Fog, of a sort, drifts at ankle height and waist height and overhead — different layers, moving at different speeds, some rising, some falling, because the thermal gradients are complex and the air has learned to move in patterns that no weather model on any world would predict.

Intermittently, the moisture concentrates. In the broad basins, where the warm air pools and the chemical load is heaviest, droplets form on every surface — on rock, on the sparse vegetation, on the chitin and carapace of the fauna. These are not gentle dews. They are heavy, oily condensates, rich in dissolved minerals, and they accumulate in shallow pans and depressions in the stone before slowly being reabsorbed or evaporated. The sound of this process — the tiny, constant *ticking* of droplets forming and falling and forming again — is one of the few persistent sounds on the plateau. It sounds like a clock made of water and metal, counting something no one is listening for.

The vegetation is sparse and looks wrong.

It grows in the basins and the sheltered flanks of the smooth ridges, wherever mineral-rich water pools or seeps. It is not green. There has never been enough light for chlorophyll to evolve as a dominant strategy. What grows here instead is chemosynthetic — pale, translucent, fleshy structures that draw energy from the chemical gradients between the warm, mineral-saturated groundwater and the slightly cooler air. They look like coral, some of them. Others look like the fans of some deep-sea organism misplaced on a mountaintop. A few species have developed rudimentary bioluminescence — not as a light source for themselves, but as a byproduct of their metabolism, a faint and eerie phosphorescence that gives certain basins a ghostly, blue-green glow. This is the only light on the plateau that is not thermal, and it is just enough to see shapes by. Just enough to know that the ground is not flat. Just enough to realize you are not alone.

The fauna is what makes the place unbearable and magnificent.



In a world without light, without vegetation of any real abundance, in a landscape of sparse resources and vast emptiness, you would not expect to find an ecosystem teeming with complex life. And yet.

The creatures here are numerous. They are also, by the standards of any organic world, profoundly strange — not in their strangeness, but in their *efficiency*. They have evolved, or been shaped by evolution, into something that functions with an industrial precision. Their metabolisms run on the same chemical gradients that power the vegetation: hydrogen sulfide, iron compounds, methane seeping up from the warm crust below. But where the vegetation passively absorbs, the fauna *processes*. They burn these fuels internally, in specialized organs that function like engines — chambers of controlled reaction, with intake, combustion, exhaust. Some of the larger species are warm to the touch, radiating heat from the efficiency losses of their own metabolic furnaces. Their movements are deliberate, unhurried, *mechanical* in the truest sense: the movements of something that has calculated the energy cost of every motion and chosen exactly the amount of effort required and not one erg more.

The smallest fauna are arthropod-like, ranging from the size of a thumbnail to the span of a human hand. They are the decomposers and the mineral-processors, crawling in slow, purposeful columns across the rock, breaking down chemical deposits and redistributing nutrients with a thoroughness that borders on the obsessive. They are not quiet. Their passage over stone produces a faint, rhythmic clicking — millions of legs on mineral surfaces — that merges into a texture of sound so low and constant it stops registering as noise and starts registering as silence. This is why the plateau seems mostly quiet. The sound is there. It is the baseline. Everything else is measured against it.

Above them are the grazers. Large, slow-moving creatures — the size of cattle, some of them — that feed on the chemosynthetic vegetation and on the mineral deposits themselves, grinding stone in vast molar plates to extract trace elements. They move in herds, but not the panicked herds of predator-haunted prey. These herds move with the patience of geological processes, following routes that have been worn into the rock by thousands of generations of identical footsteps. The paths they leave are not trails in any traditional sense. They are *channels*, grooves worn smooth, and in the perpetual dampness they fill with condensation water that catches the faint bioluminescent glow and makes the landscape look, from a distance, like a circuit board — pale lines of light tracing connections between nodes of soft blue fire.

And above them, at the top of the food chain, are the predators.

They are few. They are large. The largest species on the plateau is something like a slow, six-limbed catamount, but built with the same industrial efficiency as everything else. Its body is a heat engine. It generates a localized electromagnetic field — not strong enough to be called a weapon, but strong enough to disrupt the rudimentary electroreception that the grazers use to navigate in the dark. It hunts by *jamming signals*. The prey goes still, confused, and the predator closes the distance with a mechanical economy of motion that is, in the darkness, almost invisible. It does not roar. It does not growl. The only sound it makes is the low hum of its own metabolic processes, a frequency just below the threshold of hearing, felt in the chest rather than the ears.

The interactions between these trophic levels are what give the plateau its quality of *activity without urgency*. Predation happens, but slowly. The grazers reproduce at a pace calibrated to the rate at which the vegetation grows, which is calibrated to the rate at which minerals cycle through the geothermal system, which is calibrated to the slow pulse of heat from below. Everything is in balance. Everything has been in balance for a very long time. The erosion patterns confirm this — the smooth valleys, the rounded ridges, the worn channels — these are the signatures of a system in equilibrium, processing itself, turning the same materials over and over in a loop that has no beginning and no end and no apparent purpose other than its own continuation.

The weirdness is subtle and cumulative.

It is not any single thing. It is the accumulation of observations that do not quite fit a purely natural explanation, but also do not fit any *constructed* explanation either. The electromagnetic fields of the predators are one such observation. Another is the regularity of the herd paths — their spacing is almost too even, almost too purposeful, as if the herds are following a plan that no individual animal possesses. A third is the bioluminescence: its spectral signature is unnervingly precise, the same wavelength emitted by every species that produces it, as if the trait was not independently evolved but *installed*. A fourth is the silence — not the absence of sound, but the particular *quality* of it, the way the ambient noise of the ecosystem arranges itself into something that almost, at the edge of perception, sounds like information.

None of this adds up to anything definitive. The plateau does not explain itself. It simply persists, warm and dark and vast and ancient, processing its own substance in an endless loop, populated by creatures that may or may not be what they appear to be, in a landscape that may or may not have been shaped by something that has long since gone quiet, leaving only the machinery running.

The dampness continues its slow ticking. The condensation pools in the worn basins. The herds move through the channels of faint light, grinding stone to nourishment. The predators hum in the dark. The geothermal pulse rises and falls in its centuries-long breath. And somewhere in the mineral haze, in the space between the fog layers, something that is not quite wind and not quite sound moves across the plateau like a thought that cannot quite complete itself.

The place does not need you to understand it. It has been running for longer than your species has existed, and it will continue long after you are gone, and the darkness will remain absolute, and the warmth will continue to rise, and the fauna will continue its precise and purposeless industry, and the condensation will tick, and the faint light will glow in the basins where the chemosynthetic fans unfurl their pale, translucent bodies toward a warmth that comes from below, not above — always from below — and this will be enough.
