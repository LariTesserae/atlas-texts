# The Col of Shattered Pillars

The air is thin enough to taste its absence — not empty, exactly, but reduced, as though the world has been strained through gauze. What remains is sharp and bright beyond endurance. Sunlight here doesn't filter or soften. It strikes. Every surface is a mirror or a blade, and the eye has no shade to retreat into, no canopy, no shadow deeper than a fracture in the rock. The brightness comes from above and from below: from the white-grey sky pressing close to the peaks, and from the mineral surfaces themselves — feldspar, quartz, mica — throwing light back in directions that make it impossible to feel certain where the sun actually sits.

The place is a col, a saddle between two sharp ridgelines that rise like broken teeth on either side, their flanks streaked with snow that never fully melts and never quite accumulates. The floor of the col is small — perhaps two hundred paces across at its widest — and it is *dense* with stone. Not the sparse scree of a mountain pass but a packed and cluttered field of formations: columnar basalt standing in tight clusters like the pipes of a dead organ, their hexagonal faces weathered to a pale grey-brown and slick with condensation. Between them, shattered slabs of granite lie at every angle, stacked and wedged by millennia of freeze-thaw into configurations that suggest intention without delivering it. Everything is near-touching. There is no walking here that does not involve placing a hand on rock.

The dampness is intermittent and particular. It comes as condensation — always — never as rain. The air is too cold and too thin for proper rain. Instead, moisture condenses directly onto the colder surfaces in a fine, granular frost that forms in the hours around dawn and persists into midday on shaded faces before sublimating back into the air with a faint, barely audible hiss. In sheltered hollows between the basalt columns, small pools of meltwater gather and persist, their surfaces dusted with needle-thin ice crystals even in the brightest hours. The water is extraordinarily clear. Looking into these pools is like looking into lenses — the rock beneath them is magnified and distorted, and the refraction plays tricks with depth. Some of these pools appear to be a hand's breadth deep. They are far deeper.

The temperature is constant in its severity. There is no warmth here, no seasonal relief, no hour of the day that could be called comfortable. The cold is not dramatic — it is not the howling, knife-edged cold of a storm — but it is total and unyielding, the kind that seeps into stone itself. The rock is cold to the touch in a way that feels less like temperature and more like *absence*, as though the mineral is drawing something out of the hand that touches it. Frost-weathering is the col's only sculptor and its most patient one. Every surface is being slowly disassembled — cracks widened grain by grain, edges rounded by the slow expansion of ice in pore and fissure. The erosion is ancient and settled. This is not a landscape in crisis. It has been weathering like this for longer than any single process could be meaningfully measured, and it has reached a kind of equilibrium: the shattered pillars are breaking down, yes, but new surfaces are always being exposed, and the geometry of the collapse has found its own stable angles. It looks ruined and permanent at the same time.

And then there are the strange geometries.

It is difficult to be certain what is natural and what is not. Mineral deposits — iron-rich, perhaps, or manganese — have formed across some of the basalt faces in patterns that are difficult to dismiss as random: concentric rings, branching lines that fork at consistent angles, spirals that tighten toward a center with mathematical precision. They are not organic. They are not alive. They are mineral precipitates, the result of water percolating through specific grain structures and depositing oxides in ways that follow the logic of crystallization. But the logic is *eager*. It produces forms that hover at the threshold of recognition — shapes that suggest, without ever resolving into, faces, hands, branching vessels, eyes. Standing among the pillars, it is hard not to feel that the stone is *organizing* itself, not with purpose but with a density of pattern that produces the same unease as purpose.

This is the "unusual" thing about the col: not that anything here is impossible, but that the mineral world, given enough time and the right chemistry, has begun to *echo* the language of life without ever learning to speak it.

There are other echoes. On a flat-topped boulder near the col's center — the largest unbroken surface in the area — someone once built a cairn. It still stands, barely. Seven stones, each roughly fist-sized, stacked in a column that has been knocked and rebuilt so many times that the contact surfaces are worn smooth and concave. The stones are different from the surrounding basalt: they are river-worn, rounded, transported from somewhere lower and wetter. Someone climbed to this place — this small, bright, cold, uninhabitable place — and carried seven rounded stones with them, and stacked them, and left. Around the cairn's base, smaller stones have been arranged in a shallow ring, partially buried by frost-shoved gravel. Faint marks on the flat boulder's surface — scratches, deliberate and parallel — suggest the use of a hard tool, a stone point perhaps, though no point has been found. The marks are arranged in groups. They might be counting. They might be nothing.

The technology of whoever passed through here was simple and left almost no trace. A few flakes of worked stone — chert, not native to this elevation — can be found in the crevices between basalt columns, carried by wind or by the hands that dropped them. They are sharp. They were meant to be.

There are no animals here. There have never been animals here — not in any meaningful sense, not at this scale and temperature and altitude. The density of the place is geological: crystals packed into matrix, minerals layered in veins, air compressed into frost, water sealed in pores. Everything that is *alive* about this landscape is a process, not a creature. Ice grows. Minerals precipitate. Rock splits along its grain. Water sublimates. The col is active in the way that chemistry is active — ceaselessly, silently, without agenda.

The quiet is the thing you notice after the brightness. It is not silence. There is the sublimation hiss, faint and periodic. There is the rare, sharp *tick* of a frost-crack propagating through a slab — a sound like a knuckle popping, startlingly loud in the stillness and then gone. Wind moves through the col in thin, irregular gusts that produce no howl — the air is too sparse for howling — but instead a high, glassy whisper as it moves between the columnar joints. Occasionally, if the wind shifts just right, one of the narrower gaps between pillars produces a single resonant tone, almost musical, that lasts for a few seconds and fades. These sounds are geological. They are the col breathing in its slow, mineral way.

The light changes the col's character hour by hour, though "hour" means something different here — the days are short or the light is constant, depending on the season, depending on the latitude that produced this place. At its brightest, the col is almost painful to inhabit: white light on white frost on grey-white stone, the sky bleached to a pale, directionless glow. At its dimmest — the brief, deep-blue twilight that precedes what might be night — the iron-oxide patterns on the basalt faces seem to darken and intensify, and the concentric rings on the rock look almost like they are *pulsing*, though this is only the eye adjusting, only the brain reaching for pattern in the failing light.

The col has edges. You can walk its full extent in a few minutes. The ridgelines on either side are climbable but steep, their crests sharp enough to straddle, their far sides dropping away into valleys you cannot see from the col floor. Whatever lies beyond these ridges — wherever the moisture comes from, wherever the river-worn stones in the cairn originated — remains unseen. The col is a small, dense, bright, cold, quiet room in the roof of the world, and it is enough. It does not need to be larger. It has filled itself completely with stone and process and the faint, persistent suggestion that the mineral world, if left alone long enough, will almost — *almost* — remember what it looks like to be alive.
