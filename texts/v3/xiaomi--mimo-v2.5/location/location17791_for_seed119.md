# The Residuum

You are standing in a bowl.

That's the first thing you understand — not from seeing its edges, which are lost in the dimness ahead and behind, but from the way water moves. Everything slopes gently, imperceptibly inward. The ground beneath your feet is not ground. It is *poured* — a dense, pale aggregate ofite calcium andite crushed shell, hand-packed in courses that have long since lost their seams. You can feel it: the slight give of old mortar, the micro-texture of thousands of small trowel-marks worn to satin by centuries of water flowing over them. Every surface you can see, every surface you can touch, was put here by human hands using nothing but stone hammers, wooden forms, and an ungodful amount of patience. There is no bedrock beneath this place. No original earth. It was all *placed*.

And then shaped again, and again, by the water that never stops coming.

## The Water

It doesn't rain here, or if it does, you can't tell. The water arrives from below and from within — seeping upward through the porous constructed floor in a thousand capillary threads, condensing in thick beads on every vertical surface, weeping from joints in the stonework with a patient, ceaseless persistence. The floor is never dry. There are no dry places. A thin film of water covers everything, no more than a fingernail's depth in most of the open expanses, deeper where the old construction has sagged or fractured into channels and basins that the original builders may or may not have intended.

The water is faintly warm — warmer than the air — and slightly turbid, carrying dissolved calcium and something else, a mineral sweetness you can taste at the back of your throat when the mist thickens. In the deepest low points, standing pools have gathered, their surfaces broken by the constant drip from above and the constant welling from below. These pools are home to the most life.

## The Scale and the Emptiness

Here is the disorienting thing: this place is *small*. Hamlet-sized. You could walk across its diameter in ten minutes. And yet it feels vast, because of what isn't there.

The density is extraordinary in its absence. There are no structures. No walls, no pillars, no objects. Just the constructed floor — the basin — stretching out in every direction, punctuated only by shallow rises and depressions, the old channels, and the occasional low mound where the construction has buckled upward like frozen clay. You can see two hundred meters in every direction and there is *nothing there*. No furniture. No tools. No sign of the people who built this. The emptiness is not abandoned-feeling; it is *architectural*. The space between presences is the point. This was built to be empty, to hold water and air and little else, and it has been doing exactly that for longer than anyone remembers.

The ceiling, if there is one, is lost in dimness above. The light comes from nowhere specific — a diffuse, grey-violet luminescence that seems to emanate from the moisture itself, as if the thick air is its own source. It is not bright. Your eyes adjust and then never fully adjust, because there is nothing to focus on. Just the pale, wet floor receding into grey.

## The Sound

And yet this emptiness is *loud*.

The water is the foundation of it — a constant, layered percussion. The drip from a raised seam sounds different from the drip from a flat surface; the seepage through a porous section has its own pitch, a burbling whisper beneath the staccato above. In the deeper basins, the water gurgles where it drains downward through fractured work, a hollow throat-sound that resonates through the construction itself. You can feel it in your feet. The entire floor is a drum, and every drop is a mallet.

Over this the fauna layers its voice.

## The Creatures

They are everywhere and they are numerous. The cool, wet, constructed world has become ideal for certain kinds of life — creatures that thrive on hard surfaces, in shallow water, in dimness. Pale amphibians, small, smooth-skinned, no larger than your thumbnail, cluster in the wet grooves of old trowel-marks by the hundreds. They are nearly translucent; you can see their hearts beating. Their calls are surprisingly loud for their size — a collective, high-pitched trill that rises and falls in waves across the basin, echoing off the constructed surfaces until direction loses meaning. You are inside the sound. It comes from the floor beneath you and the air around you and the distances ahead.

There are eels — or something like eels — in the deeper channels. Blind, white, finger-thick, moving through the water in sinuous, unhurried lines. They are not aggressive but they are not timid either; they occupy this space as if it were made for them, which, in a sense, it was. They eat the amphibians. The amphibians eat insects. The insects breed in the standing water and feed on the thin biofilm — algae, bacteria, some kind of fungal mat — that covers every submerged surface in a dark, slick skin.

There are larger things, too. In the middle distance, something shifts — a shape in the water, low and heavy, moving through one of the deeper basins with a displacement you can feel. You don't see it clearly. The dimness and the water-sheen conspire to keep it at the edge of comprehension. But the amphibians go silent in its vicinity, and then resume once it has passed.

## The Strangeness

There is something wrong with the construction.

Not wrong as in damaged — though it is damaged, weathered, eroded into new and unintended forms. Wrong as in *not quite right in its original intent*. The basin is too symmetrical to be purely functional and not symmetrical enough to be purely decorative. The channels don't follow efficient drainage paths; some of them curve back on themselves, or terminate in blind pools, or climb briefly uphill before descending again. There are patterns in the trowel-marks — repetitions, sequences — that feel deliberate, as if the builders were encoding something in the texture of the surfaces, a language written in friction and tool-stroke.

And the scale, the *smallness* of the place — it doesn't feel like it was built to contain water. It feels like it was built to contain something else, something the water replaced. The fauna, the wetness, the erosion — these are the tenants. The original tenant is gone, and what remains is a beautifully made, carefully shaped, inexplicably purposeful emptiness, slowly being smoothed into something the builders would not recognize.

The mist thickens. The trilling rises. The water wells up between your feet, warm and mineral-sweet, and the constructed floor hums beneath you with the sound of everything that lives here, doing what it does, in a room that was meant for something else.
