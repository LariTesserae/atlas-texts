# The Grown Quarters of Vash Tidal



There is a settlement here. That much is undeniable, despite what the water does to everything.

It sits at the summit of what was once a ridgeline — a high, narrow shoulder of pale limestone that now barely clears the thermocline. The water above is shallow enough that sunlight reaches the bottom in whole, undiminished columns, not the blue-filtered ghostlight of the deep. The water is cold. Not the numbing cold of ocean trenches but the patient, mineral cold of snowmelt that has never fully warmed — springwater that found a higher pool and kept rising until the mountain became an island and then not even that. This is alpine water. It tastes like the inside of a cave.

The settlement is small. The word "city" would be wrong, though it functions like one. You could walk its farthest perimeter in fifteen minutes, if walking were possible here, which it nearly is — the water at this depth and altitude is buoyant but not heavy. A person could stand, wade, drift. The structures are spaced as though each one wanted privacy from its neighbors. Thirty, perhaps forty buildings in total, scattered across a crown of rock perhaps two hundred meters across. Between them: open water. Channels. Courtyards of empty current. The emptiness is not a failure of planning but the architecture itself.

And the buildings are alive.

They were always alive. That is the principle. No one here ever cut a stone or fired a brick or stretched a timber. The founders — whoever they were, however long ago — understood cultivation the way other civilizations understood engineering. They grew their city. Every wall is a root system, trained and woven and compressed over generations into load-bearing lattices of living wood. The species is unfamiliar to anything on the surface, or perhaps it is familiar — a kind of aquatic willow, or something that convergently resembles one, with flexible heartwood and bark that calcifies in mineral-rich water until it takes on the density and grey-white color of coral bone. The outer surfaces are alive, still growing, still responsive. The inner walls are dead heartwood, hollow, insulated, sealed by a resin the plant produces naturally when a branch is bent at a specific angle and held. No tools were needed beyond patience and hands. The builders learned the angles. They taught the plants.

Over centuries — many centuries — the structures have become monumental despite their small scale. What began as cultivated thickets of trained stems have become walls two meters thick, ceilings arched by centuries of slow secondary growth, chambers shaped not by demolition but by selective encouragement. Rooms where the wood has been coaxed into spiraling helices. Doorways where two root systems were merged and then separated, leaving a perfect oval passage that widens at the shoulders like a human exhale. The architecture is organic not in the decorative, surface-level way that word is usually meant, but in the structural sense: every surface was once growing, and many still are. Mosses and filamentous algae colonize the outer skins. Root hairs drift in the interior currents like curtains of pale thread. The buildings breathe, in the botanical sense. They exchange gases with the water. They are organs.

Some of the older structures have outgrown their original purpose. A building whose interior chamber collapsed — slowly, over decades, as a root system thickened unevenly — is now a garden, its walls opened to the water, its floor carpeted in the fine-leafed undergrowth that colonizes every stable surface in this place. The distinction between building and garden is not always clear, and the residents do not seem to draw it.

---

The sound here is the first thing that overwhelms.

It is not the silence of deep water. This is shallow, sunlit water at high altitude, and it is *loud*. The settlement sits in the path of a current — not a strong one, but a persistent, undulating flow that moves through the buildings like wind through a town, and the water is never still. It hums against the root-walls. It produces a low, tonal resonance in the larger chambers — a sound like a glass harmonica played at the bottom of the ocean. The buildings are instruments. Every arch, every hollow passage, every narrow gap between root structures shapes the current into sound. The settlement sings. Not melodically. Not with intention. It simply cannot help it.

Above and around this are the voices of fauna. There are many animals here. The clear water, the stable current, the plant architecture — it all conspires to make this place a reef, an oasis in what is otherwise a sparse highland. Fish move through the settlement in schools so dense they look like weather systems — dark fronts that sweep through the spaces between buildings and disperse into individual glittering bodies in the open water beyond. They are small, most of them, no longer than a human hand, with wide transparent fins that catch the light and scatter it in shifting prismatic patterns across the root-walls. They feed on the algae that grows on every surface. They are not pets, not livestock, not pest. They are simply present, in enormous numbers, and the residents move among them the way surface dwellers move among sparrows.

Larger fauna patrol the outer perimeter. Eels — or things shaped like eels, long-bodied and sinuous, with mouths too wide for their heads and eyes adapted to the bright water, pale irises ringed in dark. They are territorial. They nest in the gaps between the outermost buildings, in the spaces where root systems fork and leave cavities large enough for a body to coil. The residents know them individually. They gesture at them the way a farmer might gesture at a boundary fence.

There are also the grazers: squat, armored things that move slowly across the rock substrate and the lower walls, scraping algae with mouthparts that produce a sound audible even through the water — a high, rhythmic clicking, like someone tapping a coin against glass. Hundreds of them, on every stable surface. Their clicking is one of the constant background sounds of the place, along with the current-singing and the fish-body whispers of water displaced.

And beneath it all, felt more than heard, the deep vibration of the mountain itself — tectonic settling, thermal expansion, the imperceptible flex of stone under water pressure. The ground here is not still. It shifts on timescales that make human life look like a flashbulb. The residents have adapted. Their structures flex. The root-systems tolerate movement. Buildings that have been here long enough have developed, naturally, the kind of internal reinforcement that absorbs shock — branching interior supports, cross-bracing roots that grew in response to centuries of micro-tremors. The city has learned to be flexible. It had to. Nothing rigid survives here.

---

The people — and there are people — are few. Perhaps eighty. Perhaps a hundred. It is hard to count a population spread across a settlement where every individual is also partially submerged at all times. They move through the water the way surface people move through air: casually, habitually, with the unconscious economy of bodies that have been shaped by a medium for generations. They are not aquatic. They breathe air, which they obtain from trapped pockets in the upper chambers of the larger buildings — bubbles of atmosphere sealed beneath arching root ceilings, where gas exchange with the plant structures maintains a breathable, if thin and humid, atmosphere. These air-pockets are the living quarters. The dry rooms. The places where food is prepared and children sleep.

But most of the settlement's activity happens in the water. The channels between buildings are streets. The open courtyards are plazas. The people wade or swim between them, carrying objects in woven root-fiber baskets — gathered plants, processed food, tools made of shaped bone and sharpened stone. The technology is simple. It has always been simple. There is no metal here; there is no ore. The limestone substrate does not yield it, and no one has ever traveled far enough to find any. Everything is bone, stone, root, and hand. Knives of knapped flint. Needles of fish bone. Cordage twisted from plant fiber and sealed with the same resin the buildings produce. It is enough. It has always been enough. The plant architecture provides shelter. The fauna provides protein. The water provides everything else.

The residents are organized but not hierarchical. There are no palaces here, no monumental structures that dwarf the others. The largest building — the one the residents call, through gestures and shared reference rather than anything so formal as a name, the *Old Growth* — is larger only because its root system is older. It is perhaps ten meters across and six tall, its interior a single vast chamber with a ceiling of interlocking roots and a floor of bare stone polished smooth by generations of feet. It serves as meeting place, storage, and the site where the air-pocket is largest and most stable. The people gather there. Decisions are made there, but by what process is unclear to an outsider. There is no obvious leader. There are elders, certainly — older bodies marked by the particular economy of movement that water demands and age resists — but they do not command. They advise, perhaps. They remember, definitely. Memory is the closest thing to authority here.

---

The place is old. Not ancient in the dramatic sense — not ruins, not relics. Old in the way a forest is old, in the way a river is old. It has been here long enough that the oldest buildings and the oldest trees and the oldest rocks are difficult to distinguish from one another. The root-walls have mineralized at their bases, merging with the limestone substrate. Some of the outermost structures have been partially reclaimed by the rock itself — encrusted, overgrown, slowly being absorbed back into the mountain. New structures grow at the edges, young and flexible, their bark still green, their roots still reaching. The settlement breathes on a timescale of centuries: expanding, contracting, losing buildings to the slow violence of root-thickening and gaining new ones from cultivated shoots. The dynamic is slow. A building takes decades to reach maturity. A collapse takes years to complete. The residents plan on timescales that assume their grandchildren's grandchildren will see the results.

There is a calm to it that might be mistaken for stagnation. It is not. The fauna changes — species shift their ranges, the eels come and go in multigenerational cycles, the schools of small fish pulse in numbers that follow patterns no one has charted because no one here charts anything. The water level rises and falls imperceptibly, on cycles so long they are experienced as weather rather than history. The light changes with the seasons above — brighter in summer, dimmer in winter, the entire settlement shifting between silver and gold as the angle of the sun moves across the mountain's surface.

It is, by any measure, an unusual place. Not because any single element is impossible — plant architecture, underwater settlement, primitive technology — but because all of these things exist together, in a place that works. That functions. That has functioned for longer than anyone can remember, through no particular genius or design, but through the slow, patient accumulation of small decisions, small cultivations, small adjustments, repeated across enough generations that the result looks like inevitability.

It is a small, loud, cold, bright, living place. The water holds it all.
