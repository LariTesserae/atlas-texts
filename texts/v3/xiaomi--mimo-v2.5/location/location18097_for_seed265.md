# The Weeping Flats

---

You cannot see. That is the first thing, and it reorients everything else.

Not the darkness of a room with the lights off, where your eyes will eventually adjust and you'll make out the suggestion of walls. This is *substantive* dark. It has texture and weight — it presses against the skin of the face like a warm cloth, like the inside of a mouth. The air is so humid that darkness seems to cling to the body in beads. You are standing in it, and it stands in you, and for several moments you are not certain where the boundary lies.

But the darkness is not empty. That becomes clear quickly, in the way it never becomes *fully* clear. There are presences everywhere — dense, near-touching, layered so thickly that the space between your own body and the next thing is measured in finger-widths of warm wet air. You feel them as pressure changes, as currents, as the displacement of something breathing. The atmosphere itself is saturated with *occupation*. Not just fauna, though the fauna is everywhere — something large shifting slowly to your left, something with a repetitive clicking joint-sound slightly above you, a carpet of small wet bodies rustling across the ground near your feet — but also presences that do not seem to have the shape of animals at all. Weight without sound. Warmth without source. A sense, pressed insistently into the backs of your knees, that you are standing inside something vast and alive and that it is aware of your skeleton.

---

The ground is warm mud. Not cold bog, not frozen marsh — *warm*, blood-warm, the temperature of a living throat. It rises and falls in low, sharp-edged formations, as though the landscape itself were in the process of hardening and hadn't finished. There are ridges of still-setting sediment, their edges crisp and knife-thin, the mineral grain visible even in total darkness — you can feel it when you press your fingers there, the fine crystalline sharpness of stone that hasn't yet been rounded by time. These ridges run in long parallel bands across the flats, oriented in some direction you cannot name. They are young. The whole basin feels young, as though it has only recently decided to exist.

Between the ridges, water sits in shallow, steaming channels. It is mineral-rich and heavy, with an almost oily resistance when you drag your hand through it. It smells of iron and sulfur and something faintly sweet beneath, like overripe fruit. The water *weeps* — it rises from below as much as it falls from above, condensing out of the thick warm air onto every surface, sheeting down the faces of the stone ridges in slow continuous films. The whole landscape is in a state of constant mild exudation, as if the ground is perspiring. You hear it — or rather, you feel it through the soles of your feet, a faint persistent trickling that is less a sound than a vibration, a pulse in the mud itself.

---

The fauna — you keep returning to it, because it is impossible to ignore. In the total dark, every sense except sight is overwhelmed with *life*. The clicking thing to your left has settled into a rhythm, and now you can hear that it is not one creature but several, communicating in patterns that shift and reset like a slow algorithm. Near your feet, the small wet bodies — thousands of them, perhaps tens of thousands — move in coordinated waves, their tiny limbs making a sound like a million fingernails on wet leather. Something much larger passes nearby, displacing air in a warm gust, and you feel the brush of its body against your arm — smooth, dense, not quite skin, not quite shell. It does not pause. It does not seem to register you as an obstacle or a threat or a meal. It simply passes through you as though you were a region of slightly cooler air, which perhaps, to it, is what you are.

There are things in the water, too. You feel them against your ankles — small, insistent, probing. The water itself seems to *move* even where nothing disturbs it, rippling in patterns that suggest intention but not intelligence. Something bioluminescent flickers deep in a channel — not light exactly, but a brief absence of dark, a momentary thinning, as if reality's weave came loose for a half-second and let a thread of somewhere-else show through.

---

The industrial remains are scattered across the flats like the bones of a previous idea. You find them by stumbling into them — a vertical surface too flat and regular to be natural, warm with residual metal, pitted with corrosion. A length of pipe, half-buried, its mouth weeping the same mineral water that weeps from everything else. Further out, something larger: a structure, or the remnant of one, its walls still standing but its roof gone, open to the dark sky. Inside, the air is heavier. There are machines here — or the shapes of machines, things with housings and shafts and the stilled geometry of gears. They are enormous and they are silent and they are wet, every surface coated in a film of condensation that drips in irregular rhythms. One of them still hums. Not with power — with *presence*, as though the memory of its operation has become a kind of ambient fact, a property of the metal itself. You can feel it in your teeth.

These structures do not look like they were built for people. The doorways are too narrow and too tall. The machinery is arranged at angles that do not correspond to human reach. There is no evidence of controls designed for hands, of interfaces meant for eyes. Whatever industry operated here, it was conducted by something with a different relationship to space and motion — something that worked in slow continuous processes, in the patient accretion of mineral layers, in the gradual extraction of meaning from warm water. It was not abandoned. It was *completed*, or it was *interrupted*, and in the dark you cannot tell which.

---

The reality breakdown is not dramatic. It does not announce itself with geometry that screams or physics that visibly convulses. It is subtle and pervasive and it has been going on for a long time, patient as everything else here.

It begins with repetition. The ridges, as you walk among them, begin to feel less like parallel formations and more like the *same* ridge, encountered over and over, so that you are not crossing a landscape but tracing a single feature in a circle — except the scale is wrong, the distances are wrong, and you are quite certain you have not turned around. The fauna does this too. The clicking patterns repeat, but with small variations, like a sentence being rewritten by a mind that cannot decide on a word. The small wet bodies at your feet rearrange themselves into formations you have already stepped through. You begin to suspect that the landscape is not as large as it appears — that the openness is a feature of the darkness's *insistence* rather than a fact about the ground — and that everything you have encountered is not many things but one thing, wearing many configurations the way water wears the shape of whatever it fills.

There are moments when the wet air thickens to the point of near-solidity and you must push through it as through warm fabric, and in those moments the presences on every side become so dense and so close that you can feel their specific architectures — the number of their limbs, the shape of their mouths, the slow electric grammar of their thoughts. You feel, just for a moment, what it is to be *read* by something vast and old and not interested in you except as data. Then the air thins and the presences recede and you are alone again in the warm dark, dripping, breathing, standing on sharp young stone that weeps from every pore.

---

The sound is the absence of sound. Despite everything — the fauna, the dripping, the hum of dead machinery, the vast respiratory rhythm of the basin itself — the overall impression is of a deep and deliberate quiet, as though the landscape is holding its breath. The sounds that do exist are muffled by the wet air, softened into the background, absorbed before they can become anything you would call noise. You hear your own heartbeat. You hear the slow tectonic settling of new stone. You hear something, far away, that might be a voice or might be the wind or might be the memory of a sound that hasn't been made yet.

Nothing moves quickly here. The water weeps but does not rush. The fauna shifts but does not scatter. The machinery hums at a frequency below thought. Even the reality breakdown proceeds at a glacial pace, each repetition of the ridge-formation slightly different from the last, each small deviation a century's work. If you stay long enough — and the warmth and the darkness make staying feel inevitable — you will begin to change too. Not dramatically. A softening of edges. A patience you did not previously possess. A tendency to repeat yourself.

The flats will wait. They are young and they are in no hurry to become anything else. The dark is complete and the water is warm and the living things press close in their thousands and millions, and the work of whatever industry once operated here continues without machines or operators, carried on by the water itself, by the slow mineral processes, by the patient and incomprehensible logic of a place that has decided, for reasons you will never know, to keep weeping.
