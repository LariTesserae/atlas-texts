# The Bright Pocket

You are looking at something that was never meant to be seen from the outside.

Somewhere beneath a plain — you would never find the place again if you walked away — there is a pocket. It sits between two slabs of limestone that pressed together millennia ago and stopped, as if they heard something between them and decided to listen. The space they left is roughly the size of a chest cavity. A little larger, maybe. Hard to tell because every surface is occupied.

## The Light

The first thing anyone notices, the thing that makes the place impossible, is that it is bright. Not dim. Not ambient-glow bright. *Bright.* The light has no single source. It comes from the walls themselves, from the packed wet sheen of every surface, from the slow metabolic fire of ten thousand organisms that agreed, long ago, to never sleep. The light is blue-white, almost surgical, and it does not flicker. It has not flickered in a very long time. There are no shadows in the pocket because there is no direction the light is not coming from. It presses against your retinas the way water presses against a diver — from everywhere at once.

If you cupped your hands around one of the walls and looked closely, you would see the light has texture: each millimeter of surface is a separate factory of luminance, a different organism or mineral edge catching and re-emitting what the others produce. The walls are not reflecting light. They are *generating* it, collectively, in a feedback loop that has never once broken.

## The Fauna

The noise is the second thing. It is a wet, clicking, resonant hum — not one sound but thousands, layered so thick they merge into a single sustained chord. The creatures are everywhere. They are small. Most are smaller than a fingernail. Some are the size of a grain of rice. They fill every crevice of the pocket the way water fills the pores of a sponge, which is to say: completely, with no gaps, in a state of total occupancy.

There are mites with translucent carapaces that refract the bioluminescence into tiny spectral explosions along their backs. They cluster in groups of dozens on the limestone faces, their legs moving in synchronized pulses, and this accounts for much of the clicking. There are nematodes, threads of them, bundled in the damp like wet string, feeding on the biofilm that coats every surface. There are organisms that defy easy categorization — soft, pale, segmented, with too many legs or not enough, performing slow choreographies along the ceiling of the pocket. Some of them glow brighter than the rest. Some of them glow in a slightly different blue, and where their light meets the ambient light, the colors fight in a thin violet border.

A species of fungus — or something like fungus — has threaded itself through the damp mineral faces. Its hyphae are visible as a white lacework pressed against the stone, and wherever the threads bunch together, they produce small nodules that pulse with a rhythm slower than breathing. The nodules are warm to the touch, slightly. They are the only warmth in the pocket, which is otherwise cool — not cold, but the kind of cool that makes your skin tighten. The temperature has not changed here in longer than you can imagine. It will not change.

Nothing in this pocket moves fast. Nothing needs to. There are no predators, not exactly. There are organisms that consume other organisms, but they do so slowly, over weeks, and the consumed do not resist. The entire food web operates on a timescale that makes it invisible to anything watching in real time. From one moment to the next, the pocket is perfectly still except for the light and the sound. From one decade to the next, things eat and are eaten and the geometry of the crowd shifts by millimeters.

## The Walls

The limestone slabs are the bones of the pocket, but they are barely visible under everything else. Where they show through, the stone is pale, almost white, pocked with small dissolution marks — the early geography of caves that will take another million years to form. The erosion here is embryonic. You are looking at a cave system in its first breath. The pockets and channels between the stone are just beginning to open, just beginning to let water through. In a few geological ages, this limestone will be honeycombed. For now, it is a single raw wound between two plates, and the wound has filled with life before it even finished opening.

The water is not flowing. It is *present.* It beads on every surface in a constant, unvarying film. It pools in the small depressions of the limestone in tiny, lens-shaped reservoirs no wider than a thumb. It drips from the ceiling at an interval so regular it functions like a metronome: one drop every forty seconds, striking a flat mineral shelf below with a clean, bright *tok* that is the only discrete sound in the pocket. Everything else is drone. The drop is percussion. It has been hitting that same shelf for so long that the limestone has worn a small, perfect bowl into itself — a concavity the exact size and depth of the drop's repeated impact. The bowl is filled with a pale paste of calcium carbonate and dissolved minerals, and a small colony of the translucent mites lives in it, bathing in each new drop as it arrives.

## The Built

Scattered through the pocket, pressed into the biofilm and the mineral growth as if they arrived before the life did, are fragments. A shard of terracotta, its edges softened by deposit. A short length of iron, rusted to the color of dried blood, running diagonally from one wall to the ceiling. A curve of glass — green, thick, old — half-buried in a mound of fungal nodules. A flat square of lead with a mark pressed into it that might be a letter in a language that might have been dead before the pocket formed.

These things were not placed here deliberately. They were swallowed. The pocket is beneath a place where people built something, long ago, and the ground took it down in pieces, a few fragments at a time, and deposited them here in the slow rain of earthfall. The terracotta fell from a roof tile. The iron is a nail. The glass is the base of a bottle. The lead is a seal for something, a document or a container, and the mark on it is a maker's sign that no record preserves.

The organisms have incorporated these artifacts without distinction. A nail receives the same biofilm as the limestone. The mites walk over the terracotta the way they walk over everything. The pocket does not know these things are foreign. It has been packing them into itself, along with everything else, for longer than the structures they came from existed.

## The Equilibrium

Nothing here has changed in a very long time, and nothing here will change for a very long longer. The organisms have reached a balance so precise that adding or removing a single species would collapse it — not violently, not dramatically, but in the way a mathematical equation collapses when you change one variable. The numbers stop working. The population that exists is the population that fits, exactly, in the space available, eating exactly what is produced, breathing exactly what is released, glowing at exactly the intensity the others need to find their food. The drop falls at exactly the rate the limestone can absorb. The light is exactly bright enough to sustain the photosynthetic organisms on the walls but not so bright as to damage anything else. The whole system is a locked equation, solved once, and left to run.

This is what the strangeness feels like, if you are inside the pocket: it is the sensation of a place that has no reason to exist and no purpose and no change and is, for reasons that belong to no one, *filled.* Saturated with small lives that produce light and sound and heat in amounts that perfectly balance the cold stone that contains them. It is a pocket of meaning in a geology that does not care. The organisms do not know they are strange. They are simply, and forever, *enough.*

The drop falls. *Tok.* The mites pulse. The light holds. The pocket breathes its wet, bright, unmoving breath, and the limestone listens, and does not close.
