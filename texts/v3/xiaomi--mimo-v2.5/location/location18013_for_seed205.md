# The Table

---

From a distance — if you could see a distance, which here you can — the plateau looks like nothing. A flat line dividing sky from sky, heat from heat, one shimmer from another. You could mistake it for the edge of the world. Perhaps it is one.

The Table is vast. It stretches in every direction farther than a person could walk in a season, and it is high — not the thin-air height of mountain peaks, but a raised, ancient shelf, as though the planet's crust buckled upward here a billion years ago and never settled back. The sky above it is wide and pale. The sun is ordinary — a clean, white, unsparing star — but the heat it delivers to this elevation is punishing. The air itself feels alive with warmth, almost viscous, carrying scent and moisture and the low seismic hum of a landscape that is not geological but biological.

Because the ground is not ground.

---

## The Living Substrate

Walk here and your feet sink — not into soil but into tissue. The surface of the Table is a mat of living plant-matter, meters deep in most places, interwoven so densely over so many millennia that it has become something between peat and bone. In the low, flat stretches between the great formations, the mat is soft: a spongy, warm, fibrous surface that yields underfoot with a faint sound, like stepping on a living thing — because it is one. Rootlets squirm beneath your weight. Fungal filaments thread through the upper layers, white and glistening, breaking down shed material and recycling it in a closed loop that has never known interruption.

In other places the mat has compacted into something harder. Heartwood-analogues: dense, petrified trunks of ancient growth, laid horizontal and compressed, forming ridges and shelves that jut above the living surface. They are warm to the touch, even in shade. They creak faintly — not from wind, but from internal growth pressure, from the slow, inexorable expansion of living tissue trying to become something larger.

The mat breathes. Stand still and press your palm flat against it and you can feel it — a faint rhythmic pulse, not quite a heartbeat, not quite respiration, but something between the two. The surface temperature is higher than the air. In the early hours before dawn, when everything else has cooled, the mat still radiates warmth, and the air immediately above it shimmers.

---

## The Columns

Rising from the mat at irregular intervals — scattered like stones thrown across a table — are the great structural growths. They are not trees. They have no trunks, no branches, no leaves as any forest would recognize them. They are *columns*: massive, cylindrical, some no taller than a person, others rising two or three hundred meters into the pale sky, their surfaces ridged and grooved and layered with decades of accumulated growth.

Each column is a single organism — or perhaps a colony, the boundaries between individuals lost in the tangle of their root systems below. Their surfaces are covered in photosynthetic tissue: not green, or not only green, but a mottled patchwork of ochre, rust, pale yellow, and deep viridian, folded and ruffled into intricate ridgelines that maximize their surface area. They look, from a distance, like enormous candles that have burned and dripped and re-formed, or like termite mounds scaled up to the size of buildings. Up close, their surfaces are wet. A constant film of moisture beads on the photosynthetic ridges — exhaled by the organism, drawn up from deep below, held in place by surface tension and the micro-texture of the tissue. This film is what feeds the insects.

The column bases flare outward where they meet the mat, fusing with it. The boundary between column and ground is not a line but a gradient — the tissue thickening, densifying, becoming architectural. Around the base of a large column, the root network radiates outward for a hundred meters or more, its ridges visible on the surface like the raised veins on the back of a hand.

At the top, some columns flare into broad, cap-like structures — parasol canopies that shade the area below. Others remain blunt-tipped, their apex a crater-like depression that collects condensation and rainwater in a shallow pool. These pools are the Table's most precious feature.

---

## Water

Water is scarce here in the sense that rivers are scarce, that lakes are scarce, that any large standing body of water does not exist. The moisture budget of the Table is maintained by a different system entirely.

The root networks of the great columns plunge deep — far deeper than the living mat, down through layers of compacted and fossilized plant-matter, through substrate that may be kilometers old, reaching toward aquifers or water-bearing rock far below. They draw this water upward through their vascular systems and release it, gradually, through their surfaces. The columns exhale moisture. In the cool hours before dawn, the exhalation condenses on every surface — a fine, universal dew that coats the mat, the ridges, the crater-pools, the shed husks of insects, the walls of human shelters. It lasts for hours after sunrise. By midday it is gone, burned off, and the columns resume their slow, patient transpiration into air too hot to hold it.

Occasionally — unpredictably, on a cycle the people here have learned to read but not to predict — atmospheric conditions align and rain falls. It comes in intense, brief bursts: hammering down from the thermally-generated cloud banks that build over the Table each afternoon, driven sideways by hot wind, striking the mat with a sound like applause. The mat drinks it instantly. There is almost no runoff. The crater-pools overflow briefly, then settle. The columns flush darker with hydration, and for a day or two afterward their exhalation doubles, the dew runs thicker, and the insect swarms erupt into feeding frenzies.

In the driest seasons — and there are seasons, marked not by temperature but by the interval between rains — the crater-pools shrink to sticky residues, and the only free water is the dawn dew. The people go to the columns then, pressing cloth to the wet surfaces, wringing out what they can.

---

## The Sound

It is deafening. There is no other word.

Around every major column, a sound like a waterfall, like a factory, like a sustained chord played on an organ made of insects. It is the fauna — the overwhelming, concentrated, teeming fauna — and it is everywhere the columns are.

The insects are the foundation. Clouds of them — not metaphorical clouds but visible, dense, pulsing masses — swarm around the column surfaces, feeding on the exhaled moisture, on the photosynthetic tissue, on each other. Their wings generate a drone that sits beneath everything, a physical pressure in the air. Some are small, gnat-like, moving in coordinated murmurations that shift and swirl against the sky. Others are large — fist-sized beetles with iridescent carapaces, mantis-analogues that perch on the ridges and vibrate their forelimbs in mating displays that produce a sharp, metallic clicking.

Vertebrate life clusters in the columns' shelter. Lizard-like creatures — broad-bodied, heat-adapted, their skins a waxy matte that resists desiccation — cling to the column surfaces in ambush postures, their throats pulsing with vocal sacs that produce deep, resonant calls. These calls overlap, layer, interfere with each other. A single column might host thirty, fifty, a hundred individuals, each calling at slightly different intervals, creating a polyrhythmic roar that never pauses, never resolves. Bird-analogues — heavy-bodied, short-winged, evolved for gliding between columns rather than sustained flight — roost in the parasol canopies and call down in cascading sequences, each bird triggering the next, a chain reaction of sound that ripples outward from column to column across kilometers.

Amphibian-analogues pool in the crater-pools and the water-cups, their calls high-pitched and trilling, almost ultrasonic. They breed explosively after rains, filling the pools until the water writhes with them.

The sound is not uniform across the Table. In the open stretches between columns — the sparse zones, where the mat stretches flat and low and featureless — the roar recedes to a distant, continuous thunder, like a highway heard from kilometers away. Walk toward a column and the sound builds. The insects reach you first: a thickening in the air, a sudden increase in wingbeats against your face and arms. Then the calls begin to resolve into individual sources. Then the volume swells until thought becomes difficult, until you feel the sound in your sternum, in the roots of your teeth.

The people here do not whisper. They do not need to shout, either. They have adapted — their vocalizations are pitched to cut through the biological noise, high and sharp and brief. Communication is gestural more than verbal. Many of them are partially deaf in one ear.

---

## The Feedback

The strangeness is this: the sound is not merely a byproduct of the ecosystem. It is a mechanism.

The columnar growths respond to vibration. Not all vibration, and not equally, but specific frequency ranges produced by the fauna stimulate specific biological responses. The deep calls of the lizard-analogues, sustained below 200 Hz, trigger thickening of the column surface — a defensive growth that hardens the tissue against feeding and makes the photosynthetic ridges more resilient. The trilling of the amphibians, pitched between 800 and 1200 Hz, stimulates the production of the exudate — a sweet, protein-rich secretion that oozes from the column ridges and feeds the insects. The insect wingbeats themselves, at a broad spectrum of frequencies, promote the growth of the aerial rootlets that descend from the column surfaces, which in turn provide attachment points for more insects.

A column surrounded by active fauna grows faster, exudes more, produces more structural complexity, lives longer. A column that falls silent — because its fauna has been displaced, or a disease has thinned the insect population — begins to deteriorate within seasons. Its tissue dries. Its ridges crack. The mat around it retracts, leaving a bare patch of compacted, dead substrate.

The people understand this. They call it — in their language, which has no written form — something that translates imperfectly as "the loudness that feeds." They do not interfere with the fauna. They do not hunt near the columns. Some settlements maintain what might be called orchestras: groups of people beating resonant objects — stretched membranes, hollowed plant-shells, taut root-cords — in patterns that imitate the fauna's frequencies, supplementing the biological sound in periods when insect populations dip. It is not worship. It is agriculture.

---

## The Patterns

From the mat's surface, the columns appear scattered at random. But they are not. The people know this because they have walked the Table for generations, and because the distances between columns follow patterns that recur too precisely to be coincidence.

The columns are arranged in spirals — logarithmic, expanding outward from certain focal points on the Table. From the air, if you could gain that vantage, you would see spirals within spirals, nested and overlapping, their arms defined by the spacing of the columns. The spirals are not perfect. Some arms are broken, columns missing — dead or yet to grow. But the geometry is unmistakable.

The focal points — the centers of the spirals — are locations where the mat is thickest, where the root systems plunge deepest, where the air smells of deep earth and something older than earth. The people avoid them. Not out of superstition — or not only out of superstition — but because the mat there is unstable. The surface undulates visibly, rising and falling in slow waves. The root-mass below is so dense and so active that the ground is less ground than a surface over a churning depth. Occasionally, a section of mat collapses inward, opening a pit that reveals darkness below and the sound of moving water, far down, and a rush of warm, mineral-scented air.

What the roots are reaching for, at the bottom of these pits, no one has found. No one has gone deep enough to find out, or gone deep enough and returned.

## The People

Few. Scattered. A sparse population distributed across a vast living surface, occupying sheltered positions at the bases of columns — always the larger, older columns, the ones with flared parasol canopies and well-established fauna colonies.

Their shelters are woven from shed plant-material: the columns periodically slough outer layers of tissue, and this material — fibrous, tough, waterproof when dried — is harvested, soaked, beaten, and woven into walls, roofs, floor-mats. The structures are small, low, designed to hold heat at night and shed it during the day. They smell of the living plant: green, faintly sweet, resinous.

Their tools are simple. Blades shaped from the heartwood-analogues — the dense, petrified material that forms when plant-tissue compacts over centuries. These blades take an edge, of a sort. Needles of thorn — the columns produce them in profusion, rigid black spines the length of a finger, growing in dense clusters along the lower trunk. Cordage from root-tissue, braided, strong. Containers gourd-like growths that the columns produce at irregular intervals, hard-shelled and hollow, useful for carrying the dawn-dew.

They cultivate nothing in the way a farmer cultivates. They do not plant or prune or irrigate. Their agriculture is the orchestra — the maintenance of sound, the management of vibration, the careful preservation of the feedback loop that keeps the columns alive and productive. They move between columns on seasonal circuits, monitoring the fauna populations, listening for the signs of decline, supplementing the sound where needed. They are shepherds of an ecosystem that shepherds itself, given the right conditions.

They are wary of the open stretches. Not afraid, exactly, but conscious of exposure — the heat, the lack of shelter, the distance between columns. Crossing a sparse zone is a deliberate act, undertaken with preparation: water carried in gourd-containers, timing aligned with the cooler hours, a awareness of the weather patterns that build over the Table each day. Storms can catch you in the open. The rain is violent and brief and the wind that accompanies it is hot and laden with grit — fragments of dried plant-matter, shed insect carapaces, mineral dust blown up from the column-craters.

## The Atmosphere

Standing in one of the open stretches at midday, in the heat, with the columns' roar reduced to a distant, shimmering hum, the Table feels like a desert made of life. The mat underfoot is warm, almost hot, alive, breathing, growing. The air above it shimmers with heat-rising. The sky is enormous, pale, empty. There is no horizon line that does not eventually blur into the same haze — the same heat, the same pale light, the same suggestion of more Table, more living ground, more columns, more sound, extending to every limit of vision.

It is not hostile. It is indifferent. The Table does not care about the people on it any more than it cares about the insects or the lizards or the clouds. It is a system, ancient and self-sustaining, operating on a timescale that makes a human life look like a single breath. The columns grow. The fauna calls. The mat expands by millimeters per year. Water rises from below and falls from above and is absorbed and released and absorbed again. The sound never stops.

At night, the columns are dark masses against a star-filled sky. The fauna's calls shift — the diurnal species falling silent, the nocturnal ones rising. A different roar, deeper, more resonant, dominated by the amphibians and the large beetles and a creature no one has catalogued that produces a sound like a struck bell, low and sustaining, echoing off the column surfaces. The dew begins. Surfaces grow wet in the darkness. The mat's breathing slows but does not stop.

The stars above the Table are sharp and numerous and unobstructed. There is no light pollution. The only light, besides starlight, is the faint bioluminescence of certain fungal growths in the mat — a dull, greenish glow that pools in depressions and along the older root-ridges, marking paths between columns that the people follow in darkness without thinking, the way a person follows a road they have walked a thousand times.

The strangeness is not any single thing. It is the totality. It is a world where the ground is alive and the sound is food and the water rises from below and the patterns in the columns are too precise to be accidental and the focal points at the spiral centers hum with something that might be geology or might be biology or might be something that has no name yet, something that the people have learned to live alongside without understanding, the way a person lives alongside their own heartbeat — constant, necessary, too deep to examine.
