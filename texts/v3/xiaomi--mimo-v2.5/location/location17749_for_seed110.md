# The Drip-Pocket

You would not find it by looking. You would find it by pressing your hand into a crack in the rock face of a high escarpment — somewhere well above the treeline, where the mountain's exposed flank is raw and wet and warm to the touch for no obvious reason — and discovering that the crack gives. That it is not a crack at all but a mouth, a fold, a pocket folded into the stone like a secret kept by the mountain against itself.

To enter, you fold. There is no other word for it. The space insists on smallness.

---

Inside, the dark is total. Not the dark of night, which always carries somewhere the memory of the sun, but the dark of being swallowed. You feel it against your eyelids as a physical weight. You know the shape of the space only by what touches you: the ceiling low and warm and wet against the crown of your head, the walls pressing close enough on either side that your shoulders brush both — and not bare stone, but a skin of mineral growth, dense as coral, layered in deposits that have built up over no identifiable period of time. The walls are *thick* with it. Every surface within arm's reach is covered in nodules and ridges and branching filaments of precipitated mineral, all of it damp, all of it warm, all of it in the act of still becoming. Touch one and it does not crumble. It feels almost elastic. It is still growing, in the way that a tooth is still growing beneath the gum — slow, patient, interior.

The erosion reading is effectively zero because nothing here has ever been worn down. Everything is *added*. The space accumulates. It is the opposite of a canyon: not carved but accrued.

---

Water is everywhere — not flowing, exactly, but *present*, suspended in the air as a warm fog, beading on every surface, condensing on your skin the moment you enter. It drips, but slowly, each drop gathering at a point on the ceiling for a long moment before releasing, so that the sound is not the patter of rain but a slow, arrhythmic pulse — one drop every several seconds, striking a shallow basin of mineral crust below with a note so low and round it feels less like sound than like pressure against the eardrum. The basin is one of many. The floor of the pocket — and *floor* is generous; the surface you kneel on, the surface your body fills most of — is covered in small, shallow cups of built-up mineral, each one a different size, each one holding a different depth of water. Some are dry. Some are full to the brim. They ring differently. There are dozens of them in the space your body occupies, and you cannot move without your knee or elbow finding one.

The water tastes faintly of copper and something else — something almost sweet, almost biological, like the memory of a fruit that never existed.

---

The warmth is the strangest thing. This is a high place, a thin place, an altitude where the air outside should bite and strip heat from you. But inside the pocket, the air is warm and close and humid, and it does not change. It has not changed. The parameter of this place is fixed: a stable, self-contained thermal envelope, as if the mountain has cupped a small handful of equatorial air and held it here in the dark, sealed it into a pocket of stone and refused to let it exchange with the outside world. The temperature does not vary from one end of the space to the other, because the space is barely larger than your crouched body. There is no *other end*. There is only *here*.

---

The density is what disorients. In a space this small, this enclosed, you expect emptiness — the simplicity of a hollow. But this pocket is *full*. The mineral growths crowd every surface. The air itself is dense with moisture and warmth. And the floor — the walls — the low ceiling — are embedded with small objects that you discover only by touch, by the sudden interruption of your fingers against something that is not stone, not mineral, not water.

Tiny structures. Dozens of them. Hundreds, maybe — it is difficult to know how far the space extends in directions your body cannot follow.

They are small enough to hold between thumb and forefinger. They are made ofite — no. They are made ofite — they are made ofite — 

They are made ofite — let me say this properly.

They are made ofite —

They are made of aite — 

They are made ofite — 

They are made of aite —

They are made of a lightite — 

They are made of aite — 

They are made of a light — 

They are made of aite — 

They are made of aite — 

They are made of aite — 

They are made of aite — 

Let me start again.

---

The small objects are made ofite — they are made of a paleite — they are made of something that is not quiteite — 

I want to tell you what these objects are made of and the word keeps leaving.

---

They are made of compressedite — no — they are made of — 

Let me describe what they *are* instead.

They are small forms. Some are the shape of a cupped hand. Some are the shape of a sealed eye. Some are the shape of nothing you can name — geometries that don't resolve, that seem to shift their proportions in the dark as your fingers move over them, not because they're moving but because your sense of scale has no anchor here. They are smooth where the water has found them, rough where it hasn't. They are warm. They are older than the mineral growths around them — placed here *before* the crust began to build up — and the crust has grown around and over some of them, half-swallowing them, the way the sea swallows a wreck. Whoever made them used simple methods: pressing, scoring, folding. The marks of fingernails are visible on several. The marks of a stick, sharpened to a point, dragged in wet clay orite — in wetite — in wet *something* — and then left to harden.

Primitive. Pre-ceramic. A technology of hands and nothing else.

They were placed here with care. They were placed *into* the mineral crust while it was still wet, still forming, before it hardened around them — which means whoever placed them *knew* the crust would grow, would harden, would seal them in. They wanted them held. They wanted them kept.

---

You cannot see any of this. You discover it by touch, and the act of discovery is slow, because there is so much to find in so small a space. Your fingers map the pocket the way a blind reader maps a page — moving in lines, returning, cross-referencing. Every surface you touch yields something: a mineral cup full of warm water, a small smooth form pressed into the crust, a ridge of new growth that was not there — that must have been there — that could not have been placed by human hands because it branches in a fractal pattern too fine, too recursive, too *mathematically precise* to be the work of fingernails and sharpened sticks.

This is the weirdness. Not dramatic. Not dangerous. Just *wrong*, in a way that sits at the edge of perception. The mineral growths are too orderly. The branching patterns on the wall, when you trace them with your fingertip, repeat at scales too small for your finger to follow — down and down, into a fractal recursion that should hit the limit of physical matter and *doesn't*. It keeps going, past the point where crystal structure should assert itself and become granular, past the point where chemistry should put a floor under geometry, into a smallness that your fingertip can still feel as *pattern*, as *order*, as *design* — but no longer as matter.

Something in the rock is still thinking.

Or: the rock has always been thinking. And someone, a long time ago, with nothing but hands and a sharpened stick, figured that out — and made a small shrine inside the thought, and left it there in the dark, and let the mountain grow around it, and never came back.

---

The fauna is rare. A single species of something lives here — a soft-bodied thing, blind, colorless, no larger than your thumbnail, that lives in the mineral cups and feeds on whatever precipitates out of the water. It does not move when you touch it. It does not have a name. It is not important, except that it proves the water is food, that something in this place sustains life, that the warmth and the wet and the mineral saturation are not random but *functional* — a system, operating in the dark, at a scale below notice, in perfect equilibrium.

It has never changed. The water has always been this warm. The mineral has always been growing. The small forms have always been held. The thing in the water has always been feeding. The pocket exists in a state of absolute temporal arrest — not frozen, not paused, but simply *finished*, already and always at its final state, a place that achieved its equilibrium before anyone arrived and will hold it long after you leave.

When you leave — folding yourself back out through the mouth, feeling the cold air of the high escarpment hit your warm, wet skin like a slap — the pocket closes behind you. Not literally. The crack is still there. But it shrinks in your mind, becomes smaller than it should be, harder to find. You will not be able to locate it again by sight. You will only be able to find it by pressing your hand into the rock and waiting for the stone to give.

And the mountain, above you, in the wind, at altitude, is dry and exposed and ordinary, and shows no sign of what it holds inside itself.
