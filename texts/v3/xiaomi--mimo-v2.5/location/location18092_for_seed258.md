# The Growing Plateau

You stand on a height that has no bedrock — or rather, the bedrock is alive. Everything beneath your feet and in every direction is built. Constructed. But not by hands, not by tools, not by anything that intended a shape and set about making it.

The structures are plant.

They are always plant.

Vast, hollow, architectural forms rise from the plateau's surface like the works of some architect who only understood walls and arches but not why walls and arches exist. They are towers and bridges and vaulted chambers and long curving retaining walls, and every surface is living tissue — wet, green-white, fibrous, warm to the touch. They pulse faintly when you press your palm against them. You can feel water moving inside. Vascular channels run through the walls like plumbing, carrying moisture from deep roots up to crowns that open into the warm, high air. The structures are enormous — some rise forty, sixty meters — and they are spread far apart. Between them, open ground stretches in every direction, a gently uneven plain of the same living substrate: not soil, not rock, but a dense mat of root-flesh and mycelial tissue, spongy and warm, exuding moisture at every seam.

The emptiness between the structures is the strangest part. You would expect this much growth to be dense, choked, jungle-crowded. Instead there is an almost deliberate spaciousness — wide boulevards that no one planned, generous plazas between towers, long clear sightlines down corridors formed by the gaps between walls. The architecture breathes. It has room. It has chosen, if choosing is what it does, to spread itself thin.

Everything is wet. Not rain-wet — there is no rain. The warmth of the living structures meets the high-altitude air and the air cannot hold what the plants give it. Condensation gathers on every surface. You watch a bead of water form on the lip of an arch, swell, hold, and fall — and another begin forming in its place before the first has landed. The walls weep. The ground glistens. The air itself is a warm mist that never thickens to fog and never clears, hanging at a constant transparency that turns distant structures into soft green smears.

The sound is everywhere and it is enormous.

It is the sound of growth.

Not the slow, silent growth you imagine when you think of plants. This is growth made audible — a deep, continuous, organic creaking as the structures expand. It sounds like the hull of a wooden ship in heavy seas, but constant, omnidirectional, coming from every wall and tower and root-mat simultaneously. Layered over it is the rush of water — millions of gallons moving through vascular tissue, a sound like standing inside a waterfall that surrounds you at all distances. And beneath both, a low, rhythmic thrumming — the pulse of the whole system, the peristaltic contraction that moves water from deep below to the open crowns high above. It never stops. It never changes. It is exactly the same volume and rhythm it has always been and always will be.

You notice that the structures are new.

Not young — *new*. The surfaces are smooth, the edges soft, the tissues still swollen with the water of recent formation. There are no scars, no weathering, no signs of age. Moss has not had time to establish on surfaces that are themselves moss-like. The highest towers still show the pale, translucent coloring of tissue not yet fully differentiated — parts of them that could still become wall or could still become root or could still become something that has no name in the language of things that are not alive. Everything looks as though it finished becoming itself moments ago. As though you have arrived at the exact instant of completion.

And yet nothing changes. You wait for something to shift — a structure to grow visibly, a channel to change course, a wall to lean. Nothing. The weeping, the creaking, the rushing, the thrumming — all of it is in perfect, frozen equilibrium. The system is complete and total and it will not develop further. It is a city that built itself and then stopped at the exact moment before it was finished, and that moment is permanent.

The high air is warm. Warm from below, from the metabolic heat of the living architecture. The warmth carries the green, vegetal smell of cut stems and crushed leaves, concentrated to an almost narcotic intensity. You breathe and your lungs taste chlorophyll.

There are almost no animals. A few things — small, pale, shell-less creatures — move slowly along the walls, grazing on surface tissue. They leave behind trails of slightly darker green that close over within minutes. No birds. No insects, or almost none. A single winged thing drifts between two towers in the distance, moving too slowly for a bird, more like a seed pod carried by a wind that isn't blowing. The structures do not need pollinators or seed-carriers. They reproduce the way they do everything else: by growing. By extending root-tissue outward until a new wall rises, until a new arch springs between two existing walls, until the network expands by one more room, one more corridor, one more tower that no one will enter and no one designed.

At the far edge of visibility — and the visibility is considerable, the open spacing allowing you to see for kilometers — the plateau seems to continue without limit. More towers. More walls. More open ground. The same wet, warm, loud, living, unchanging construction, stretching on and on, built by nothing, inhabited by almost nothing, purposeless and total and absolute.

The erosion has just barely begun. In a few places — very few — you can see the first signs: a surface that has dried slightly and begun to crack, a root-channel that has weakened and partially collapsed, a wall whose crown has lost its translucency and turned the flat, dead color of tissue that has stopped receiving water. These are the first gestures of decay in a place that does not decay, because nothing here changes. The cracks form and stop. The collapses halt mid-collapse. The dead patches remain exactly as large as they were when they first appeared. Even entropy cannot commit.

You stand on the warm, wet, living ground between the weeping towers and the air hums with growth that has already happened and will not happen again, and the condensation gathers on your skin, and you understand that this place is not a ruin and not a paradise but a completed sentence in a language that has no speakers.
