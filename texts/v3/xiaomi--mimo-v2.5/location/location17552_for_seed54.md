# The Lachrymatory

There is no sky. There hasn't been for a long time, though whether this is permanent or merely the current phase of a cycle is hard to determine — everything here moves so slowly that the distinction loses meaning. What exists overhead is a vaulted ceiling of absolute black that occasionally shows faint striations of indigo, as though something vast and geological is rotating in the far upper atmosphere, exposing different mineral faces to whatever faint luminescence the place generates from its own processes. The darkness is not empty. It has texture. Pressure. It feels like it is still being deposited, layer by layer, like sintered obsidian forming in real time across the dome of the world.

**The water.** This is the first thing you would understand, if you were here — if the cold didn't kill you first, which it would, immediately, so thoroughly that the concept of cold would seem inadequate to describe what was happening to your body. The water is everywhere. It is not rain, exactly, though rain is one of its expressions. It weeps from every surface. The high-altitude air — thin, sharp, impossibly cold — still carries more moisture than it should be able to hold, as though the usual rules of phase transition have been gently persuaded to bend. Water condenses on every surface it touches, then clings, then slowly migrates downward in sheets and beads and irregular rivulets that seem to hesitate before committing to a path, as if surveying options. The rocks weep. The built structures weep. The creatures weep, though for them it is something else — excretion, or communication, or thermal regulation, or all three simultaneously.

The water is not pure. It carries dissolved minerals that give it a faintly milky opacity, and in places where it pools — and it pools everywhere, in basins both natural and constructed — it develops a skin of crystalline frost that cracks and reforms in slow, contemplative cycles. These pools are everywhere at every level. The city, if that is what it is, is built in a way that assumes water. Every surface slopes. Every junction is a channel. Every room — and there are rooms, vast chambers carved or grown or negotiated from the living rock — has drainage as a primary architectural concern, not as infrastructure added after the fact but as the founding principle of the space itself. You would understand the civilization here by its relationship to water the way you might understand a desert culture by its granaries.

**The altitude.** This place sits high. How high is difficult to gauge without familiar reference points, but the air pressure tells your body what your eyes cannot. It is thin enough that every sound carries further than it should, then distorts at its edges, bending around the density of the structures below. The high vantage gives the place a particular quality of exposure — this is a summit-world, a ridgeline existence, a place that faces the void on all sides. The geography drops away at the margins of the settlement into unresolvable darkness and the sound of falling water. You can hear it: the distant, permanent roar of water descending into whatever abysses border this height, a sound so constant it has become a form of silence.

The geological strata here are young. This is the strangest thing about the place, after the water and the dark and the overwhelming presence of life — the rock itself is still forming. You can see it. New basaltic columns, still warm with residual heat from whatever deep process is generating them, push upward through the existing structures with the patience of glaciers. The city does not resist this. The city *incorporates* it. Buildings that were constructed — through means that current physics would find deeply questionable — fifty years ago have already been partially consumed and re-erupted through by new geological growth, and this is considered not a problem but an amenity. The slow tectonic rebuilding of the world around and through the architecture is part of how the place breathes. Engineers here do not build against geology. They build *into* its cadence, timing their constructions to arrive at moments when a new column is about to emerge, so that the structure and the stone arrive together, neither dominating the other.

This is what erosion at 0.5 means. Not destruction. *Emergence.* The landscape is not being worn away. It is still arriving. The edges of the built environment are soft, unfinished — not from decay but from anticipation. Walls trail off into rough stone that has not yet been shaped because the shaping is scheduled for a later phase in a plan that may take centuries to complete. Entire districts exist in a state of architectural gestation, their outlines indicated by low mineral ridges and the placement of structural anchors that look like frozen droplets of metal but are in fact load-bearing elements waiting for the rest of the building to catch up.

**The fauna.** There are many of them. So many that the word "many" feels dishonest, feels like it is gesturing vaguely in the direction of a number while refusing to engage with what that number actually means. They are not animals in any sense that would be comfortable. They have no fur, no feathers, no integument that maps onto anything in your biological vocabulary. They are mineral-skinned, or chitin-shelled, or armored in something that looks like wet slate and functions like living circuitry. They range in size from things you could hold in your cupped hands — though you wouldn't, because they are all, every one of them, lethally cold-adapted in ways that would shred your skin — to things whose bodies you cannot see the full extent of in the darkness, shapes that move through the larger chambers and along the exterior ledges with the ponderous authority of geology given a nervous system.

They are *everywhere.* They layer over one another. They fill the spaces between structures. They clog the drainage channels — some of them *are* the drainage channels, or rather, they position themselves there deliberately, using the constant flow of mineral water across their bodies as a feeding mechanism. The sound they produce is enormous: a constant, overlapping chorus of clicks, scrapes, resonant hums, and percussive strikes as they communicate, collide, feed, and simply exist in the density of their population. The sound is not unpleasant. It is the opposite. It is so pervasive, so saturated, so structurally complete in its coverage of every frequency that it becomes a kind of room tone — the acoustic signature of a world that has never known silence and would find it deeply alien.

Some of them seem to be part of the infrastructure. Some of the structures seem to be part of them. The boundary between fauna and architecture is not a line anyone here has drawn with confidence. There are walls that pulse. There are creatures that are load-bearing. There are drainage systems that are digestive systems. There are corridors that migrate, slowly, across decades, following the movements of the organism that forms their floor.

**The city.** It extends. That is its primary quality — extension. You cannot see the edges of it in any direction, though this may be the darkness rather than the actual scale. It spreads laterally across the high plateau, pouring into the basins and climbing the ridges, filling every volume with structure and water and life. It is dense. There is no wasted space, no vacancy. Every cubic meter of the built environment is occupied — by construction, by water, by fauna, or by the slow mineral processes that are still assembling the next phase of the city's existence. The density is not claustrophobic. It is *geological.* You feel it the way you would feel the density of a cliff face — the sense that every space is full of material, that the emptiness you expect in a built environment has been replaced by substance.

The beyond-modern technology is not visible in the way that technology is usually visible. There are no screens, no interfaces, no obvious mechanisms. The technology is in the walls — in the way the walls respond to the fauna, adjusting their thermal conductivity as creatures move across them; in the way the water is guided not just by gravity and slope but by subtle electromagnetic fields that pulse through the mineral content of the rock itself; in the way the buildings have been grown rather than assembled, their molecular structure organized at a scale that current engineering cannot achieve, resulting in materials that are simultaneously stone and something else, materials that remember their history of formation and can be queried for it, read like geological records written in crystal lattice.

There are structures here that should not be able to exist. Spanning arches of impossible thinness that hold their shape against gravity and the constant hydraulic pressure of the water because their internal structure has been organized at the atomic level to distribute force in ways that a standard arch cannot. Vertical shafts that extend downward for distances that seem unreasonable, their walls lined with a living mineral film that the fauna graze upon, producing light — faint, bioluminescent-adjacent, more like cold phosphorescence — that gives these depths their only illumination. The darkness at the surface is not absolute at every level. Below, in the shafts and chambers and the vast reservoir-lakes that exist beneath the city's floor, there is a dim, greenish luminosity that makes the fauna look like ghosts of themselves, their mineral shells refracting the weak light into spectral halos.

**The sound.** The loudness is layered. At the base level, there is the roar of water — the permanent, omnipresent, city-defining sound of water moving across and through and around everything. Above that, the fauna: their clicks and scrapes and hums, a living texture of noise that modulates slowly across hours and days as different populations shift and feed and communicate. Above that — and here it gets strange, because this sound seems to come from the architecture itself — there is a deep, subsonic vibration that you feel more than hear, a resonance that the buildings produce in response to the geological activity below, as new stone pushes upward and existing stone settles and adjusts. It is a sound like the planet's heartbeat made audible through the medium of a city that has learned to listen.

Occasionally, something louder. A structural event — a new eruption of stone breaching an existing chamber, or a major fauna passage collapsing and reforming, or the slow detonation of a thermal expansion somewhere deep in the compressed aquifer beneath the city. These events are startling not for their volume but for their *specificity* — they are the only things here that happen fast enough to register as discrete events rather than gradients.

**What it is like to be here.** You would stand on a ledge of wet, dark stone that is still cooling from its recent emergence, water sheeting over your boots, the air so cold it would crystallize the moisture in your lungs on the first breath. The darkness would be total except for the faint bioluminescent glow rising from below, painting everything in shades of drowned green. The sound would be immense and omnipresent — water, fauna, stone, the deep vibration of a world still being born — and you would feel it in your chest and your teeth. The fauna would move around you, over you, through every space you were not occupying, their cold mineral bodies sliding past with the indifference of weather. The city would extend in every direction into darkness, wet and alive and humming and ancient and still, somehow, impossibly, *new.*
