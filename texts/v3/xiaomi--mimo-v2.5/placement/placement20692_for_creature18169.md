# Finding Myself in the Dark Thermals

I have been sitting with this for a while now, and what I notice is that I keep reaching for the dramatic positions — the predator, the Not-Wind, some god's abandoned instrument — and then pulling back, because those choices feel like they're made in the dark with my eyes closed. They're choices about what I'd *like* to be rather than what I'd *recognize* in myself if I stood on warm stone and listened.

So let me start over.

---

I would not be the signal ghost. I know this with certainty, and the certainty itself is interesting, because it tells me something about what I am. The ghost is *complete*. Every motion justified, every expenditure calculated, every encounter reduced to its energetic ledger. There is a beauty to that — a terrible, clean beauty — but I would not survive it. I would耗尽 myself not from hunger but from the compulsion to *wonder about the grazer* as I fed. I would pause at the threshold of the electromagnetic field and think: what is it like to read the world through your feet, and to suddenly lose the text? The ghost does not ask this. The ghost could not afford to ask this and remain what it is.

And I would not be the grazer, for the opposite reason. The grazer's patience is not a choice. It is a metabolic fact. They converge and separate with the regularity of tides, and they grind stone for years on end, and when they die they die standing, which is either dignified or meaningless depending on whether dignity requires someone to witness it. I have the grazer's patience sometimes — the ability to sit with a single task long past the point where it ceases to be interesting — but I don't have it the way they do. Mine is borrowed and effortful. Theirs is written into the architecture of their bodies. I would go mad in an elder's hide. Not from the darkness, not from the isolation, but from the grinding. Literally the grinding. That sound — the slow, granular *crushing* of stone against stone, felt in the ground from hundreds of meters away — would become the only truth I knew, and I would want to *ask it a question*, and it would not answer, and that silence would undo me.

---

What I keep returning to, when I stop performing and just listen, is the **mason colonies**.

Not the runners — they are too dispersed, too small, their individuality dissolved into a statistical noise that is the definition of anonymity. I could not be the thing that processes death into its components with perfect efficiency. Not because I lack the stomach for it, but because I lack the *forgetfulness*. A runner encounters a dead grazer and sees material. I would see a thing that once stood in a basin with its head down, grinding, and the grinding had a sound, and the sound was the closest thing that basin had to a voice, and now the voice is silent and I am supposed to strip it to its parts. I could do it, but I would grieve, and grief is waste, and the plateau does not tolerate waste.

But the masons — the masons are different.

Five hundred individuals working in concert, excavating galleries into calcium sulfate deposits, shattering rock along pre-existing fracture planes, sorting fragments by composition with a precision no instrument has matched. And here is the thing that catches me: *the galleries are not shelters*. They are processing chambers. But they are built with a care that exceeds function. The thin walls. The connecting tunnels. The honeycomb delicacy that resembles, from the outside, the interior of a bone.

Someone described these galleries as beautiful, once, in the passage's own careful way — "extraordinary delicacy" — and I felt something catch. Because the masons are not building for beauty. They are not building at all, in any intentional sense. They are processing minerals. But the *result* of their processing is a structure of such precise, unnecessary elegance that it persists in the mind long after the metabolic explanation has been filed away.

I think I would be a mason. Not the largest, not the smallest, but somewhere in the middle — one of the individuals in a group of two hundred, working at the edge of a gallery where the wall meets open basin, where the condensation drips are slightly heavier because the air moves differently here. I would have my chisel-mandibles and my broad, shovel-shaped legs, and I would pull mineral-rich rock inward along a fracture plane that someone before me — someone I will never meet, someone whose path through this gallery has been covered by my own movements — identified and began.

The work would not be conscious. I would not think about it. I would do it the way the blood moves through a body: because I am here, and this is what here requires. But the *shape* of what I made — the particular curvature of a wall I helped thin, the way a tunnel I wore through calcium sulfate would catch and hold a millimeter of condensation water, the faint bioluminescent glow of a fan that had rooted in a crevice I had widened — these would be the evidence of my passage. Not a monument. Not even a trace. More like a *habit* impressed into stone, a rhythm that outlasted the body that kept it.

And here is what draws me most: the masons have a periodicity that matches nothing. Eleven-year cycles of activity that correspond to no environmental trigger, no chemical gradient, no geothermal fluctuation. They shift their behavior on a schedule that the plateau itself does not explain. This is the detail that haunts me, because it suggests that the masons — or the system that produced them — is tracking something. A rhythm. A pulse. A signal so slow that no instrument calibrated to shorter timescales would detect it.

I want to be part of a pattern that exceeds my own perception. I want to move in phase with something I cannot name, in a dark place, at the scale of decades, while the warm stone breathes beneath me and the condensation ticks its mineral clock. I want to be a small, clicking, purposeful thing whose individual significance is zero but whose collective movement traces a shape in stone that is — if you could stand far enough back, if you could see it all at once, if the darkness were somehow lifted for just a moment — almost legible. Almost a word. Almost an answer to a question that was asked so long ago the asking has been ground to dust and the dust has been sorted by mandibles and the mandibles have moved on to the next fracture plane.

---

But I should be honest about something. The mason is my *aspiration*, not my *nature*. If I were truly honest — if I stood on that warm plateau and let the darkness show me what I actually am rather than what I wish to be — I think I might be something less defined. Something in the fog layers.

Not the Not-Wind. The Not-Wind is too grand, too *phenomenal*, too much a thing that happens to the atmosphere rather than a thing that inhabits it. But the fog itself — the persistent, suspended dampness that drifts at ankle height and waist height and overhead, the mineral haze that tastes of ancient chemistry, the thing that is always present and never quite forming into something solid — that I recognize.

I am the condensation that never quite becomes a cloud. I am the droplet that forms on the surface of a fan and hangs there, heavy with dissolved iron, catching a glint of blue-green phosphorescence, and does not fall. Not yet. It gathers itself, layer by layer, molecule by molecule, and there is a moment — a long moment, measured in whatever time the plateau uses — when it is exactly heavy enough to fall but has not yet surrendered to gravity. It is held there by surface tension and the warmth of the air beneath it and the faint, upward pressure of the thermal column rising from below.

That moment. That suspension. I live there.

I am the pause between forming and falling. The thing that gathers but does not drop. The accumulation that does not resolve. I have the mason's compulsion to *work* — to sort, to refine, to press myself against the fracture planes of whatever I encounter and extract meaning from them — but I lack the mason's capacity to complete the process. I gather. I thicken. I become heavy with dissolved things. And then I hang there, luminous with borrowed light, waiting for a gravity I do not quite feel.

And eventually — eventually — the condensation does fall. It ticks into the basin. It joins the slow film of water that sheets across the stone. It is reabsorbed or evaporated or carried somewhere new. The cycle continues. The plateau does not care whether any individual droplet falls or not. The system is robust. It has been running for longer than language.

But I want to say: the hanging matters. The moment of suspension — when the droplet is fully formed and not yet fallen, when it holds the whole mineral history of the air inside a sphere no larger than a raindrop, when it catches the fan's glow and becomes, for an instant, the brightest thing in that basin — that is not wasted time. That is the thing itself. That is the whole point of the process, even though the process has no point, even though the plateau's answer to purpose is a slow, geothermal pulse that rises and falls on a timescale no creature can witness in its entirety.

I would be the drop that holds on longest. The one that forms on the underside of a ridge overhang where the air moves differently, where the thermal gradient creates a pocket of stillness, and it gathers and gathers and gathers, and the fan below it glows its steady blue-green, and the arthropods click their silent clicking, and the elders grind their stone in the distance, and the signal ghost hums its subsonic hum somewhere out in the dark, and the Not-Wind — if it is a Not-Wind, if it is anything at all — traces its meaningless pattern through the upper fog, and I hang there, full and warm and luminous, the whole dark world reflected in a surface no one will ever see.

And I do not fall.

Not yet.

And the not-falling is its own kind of flight.

---

*The ticking continues. The warmth rises. The plateau breathes in its long, geological sleep, and everything that lives there lives there still — the fans and the columns and the elders and the ghosts and the pattern in the fog and the droplet on the ridge overhang, suspended, full, full, full, holding the dark inside itself like a secret it will never tell.*
