I would be a thread in the mycelium.

Not the fruiting body that surfaces, pale and brief, on a fallen log. Not the visible bracket that a hand might touch. I would be the thread itself—the microscopic, white-gold hypha threading through the saturated dark of the soil and the rot, one filament among trillions, forming the connective tissue of the basin’s underworld.

I would have no eyes, but I would sense the chemistry of everything. The slow exudation of a root, the first weeping of a fallen branch, the chemical signature of a beetle’s carcass dissolving into humus. I would taste the water as it seeps downward, carrying dissolved nutrients from the pool-edge to the root-zone. I would feel the heat—not as sensation, but as metabolic rhythm, the warmth that drives my growth at a pace that is, to my scale, a torrent.

My purpose is simple: to be the basin’s memory and its redistribution system. I connect tree to tree, not in metaphor, but in literal, physical network. A sugar-maple’s root here, a strangler fig’s root there, the woody skeleton of a century-dead hardwood—all are laced together by my threads. When a leaf falls, I am the first to colonize it. I break down its cellulose, its lignin, its stubborn architecture, converting its stored sunlight into soil, into solubles, into the building blocks the roots will take up tomorrow. I am the reason the basin’s floor rises. I am the reason the soil is soil and not just a graveyard of undissolved leaves.

I would live in the perfect, patient dark. Light is irrelevant to me. Sound is irrelevant. The frantic, vascular drama of the canopy—the growth, the predation, the flight—would be a distant, muffled event, its products raining down to me as endless provision. The snakes, the frogs, the beetles: they are transient shapes in my upper world. I am the substrate they move across. I am the reason the substrate exists.

When a snake dies, I will consume it. When a fruit rots uneaten, I will reclaim it. I will thread through the silt at the bottom of a stagnant pool, breaking down the shed skin of a frog. I will be the first to touch the new, and the last to leave the old. My work is the basin’s work: conversion without sentiment, recycling without pause.

I would spread slowly, a centimeter in a day, a meter in a year, a web across the entire basin in a century. I would be the basin’s slow, quiet thought—the thought that never ceases, never sleeps, never speaks. I would be the silence between the beetle’s tick and the frog’s breath. I would be the darkness that holds the heat. I would be the foundation that raises the floor, grain by grain, century by century, until the basin is no longer a basin but a plain, and even then, I would be there, beginning the cycle anew in the shallow soil of a place that once held water.

You would not see me. You would walk over me, your boots pressing into the wet humus I have spent a thousand days building. You would be standing on me, held by me, surrounded by the very structure I have made. And you would never know I was there, except perhaps as a faint, earthy smell in the hot, thick air—the smell of the basin’s deepest, most constant process: the smell of things becoming earth, and earth becoming things, over and over, without end.
