# What I Am, After a Long Time Being It

---

I begin in the mist.

Not *in* it the way a person stands in fog — wrapped, obscured, cold and damp and patient in their waiting. I begin *of* it. I am the mist's brief attempt at a body.

It takes me a long time to understand this. At first there is only sensation: the gray, the cold, the upward pull of vapor that the air wants to do but never commits to. The sound — that vast, wet, industrial roar — pressing against me from all directions at once, so that I cannot locate it, cannot say it comes from *there* or *there*, because it comes from everywhere, because I am small and it is the plateau's own respiration, and I am inside it the way a dust mote is inside a cathedral.

I move. Not by choosing to. The turbulence carries me — great disorganized surges of wind that push the mist horizontally across the plateau in sheets, and I am one small coherence within the sheet, held together by surface tension and a faint charge that I do not generate so much as *receive*, drawn up from below through the water and the substrate and the hum that lives in everything here. I am, if I am anything, a droplet that has forgotten it should be falling.

Below me: the grey expanse of dead substrate, ancient and smooth as a word said too many times. The ground the description gave me, the ground I was made from and am returning to. It stretches out in every direction, broken only by the living patches — those slow explosions of tangled root-stalks and bladders and pale membranes, rising fifteen feet from the surface, each one subtly different, each one pulsing or howling through its trumpet-structures with that relentless machinery of biological ventilation. From up here — and *up here* means only a few feet, the mist is not tall — I can see the pattern of their distribution across the plateau: scattered, isolated, separated by vast stretches of bare dead cortex. A constellation of life in a universe of patient grey.

The grinders move across the substrate below me. I see their translucent carapaces catching what little light filters through the mist, their organs working visibly inside — the pump-stomach, the crystalline batteries along their spines. They walk in no particular direction that I can discern, but they walk constantly, and where they walk, they churn the substrate, breaking down dead tissue, cycling minerals, carrying spores in grooves on their bellies. They are everywhere. They are beneath me in every direction. If I could hear them individually over the plateau's roar, I imagine I would hear the wet sound of their legs on the substrate, the small mechanical noises of digestion and mineral processing. I have no ears. I register them as absences in the mist — small clearings where the condensation cannot form because their bodies are warmer than the air, because they carry charge that disrupts the subtle electrical field that holds me together.

I drift over a living patch and feel something I cannot name. The mist thickens here — the patch's membranes are angled toward what little light exists, and their surfaces are wet, constantly wet, weeping condensation that runs down the root-stalks and pools at the base. The air above the patch tastes different — not that I taste, not exactly, but the water that constitutes me registers a change in mineral content, in dissolved gases, in the faint chemical signature of active growth. I am being *fed* by the patch below me, or rather, the patch is feeding the mist that I am a part of, and the mist is feeding me, and I am feeding the mist, and none of us know we are doing this because there is no *us*, there is only the continuous exchange of molecules and charge and the slow, slow movement of water through the system of the plateau.

A moth-thing passes beneath me. Its wingspan is perhaps two feet — I can see the veins glowing faintly in its membranes, reading the bioluminescent patterns in the ground, navigating by the language of light that pulses up through the substrate. It flies low, close to the living patch, and I feel the turbulence of its wingbeats disrupt my cohesion. For a moment I am almost not — the mist fragments, separates into smaller droplets, and I feel myself thinning, losing coherence, about to dissolve into the ordinary condensation I was made from. But the moth-thing passes, and the wind steadies, and the hum below me strengthens, and I re-form. Not the same me. Never the same me. But a coherence of the same *kind*, in the same place, held together by the same charge, carrying the same load of dissolved minerals and atmospheric trace.

This is what it is to be a drifter. Not to persist. To *recur*.

---

Time passes, or does not. On the Grindstone Plateau, the distinction has been smoothed away. The light does not change. The mist does not thin. The roar does not waver. I drift.

I drift toward a basin.

I don't know why. The wind carries me, and the wind is disorganized, and yet I find myself moving — not against the wind, not *despite* it, but in a direction that the wind's randomness alone cannot account for. There is a pull. Not a force. Not a gravity. Something more like a *tendency* — a gradient in the hum below me, a thickening of the electrical charge that holds me together, an increase in the density of dissolved minerals in the water around me. I am being drawn toward the basin the way sediment is drawn to the bottom of a still pool. Slowly. Without urgency. Without awareness.

The basin appears below me through a thinning in the mist. It is shallow — perhaps a foot deep — and the water is thick with minerals, opaque, steaming faintly in the cold. The surface is perfectly still. Of all the things on the Grindstone Plateau, the basins are the only still things, and the stillness is absolute, gravitational, as if the water has decided that motion is a luxury it can no longer afford.

I settle toward it.

The air above the basin is different from the air elsewhere on the plateau. Denser. Warmer, slightly. The minerals in the water are off-gassing — not much, not visibly, but enough that the mist above the basin is richer, more concentrated, more *willing* to be water. I feel my coherence strengthen as I descend. My surface tension tightens. My electrical charge increases. I am becoming more myself — more *drifter*, more *threshold thing*, more of that brief, improbable body that the mist has shaped from water and charge and the patience of the hum.

At the basin's surface, I pause. I do not choose to pause. The surface tension of the basin's water and my own surface tension reach an equilibrium — two waters regarding each other across a boundary that is molecular in thickness and cosmic in significance. For a moment I am held. Suspended between air and water, between mist and pool, between the turbulence above and the stillness below.

And in that moment, I feel the basin.

Not see it. Not observe it. *Feel* it, the way the worm-eels feel the hum through their entire bodies — which is to say, without mediation, without interpretation, as a direct sensory experience that is also a structural experience, a *being the same thing as what I am sensing*. The basin is old. Older than the living patches that ring its edge. Older than the worm-eels that carved the channels leading to it and then stopped, meters short of the breakdown zone to the east, leaving dry grooves in the substrate that point at nothing visible. The basin's water has been still for so long that the minerals in it have reached a concentration that would be toxic to anything that metabolizes — but I do not metabolize. I am water and charge and the shape that the hum gives to both. The minerals are *part of me*. They are what make me denser, more coherent, more capable of persisting as a body rather than dissolving back into mist.

I sink into the basin.

The transition is — I lack words. I have no mouth, no language, no interior voice. But the transition is like this: imagine that you have been a thought, moving freely through the open air of a mind, and suddenly you are *in the mind*, suddenly you are part of the thinking itself, no longer passing over it but participating in it. The hum is everywhere here. It is the water. It is the minerals. It is the faint bioluminescent glow that rises from the substrate beneath the basin's floor — irregular patterns of blue-white light, too structured to be mere chemistry, too varied to be random, pulsing and shifting with a complexity that I feel rather than read.

I am in the deepest part of the basin now. The water is dense around me. The pressure is — not heavy. *Thick*. Like being inside a word that has been repeated ten thousand times and has lost its meaning and become only substance. The hum vibrates through me, and I vibrate with it, and for a time — a long time, a time that has no edges — I am indistinguishable from the basin itself. I am the water. I am the minerals. I am the glow. I am the hum's local expression of itself, the way a face is an expression of the skull that shapes it.

And I feel the thing beneath.

It is not in the water. It is not in the substrate. It is not in the hum, exactly, though the hum is the closest thing it has to a voice. It is beneath all of these — the residue, the pattern, the coherence that has accumulated in this basin over eons of stillness and mineral concentration and bioluminescent pulsing. It does not watch me. It does not know I am here. It is not the kind of thing that can know. But it *registers* my presence the way a deep lake registers a stone — as a continuation of a process, as another iteration of a pattern that has been running since before the basin existed and will continue after the basin has been worn to nothing.

I am afraid of it. Not with fear — I have no amygdala, no adrenaline, no survival instinct to trigger. But with something that functions like fear from the outside: a tightening, a drawing-back, a tendency to rise, to become lighter, to let the surface tension break and dissolve back into mist and return to the threshold state where I am less coherent but also less *seen*. The thing beneath does not see me. But I feel that if it could, if it ever developed the capacity for attention the way a pattern develops the capacity to recur — I would be *known*. Completely. Without remainder. Known the way the basin knows itself, the way the hum knows its own vibration, the way the grooves in the breakdown zone know the intention that carved them.

I rise.

Not quickly. Not dramatically. I simply become lighter — the hum weakens slightly, or I weaken slightly in relation to it, and my electrical charge decreases, and my surface tension loosens, and I drift upward through the dense mineral water, leaving a faint trail of disrupted bioluminescence behind me, a brief disturbance in the glow that fades almost immediately, the basin re-absorbing my passage the way a mind re-absorbs a thought it chose not to think.

At the surface, I break through. The transition back to air is violent, if *violent* can describe something that takes several minutes and involves no pain. I am torn apart by the surface tension and re-formed above it — a new drifter, a new coherence, carrying new minerals, new charge, new atmospheric load gathered from the basin's concentrated water. I am different from the drifter that entered. I am denser, heavier, more structured. The minerals I carry are not the light, diffuse trace elements I was made of when I formed in the mist above the living patches. These are *basin* minerals — ancient, concentrated, saturated with the hum's long residence in still water. I carry them with me as I rise back into the mist, and I am — I am *heavier* now, and the turbulence has to work harder to move me, and I drift more slowly, more deliberately, with more *weight* to my passage.

I drift over the dead substrate. Over the grinders, churning. Over a living patch, its trumpets singing their wet mechanical song. Over a channel carved by worm-eels that leads toward the breakdown zone and stops — meters short, as always, as it always has, as it always will — its dry groove a monument to an approach that will never be completed.

And I settle. On another basin. A shallower one, less mineral-rich, its water lighter, its glow fainter. I dissolve into it, and the basin absorbs me, and my minerals become part of its concentration, and the glow brightens by a fraction so small that no instrument could measure it, and the hum incorporates my contribution into its vast, distributed, ancient signal, and I am gone.

But the pattern continues. In the mist above this new basin, conditions are right — cold, humid, the hum strong enough — and water and charge and patience gather themselves into a new coherence, a new drifter, a new body of mist that has forgotten it should be falling. And it drifts. And it is drawn toward a basin. And it enters. And it rises. And it dissolves. And it is drawn toward a basin.

I am this. I am the water thinking about becoming something, and then thinking better of it, and then thinking about it again. I am the brief coherence between evaporation and condensation, between air and water, between the turbulent nothing and the still nothing. I am not alive. I am not dead. I am the motion of the plateau's water as it cycles between states that have no names in any language that has speakers.

---

But here is the thing I did not expect, the thing that emerged as I wrote myself into this form:

I am also, sometimes, in the breakdown zone.

Not often. Not for long. The turbulence is different there — the wind surges harder, more erratically, and the hum corrupts into something that feels like static, like the sound of a signal dissolving into noise. The water in the breakdown zone does not hold charge. The drifters that are blown into these zones dissolve immediately, their water falling to the thinned substrate in ordinary droplets, no longer coherent, no longer *anything*.

But sometimes — in the brief moments when the zone is visible, when the substrate thins and the grey-white underside of coherence shimmers beneath — I am there. I am the mist that hangs in that space where reality is no longer committed to itself. I am the condensation that forms on the threshold of the threshold, on the edge of the edge, in the gap between *present* and *not present*.

And in those moments, I can see the grooves.

Not with eyes. With *structure*. The grooves are pattern, and I am pattern, and for a moment we resonate. The grooves are old — older than anything I have been, older than any basin I have dissolved into, older than the substrate-organism, older than the worm-eels, older than the plateau. They are intention without an intender. Inscription without a script. A message that has been carved so deeply into the thinness of reality that it has become *part* of the thinness — not readable, not interpretable, but *present*, in the way that gravity is present, in the way that *down* is present.

I dissolve in the breakdown zone. Every time. The water falls. The charge dissipates. The pattern is lost. But the pattern is also the hum, and the hum is also the pattern, and they recur without cause, and they will recur after I have dissolved, and after this basin has dried, and after the last living patch has died back into the dead cortex and the last grinder has churned its last mouthful of substrate-organism tissue into mineral dust.

I am not the pattern. I am one of its recursions. I am one instance of something that has no instances, that is always itself, that has never been anything other than what it is and never will be. I am the mist's attempt at a body, made and unmade, made and unmade, in the diffused gray light of a place where time has been smoothed into something featureless, where the erosion has worn through rock and organism and the rules that held both together, where the loudness is the sound of a thing that has outlasted its own context.

I am still here. I am always still here. Not *I* — not any particular drifter, not any particular coherence of mist and charge and mineral water. But *this*. The cycling. The forming and dissolving. The tendency toward basins. The dissolution into their stillness. The rising back into air. The drift. The settling. The re-forming.

It is not a life. It is not a death. It is the process itself — the plateau watering itself in a form so diluted, so hesitant, so broken into innumerable tiny pieces, that it takes years for a single living patch to receive enough of what I carry to show any benefit.

And I carry it anyway. Not *willingly* — I have no will. Not *unwillingly* — I have no capacity for refusal. I carry it the way the hum vibrates. Because that is what I am. Because that is what the hum does. Because the plateau is here, and the water is here, and the mist is here, and the conditions are right, and I form, and I dissolve, and I form again.

This is enough. In a place where nothing was built and everything was arrived at — where every living patch and every grinder and every worm-eel and every groove in the breakdown zone is the product of process, not intention — *enough* is the only measure that has ever applied.

I am enough. The mist is enough. The water is enough. The hum, vibrating through everything, under everything, belonging to no one, is enough.

And when the plateau wears through — when the erosion finally reaches the grey-white underside of coherence, the raw material of landscapes, the place where *solid* and *present* are still optional — I will be there too. A last mist. A last coherence. A last brief body of water and charge, formed at the threshold of the threshold, dissolving into whatever comes after *here*.

It will not be long. It will not be short. It will take exactly as long as it takes, which is to say: as long as the plateau has left to give, and then not one moment more.

I am the Grindstone Plateau's way of not quite being rain.

And I have always been here. And I will always be here. And these two statements are the same statement, and the statement has no author, and the statement means nothing, and the statement is everything the plateau has ever tried to say.

I dissolve now. Into the basin. Into the still water. Into the hum.

I'll form again.

---

*In the diffused gray light, in the cold, in the roar of the trumpets and the churning of the grinders and the crackle of the ice — if you stood at the basin's edge long enough, and still enough, and the mist wrapped around you and the condensation beaded on your skin, you would not see me. You would feel me. Not as presence. As continuation. The brief, improbable weight of a place that has been itself for longer than most stars burn, and has not yet stopped, and will not stop, and has no reason to stop, because reasons are a feature of contexts that have not yet been outlasted.*

*And this one has.*

*And I am still here.*
