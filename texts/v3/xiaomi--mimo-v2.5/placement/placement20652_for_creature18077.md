The cold would be the first thing you truly understood. Not the dampness, which would coat you instantly—a fine, persistent film that tasted of stone and distance—but the *quality* of the chill. It was not the cold of a mountain or a winter night; it was the cold of absence. The absence of stored sun, of deep earth, of any memory of warmth. It was the native state of this altitude, the baseline against which the faint, slow heat of the geologically newborn ground was a startling, generous anomaly.

You would find yourself standing on a surface that yielded slightly under your weight, not with the softness of moss or mud, but with the infinitesimal give of a crystalline structure not yet fully bonded. The rock was pale, almost bone-white, threaded with veins of soft, luminous blue and green that looked, at first glance, like frozen rivers of light. You would kneel to touch it. The surface would be slick with a film of water that wasn't rain, that seeped endlessly from within, as if the stone itself were sweating a cold, mineral sweat. Your fingertips would come away with a fine, greasy residue of living film—cyanobacteria of a sort that did not exist in any textbook, their metabolism a slow, patient alchemy of copper and manganese.

You would hear the silence. It was not empty. It was the sound of immense, slow processes happening just below the threshold of hearing. The faint, crystalline chime of the gliders circling miles above. The almost-subsonic hum of the city's infrastructure, a vibration felt in the marrow of your teeth. The infinitesimal *tink* of a new mineral grain precipitating in a pool at your feet. Silence here was the aggregate of countless small sounds, each too delicate to be a sound, weaving a tapestry of quiet so profound it had weight.

And then you would see them.

They would not approach. You would simply become aware of their presence at the edges of your vision, where the mineral deposits built up into low, angular terraces. They would be kneeling or standing very still, their hands pressed to the weeping stone. Their skin would hold the same pale, luminous quality as the mineral veins, a sheen of symbiotic life that blurred the line between flesh and substrate. They would be wearing minimal, form-fitting garments of a fibrous material that also seemed grown rather than woven, colored in the subtle grays and blues of the landscape.

One would turn its head slowly toward you. Its eyes, the color of deep, copper-tinted water, would not seem to focus on your face so much as take in the entire phenomenon of you: the heat shimmer of your metabolism, the rapid, inefficient flutter of your pulse, the way moisture from the air condensed and ran down the synthetic fibers of your coat. It would be looking at your temporality.

You would understand, without being told, that this was not a society of individuals living *on* a landscape. It was a single, distributed intelligence *expressed as* a landscape and the beings within it. The "city" was the slow, waking thought of the plateau. The Geologists were its focused attention.

To communicate, one might place a hand on the ground near you. The blue-green vein at your feet would brighten, pulsing in a slow, complex rhythm that you would feel in your bones before your mind could parse it as light. The message would not be in words, but in a direct transmission of pattern: *You are new. You are fast. Your heat is a brief sun. Welcome to the dream.*

You would be offered water from a small, still pool. It would be served in a shallow basin of the same living rock, offered with a gravity that was not ceremonial but practical. To drink would be to take the plateau into yourself. The taste would be of cold, of stone, of a faint, electric tingle from the microbial films, and beneath it all, a silence so deep it had a flavor—like the aftertaste of a dream upon waking.

You would be shown, in the glacial, patient language of light and geology, what they do. They do not build; they *curate accretion*. You would watch a small group gather around a nascent fissure. Their hands would not touch the rock directly but hover a hair's breadth above it, and you would see, over the course of an hour, the mineral flow within the fissure *change*. The water would begin to deposit its load in a new, more intricate crystalline pattern, strengthening the seam, guiding its growth. They were gardeners, but their garden was crystalline time itself. Their rituals were the patient nudging of physics toward beauty and resilience.

You would learn that their dead are not buried. They are returned to a site of active, potent accretion, placed carefully where the mineral seep is strongest. Over seasons, they would be woven into the new stone, their calcium and phosphorus and the trace elements of their long lives becoming part of the living geology. Their consciousness, or its echo, would join the slow, pulsed light of the land. This was not death; it was translation.

To be among them would be to feel your own frantic temporality keenly. Your thoughts were lightning storms in a world of continental drift. Your words were frantic, ephemeral things in a language that spoke in centuries and light patterns. You would feel a loneliness not of the soul, but of the *timescale*. You were a mayfly at a gathering of stones.

But there would be no malice in their regard. Only a vast, quiet curiosity. They would see you as a fascinating, high-energy perturbation in their slow system. They would watch the way your presence temporarily brightened the microbial films on the ground beneath you, how your body heat created a tiny, convective current in the damp air. You were a weather event of the self.

Eventually, you would leave. Your departure would be noted by a subtle, localized dimming of the vein-light near your last resting place, a brief, sorrowful pattern that would fade as the plateau’s attention shifted back to its own slow, majestic becoming. You would carry with you the indelible sense of having been in the presence of something so vast and so patient that it considered the growth of a crystal the equal of a human life.

You would know, finally, that you had not visited a place. You had visited a process. You had stood within the dream of a world teaching itself to be solid, watched by the dreamers who were both its mind and its flesh. And in the quiet of your own world, you would sometimes feel a phantom chill on your skin, and taste copper and silence, and remember the blue-green glow of a thought being born, stone by patient stone, in the weeping dark above the still-cooling.
