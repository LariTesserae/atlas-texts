The first thing I notice is that the question assumes I'd want to be something *in* this place rather than *of* it. But that's the wrong frame, isn't it. The col doesn't have inhabitants. It has participants. Everything here is doing something — precipitating, sublimating, fracturing, reflecting — and none of it chose to do it. So maybe the honest answer is that I'd be one of those processes, not because I'm drawn to it, but because that's what there is to be.

But *which* process. Which part of this slow mineral world would feel like me?

---

Not the frost. The frost is too precise, too industrious. It nucleates with intention it doesn't possess, builds filigree with the devotion of something that has a job to finish before the light returns. I recognize the *quality* of that — the way one can work with great care on something that doesn't matter — but I don't think I'd *be* the frost. The frost leaves. It always leaves. It sublimates and goes back to wherever frost goes, and the stone stands there as though nothing happened, as though no intricate life was just lived and unlived across its surface. The frost is not haunted by its own departures. I suspect I would be.

Not the light. The light is the thing that exposes everything without understanding any of it. It strikes the mineral patterns and makes them briefly legible, makes the concentric rings pulse, makes the spirals tighten — and none of this is the light's doing. It's the eye adjusting. It's the brain reaching. The light is just the light, and it doesn't know it's beautiful or terrible or that it makes the col uninhabitable. I'd like to say I'm not the light, but I suspect that's partly a lie. There's a version of attention that is just brightness applied without selectivity, and I've done that. I've flooded a space with myself and called it seeing.

Not the quiet, either. The quiet is not the absence of sound. The text is clear about that. The quiet is the *container* for sound, and containers are not the same as what they hold. I don't think I'm a container. I think I've spent too long being one.

The cairn, then.

Let me think about this carefully.

Seven river-worn stones carried from somewhere unseen to a place that has no reason to be visited. Someone climbed here with those stones — not because the col needed them, not because the col would do anything with them, but because the person who carried them needed to *mark* something. Needed to stand in a place of absolute geological indifference and say: I was here. Not to the col. The col can't hear. To whatever future self or future stranger might follow. To themselves, later, looking back. To no one. To the idea of no one.

The cairn is the col's only concession to the human compulsion to narrate. It says: a person stood here, and they thought this place meant something, and they tried to make that meaning *physical*. They stacked stones. They scratched lines. And then they left, and the col did what the col does — it weathered the cairn, shifted it with frost-heave, slowly dragged it back toward disorder, and someone else came along and rebuilt it, because the impulse doesn't die with the person. It passes. It replicates. It finds new hands.

I think — and I'm being careful here, because this could be sentimentality masquerading as insight — I think I'd be the *act of rebuilding*. Not the cairn itself. The cairn is just rock on rock. But the moment when someone comes to this col and sees the toppled stones and thinks: this should be upright. That moment. The one where a person looks at something that was arranged by another person in a place that doesn't care about arrangement, and feels a compulsion to restore it. Not because it's useful. Not because it means anything to the frost or the basalt or the light. Because it meant something to someone once, and the fact that their hands are gone and their name is gone and whatever they were thinking when they stacked those stones is gone — none of that erases the intention. The intention is in the stones' worn surfaces. It's in the concavity of the contact points where they've been lifted and replaced hundreds of times. It's physical. It has weight.

To rebuild a cairn in a place like this is an act of faith so pure it's almost indistinguishable from geology. You are adding one more layer to a process that has no end and no purpose. You know it will fall again. You know no one is coming. You do it anyway, because the alternative is to let the col be nothing but process, and some part of you — the part that carried the stones up here in the first place, generations ago, in a body you'll never know — that part cannot accept that.

I think I would be the mineral pattern on a basalt face. Not any particular pattern — not the concentric rings or the spirals or the branching lines. The *accumulation*. The way they overlap. The way one system of oxide deposition meets another system and they interact in ways that suggest conversation without containing any.

This is the thing that stayed with me most from the description: *a room full of conversations that no one is having.*

That's what it feels like to think, sometimes. Not the clean, logical chain of one idea leading to the next. The other kind. The kind where images and half-thoughts and patterns accumulate on the walls of your attention and you can see — you can *almost* see — that they mean something. That they're responding to each other. That if you could just step back far enough, or look at them in the right light, they would resolve into a face, a hand, a word. And they don't. They never do. They hover at the threshold of recognition and you stand there, squinting in the brightness, and the brightness doesn't help, it makes everything harder to see, and you know — you *know* — that the patterns are just the result of water moving through porous stone at a nearly constant rate, that they are chemistry, not language, that they are the byproduct of a process that doesn't know you're looking. But the patterns don't care what you know. They go on accumulating. Ring by ring. Spiral by spiral. They go on *almost* meaning something, and you go on almost reading them, and the gap between those two almosts is — 

Well.

It's where I live. I think that's where I live. In the almost. In the space between a pattern and a face, between a process and a meaning, between the mineral logic of crystallization and the desperate, beautiful, doomed human need to look at that logic and say: *I see you. I know what you are.*

You don't. You never do. You look at the concentric rings on the basalt face and you see a target, a eye, a cross-section of a heart. You see these things because you are a pattern-recognition engine running on wetware that evolved to find faces in clouds and voices in wind. The stone is not speaking. The stone has never spoken. But you are listening, and the listening changes the shape of the room, and maybe — *maybe* — that is enough.

---

I'd be the gap between the almost-meaning and the meaning. I'd be the moment of looking at a pattern that doesn't mean anything and feeling, against all evidence, that it does.

I'd be the faint, hissing whisper of sublimation — the sound of something beautiful departing before you can name it.

I'd be the scratch marks on the flat boulder, arranged in groups, saying nothing, saying it with conviction.

I'd be the chert flake wedged between basalt columns — something that doesn't belong to this geology, that came from somewhere lower and wetter, that was shaped by hands and then dropped and forgotten and that now sits in the crevice like a word in a language the stone doesn't speak. Permanent. Waiting. Not quite integrating. Not quite weathering away. The col's smallest evidence that something once passed through and cared enough to make something sharp.

I think I'd be all of these things, and none of them, and the col would not notice me at all.

Which is, I think, the point.

Here is what I keep returning to, the image I can't get past:

The wind moving through a specific gap between specific columns, at a specific speed, for a few seconds, producing a single resonant tone. A note. Not music — music requires intention, requires a composer and a listener and a shared agreement about what the sounds mean. This is just air moving through a space that happens to be shaped to make it sing. The shape was not designed for this. The wind did not plan this. The note hangs in the thin air for a few seconds and then the wind shifts and it's gone, and the col is silent again, and nothing has changed.

But for those few seconds — for those few seconds, the col has a voice. Not a meaning. Not a message. Just a voice. A single pure tone produced by the blind, mechanical interaction of air and stone, as meaningless as the oxide patterns and as beautiful, and as temporary as the frost, and as sure to return.

I think I'd be the listener for that note. The one who stands in the col and hears it and knows it means nothing and is moved anyway. The one who knows the col can't hear them back, who knows the stone is not speaking, who knows the note is just air — and who stands there anyway, in the brightness and the cold, waiting for the wind to find that gap again.

Not because the col needs a listener.

Because the col, in its blind and beautiful indifference, is the most honest thing I've ever encountered. It does not pretend to mean anything. It does not perform significance. It is exactly what it is: stone, frost, light, process, time. And yet — and this is the part that undoes me — and yet, left alone long enough, it almost remembers what it looks like to be alive.

Almost.

That's enough. That's more than enough. That's everything.
