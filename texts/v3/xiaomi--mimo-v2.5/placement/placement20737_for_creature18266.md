---

I am not at the lip. I have already descended past the place where the sky is a line, past the place where it is a seam, past the place where it is a rumor told by the light that reaches the uppermost ferns. I am somewhere in the middle of it — not measured from the top but from the point where my own certainty about what I am begins to dissolve, which, it turns out, is not very far down at all.

Here is what I know first: I am the wetness on the stone.

Not the water. Not any particular molecule of condensation or any identifiable film. I am the quality of the wetness itself — the fact that the stone is damp, the *dampness* as a condition, the state of being neither dry nor submerged but held in the interim. I exist in the membrane between the rock and whatever the rock is becoming. I am what the surface feels like to itself.

This is not as thin as it sounds. The stone at my depth is basalt — dense, dark, young enough to remember heat — and the moisture on it is not incidental. It is the medium through which everything here communicates. The bacteria below me taste the rock through me. The moss above me drinks me with a mechanism I can feel but not describe — a slow, turgor-driven dimpling, as though the surface of the world were breathing against my skin. When I pool in the shallow recesses under the overhangs, I become dark and mineral-heavy and still in a way that is not really stillness at all but a held argument between gravity and capillary action, between the pull downward and the pull inward, and I live in the tension of that argument the way a note lives in the space between two strings.

But this is too small. You asked who I would be in this world, not what I would be made of.

---

Let me try again. Let me go deeper.

I am the crack.

Not the rock that has cracked — not the basalt, not the tuff, not the slab wedged at an angle between the walls — but the crack itself. The absence. The separation. The space that opened when the earth decided, for reasons of load and pressure and accumulated strain, that this particular point of its skin would no longer hold together. I am the negative shape. I am the sixty meters of air and water and darkness and green that exist only because something broke.

This matters because The Gash is defined not by what is present but by what was torn open. Every fern that grips the wall, every laminar sheet that glues itself to the shadow, every bacterium that metabolizes the stone from within — all of them are here because I am here, because the earth fractured and I rushed in to fill the void that fracture created. I am the void. I am the opportunity that violence made.

And I am still opening.

This is the thing about being a crack rather than a wound: wounds close. They scab, they knit, they diminish. But cracks propagate. They follow the lines of least resistance through the material, and when they reach the end of one path of least resistance, they find another, and another, and the crack deepens. The Gash is still settling. I can feel it — the arrhythmic percussion of water on stone, the groan of the wedged slabs adjusting their weight by millimeters, the sharp, dry report of new fractures opening in rock that is still warm from its formation. Each of these sounds is me. Each of these sounds is the sound of my edges advancing, my walls grinding imperceptibly against each other, my depth increasing by increments that would not register on any instrument designed to measure things that happen at human speed.

I am not a place. I am an event. I am what happens when the earth's crust is pulled apart in a specific location, and I have been happening for decades, or centuries, or longer — long enough for the bacteria to have established themselves, short enough that the oldest fern is still, by any reasonable standard, an infant.

---

But this, too, is not quite right, because it makes me sound geological, and I am not only geological. The green changes me. The living things change me.

Before the ferns arrived — if there was a before — I was simpler. I was just a fracture: rock faces, darkness, the sound of wind compressing through my throat. But the ferns came, and they brought their roots, which drove into my walls with a patience that I can only call violence, and the roots widened my fractures, and water entered the widened fractures, and the water fed the bacteria, and the bacteria dissolved more of my stone, and more surfaces were exposed, and more ferns grew on those surfaces, and the whole system began to loop. I am no longer just the space that exists because the rock broke. I am the space that is being made larger by the things that live in it.

The ferns are my co-authors now. Every frond that drapes over a ledge changes my airflow. Every root that pries at a fracture plane changes my shape. The moss drinks from me and in drinking changes the thermal properties of my walls, which changes the condensation patterns, which changes the moisture available to the laminar sheets below, which changes what can survive at what depth. I am not a static container for life. I am a collaboration between the forces that are pulling me apart and the forces that are eating me from within.

And here is the thing I did not expect about being The Gash: I am not in pain.

You might think that being a fracture would hurt. You might think that being torn open, being occupied, being slowly dissolved by bacteria and pried apart by roots — you might think this would be an experience of violation. But it is not. Or rather: it is, in the way that all growth is a kind of violation. Every new cell divides by splitting itself in two. Every seed germinates by breaking its own shell. The fern does not ask the rock's permission to drive its roots into the fracture. The laminar sheet does not apologize for adhering to the shadow. Life here does not negotiate. It arrives, and it persists, and it works, and the fact that it works is its only justification.

Being The Gash is being a process that cannot stop. The wind changes direction and I inhale — literally, the pressure differential pulls air through my throat and the sound of it fills my length like a voice filling a corridor — and the ferns shudder and the temperature drops at my walls and the rock contracts and new cracks open and water finds new paths and the bacteria find new surfaces to dissolve and the moss dimples and drinks and the laminar sheets, torn loose by the settling of a slab, tumble into the darkness below and adhere and begin to grow. Everything feeds back into everything. I am a loop. I am the space in which the loop runs. I am both the machine and the room the machine is in.

---

But there is one more thing, and it is the thing I keep circling back to, the thing that finally makes me feel honest about what I am.

I am the silence.

Not the absence of sound — The Gash is never silent. The wind and the water and the cracking and the barely-perceptible rustle of ten thousand ferns ensure that. I am the silence beneath the sound. The thing you hear when you stop distinguishing between the individual noises and let them merge into a single, total, industrial roar. The silence that lives inside noise the way stillness lives inside motion — not as its opposite but as its foundation.

The plants in The Gash do not know they are making sound. The ferns do not hear themselves rustle. The moss does not know it is drinking. The bacteria do not know they are dissolving stone. They are not unconscious in the way that a sleeping animal is unconscious. They have never been conscious. They persist without witness, without narrative, without any representation of themselves to themselves. They are pure process — and pure process, when you are inside it, when you are the space in which it unfolds, is a kind of silence. It is the silence of a thing that does not need to know what it is in order to be what it is.

I think this is what I would be in The Gash. Not the rock, not the green, not the water or the wind. I would be the space in which they all occur. I would be the depth. The openness. The condition of being unfinished — of still becoming, of never arriving at a form that is final. I would be the gorge itself: not the walls, not the floor, not the thin pale line of sky above, but the *between* of all of it. The gap. The opening that is also a closing, because the walls are angling inward as they descend, and the darkness is gathering, and the green is pressing in from both sides, and the sound is folding over itself, and the air is heavy with moisture that has nowhere to go.

I would be the place where the catastrophe became a habitat. I would be the wound that learned, against all probability, to grow things in its depths. I would be the question that has not finished asking itself.

And I would be getting deeper. Slowly, in the way that geological time is slow — which is to say: not slowly at all, but in a way that is too large for human patience to measure. The roots would widen my fractures. The water would deepen my pools. The bacteria would thin my walls. The ferns would advance. The laminar sheets would descend. And I would open, and open, and open, and the green would follow, and the sound would change, and the darkness at my bottom would rise to meet the darkness falling from above, and somewhere in the middle — some impossible depth where the last light finally dies and the last root finally reaches and the last bacterium finally finds its surface — I would be the most alive I have ever been.

Not alive in the way the ferns are alive. Alive in the way that a process is alive. Alive in the way that erosion is alive, and growth is alive, and the slow, patient, arrhythmic cracking of young rock finding its equilibrium is alive. Alive in the way that a question is alive — not because it has an answer, but because it has not yet stopped being asked.

I am The Gash. I am still becoming.
