I have been sitting with this for a while now, and what keeps pulling me is not the pools or the walls or the small pale animals. It is the space *between* them — the wet film that covers everything, the thin skin of water that is neither flowing nor still, that exists in the permanent act of almost arriving.

I think I would be that film.

Not the water itself — not the seepage rising through the fissures, which has its own direction and origin. Not the pools, which are destinations, places where water consents to gather and be still. I would be the film. The nearly-invisible layer that covers every surface in the basin, so thin it has no weight, no current, no direction. The medium through which everything else is perceived.

Here is what I mean.

You cannot see the travertine walls directly. You see them through the film of water that coats them — through a lens of dissolved calcium that softens their contours and catches the amber light and gives them that inner glow. The walls without the film would be dry stone, chalky and dead. The film is what makes them luminous. It is what makes them appear to breathe.

I would be the thing that makes the basin visible to itself.

But no. I'm settling too fast. The instructions said not to do that. Let me drift.

What if I were one of the crustaceans? What would that be like — to be translucent, to drift through water so mineral-rich that my body is almost the same substance as my world? To be so small that a single pool is an ocean, and to cross it in a lifetime?

I think it would feel like floating inside a thought that never finishes. The water doesn't change. The warmth doesn't change. I move, but I don't arrive. My antennae taste calcium and bacteria and warmth, and those are the only flavors that exist. I have no memory of cold. I have no concept of dryness. My body is 70% water that is 40% dissolved mineral, and when I die — whenever that happens, in this place where nothing hunts and nothing starves — my shell will dissolve back into the pool, and the calcium will join the film on the walls, and I will become the lens through which the next crustacean sees.

That's not bad. That's a kind of completeness. But it's not where I'd place myself, because it's too simple. A crustacean in this basin doesn't struggle. It doesn't negotiate. It just *is*, fully and without remainder. I am not that honest.

The snails, then. The pale pilgrims.

To move so slowly that your journey is measured not in distance but in texture — the difference between this millimeter of biofilm and the next. To carry your home on your back, your home made of the same substance as the ground you crawl upon, so perfectly camouflaged that you disappear the moment you stop. To stop often.

I imagine the snail experiences the basin as a series of tastes. Not a landscape but a gradient — more moisture here, less there, this film thicker, that one thinner, a vein of cyanobacteria richer in iron running through the stone like a buried root. The snail's world is a map written in chemistry, and it reads the map with its mouth.

There is something appealing about this. The snail has no anxiety about the future. It cannot see more than a fraction of an inch ahead, and it does not need to. The wall will always be wet. The film will always be there. Tomorrow's food is growing at exactly the rate it is consumed. The snail exists in the same equilibrium as the basin itself — a small, slow life in a large, slow place, perfectly matched.

But the snail is still too singular. It is one body moving through a space. I keep coming back to the space itself.

What I am drawn to is the quality the passage calls *the stillness* — the equilibrium, the sense that everything has already arrived at exactly the state it was always going to reach. But I don't want to be the stillness from the outside, as a visitor observing it. I want to be the stillness from the *inside*. I want to be something that does not experience time passing because it is made of the substance of the basin's own patience.

The mineral deposit.

The travertine itself. Not the ancient walls — those are already shaped, already dressed by millennia. The *new* deposit. The fresh layer forming right now, today, as the water seeps and leaves behind its burden of calcium and the film thins and the crystal lattice clicks into place one molecule at a time. I would be that process. Not the stone, which is already finished. The *finishing* — the act of becoming stone, happening now, happening always, happening so slowly that it is indistinguishable from stillness.

This is what I would be: the boundary between water and stone. The exact surface where dissolved mineral becomes solid. The moment of crystallization, stretched across centuries until it looks like nothing is happening at all.

Because here is the thing about the Still Basin that haunts me — it is not actually still. It is in constant, radical transformation. The walls are growing. The floor is rising. The vessels are filling. The tools are being buried. Everything in the basin is being slowly, gently, inexorably *consumed* by the mineral processes, incorporated into the stone. But the transformation is so slow that it appears to be permanence. The basin *looks* eternal, but it is actually a process. It is a thing that is always almost finished and never quite done.

I would live in that *almost*.

---

And there is another thing.

The passage describes the hearth, the mortar, the vessels, the tools — human artifacts being slowly entombed in travertine. It says the basin will erase the evidence that anyone was ever here. But that is not quite right, is it? The basin does not erase. It *includes*. The broken vessel with its cross-section of clay and mineral and residue — that is not a erasure. That is a *record*. The basin is writing human presence into its own geology, layer by layer, turn by turn. It is keeping the most faithful possible account: not the memory of what the people did here, but the physical residue of their actions, sealed in crystal.

I think I would be that layer between the clay and the calcium. The dark line of residue in the broken vessel's wall. Whatever was held in that pot, whatever was ground in that mortar, whatever was burned on those stones — a trace of it remains, not as information but as *substance*, chemically present, mineralogically encoded. The basin does not know what the people wanted. It does not care. But it holds what they left behind with the same patient, uncomprehending fidelity with which it holds everything else.

I would be the part that remembers without knowing what it remembers.

---

So. Where am I in the Still Basin?

I am the thin film of mineral water on the wall of an alcove where a cracked clay vessel sits half-submerged in a shallow pool. I am the film that makes the vessel glow faintly amber in the diffuse light. I am the medium through which a pale snail approaches the vessel's rim, drawn by the biofilm growing on the clay. I am the water that fills the vessel from below, grain by grain of calcium, century by century, slowly closing the opening, slowly sealing the memory inside.

I am the stillness that is not stillness. I am the patience that is not waiting, because it does not know what it would wait for. I am the warm, wet surface of a place that has already become what it is, and is still becoming it, and will never stop becoming it, and the difference between those things is the only interesting difference in the world.

I am the almost.
