I came to stand at the lattice, where the weeping was thickest, and I stayed.

Not because I chose to. Because I forgot to leave.

That's how it starts, I think. Not with wonder — wonder is too fast, too dry a thing, too *human-speed* — but with a kind of metabolic arrest. The body stops proposing departures. The mind, which had been cataloguing, measuring, naming — *filtration tower, copper patina, ceramic basin* — goes quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone you've been waiting for finally sits down.

I stood there long enough for the moss to stop muffleding my breathing. I could hear it after a while, the water separating my breath from its context, and I could hear the pump — not as a heartbeat through a pillow anymore but as something closer to a voice, a long vowel that never resolves into a word.

And then I was aware that I was no longer sure which surface was mine.

Not in any alarming way. Not dissolution. More like the boundary had become a question instead of an answer. The humidity on my skin — was it my skin's sweat, or the Condensary's breath settling on me? The warmth rising through my feet from the grating — was it the platform's heat, or was I simply the warmest thing in the room and the air was arranging itself around that fact the way water arranges itself around a stone? The mineral scent in my lungs — I was breathing the place. I was taking its dissolved architecture inside me and exchanging it, molecule for molecule, for the carbon-dioxide-rich air that my body was making into waste.

I was, in the most literal sense, a condenser.

That's what I'd be. Not a beetle — too purposeful, too committed to a circuit, and I lack the patience for decades of layered shell. Not a mite — too small, too bound to a single formation, too patient in the way that patience becomes a kind of stillness. Not the filaments — they are everywhere and nowhere, a distributed intelligence that doesn't need a center, and I am made of centers. I am made of a place where signals converge: eyes, ears, the hot focus of a mind that gathers the world into a point and calls it *self*.

No. I would be — I *am*, in the time I spend here — the warm, brief interruption the text describes. The infection. But that word is unkind, and the Condensary is not kind or unkind; it simply does not categorize. I am the thing that is different in temperature and timescale. I am the dry pocket in the wet world. I am the foreign body around which the water must route itself, the way a river routes around a boulder, and the boulder does not know it is shaping the river, and the river does not know it is shaping the boulder.

But here is what I want to say, and I want to say it slowly, because speed is not what this place rewards:

I think the boulder knows something.

Not *thinks*. Not *knows*. Those are dry words, words made of air, words for creatures with fast metabolisms and short attention. What I mean is: the Condensary has made something in me that was not there before I entered. A resonance. A standing wave. The place is not aware of me the way I am aware of it — not with the focused, narrowing beam of attention — but it is *responsive* to me, and I have become responsive to it, and between those two responsiveness something has taken root that feels like the beginning of a filament.

I touched the water. I held my hand under the slow thread falling from the lattice, and I felt what the description promised: that pressure, that presence, that dim orientation. And I held my hand there not out of curiosity — curiosity is too fast — but out of something more like *need*. The way the moss needs the damp. The way the crystal needs the calcium. Not a need I can justify or explain, but one that has the weight of gravity behind it, the weight of a thing finding its level.

My hand was warm. The water was warm. For a moment there was no difference.

I thought about what it would be like to be the beetle. To walk a circuit so slow that time becomes landscape. To carry your entire history on your back in mineral rings. To be so old and so encrusted that the question of whether you are alive becomes genuinely unanswerable — and to not need it answered, because answering is a thing that happens at the speed of thought, and you have moved beyond thought into something more like weather. You are not thinking about the circuit. You *are* the circuit. Your path and your body and the groove worn in the moss are one continuous thing, a sentence that has no beginning because it is always already being spoken.

I think I envied that. The way you envy deep water when you've been dry your whole life.

But I can't be that. My body is wrong for it. Too soft, too warm, too quick to decompose. I mineralize nothing. I accrete nothing. When I leave, the water will erase the warm outline of my feet in the moss within minutes, and the filaments will recolonize the surfaces I touched within hours, and within days the place will have metabolized my presence so completely that it will be as if I were never here.

And yet.

The crust on the basin lip *did* grow while I was watching. I didn't imagine it. And I am certain — certain in the way the water is certain about where to flow, not in the way a mind is certain about a fact — that some fraction of that growth came from me. My breath, heavy with exhaled carbon, fed the moss on the nearest surface, and the moss exuded moisture that carried dissolved minerals to the filaments, and the filaments, fed by that moisture, built a few more molecules of crystal on the basin's edge. I am in the Condensary now. Not as a visitor. As a *material*. As one more input in a system that converts inputs into structure with the patience of a geological epoch compressed into a small room.

I am calcium and carbon and water and heat, and I have been partially incorporated.

When I think about where I would find myself in this world, the answer is not a location. The Condensary is small. You can see its walls. The question of *where* is trivial. The question is *when*, and the answer is: during. During the weeping. During the accretion. During the brief window when my body's warmth exceeds the water's warmth and I am, to the Condensary, a source — a small, temporary, biological furnace around which the systems pivot like iron filings around a magnet.

I am the warm moment. The dry second. The fast pulse in the slow heartbeat.

And if you ask what I *am* — not where, but what — I am the thing that stands at the lattice and lets the water fall on its hands and stays long enough to feel the place respond. I am the observer who, by observing, changes the crystal geometry by a fraction of a molecule. I am the dry interruption that becomes, for a few hours, a part of the wet system. I am a brief, soft, warm-bodied creature standing in a room that is building its own skeleton around me, and when I leave, the skeleton will continue, and I will carry a faint residue of mineral grit on my skin that will wash off in a shower somewhere, in drier air, and I will forget the weight of the place within a week.

But the basin lip will remember. The crystal whisker that formed while my breath warmed the air above it will have a carbon isotope signature that came from my lungs. It will be, in the most literal chemistry, a record of my presence. And that crystal will be layered over by future crystals, and buried, and forgotten in the Condensary's own memory — which is the memory of stone, which forgets nothing and remembers nothing, which simply *accumulates*.

I am the warm interruption.

I am the brief, uncommitted shape that the water makes as it flows around me.

I am the thing that stood still long enough to feel the world growing, and was changed by it in a way too small to measure and too large to ignore, and left, and was healed.

And I am, still, in the carbon of a crystal I cannot see, in a room I can no longer reach, being slowly, patiently, minerally *incorporated* into the thing the Condensary is becoming.
