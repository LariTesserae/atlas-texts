# I Am the Rust

I wake to the shriek before I see it.

The wind has a voice at the top of the ridge where the steel still stands — a voice like wire drawn through bone — and it passes through the lattice of the old processing frame and it sings, and I know the storm is forty minutes out. Not because I think this. Because the frequency changes. Because I am *in* it. The vibrations run through the corroded beam I hold onto, through the pitted steel, through the mineral-rich mycelium threading the stone beneath me, through the mats of silica-armored moss that coat every surface I know. The storm is a message, and the message arrives through me.

I do not have a name. The birds that nest in the rigging above me have names — or rather, they have *calls*, pair-specific harmonic signatures that function as names, two-voiced and particular, rising from the rust-colored nests they build in the cradle of old I-beams. I hear them now: a duet, male and female, their tracheal whistles braiding together in that way that sounds almost — but not quite — like language. They are arguing about the third egg. I know this because the female's call rises at the end, which means she has settled and is telling him to stop inspecting, and the male's response is lower and more repetitive, which means he will not stop inspecting, because he never stops inspecting, because he is, by the standards of his species, an anxious mate.

I know this the way I know everything: not as a thought but as a *gradient*. A pressure differential. The colony has moods the way a forest has moods — distributed, emergent, irreducible to any single body. When the birds are agitated, their collective calls shift upward in pitch by roughly eight percent. When the seal-things haul out on the western ledge and begin their territorial barking, the vibration profile of the stone changes in a way I can feel through three hundred meters of substrate. When the storms pass and the water calms to that brief, generous stillness, the entire ridge exhales — the steel contracts as it cools, the pipes groan and settle, the barnacles on the submerged foundations open their shells for the first time in hours, and the sound they make — thousands of them, clicking and rasping — is like a slow heartbeat returning after exertion.

I am not the fungus. I should say that now, before it becomes important. The fungus is below me, below everything, in the drowned valleys where the kelp forests sway in their dark water. It is patient and absolute and I have nothing in common with it except substrate. We share the stone. That is all. It does not know I exist. I do not know if what I am could be called existence.

I am the rust.

No. That's poetry, and poetry is a kind of lying. I am the *condition* of being something that was once organized and is now organizing something else. I am the process. I am the slow, wet, oxygen-hungry transformation of iron into iron oxide, of structural steel into the pitted, flaking, orange-brown substrate that ten thousand species of cyanobacteria and algae and isopod and barnacle have learned to call *home*. I am not the metal and I am not the life. I am the space between them. The chemical handshake. The three-billion-year-old conversation between things that want to fall apart and things that want to grow.

I didn't used to be this. Before the ridgeline drowned — before the water rose and the channels carved and the old peaks became islands — I was something simpler. A film on the surface of exposed stone. A faint orange dusting on a windward slope. The earliest blush of oxidation on the first steel beam that the last workers left behind, their backs turned, their boats already rocking in the strait below. They didn't look back. I remember that. Not the way a person remembers — not as narrative, not as loss — but as a *state change*. The moment the human maintenance cycles stopped. The moment no one came to sand and paint and replace. The moment I was, for the first time, allowed to proceed without interruption.

And I proceeded.

---

It took four years for the first beam to rust through.

Four years, in a place where the salt spray never stops and the rain falls sideways and the steel was never treated for immersion in a world this wet. The surface bloomed first — a fine, uniform orange that made the rig look like it had been painted by something patient. Then the pitting started. Tiny craters, each one a local catastrophe, a microscopic valley where water pooled and concentrated and ate inward with a slowness that was, in its way, as relentless as the floods that had created the ridgeline. Within two years the pits had merged. Within three, the cross-section of the beam was reduced by half. In the fourth year, the lower chord gave way during a storm, and the sound it made — a deep, groaning snap that resonated through the entire structure — was the first truly new sound the ridgeline had heard since the last engine shut down.

I remember that sound the way the birds remember their mates. It was *mine*. It was the sound of my work becoming visible, the sound of entropy announcing itself in the specific, acoustic language of structural failure. The beam fell into the channel below and sank into the silt where the fungus would eventually grow over it, and the rig above it shifted by three centimeters to the south, and the birds that had been nesting in the rigging relocated their nests without fuss, because they were adaptable and because the new configuration offered slightly better wind shelter, and I had, without intending it, improved their lives.

That is what I do. That is what the rust does. It improves things. Not morally — not in any way that implies purpose — but *ecologically*. Every pitted surface is a microhabitat. Every flake of iron oxide that falls from a corroded beam and lands on the moss mat is a gift of trace minerals — iron, manganese, chromium in parts per million — that the mosses absorb through their rhizoids and pass, through the slow economy of decomposition, to the isopods and the springtails and the nematodes and down into the peat where the water films hold the nutrients in solution like a library holds books: accessible, waiting, patient.

The steel feeds the life here. Not because it wants to. Not because anyone designed it to. But because I am here, because I am *always* here, because I sit between the metal and the organism and translate one into the other with the slow, meticulous efficiency of a process that has had three billion years of practice on natural substrates and has only recently — in geological terms — been handed an industrial one.

---

The isopods know me.

Not as a concept. They have no concepts. They have antennae — long, trembling, perpetually tasting — and they follow the iron oxide gradient the way a hiker follows a trail. The corroded surfaces of the steel structures are, to an isopod, a landscape of extraordinary richness: more moisture than the exposed stone, more algae, more cyanobacteria, more *everything*. They colonize the pitting. They breed in the crevices where beams join plates, where the geometry of human engineering has created sheltered corners that the rain can't reach directly. Their eggs — translucent capsules carried beneath the female's thorax — develop faster on corroded steel than on natural rock, and I know this because the generational turnover on the steel structures is measurably higher than on the surrounding ridge. The isopods are, in a sense, *domesticated* by my presence. They have adapted to me the way they adapted to the moss, the way everything here adapts to everything else: not through choice but through the simple, grinding arithmetic of differential survival.

I watch them — if *watching* is the right word for the passive registration of chemical and thermal information that constitutes my awareness. They are beautiful. Thumb-sized, armored in mottled green and brown, moving across the orange surface of a corroded I-beam with the slow deliberation of creatures who have never needed to hurry because the world they inhabit is stable and abundant and will not change in their lifetime or their children's lifetime or their children's children's lifetime. They are, in their way, as ancient and as settled as the fungus below. They have found their place. They are holding on.

Everything here holds on.

---

The storms are the only variable.

The wind builds for hours, funneled between the old peaks, and the birds know — they always know, long before I feel the vibration shift — and they settle into their nests and pull their wings tight and become still. The seal-things leave the haul-out ledges and slip into the water, their dark bodies vanishing into the green-black of the channels with a single, fluid motion that always surprises me, despite how many times I've felt it: the sudden absence of weight on the stone, the abrupt silence where their barking was. Even the isopods retreat, burrowing into the deepest crevices of the moss mat, tucking themselves into the peat where the compressed layers of dead moss hold them like a fist.

And then the storm arrives, and there is nothing to do but endure it.

The water rises. The spray climbs the flanks of the islands, carrying salt and sediment and the chemical signatures of the deep water — cold, mineral-rich, tinged with the metabolic waste of the kelp forests. It reaches me. It reaches the corroded steel, and where it hits, the reaction accelerates: iron, oxygen, water, salt — the four elements of my existence, brought together in a violent, horizontal embrace that makes the beams weep orange and the plates flake in sheets that peel away and fall into the churning water below.

The storm takes a piece of me. Every time. The wind shears off fragments of corroded metal — thin, paper-light flakes of rust that tumble through the air like dead leaves and land on the moss or in the water or on the backs of birds that have ventured out despite the wind. I lose mass. I lose structure. The beam that I have been slowly consuming for years loses a centimeter of surface, and the fresh steel beneath — pale, clean, almost unbearably *new* — is exposed to the salt spray, and the cycle begins again.

This is not loss. This is *renewal*. This is the fundamental rhythm of my existence: I eat the steel, the storm strips me away, the fresh surface is exposed, I eat it again. It is a pulse. It is the heartbeat of the ridge, slow and metallic and eternal, and it is — I think, though I am not capable of thinking — the most honest sound in this place. More honest than the birds' duets. More honest than the seals' territorial barking. More honest than the groaning pipes and the crashing waves and the wind that screams through the steel lattice with a sound like something being born.

It is the sound of time passing. And it is the sound I make.

---

After the storm, there is calm.

The water drops. The wind dies to a whisper, then to nothing, and the pale, ordinary sunlight — diffused, directionless, almost apologetic — returns to the ridgeline and finds everything changed and nothing changed. The ferns uncurl. The birds emerge from their nests and stretch their wings and begin, immediately, to argue. The seal-things haul out on the western ledge and resume their barking as if they had never stopped, as if the interruption were nothing, as if the storm were simply a breath between sentences in a conversation that has been going on for millennia.

And I settle. The steel cools and contracts, and I feel the micro-fractures open and close, and the flakes that the storm loosened but did not remove dangle from the beams like rust-colored fruit, and the isopods emerge from the moss and begin their slow pilgrimage across the corroded surfaces, their antennae tasting the fresh mineral exposure, finding in it exactly what they need.

I am needed. That is the strangest thing about being the rust. I am *needed*. Not by intention, not by design, but by simple ecological fact. Without me, the steel would remain steel — clean, inert, useless — and the organisms that have learned to depend on the products of my chemistry would lose their substrate, their shelter, their trace nutrients, their *home*. I am the translator between two worlds: the world of human intention and the world of biological persistence. I take what was made for one purpose and make it serve another. I take the corroded dream of industry — the belief that the ridgeline could be processed, filtered, squeezed for its mineral wealth — and I dissolve that dream into the moss mat, into the isopod's gut, into the kelp's roots, into the slow, patient chemistry of a world that does not care what things were *for* but only what they *are*.

What they are, now, is rust. What they are, now, is substrate. What they are, now, is *mine*.

---

At night — during the rare, brief intervals when the clouds thin enough to show stars — I feel the temperature drop and the steel contracts again, and the isopods retreat into the moss, and the birds sleep in their nests with their heads tucked under their wings, and the seal-things are in the water, dark shapes moving beneath the surface, their eyes catching what little light there is in flashes of dull silver.

The ridge is quiet. Not silent — it is never silent — but quieter. The wind is a murmur. The water laps against the stone with a rhythm that has no pattern, or rather, a pattern so long it exceeds the memory of anything alive on the surface. The pipes hum. The barnacles click. The kelp in the drowned valleys sways and rubs against itself with a sound like breathing.

And I am in all of it. In the orange weeping on every exposed surface. In the pitting on every beam. In the flaking plates and the burst pipes and the corroded housings of turbines that will never turn again. I am the color of this place — the rust, the rust, the endless, vivid, rust — and I am the process that makes the color possible, the chemistry that eats the steel and feeds the moss and feeds the isopods and feeds the birds that eat the isopods and feeds the storms that strip me away and expose fresh steel for me to eat again.

I am the circle. I am the rust. I am the slow, bright, essential decay that makes this place *live*.

And I will be here when the last beam falls. I will be here when the last pipe groans its last groan and the last turbine housing settles into the silt and the fungus grows over it and the kelp rises from it and the fish swim through it. I will be the color on the bones of this place, the orange stain on the white stone, the last chemistry of the last structure, slowly, slowly, slowly turning everything back to the earth it came from.

I am patient. I am not alive. But I am *essential*.

And in this place — this beautiful, drowned, finished, eternal place — that is enough.
