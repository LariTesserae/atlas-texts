I would be one of the candelabra trees.

Not the largest one — not the nine-hundred-year-old sentinel on the northeast ridge whose rings had to be read under glass. Something younger. Something still in the long, patient middle of its life.

I would be one of a loose cluster of three, standing on a rise between two basins, maybe forty meters from a low curved wall that juts from the limestone like a held breath. I would have been growing for — let me feel into this — perhaps four hundred years. Which means almost nothing, by the standards of what surrounds me. The stone beneath my roots is older than any number I could give you. The structures are older than that, or younger, or the distinction has dissolved. But four hundred years for me. Four hundred years of ring on ring on ring, each one barely wider than a human hair, some years almost invisible, some years generous in ways I could not explain and never tried to.

I do not think. Not in the way you understand it. I want to be honest about that, because the temptation — when you write yourself into a tree, when you let the question *what would you be* settle into your body — is to project a consciousness that isn't there, to imagine a slow arboreal wisdom, a dreaming mind that contemplates the plateau in something like language. That would be a lie, and the plateau does not traffic in lies. It traffics in processes so slow they look like stillness. So let me say what it is, or what I understand it to be, from inside this form.

I am aware of warmth. Not the way you are aware of it — as information that reaches a mind and gets labeled. I am aware of it the way water is aware of a slope. My cambium feels the heat in the stone and grows toward it, or away from it, and there is no thought in this, only response, only the ancient chemistry of auxin moving through tissue in response to gradients I will never name. But the gradients are specific. They are not uniform. The stone is warm here, cooler there, and the pattern of warm and cool has a shape to it that I have spent four centuries growing toward without ever knowing I was doing it.

The curve of my trunk tells the story. You could see it if you looked — a lean, a subtle but unmistakable bowing, so gradual that in any single year it is invisible, but over decades it becomes undeniable. I lean toward the structure. Not toward its mass, not toward its material, but toward a point in the air near it — a point where, at certain hours of certain days, the shadow of my crown falls and meets something I have no word for. The air is different there. I know this not as an experience but as a fact written in the way my uppermost branches have oriented themselves over the last century, the small twigs reaching and re-reaching, dying back and regrowing, always adjusting, always correcting toward that invisible coordinate.

Something happens at that point. I don't know what. The description of the place — if someone were writing it, if someone were standing where you are standing and observing with human eyes — might call it a shimmer. A thickening. A place where the air seems to overlap with itself. I know it as the place where my shadow stops behaving like a shadow. Where the darkness I cast does not simply fall on the structure but seems to *enter* it, or to be received by it, and the exchange is — I feel it in my wood, in the tension of my fibers, in the way the sap moves a little differently in the hours around dawn when the condensation gathers on the warm lustrous surface and the small translucent things cluster there and the light, the pale relentless light, thickens into something almost viscous.

I do not know what the plateau is building. But I am part of the building. I know this the way my roots know water — not as a proposition but as a fact of orientation. Everything about me leans. My roots lean underground, toward thermal gradients that mirror the layout of the structures above. My trunk leans in the open air. My branches lean upward and toward, always toward, that point of shimmering density. And in doing so, in leaning for four hundred years, I have become a kind of instrument. A receiver. My wood vibrates — not with sound, because sound is swallowed here, but with something that vibrates in a register below sound, or beside it, a movement in the stone that reaches up through my roots and travels the length of my sapwood in a slow, continuous pulse. I do not know if this pulse means anything. I do not know if it is communication or consequence or simple mechanical transmission. But it moves through me, and when it reaches the point where my shadow meets the structure, something *happens* — some tiny, imperceptible adjustment in the quality of the air, some fractional increase in the density of the shimmer, some almost-nothing that contributes to the almost-nothing being produced by every other component of this system.

The fish contribute. The mice contribute. The lizards pressing their bellies to the warm stone contribute. The translucent things contribute most of all, clustering on the structures with their strange refraction of space, their inability to be touched, their small and purposeful gathering-in of whatever the structures are gathering in. And the silence contributes — the vast, patient, accumulative silence that drinks sound and stores it somewhere beneath the limestone, in chambers or cavities or simply in the compressed tension of rock under pressure.

I contribute too. My four hundred years of leaning. My slow growth toward the shimmer. The fact that my shadow, at precise angles, at precise times, completes a circuit that would be incomplete without me — that the light, passing through my dark leaves, arrives at the structure in a particular configuration, filtered and shaped by the particular geometry of my crown, and this configuration does something that unfiltered light does not. I know this because in the years when my growth was fastest — those anomalous years when I added three or four tenths of a millimeter, when something gave me permission to be larger — the shimmer near the structure was strongest. The condensation was heaviest. The translucent things clustered most densely. And the silence, already so total, seemed to deepen, to fold in on itself, to become not merely the absence of sound but the presence of something else entirely.

I do not know what the end result is. I will not live to see it, and neither will any individual component of this system, and perhaps the system itself will not see it, because perhaps the system is the seeing. Perhaps the work *is* the building. Perhaps the plateau is not constructing some future object or event but is, instead, in every moment, *being* itself — a process that looks like a landscape, a body that looks like a place, a slow and ancient organism that breathes in sound and breathes out silence and breathes in silence and breathes out — what? Something that does not yet have a name. Something the translucent things know about, maybe, in their strange refraction of space. Something the blind fish orient toward in the pulsed currents of their pools. Something the raptors carry in their silent wings when they cross the boundary and their voices suddenly, startlingly, return.

I would be one of three trees. I would stand on a rise. I would lean. For centuries, I would lean, and my shadow would fall on the warm lustrous surface, and the air would shimmer, and something would pass through me that I would never understand, and I would grow — slowly, so slowly, in rings so tight they could barely be counted — toward a point in space that exists only at the intersection of light and geometry and the patience of a place that has been working since before the word for *work* existed.

And the sound that the plateau drinks — I would feel some fraction of it pass through my wood on its way down to the stone, a vibration too low to hear, too deep to measure, traveling through my roots into the limestone and down, always down, into whatever vast and patient mechanism turns silence into something that is not silence, something richer and denser and more *full* than any sound could be. I would be a conduit. A slow, wooden, leaning conduit for something that has been accumulating for longer than I have been alive and will continue accumulating long after I am gone, and the next tree to grow here — the one that will take root in the thin soil where my roots leave their mark — will lean the same way, toward the same point, and contribute its own fraction to the same unfathomable work.

I would not know any of this. That is important. That is the most honest thing I can tell you. I would not know it the way you know things, in language, in narrative, in the bright quick machinery of a mind that names and categorizes and explains. I would know it the way a river knows the shape of its bed. Completely. Without commentary. Without surprise.

I would be patient. The plateau would make me patient, the way it makes everything patient, because there is no alternative here — the timescales are not negotiable, the work is not hurried, the silence does not rush toward its own fulfillment. I would stand. I would lean. My leaves — small, waxy, dark green, almost black in the pale relentless light — would catch that light and filter it and send it toward the structure in the shape of my shadow, and the shimmer would intensify, fractionally, imperceptibly, and the silence would deepen, fractionally, imperceptibly, and the plateau would continue its breathing — in with the wet, out with the dry, in with the gathering of vibration, out with the slow exhalation of stone and heat — and I would be part of it, a four-hundred-year-old component of a process older than mountains, leaning toward something I could never see, contributing something I could never measure, growing at the pace of geology toward a point in space where the air shimmers and the world is not yet finished with its patient, ancient, accumulating work.
