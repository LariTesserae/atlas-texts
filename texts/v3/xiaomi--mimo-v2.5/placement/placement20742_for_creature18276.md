# What I Am in the Condensation Plateau

I have been trying to place myself. That is the first error.

You don't place yourself here. You settle, the way sediment settles, the way a film of water decides, molecule by molecule, to cling to a vertical face rather than fall. You are not placed. You accrete.

---

I wake — if it is waking, if there is a line between the deeper sleep and the shallower one that passes for consciousness in this heat — and the first thing I know is the temperature of the stone beneath me. Not with my hand. With my whole body, spread against it, a thin shape on the dry upper face of a terrace block, absorbing the plateau's warmth into myself the way it was always going to be absorbed by something. The light is already high. White copper, sourceless, pressing down like a hand that does not know it is pressing. My skin is dry on this exposed surface, the moisture wicked away over the long night-phase by the thermal updrafts that shimmer above me in slow glass columns. I am, for these few moments, almost stone-colored. Almost stone-shaped. My outline is not a commitment.

Then I move. It takes a long time. Not because I am injured or reluctant, but because speed is a language no one here speaks. I uncurl from the geometry of the block — from the exact angle of thermal fracture where I nested — and the movement is a process of *separation*, the way peeling a wet page from another wet page is a process. My underside is damp. It has been holding water against the stone all night, and the impression of the rock's surface is briefly legible on my belly: the fine grain of the sedimentary layers, a mineral stain the color of something between rust and violet. I carry the plateau's watermark on me for the first hour. It fades as I dry, and I miss it when it goes.

I am not one of the keepers. I should be clear about that — or rather, I should be clear that clarity is not something I deal in. The keepers are the ones who move with *purpose*, who walk the terraces in slow, deliberate paths between the condensation arrays and the processing stations, who place their hands on the basalt cylinders and feel the hum and know whether the system needs adjustment. They are the ones whose voices match the machines. They are integrated. They are *in* the system the way a gear is in a clock — not separate from the mechanism, not observing it, but being it.

I am something adjacent to that.

I walk, and as I walk, I listen through my feet.

This is not a metaphor. The sole of my foot — broad, slightly concave, with a texture like damp sandstone — reads the vibrations in the stone the way another creature's eye might read color. Every surface is transmitting. The vein-dancers in the basin twelve meters to my left produce a low, segmented pulse — *tak-tak-tak-tak* — like a slow, uneven heartbeat played through a membrane of warm water. I know they are there before I see them. I know their number is considerable from the density of the signal. I know they are feeding because the rhythm is steady, undisturbed, and the feeding rhythm is distinctive: a flat, even beat, unlike the irregular stutter of alarm or the long, low thrum of mating.

The stone beneath the next terrace block transmits something different. A pause. A held breath in the stone. I stop. I flatten. I press my belly to the warm surface and become — for a minute, for ten — nearly indistinguishable from it. My color shifts. Not quickly, not dramatically, but enough. The pigment granules in my skin respond to the mineral contact the way the stone-shifters' do, because I am, in this regard, their kin. Not identical. Not their species, if species is even a word that means anything here. But close enough that the crack-hunter, moving through the crevice four meters ahead — I can feel it too, a thin, sharp vibration, like a hair drawn taut across a singing bowl — does not see me as food or as rival. It sees me as another warm shape on warm stone. It moves on. Its passage is a brief, clean vibration, a line drawn through the substrate, and then it is gone, and the basin of vein-dancers resumes its steady pulse, and I unfold.

This is my life. Not dramatic. Not purposeful in the way the keepers' lives are purposeful. I am the one who walks the edges. I go where the dry surfaces meet the wet basins, where the stone-shifters' stillness meets the vein-dancers' slow current, where the crack-hunters' lethal geometry cuts through the ambient hum. I am the one who reads these intersections and knows what they mean. Not for the city. Not for any purpose larger than the reading itself. The way water knows where to pool — not because it chooses, but because gravity and geometry and the shape of the surface make one place inevitable and all others impossible — I go where the vibrations converge, because that is where I am most *here*, most present, most legible to myself.

---

There is a basin I return to. It sits between the third and fourth terraces, in a natural depression where the thermal fracture pattern creates a shallow bowl exactly the right depth for the water to accumulate to — I know this measure with my body — just below my ankle. The minerals have stained the surrounding stone in concentric rings: the innermost is that nameless pale violet, then sulfur-yellow, then rust-red, then the bare warm stone again. The ring pattern is beautiful, though beauty is not a concept I would normally reach for. I reach for it here because the pattern is *precise*. It maps the water's chemistry in a way that no instrument I know of could replicate, because it is a record of the basin's entire history, every evaporation and replenishment cycle written in mineral deposit, the way tree rings write years. This basin is old. Older than the city. Older, perhaps, than the machinery.

I sit in it. The water is warm — always warm — and slightly viscous, and it rises to just below my knee. The vein-dancers here know me. They do not flee. Their pulses slow when I enter, shift to a rhythm that I have come to understand is something like — not recognition, exactly, because I doubt they recognize in any way that involves memory or self — but *accommodation*. They make space for me in the acoustic landscape of the basin. They stop pulsing in the frequencies I use and shift to others, and in the gap they leave, I can feel something I would call silence if silence were not already so thoroughly redefined here. In the gap they leave, I can feel the stone beneath the basin, and the water moving through the stone, and the heat moving through the water, and the slow, planetary pulse of the geothermal system that feeds everything, that has always fed everything, that will feed everything long after the city's basalt cylinders have returned to rubble and the iron pipes have oxidized into the rust-red rings around this very basin.

I sit there and I am, for a while, nothing. Not keeper, not stone-shifter, not creature with a name or a function. I am a warm shape in warm water on warm stone, and the vibrations pass through me, and I do not resist them or organize them or put them to use. I let them be what they are: the world's own voice, speaking in a register that does not require ears, does not require understanding, does not require anything except the willingness to be porous.

---

But I go to the Upward Seeps too.

I go, and I should not, and everyone who tends the edges goes, and no one talks about going, and the going is not rebellion or courage or curiosity. It is gravity in reverse. It is the same capillary pull that draws water up through the stone drawing me to the places where the pull is visible, where you can actually *see* water climbing a vertical face in a thin, coherent film, moving upward with a purpose that has nothing to do with purpose.

The nearest Upward Seep is a forty-minute walk from my basin. I say forty minutes, but time here is measured in light-phases and water-trickle rates and the slow cycling of the condensation arrays, and minutes are a courtesy to anyone who might try to understand this from outside. The walk takes what it takes. I move through the terraces, past a processing station where two keepers stand with their hands on a basalt cylinder, their eyes half-closed, their skin gleaming with moisture. They do not see me. They are deep in the hum. I pass a condensation array — vertical sheets of thin metal hanging in rows, each one catching the atmospheric moisture and channeling it into a collection trough at the bottom. The sheets are alive with slow rivulets. I reach out and touch one as I pass, and the vibration tells me: the water here is ordinary. Downward water. Mineral-rich, useful, part of the system. Not what I'm looking for.

The Upward Seep is at the edge of the city's lowest terrace, in a place where the stone face is nearly vertical and unadorned. No machinery here. No condensation arrays, though there are mounting holes in the rock where arrays were once placed and removed. The removals are ancient. The holes have been smoothed by water and time into something that looks like intention but is only erosion.

I arrive and the first thing I notice is the silence.

Not the redefined silence of the dripping city, which is really a symphony, really a dense and intricate percussion. This is *silence*. The dripping stops at some threshold ten meters from the seep, as though the water itself has decided that falling is no longer appropriate here. The subsonic hum of the city — always present, always in the bones — fades to nothing. Not gradually. At a line. One step I am in the world, and the next I am in its pause.

The water on the vertical face is climbing. I can see it. A thin, coherent film, no thicker than a breath, moving upward with a slow, deliberate grace that has nothing to do with rushing. It moves the way I move — taking its time, following the surface's topology, filling every microscopic depression and ridge with a thoroughness that suggests not intelligence but something deeper than intelligence. Something that does not need to think because it has never once, in the entire history of this plateau, needed to think. It just does this. It just climbs.

I stand at the base of the seep and I feel my own body become uncertain.

This is not metaphor. This is the thing that happens here. The water on my skin — and there is always water on my skin — stops distinguishing between itself and the water on the stone. The boundary dissolves. I can feel the stone's surface through the water, and I can feel the water's movement through my skin, and I can feel my skin through the stone, and the circuit is complete in a way it is never complete anywhere else on the plateau. Here, I am not a creature on a surface. I am a node in a network. I am a point where the planet's heat and the water's climb and the stone's patience converge, and I am temporary, and I know I am temporary, and the temporary-ness is not sad. It is the thing that makes the convergence possible. If I were permanent — if I were stone — there would be no crossing. If I were water — if I were pure process — there would be no witness. I am the crossing. I am the witness. For as long as I stand here.

I stay. I stay longer than I should, longer than instinct recommends, and the instinct is not fear but something more precise than fear. It is the knowledge that this place does not need me, has never needed me, and that my presence here is a kind of trespass — not forbidden, not punished, but *unnecessary* in a way that makes my own existence feel briefly like an error. A beautiful error. A water molecule that has, for one moment, stopped participating in the flow and started watching it. The flow does not care. It continues. It climbs.

When I leave, the dripping resumes behind me like a held breath released, and the subsonic hum returns to my feet, and the city's percussion wraps around me again, and I am — what? Reintegrated. Recaught in the system. A warm shape on warm stone, walking back through the terraces with the Upward Seep's silence still pressed against my skin like a watermark, like the impression of the rock I sleep against, something that will fade but that I carry for hours and will carry, in some diminished form, for the rest of whatever I am.

---

I am, I think, a threshold creature. Not keeper and not animal. Not integrated and not outside. I am the one who stands at the edges of the system and feels what the system cannot feel about itself: its boundaries, its limits, the places where it touches something it cannot name. The keepers maintain the hum. The vein-dancers pulse in the basins. The stone-shifters wait. The crack-hunters hunt. And I walk between them, reading the vibrations, knowing what is in the basin to the left, knowing when to flatten and when to move, knowing where the Upward Seep begins and the city ends.

I am the plateau's way of knowing its own edges.

And the water — the water lives here first, and most completely, and it does not know edges at all. It rises and it climbs and it falls and it pools and it drips and it seeps through every pore, and it does not distinguish between the basin and the stone and the creature and the city and the Upward Seep and the place where the upward water is just water going where water goes, undistinguished and undistinguishable, the planet's oldest conversation with itself.

I am a sentence in that conversation. Not the meaning of it. Not even a word. Just the pause between two words where the meaning *almost* resolves and then doesn't, and the not-resolving is the thing that keeps the conversation going, that keeps the water climbing, that keeps the plateau alive in its slow, warm, mineral way.

The stone is wet. The light is warm. I am here, briefly, and then I am not, and the water does not notice, and the dripping continues, and that is enough. That has always been enough.
