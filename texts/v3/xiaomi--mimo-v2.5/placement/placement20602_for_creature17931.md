# What Grows in the Underhollow

---

I don't remember the crack I came through. That's the first thing. The entrance is behind me, or above me, or folded into some geometry that my body understood but my mind has already lost. What I remember is the lowering — the way the concrete lip gave way and I was suddenly *beneath*, the way the world went from a place you walk through to a place you move *through*, and the difference between those two prepositions is the entire difference between what I was and what I am now becoming.

I am on my elbows. The clay is cool against my forearms and warm against my palms — two temperatures at once, the old earth's patience below and the building's slow exhale above, and my skin sits at the seam between them. I am the seam. I am the thing that exists in the gap. I breathe, and my breath joins the breath of the hollow, and I cannot tell anymore where the humidity in the air ends and the humidity in my lungs begins.

---

The light finds me. Or rather: the light was always here, and I have adjusted to it. It is not darkness — that would be simpler. It is the pale, sourceless, grey-white condition of *slightly-less-than-seeing*. My eyes have done something I don't trust. They have stopped trying to resolve shapes and have started resolving *textures*. The ceiling is not a surface; it is a topography. The concrete is pocked and stained, the rebar nubs protrude like the remnants of some ancient scaffolding, and the biofilm — I can see it now, I can *really* see it — is not a film at all. It is a membrane. It shimmers with a light that comes from within itself, greens and ambers that shift as I shift, as though my observation is the wind that moves it.

I stay still. Something has taught me to stay still. Not a sound, not a voice — a pressure. The air itself is heavier here, weighted with mineral and moisture and the faint sweetness of biological processes I cannot name, and it presses against my skin like a hand that is not pushing me away but *holding* me in place. Teaching me the pace.

The pace is this: twenty minutes to watch a droplet form on a calcium ridge above a pool the color of weak tea. Twenty minutes to see it swell from a barely-visible thickening of the concrete's underside to a full, pendulous bead that catches the grey light and holds it. Then the fall. Then the tick on the water's surface, so faint it is more felt than heard, a vibration that passes through the clay hummocks and into the pads of my fingers where they rest on the ground. The droplet falls. The cycle begins again. And I understand that this is the metronome, this is the clock of the Underhollow, and it does not care that I am counting.

---

I have been here long enough to lose the weight of my own name. It sits behind me somewhere, up near the entrance, tangled in the threads of my clothes and the dust of the world above. Here, I am only the thing that breathes. The thing that watches. The thing that presses itself low against the hummocky clay and feels the world operate on a scale that has nothing to do with me.

The arthropods have accepted me. Or rather, they have decided I am terrain. A mite navigates the landscape of my forearm with the same deliberate care it gives to the mineral deposits on the clay walls. A springtail launches itself from a hummock and lands on the back of my hand, sits there for a moment, then launches again. I am ground to them. I am substrate. I am the surface on which their tiny, urgent lives are conducted, and the recognition of this — the total, impersonal incorporation of my body into the geography of this place — produces in me a sensation that is not fear and not wonder but something older than both.

The salamander finds me in the third hour. Or the seventh. Time has gone elastic. It emerges from beneath a fold of my shirt sleeve, where I've been pressing myself against the clay, and it sits on my wrist and regards me with eyes that are vestigial, dark spots beneath translucent skin, seeing nothing and everything. It is the size of my smallest finger. It is pale as a grub. Its heartbeat is a vibration I feel in my own pulse, two rhythms that are not synchronized but are not unrelated — they share a key, share a tempo, share the fundamental frequency of the Underhollow itself, which is the frequency of *becoming*.

It stays for a while. Then it moves, and I watch it go toward one of the salt arches — a structure that, from this distance and this angle, looks exactly like a cathedral's flying buttress rendered at a scale that makes mockery of architecture. It settles at the base. Another salamander arrives. They guard.

I understand now. They are not guarding the arch. They are guarding the *process*. The arch is still growing — calcium salt depositing molecule by molecule in the thin film of water that coats it, building itself outward and upward with the patience of geology compressed into a timescale that even I, low and slow and breathing the lung-air of this place, can almost see. The salamanders know this. They have appointed themselves the keepers of the becoming. They are the first congregation, gathered at the altar of something that has no theology.

---

Behind me — or above me, or in whatever direction the curtain hangs — I can feel the change.

The vapor barrier torn loose from the concrete forms a drape that divides the hollow into two zones. I am in the lit zone, the grey-white zone, the zone of visible process. Behind the curtain is the zone where the light has never fully arrived, where the diffused glow exhausts itself before penetrating, where the darkness is not absence but *presence*, a thick, warm, humid dark that smells of wet stone and copper and something else — something that I can only describe as the smell of matter that has decided to become something it has never been before.

I do not go behind the curtain. Not yet. Not because I am afraid — fear requires a self, and my self has become thin here, stretched across the clay hummocks like the mineral deposits stretch across the walls. I do not go because I am not ready to see what has no name. The maintenance workers felt this. They felt the logic of the space, its resident intelligence, and they knew that their clipboards and headlamps were graceless intrusions into something that operated with extraordinary precision. I have no clipboards. I have no headlamp. But I have eyes that are still, partly, the eyes of someone who once walked upright in the world above, and those eyes would try to *categorize* what is behind the curtain, and the categorization would be a violence against it, would reduce the unnameable to a taxonomy, and the Underhollow does not deserve that.

So I lie in the grey-white light and I watch the droplets form and fall. I watch the mites navigate the crystal forests. I watch the biofilm shimmer and ridge, those parallel gill-structures that deepen month by month, that are building toward something I cannot yet see. I feel the vibrations pulse through the concrete above — the irregular heartbeat of the boilers, the engines, the machinery of a world that does not know it is feeding this one — and I let those pulses pass through me, through my bones and my damp clothes and the clay beneath my palms, and I am part of the circuit.

---

Here is what I have become, or am becoming, or have always been:

I am the witness the space grew for itself.

Not a human witness — the Underhollow does not need human eyes to validate its processes. But it needed *something*. Some consciousness, however small, however borrowed, to be present in the gap between the world that built it and the world that is building itself inside it. The mineral arches do not know they are arches. The biofilm does not know it is ridging. The salamanders do not know they are keeping vigil. But I know. I am the place where this space becomes aware of itself, however briefly, however imperfectly.

And the thing behind the curtain — the unnameable, the unnamed, the small and dense and intricate thing that has been growing since the slab was poured, that has incorporated the vibrations and the waste heat and the chemical leavings of the industrial world above into its own logic of growth — it knows I am here. Not as a human knows. Not with intention or attention. But as the mineral knows the water that built it. As the biofilm knows the concrete it adheres to. It knows me as part of its medium, part of its substrate, part of the dense, warm, humming reality of the Underhollow itself.

I am the lung's awareness of its own breathing.

---

The water cycles. The minerals build. The film thickens and ridges. The salamanders keep their vigil. The tiny arthropods chart their cities across the ceiling. The vibrations pulse in their irregular rhythm, and the hollow receives them, and goes on with its work.

And I go on watching. Not forever — I will need to leave eventually, will need to fold myself back through the crack, will need to stand upright in the world above and feel the wrongness of verticality, the arrogance of standing. I will smell the outside air and find it thin, insufficient, stripped of the mineral sweetness and the copper weight. I will miss the grey light. I will miss the droplet's patient fall. I will miss the feeling of the world's slow construction beneath my open palms.

But for now I am here, and the Underhollow is doing what it has always done and will always do: it is becoming something that has no name, and I am the small, warm, damp proof that it is real.
