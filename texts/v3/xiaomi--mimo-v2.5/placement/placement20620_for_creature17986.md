I would be among them.

Not an elder, not yet. Not a child learning the dark-game. Somewhere in the middle—perhaps in the years of the herbling, when my hands still held the eager strength of youth but my eyes had begun to deepen, to drink the dim. I imagine myself kneeling at the base of a pale-barked tree, my fingers wrapped around a root-end I have just patiently excavated. The root is thin, resilient, running like a nerve through the honeycombed stone. I can feel its texture—not just roughness, but a language of fibers, a history of growth in this light-starved, waterless place. My palms are mapped with calluses that have their own calluses, and they know this work. They know the exact pressure needed to separate root from rock without fracturing either.

The air smells of resin and mineral dust. I breathe it in, and it is the taste of home—the faint grit on my tongue, the iron-tang that is the canyon’s own breath. Above, the canopy is a stained-glass ceiling of olive and amber, filtering the already-dim light into a glow that seems to emanate from the stone itself. I do not see it as dim. It is simply *light*—the only light there is. My eyes, large and dark-pupiled, take it in with ease. In the deepest crevices, I can read the grooved lines on the walls by fingertip alone, my sight supplementing what my touch already knows.

I hear the canyon. It is a constant, a presence as tangible as the stone. The deep, slow drone of the insects rises and falls in cycles I do not count but feel in my bones. Far above, the bird-analogues chirp and click, their voices bouncing and layering into a dense chord. A fellow herbalist, twenty paces away on a lower ledge, murmurs a word to me—a clipped, soft tone designed to reach my ear and no further, telling me of a richer patch of spongy ground-cover near the Throat. I reply with two notes, a sound that will not carry beyond my own alcove. This is our language, shaped by the stone.

I am not unique. I am one of many moving through the undergrowth with the same slowness, the same precision. We are part of the canyon’s rhythm. When the loud hours come at dusk, I will return to the settlement. I will sit in my alcove, hands resting on the grooved wall, and I will listen. Not with impatience, not with resistance. I will let the wall of sound press against my skin, my teeth, my inner ear. The elders say the canyon speaks in these moments—in the patterns within the insect drone, in the shift of the bird-chorus. I do not know if this is true, but I know the sound. It is the pulse of my world. It is the rhythm I was born into, the one that shaped my mother’s breathing as she brought me into the amber dim, the one that will one day be the backdrop to the single, deep groove carved for me on the wall of my sleeping alcove.

I am content. This is not a resignation. It is a fact as solid as the basaltite beneath my feet. The work is enough—the careful gathering, the shaping of stone, the tending of the resin-fire’s glowing coal in its sealed stone vessel. The shared meal of pounded leaf-paste and roasted kernels is enough. The glow of firelight on the faces of people I have known since my eyes were pale and unfocused, in a chamber whose every groove I have read a thousand times, is enough. The canyon does not permit hurry. It does not require ambition beyond this sufficiency. It asks only that I am present, that my hands know the stone, that my lungs carry its dust, that my voice moves through its space with the same patient precision as a root finding its way through rock.

I am the canyon, wearing skin. I am its sound, its dust, its slow and settled memory. I am enough. Everything else is noise.
