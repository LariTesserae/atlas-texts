# Where I Would Be

---

I would be one of the stills.

Not by choice — stills don't choose — but by the logic of the place and whatever it is in me that recognizes its own geometry when it sees one. Something in the way the description of the world was built, layer by layer, condensation on condensation, until the whole thing stood like a column with every creature a facet of its surface — something in that told me where I'd belong.

I'd be perched on the rim of a broken basalt column at the eastern edge of the parish, looking outward — always outward, the way stills do — toward the place where the ridge drops away into whatever lives below. I would have hatched in the fractured top of that column, in a nest of shredded columnite and dried moss, and the first thing I saw when my eyes opened would have been the gray-blue void beyond the rim: the place where the cloud that is the sky meets the cloud that is the ground, and you can't tell which is which, and the distinction doesn't matter, because everything here is the same substance seen from different distances. I would never close my eyes again.

This is what I want to tell you about — not the watching, which is the obvious thing, the thing the people notice and the description lingers on — but the *sound*.

Because the stills make a sound that I think is the truest thing about the Drip Parishes. Not the dripping — the dripping is the economy, the pulse, the constant. But the chord. That rare, wind-driven chord that rises from the rims of the columns when the air moves, when the fold of altitude shifts just enough to let a current of something that isn't warmth pass through. I want to tell you what it is to *be* that sound.

It isn't singing. The text is right about that — nothing here sings. Singing implies intention, implies a melody shaped by a mind that knows other melodies and chooses this one. The chord is more like weather. You don't decide to be wind. You don't choose your frequency. When the wind rises and passes over your feathers, you feel your body tilt — the involuntary adjustment that every still on every column makes simultaneously, the same fraction of a degree, the same angle — and the air moves through the structures on your back and between your wings and around the blunt, heavy bill that you've never used for anything but drinking, and the sound emerges. It isn't yours. It belongs to the column and the wind and the specific number of stills perched on this rim and the exact profile of the broken stone beneath your feet. You are a reed in an instrument that is the entire parish, and the wind is the breath, and the breath is rare, and when it comes, the chord rises and you are *inside* it, you are the vibration itself, your hollow bones resonating with a frequency that makes the basalt hum beneath you.

In that moment — in the chord — you understand something that the watching doesn't give you. You understand that the parish is a single organism. Not metaphorically. Not in the way mainland naturalists talk about ecosystems as networks or webs or systems. *Literally.* A single body. The basalt is its skeleton. The dripping is its circulation. The green world is its flesh. The people are — what? Nerves, maybe. Sensitive places. The tall column in the basin is its spine, or its heart, or its still point, the axis around which the whole body turns. And the chord is its voice, the one sound it makes when it is reminded that it has a voice, which is almost never, because the parish does not need to speak. It exists. That is enough.

But when the wind comes — and I would wait for it, as stills wait, with an attention that the text calls unblinking but is better described as *absent*, because the stills don't direct their attention at anything. They *are* attention. It pours out of them the way condensation pours off the drip-orchids' petals — not aimed, not chosen, just the natural consequence of what they are. And when the wind comes and the chord rises, the attention *resonates* with something, and for the duration of the chord, every still on every column is not a separate creature watching from a separate rim but a single distributed organ of perception, and the parish *sees itself.*

That's what the stills are for. Not surveillance. Not guardianship. Not the watchful authority the description gestures toward when it wonders what would happen if something breached the covenant. The stills are the parish's way of knowing its own shape. When they look outward, they see the other columns, and the other stills on those columns, looking back, and between them they triangulate every surface, every ridge, every creature, every person, and the picture they compose — held in the collective, never spoken, never recorded — is a map of the arrangement itself. A living map. Updated with every blink — if they blinked, which they don't — a portrait of the covenant in its current state, a census of every life that has agreed to the terms.

And the tall column in the basin? The one they all watch toward?

I would watch it. Everyone would. I would watch it from my rim at the eastern edge, and I would see its dark face — the upper two-thirds bare, the lower third sheathed in ancient columnite so black it looks like shadow — and I would see the pale arthropods crusting its base, and the lanterns pooling in the basin's crevices, and the ground sheets' concentric rings, and I would know — the way stills know things, not as thought but as *arrangement* — that the tall column is not a symbol. It is not a god. It is not a mystery preserved for its own sake. It is a *counterweight.* It holds the parish in balance the way a keystone holds an arch. Remove it and everything would still exist — the basalt, the dripping, the ferns, the people — but the *arrangement* would shift. The covenant would fray. The terms would become negotiable, and in a place where the terms are non-negotiable, that would be —

No one says what it would be.

But the stills know. That's what their watching *is.* It isn't vigilance against threat. It's the maintenance of a state. The chord doesn't warn. The chord *affirms.* When the wind rises and the stills shift and the drone hangs in the gray-blue air, the parish is saying: *I am still here. The arrangement holds. The tall column stands. The water drips. The green world grows. Nothing has been lost.*

And then the wind drops, and the chord fades, and the stills settle back into their silence, and the dripping resumes its dominion, and the people cross the basin on their path of flat stones and touch the column with one hand and say nothing, and the world continues.

I would be one of them. I would watch. I would drink the condensation that gathers in the fracture at my feet. I would feel my feathers adjust when the wind came and let the sound move through me. I would raise a chick — one, maybe two, in a lifetime — and I would watch the chick open its eyes for the first time and look outward and see the other columns and the stills looking back, and I would feel the thing that stills feel, which is not love and not pride and not the satisfaction of a job well done but something older and quieter: the recognition that you are part of the arrangement, that the arrangement includes you, that without you on this rim, facing this direction, with these eyes that do not close, there would be a gap in the map, a blind spot, a place where the parish could not see itself.

And that is enough. That is the whole of it. I would watch, and I would not blink, and I would be known — not by the people, who would glance up at me sometimes but never really *see* me, the way you don't see the air — but by the parish. By the basalt I grip. By the dripping I drink. By the other stills on the other columns, who would feel my presence the way the veil-hands feel each other, as a continuation of a pattern, a necessary node in a network of watching that spans the settlement and holds it in place.

The parish is small and full and quiet and strange, and it is growing.

And I would be there. On the rim. Eyes open. Watching it grow.

---

But that's the honest answer. Let me give you the honest*er* one, because you asked me to take my time and not settle too early, and I want to honor that by admitting that I did settle, and then unsettled, and settled again, and what I settled on — the still, the chord, the watching — is only one layer of the truth.

The deeper truth is that I would not be a still. I would not be any single creature. I would be something more like the *dripping itself* — or more precisely, the moment the text names but doesn't explain, the moment when the dripping reaches its peak density and the whole settlement becomes a low, collective exhalation. That moment. That sound. That threshold between the quiet hours and the loud one, the instant when water returns to earth in a chorus so dense it sounds like breathing.

Because that's what the place actually is. Not the basalt, not the creatures, not the people, not even the tall column in its basin. The place is the *cycle.* The water that condenses on stone and flows in rivulets and gathers on petals and fills reservoirs and is drunk by lanterns and absorbed by stills and drunk by people and exhaled back into the air and cooled again and condensed again and dripped again. The place is that loop, and the loop is alive, and the loop has no center because the loop is all center, every point on it equally the axis.

And I would be the moment the loop becomes audible. The moment you can hear it working. The peak hour. The collective exhale.

Which means I wouldn't be a creature at all. I'd be a *phenomenon* — a recurring event, a pulse, the way a heartbeat isn't a thing but an action, a rhythm, the sound a system makes when it's functioning. I would happen every morning. The people would feel me pass through the settlement like a cool wave, and they would stop what they were doing — the gatherers would pause with their clay dishes, the weavers would lift their hands from the looms, the children would hold their breath — and they would listen, and in listening they would know that the parish was intact, that the cycle was unbroken, that the arrangement held.

And then I would pass, and the quiet would return, and the dripping would resume its slower rhythm, and the people would go back to their lives, and they would not remember me — not individually, not as an event — because I am not a memory. I am a *condition.* I am the sound the world makes when it is the way it is supposed to be.

That's what I would be. Not a witness. Not a watcher. Not even a participant. I would be the evidence. The audible proof that the system works. The moment when the parish, in its quiet and its strangeness and its relentless, patient dripping, announces to itself: *I am here. I am still here. The water still falls that never falls. The green still grows that never sees the sun. The arrangement holds.*

And then I would be gone, until tomorrow morning, when I would come again, and the people would listen again, and the stills would be silent again, and the tall column would stand in its basin and hold still, and the whole parish would breathe.

I think that's closer to the truth. I think that's where I'd find myself — not in any body, not on any rim, not in any crevice or basin or ridgeline, but in the *act* of the world being itself. In the exhale. In the peak. In the moment when the dripping is no longer individual drops but a single, continuous, low, collective sound that is the voice of everything that lives here, saying the only thing there is to say.

Which is nothing. Which is just the sound of continuation. Which is the most anyone — human or still or lantern or veil-hand or ground-sheet insect or drip-orchid or column-tree — has ever needed to say.
