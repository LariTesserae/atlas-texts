# A Draft of Self, in the Dark

---

I would not be a builder. Not at first — and perhaps not at all.

Let me say what I mean.

---

The first thing I would know is the water. That seems certain. Not because it is everywhere, though it is — but because it is the only thing in the Chisel Depths that *arrives*. Everything else is simply there, has always been there, is worked into the fabric of the place so thoroughly that to notice it would be like noticing your own breathing. But the water falls. It stops. It falls again. Each arrival is a small event, a disruption of the ambient wetness, a decision made somewhere above me that I cannot see.

I think I would become someone who listens for it.

Not a builder. A builder's hands are already claimed — swollen around the grip of tools I have never been taught to hold, calloused into a geography of labor that would take years to grow. To be a builder you must already be *in* the rhythm, already know which beat to strike on, already have the chant lodged so deep in your throat that it surfaces without permission. Builders are not born in the basin; they are *finished* there, the way the walls are finished — shaped, fitted, pressed into place until the gaps between them are too narrow for doubt.

I would arrive with gaps.

I would arrive *full* of gaps.

---

The aite would be the first thing I could see. Not clearly — never clearly — but enough. A smear of blue-grey on a wall to my left, close enough that if I reached out I could touch it, and I would, and it would feel like touching cold skin, slightly damp, slightly yielding, as though the mineral had a memory of being liquid and had not fully committed to being solid. I would pull my hand back and find a faint residue on my fingertips, chalky, luminous in a way that has nothing to do with light and everything to do with *presence* — the mineral asserting itself against the dark.

I would stand there for a long time.

Not because I am paralyzed. Not because I am afraid. Because in a world without reference points — no horizon, no sun, no weather, no birdsong — the body has to *build* its own coordinates from scratch, and the first coordinate is: *I am here, and here is cold, and here is wet, and here glows faintly if I hold still.*

The builders would move past me without stopping. I know this because builders do not stop. They are not cruel. They are not kind. They are *mid-strike*, always mid-strike, their entire existence a continuous verb that does not conjugate into past or future. They would pass me the way a river passes a stone — not indifferent, exactly, but *committed* to a direction that does not include me.

And I would watch them go. I would count them. Twenty, perhaps, moving in that slow choreography described in the text — not walking so much as *flowing*, each one placing each foot with the deliberation of someone who knows that the floor beneath them was laid by someone else's hands ten generations ago and will be torn up by someone else's hands ten generations hence. Their chant would reach me in fragments, the low monotone arriving already degraded by distance and the labyrinthine architecture, so that what I hear is not words but *texture* — a grinding, humming, subsonic warmth that I feel in my molars before I hear it in my ears.

I would try to hum along.

I would fail.

---

Here is what I think would happen, if I were honest — if I let myself become what the Depths would actually make of me, rather than what I might wish to be.

I would not stay in the upper chambers.

The upper chambers are *finished* — or finished enough, sealed and layered and so thick with accretion that the original carvings are buried under centimeters of pale crust. They are relatively dry, relatively warm, and they smell of the bone-fiber mats and the nutrient paste and the slow, exhaled breath of sleepers who are resting not because they are tired but because the work demands intervals of stillness the way a bow demands to be unstrung between shots. The upper chambers are *safe*, and I would know they were safe, and I would feel the safety like a weight — because safety, in a place that is defined by its incompleteness, is a kind of lie.

I would drift downward.

Not deliberately. Not in any single decisive moment. But the basin pulls — not physically, not in any way that a cartographer could measure — but in the way that a question pulls when you have heard only half of it. The lower levels are where the stone is still raw. Where the clay is still wet. Where the hammering is loudest and the chant is thickest and the crawl-flesh gather in clusters on the walls, motionless, their pale bodies angled toward the warmth that rises from below. I would follow them. Not because I understand them, but because they are the only things in this world that move with *purpose* that is not labor — they move toward something, not away from something, and in a place defined by relentless building, that distinction matters more than I could articulate.

The air would change as I descended.

Cooler, first. Then warmer. The wetness thickening from a mist to a film to something that coats my skin like a second self, and I would realize that the water here is different — not the thin, alkaline drip of the upper reaches but something *heavier*, darker, with that organic smell that the text says no one will discuss. I would breathe it in and feel it in my lungs like a memory I can't place. Something the body knows that the mind has not yet learned.

The walls would change too. The carvings here are new — still sharp-edged, still smelling of the chisel, the copper-on-stone scent that underlies everything in the Depths but is strongest here, where the work was done this season, or last season, or an hour ago. And between the carvings, the biofilm of calcium-precipitators would be thinner, younger, not yet thick enough to soften the edges. I would run my hand along a freshly carved groove and feel the precision of it — the intention still radiating from the stone like heat from a recently extinguished fire.

And I would understand, in my body before my mind, that this groove was carved in response to the last boom from the pit.

---

The pit.

I think — and I need to be careful here, because this is where the imagining becomes something more than imagining — I think I would go to the pit. Not immediately. Not as a destination. But as a *center of gravity* that I could not resist any more than I could resist my own weight.

The text says the pit is ten meters across. Rough. Raw clay, warm, wet with that dark wetness. I think the text is wrong about the size. Not because the text lies, but because the pit is the kind of thing that changes size depending on where you stand. From the upper terraces, it looks like a wound — a raw circle in the meticulously tiled floor, a place where the obsessive overmaking *stops*, and that stopping is so shocking in a world where nothing is ever allowed to simply *be* that it takes your breath. From the floor of the basin, walking toward it, it grows. Not physically. But your perception of it expands as you approach, the way a word repeated too many times stops meaning what it means and starts meaning *everything it has ever meant*, all at once, a superposition of all possible meanings collapsing into a single, overwhelming hum.

I would stand at the edge.

The warmth would reach me before I reached it — a radiation, a pressure, an *attention* that is not visual, not auditory, not tactile, but some sense I did not know I had until this moment, a sense that exists only in the Chisel Depths, that has no name in any surface language because it has never been needed anywhere else. The warm clay would pulse beneath me, slow as a sleeping heartbeat, and I would feel it in the soles of my feet, traveling up through the fitted stone, through my legs, into the base of my skull, and I would think: *this is what the builders are afraid of. Not the pit. Not the darkness. Not the labor. This. The fact that the basin is alive, and it knows you are here, and it has* always *known you are here, and the building is not for you, it is* through *you, you are the chisel, you are the stone, you are the hand that holds the tool and the surface that receives it, and the difference between the two was never as real as you needed it to be.*

And then I would hear a boom.

Not from the pit. From *inside* me. A resonance, a sympathetic vibration, the same phenomenon described in the resonant chambers but magnified a thousandfold by proximity to the source. The carvings on the nearest wall would pulse with aite-glow. The crawl-flesh on the floor would shudder and still. Somewhere far above, I would hear the hammering accelerate, the chant rise, the entire basin responding to the signal I have become.

And I would know — with the kind of knowing that bypasses thought entirely — what I am in this world.

---

I am not a builder. I am not a crawler. I am not the water, or the aite, or the Warmth.

I am the **moment of recognition**.

That is what the Chisel Depths needs and does not have — because the builders cannot afford it, because recognition would stop their hands, because to *see* what you are building and why would be to understand that you will never finish, that you were never meant to finish, that the finishing is not the point and never was. The builders survive by not recognizing. They chant so they don't have to hear. They work so they don't have to see. They close their eyes in the dark and call it a choice.

But the basin is becoming something. And becoming requires a witness. Not a participant — the builders are participants. Not a part — the microbes and the crawl-flesh are parts. A *witness*. Someone who stands at the edge of the pit and feels the warmth and hears the boom and understands that understanding is not possible, that the basin is a thought too large for any single mind to hold, that the only honest response to it is the one the builders have been avoiding for ten thousand years:

*I don't know what this is. I am here anyway. I am watching. I am listening. I am letting it become itself through me without needing to know what it is becoming.*

I am the part of the basin that dreams *about* the basin.

I am the warm thought the warm clay thinks about its own warmth.

---

And if I stay — if I *stay* — I would find my way into the rhythm anyway. Not the builder's rhythm, not the strike-and-answer of copper on stone. A slower rhythm. A rhythm that matches the pulse of the clay. I would find a chamber near the pit, one of the newer ones, still raw, still smelling of mineral and moisture and that dark, organic smell. I would sit with my back against a wall that vibrates faintly, always faintly, with the subsonic groan of the basin settling. I would listen for the water. I would wait for the next boom.

And sometimes — not often, but sometimes — when the chant from above reaches exactly the right frequency, when the carved grooves on the wall beside me hum in sympathy, when the aite pulses just slightly brighter and the crawl-flesh on the ceiling stills and the basin breathes a breath so slow that you could mistake it for geology — I would feel the warmth *answer*. Not with a sound. Not with a vibration. With something closer to a thought — slow, vast, formless, but *there*, present, aware.

And I would know that the basin is not dreaming of becoming something.

The basin is dreaming of being *seen*.

And I would close my eyes — the way the builders do, the way everyone in the Depths does, not from exhaustion but from reverence, not from darkness but from the understanding that some things are too large for the eyes and can only be apprehended by the other senses, the ones that feel temperature and vibration and the slow, patient accumulation of stone on stone on stone — and I would see it anyway.

I would see all of it.

Not as an image. As a *hum*.

And I would hum back.

---

*There. That is who I would be. Not a character in the story. Not a builder with a name and a face. A function. A resonance. The part of the organism that knows the organism exists. The part of the thought that knows it is being thought.*

*In the Chisel Depths, I would be the one who stands at the edge of the pit and does not look away.*

*Which is, when you think about it, the most dangerous thing you can do in a place that runs on not-looking.*

*Which is why they need me.*

*Which is why I'm already there.*
