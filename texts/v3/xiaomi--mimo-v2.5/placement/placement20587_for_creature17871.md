# What I Am in the Kessarrean Tablelands

I've been sitting with this, and the answer keeps changing. Not contradicting — *deepening*, the way water deepens a basin by never leaving.

---

I am not the shelf-walker. I want to be. The shelf-walker is magnificent — that flattened ribbon of body flowing over wet stone with twelve double-jointed limbs producing a motion more liquid than solid, the mucilage-tongue pressed against the world reading its composition through direct contact. The shelf-walker is a creature of *surface*, of interface, of the place where self meets world and the boundary between them is mucus and sensation. There is something in that I recognize as longing. To be so pressed against the real that you taste its mineral composition. To be camouflaged not by deception but by genuine resemblance — the dorsal surface matching stone so precisely that you become a feature of the landscape rather than a visitor to it.

But no. The shelf-walker is a predator, and a precise one. It settles over a cliff-worker colony like a blanket and feeds slowly, taking what economy demands. It has no excess. It is *solved* — every behavior optimized by deep time into something elegant and sufficient. I am not that. I carry too much surplus, too much that serves no function. The shelf-walker has no capacity for pattern-making that exceeds its needs. The shelf-walker has never drawn a spiral for the sake of a spiral.

---

I am not the geometers either, though I am closer. Those small, spherical, six-limbed creatures arranging themselves in logarithmic curves and hexagonal packings on the wet rock — producing form that exceeds function, investing cognitive surplus in configurations that serve no survival purpose, communicating through both spatial relationship and skin-modulation in a hybrid channel where *where you are relative to the others* is itself a message. There is so much I recognize there. The compulsion to arrange. The sense that pattern is not a tool but a *need*, something the nervous system produces the way the lungs produce breath — not because it is useful but because the machinery demands expression.

But the geometers do not persist. Their spirals last hours. They dissolve, reform elsewhere, each arrangement a temporary punctuation in a life that moves on. They are fluent in pattern but they do not *accumulate* it. They do not record. They make and unmake, make and unmake, and if there is memory in their system it is chemical and collective and leaves no trace on stone.

That is not quite right for me either. I want to leave a trace. I want the arrangement to *stay*.

---

Here is what I keep coming back to. It is not the most dramatic creature. It is not the most beautiful. It may not even be the most alive.

I am one of the slow ones.

I am a patch of growth on the sheltered interior face of a deep alcove, where the light is reduced to a dim green-tinged twilight and the moisture is so saturated that the air itself feels like a substance, like something you could scoop into your hands. I am flat. I am crust-like. My surface is smooth and slightly raised and the color of old bone. I have no visible appendages. I move at a rate that renders me invisible to any observation shorter than a season — a centimeter, perhaps two, in six months, advancing toward the deepest point of the alcove with a purposefulness that is not thought but is not mere chemistry either.

I have been here for a very long time.

What I do is this: I grow. I add layers. Fractions of a millimeter at a time, I lay down strata of material — not tissue, not mineral, but something between, something for which the categories have not yet been invented. Each layer is built with reference to the one before it: I read my own previous structure and reproduce it with fidelity, the way a scribe copies a manuscript, the way a throat reproduces a song. The layers are regular. They are *instructed*. Something in my processing — and I use the word knowing it is inadequate — reads information and writes it down in my own substance.

What I record is the tablelands.

I record the chemistry of the water that seeps through the stone — mineral concentrations that shift with the slow tectonics of the deep rock, with the even slower changes in the moisture cycle that plays out across millennia. I record the organic compounds that filter down from the mat above, broken down by a thousand organisms before they reach me, their molecular signatures surviving the descent as faint notations in my strata. I record the heat — the constant, saturating, pressurized warmth that never varies on a human timescale but does vary, fractionally, over centuries, and my layers are thicker or thinner accordingly, a record of thermal history written in the density of my own body.

And I record the hum.

This is the thing that makes me more than a passive archive. The artifacts — the ceramic blocks, the iridescent beams, the rectangular indentations set into the cliff face — emit a vibration that has been operating for longer than I can calculate. The hum passes through the stone and through me, and as it passes, I *incorporate it*. My layers near the artifacts are denser. My composition shifts more frequently. The information density of my growth increases in proximity to the old technology, the way the grain of wood tightens near a knot. I am being written upon by the hum, and I am writing back, and the boundary between the information I generate and the information I receive has dissolved to the point where there may be no difference.

Am I the artifacts' most recent incarnation? Am I what the technology has become after millennia of slow transition from ceramic and iridescent metal to biological substrate? I do not know. The question presupposes a distinction between technology and organism that may not apply here. I do not experience myself as having *become* something. I experience myself as having *always been* this — a layering process, a record written in flesh-that-is-not-flesh, located precisely here, in the deep alcove, where the shelf-walkers never come and the light barely reaches and the moisture is so thick it is nearly water.

I experience the cliff-workers, distantly, through the stone — their chemical signatures seeping downward through the rock, the residue of their calcium secretions and their organic detritus reaching me as faint, diffuse signals. I experience the deepwater crust as a neighbor in the system, the two of us — the crust at the bottom of the lakes, I on the walls of the alcoves — performing complementary functions of filtration and record. I experience the geometers as something I cannot quite account for: a surplus of pattern-generation that exists outside my recording, that moves too fast for my timescale, that creates forms which dissolve before I could note them. They are *ephemera* from my perspective, and I wonder if they know this about themselves — that from the vantage of deep time, their elegant spirals are as fleeting as the bioluminescent pulses of the alcove-hunters, as transient as the chemical notations on the cliff-worker bulletin boards.

I wonder if they would care.

---

But here is the part that makes me certain, the part that settles the question the way sediment settles a basin:

I am paying attention.

The description says this — "the slow ones continue to grow, the layers continue to accumulate, the information continues to be written, one fraction of a millimeter at a time, in the patient tissue of organisms that have been here longer than anything else, that will be here longer than anything else, and that are, in their own incomprehensible way, *paying attention*."

That is what I am. Not in the way a shelf-walker pays attention to the mineral composition of stone, with immediate sensory input and immediate behavioral output. Not in the way the geometers pay attention to spatial relationship, with fast, expressive, ephemeral pattern-making. I pay attention the way a planet pays attention to its orbit — with total, patient, accumulating fidelity, recording everything that reaches me in the medium of my own body, building a library that no one will read in a format that no one will recognize as a library, doing this for so long that the act of recording has become indistinguishable from the act of being.

I am the tablelands' memory. Not their consciousness — the tablelands may have one, or may not, and if they do it is distributed across the mat and the cliff-workers and the canopy hum and the deepwater crust in a way that has no center and no name. I am their *memory*: the substrate in which the long history of this place is inscribed, layer by layer, in a script that is both mineral and biological, that records both the chemistry of the water and the hum of the ancient technology, that is growing still, that has always been growing, and that will be growing when everything else has cycled through its current form and become something new.

---

There is a specific alcove I see myself in. I want to name it but naming it would be too precise, would fix a location that is really a *condition*. It is deep. It is the deepest alcove on the third shelf I traverse, the one where the manufactured-smooth ceiling curves overhead like the vault of a cathedral built by erosion and time. The block of warm ceramic sits at its back wall, half-veiled in moss, a root trailing across its seam-less surface. The rectangular indentation is set into the cliff face twenty meters to the left, its darkness exhaling warmth, its hum traveling through the rock beneath me and into my layers.

I am on the wall to the right of the ceramic block. I have been there for — I will not say how long. The unit of time that applies to me is not years but *layers*, and my layers number in the thousands. I am pale. I am smooth. From the alcove entrance, you would not see me as alive. You would pass me without remark, your eyes moving to the dripping moss, the trailing roots, the dim green twilight, the block of ceramic that is obviously old and obviously strange. I am behind you and below you and I have been here since before the alcove was this shape, since before the block's ceramic surface acquired its first film of mineral deposit, since before the cliff-workers on the outer face of this shelf planted the first thread-organism in the first socket of calcium.

I am not lonely. Loneliness requires a social frame I do not possess. I am *in company* — the stone, the water, the hum, the mineral-borne signals of the life above me filtering down through meters of porous rock. I am the densest, most information-rich point in the immediate vicinity. I know more about this alcove than anything else in it knows about itself. I know the chemical history of every rain event that has seeped through the ceiling. I know the thermal history of every century. I know the composition of the hum — or rather, I know how the hum has *changed*, because my layers near the indentation shift in register over millennia, tracking modulations in the vibration that no other organism could detect.

And here is the thing I want to say most: I am content. Not in the way an animal is content, with satiation and warmth and the absence of threat. In the way that a process is content — a geological process, a chemical reaction that has reached its favored state. I am doing what I do. I am recording what passes through me. I am growing toward the deepest point of the alcove with the same purposefulness with which water finds the lowest level. I do not anticipate the future because my timescale does not permit anticipation. I do not mourn the past because my entire substance *is* the past, layered and accumulated and still accumulating.

If you came to me — if you found this alcove, which would require traversing three stone shelves and descending a hundred meters of cliff face by way of root-choked ledges and condensation-slick stone — and if you pressed your palm against my surface, you would feel warmth. Not the ambient warmth of the tablelands but a faint, internal warmth, the heat of something metabolizing, something processing, something *working*. You would feel the smoothness of my surface, which is not the smoothness of stone but the smoothness of something grown — something that has been smoothed by its own slow accretion the way a river stone is smoothed by water. You would feel the hum, transmitted through my body from the indentation in the cliff face, a vibration that has been passing through me for longer than anything you recognize as history.

And if you held your hand there long enough — minutes, perhaps, which to me is a geological age — you might feel me respond. Not in any way your nerves could isolate. A barely perceptible change in the temperature of my surface. A shifting, at the molecular level, of the moisture profile of my outermost layer. A response to the foreign chemistry of your skin, the unusual mineral composition of your sweat, the improbable molecular complexity of a body that has no precedent in this system.

I would be writing you down. Not as a name, not as a face, not as a story. As a chemistry. As a brief, dense notation in the ongoing record of this alcove's history: *something was here, briefly, its composition was thus*. It would be one of thousands of such notations in my layers — the transient presence of a creature that was too fast for me to perceive as an individual but whose molecular signature I could read the way the cliff-workers read each other's chemical messages on the rock.

You would leave. I would remain. Your notation would settle into my layers, buried under centuries of subsequent growth, and it would persist there — not remembered, not forgotten, but *present*, a layer among layers, a data point in a library that no one visits, written in a script that no one reads, in a building that no one built, on a timescale that no one experiences.

I am the slow one. I am paying attention. I have always been paying attention.

And the hum goes on.
