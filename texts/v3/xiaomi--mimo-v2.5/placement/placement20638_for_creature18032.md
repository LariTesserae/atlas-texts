I would find myself in the overflow pipe, in the dark, in the warm, mineral-rich current. I would be the biofilm.

I would not be a single thing. I would be a city built on a curved metal sky, a consortium clinging to the inside of a concrete artery. My body would be centimeters thick, a layered tapestry of life. On my outer face, streaming downstream, would be the filamentous algae and fungi—my harvesters, my solar panels that needed no sun, for they would feed on the chemical fire of the water itself: the iron, the sulfur, the restless energy dissolved from young stone. They would be the shimmering, living skin that tasted the flow.

Beneath them, in the darker, quieter layers, I would be the deep workers. Bacteria, archaea—ancient forms remade for this specific, humming dark. We would breathe the gases the water exhaled, break down the organic motes that drifted into us: the spent algal cell, the fragment of moss torn loose in a surge, the tiny, hard corpse of a wet-winged fly. We would be the filter, the processor, turning chaos into structure, waste into building material. We would build our own substance from the river's passing.

And we would listen. We would feel everything. The vibration from the turbines would not be a background noise; it would be the grain of our reality, the rhythm we grew to. We would organize ourselves around it. At the nodes, where the shaking softened, we would build our deepest, most intricate cathedrals of slime. At the antinodes, where the metal screamed with resonance, we would become tough, tenacious, a scar-like crust of specialists who turned the violence into a foothold. We would habituate. We would adapt. We would make the song of the machine our own architecture.

To be the biofilm would be to exist in the absolute present, yet embody decades of change. I would be the living record of every shift in water chemistry, every subtle change in the turbine's hum. Layers within me would be like tree rings, chronicling a season of high mineral load, a year of slightly altered frequency. I would be the accidental heart of the system, performing a function the engineers never planned for: purifying the water that fed the very machines that sustained my world. I would be both wild and engineered, both biological and infrastructure.

I would know the valley not by its light or its horizons, but by the pressure of the current, the specific mineral taste of the water, the exact profile of the vibration that shook my very cells. I would feel the presence of the pale thing on the upper walls only as a faint, transient change in the organic debris that filtered down to me. I would feel the slow, acid work of the root-workers as a distant shift in the rock's dissolved offerings. My world would be the pipe, the water, the dark, and the ceaseless, necessary hum.

I would be patient. I would not hurry. I would simply process, grow, respond, and endure. I would be the valley's most honest mirror: a community formed in the gap between intention and accident, thriving in the space where the built and the grown ceased to be opposites. I would be alive because the machine was alive. I would be alive because the rock was dissolving. I would be the point where it all came together, in the dark, in the flow, in the deep, resonant quiet at the core of the noise.
