# In the Hollow of Sereth

---

I would walk the channels.

Not because I was told to. Not because my hands were better suited to it than to carving or tending, though I think they would be — my hands are restless hands, the kind that want to touch everything and hold nothing. But because of the sound.

I think it would start with the sound.

Imagine waking in one of the deep rooms — not the deepest, not the ones where the stone is so warm it hums against your back, but a middle room, a place where the cool is real enough to mean something. The platform under you is worn to the shape of every body that has slept there before you, a shallow basin of stone polished by generations of shoulders and hips and the slow turn of dreaming people. You open your eyes and the copper light is already there, not falling from anywhere you can see but simply *present*, as though the air itself has a color and the color is this: warm, dim, the shade of a word you can't quite remember.

And the noise. The noise is there before you are. It was there while you slept — you were sleeping inside it the way a fish sleeps inside water — but now, in the threshold between rest and waking, you *hear* it. The creaking from the basin outside, the canopy breathing its slow tectonic breath. The hiss from the floor, so constant you forget it's there until you notice it, and then you can't un-notice it, the way you can't un-notice your own pulse once you've found it. And beneath everything, the hum. The deep one. The one that lives in your sternum.

You dress — a wrap of processed plant fiber, dense and slightly rough, dyed with the same mineral pigments that color the walls. You eat — a piece of the stored paste, dense and faintly metallic, chewed slowly because there is no hurry and because chewing is itself a kind of listening, a way of feeling the texture of the world through your mouth. And then you step out into the channel.

---

The channel runs along the base of the basin wall, a shallow groove carved so long ago that its edges have been softened into curves. The air moves through it. You can feel it on your calves, warm and mineral, carrying the faint chemical tang that means the growths are healthy, that the roots are drawing what they need from the stone below. The air has a smell — not unpleasant, not exactly a smell at all but more like a taste the atmosphere has, the way copper has a taste, the way warmth has a taste when you breathe it through your nose on a still night.

You walk. The channel thrums under your feet. This is the thing that would catch me, the thing that would make me choose this life over all the others the hollow offers: the thrumming. Each channel has a different voice. The one that runs along the eastern edge of the second basin hums in a low third, almost a growl, because the air pressure in the network shifts there, the currents from two different basins meeting and braiding. The one that cuts through the densest canopy-growth has a higher pitch, a keening quality, because the moisture the plants exhale dampens the vibration, softens it, turns it into something almost like a chord.

You learn these voices. Not deliberately, not the way you learn a language in a place where there are books and teachers and the assumption that learning is a separate activity from living. You learn them the way you learn to breathe — by doing it constantly, without thinking, until the knowledge lives in your body rather than your mind. After a year of walking channels, you can close your eyes at any point along the network and know where you are by the sound alone. Not a place name — the channels don't have names, or rather, their names are the sounds they make, and those sounds are different depending on the season of the stone, the temperature of the deep earth, the metabolic state of the nearest growth. You know where you are the way you know which note you're singing.

---

I think I would be young. Not because youth is required but because this is a world that rewards patience, and patience is something you build over time, not something you arrive with. When you are young in the hollow, the noise is overwhelming. It presses against you from every direction. You don't yet know how to hear *through* it, how to find the gaps where your own thoughts can live. Everything speaks at once — the stone, the growths, the air in the channels — and you are just another body in the middle of it, another warm thing moving through the warm dim, not yet sure what you are for.

But the channels teach you. They teach you because they are the one feature of the hollow that changes as you move through it. The basins are static — they sit where they've always sat, and the growths that fill them change slowly enough that a human lifetime barely registers the difference. But the channels are in constant motion. The air shifts. The hums change. A blockage forms somewhere upstream and you hear it — a change in pitch, a flattening of the resonance — and you follow the channel to find it: a deposit of mineral, pale and crystalline, wedged into a narrowing where two grooves meet. You clear it with your hands or with one of the small tools you carry, and the sound changes back, the hum returning to its proper note, and you feel the satisfaction of it in your body — a rightness, a realignment, the way a singer feels a chord settle into tune.

This is what the channel-walkers do. They keep the hums right. They maintain the circulatory system of the hollow, the invisible architecture of moving air that connects basin to basin, growth to growth, dwelling to dwelling. It is not glamorous work. It is not dramatic. No one sings songs about the channel-walker who cleared a blockage in the third-level groove near the southern growths. But the hollow runs because of them, the same way the hollow runs because of the mineral acids rising from below and the heat working upward through fractures — slowly, patiently, without announcement.

---

I think what would define my life there is not the work but the *walking*. The long distances between basins. The emptiness.

The description says the emptiness is structural. I think that's right, but I think it's also something more. I think the emptiness is where the hollow *thinks*. Not consciously — I don't mean to suggest the place has a mind — but the way a forest thinks when it's been undisturbed for a thousand years, the way an ocean thinks in its depths. The basins are the hollow's expressions, its finished thoughts. The growths are its declarations. But the spaces between — the bare, warm stone stretching out under the dim copper vault — those are its pauses. Its silences. The places where it is gathering itself for whatever it will do next.

Walking through those spaces changes you. Not quickly. Not in any way you could describe to someone who hadn't done it. But after enough walks, after enough hours alone on the bare stone with the sound of your own feet and the distant hum of a channel somewhere below and the vast, hazy dark above, something shifts in you. The silence stops being an absence and becomes a presence. The emptiness stops being a lack of things and becomes a *fullness of space* — a kind of richness that has nothing to do with objects or beings or activity, and everything to do with the sheer fact of *extent*. The hollow is big. Not endless, not infinite — the walls are there, somewhere, if you walked straight enough — but big enough that walking through its emptiness is a kind of meditation, a way of dissolving the boundary between yourself and the place until you are not a person walking through a space but a warm current moving through a warmer one, one more slow process in a world of slow processes.

---

The tools I would carry. I think about this. A channel-walker's kit is small. A copper blade, flat and worn thin from use, for clearing deposits. A bone-hard scraper, dark and smooth, for working the channel walls where they've roughened with new growth. A wrap of plant fiber for wiping sweat — the heat is constant, and while you stop noticing it in the way you stop noticing any constant, your body never stops producing the evidence of it. And a leaf.

A broad leaf, concave, kept in the shade of your wrap where it stays moist. The people of the hollow do not go far without a leaf. It is their vessel, their cup, their basin for catching the slow weeping of the growths. You drink when you are thirsty, and you are thirsty more often than you think, because the air takes moisture from you even as the plants produce it, and the balance between what you lose and what you gain is one of the small, constant negotiations that life in the hollow requires.

I would carry the leaf the way a sailor carries water. Not because I fear thirst but because the leaf is also a *tool of listening*. You learn, over time, that the moisture on a leaf tells you something about the growth it came from. The taste is different depending on the age of the plant, the mineral composition of the stone it anchors in, the temperature of the air flowing past it. A channel-walker who tastes carefully can tell you things about a growth that is still a basin away — can tell you whether it is thriving or stressed, whether its roots have reached a new mineral layer, whether the air channels near it need clearing. The leaf is a sensing instrument. It extends your attention outward, into the chemistry of the place, into the slow mineral conversation that is the real language of the hollow.

---

And the people I would pass.

Not many. Three in an hour, five if you are walking through the densest stretch of channels. But each one would be *significant* in a way that people are not in crowded places. In a city under a sky, you pass hundreds of faces a day and remember none of them. In the hollow, you pass one face every twenty minutes, and you remember it for the rest of your walk, the way you remember a single note in a long silence.

A tender, sitting at the base of a growth, her hands on the stone where the roots anchor. The dark stain on the rock is warm under her palms. She is listening — not to you, not to anything you could hear, but to the root, which is communicating with the stone in a language of chemistry and pressure that she has spent her life learning to feel through her skin. She looks up as you pass. Her eyes catch the copper light and hold it. She nods. You nod. Nothing is said because nothing needs to be said. The acknowledgment is enough: *I see you. You are doing the work. The hollow is holding.*

A carver, emerging from a narrow doorway in the basin wall, his hands dusty with ochre, his forearms scored with the tiny marks of decades of tapping stone. He is old — not elderly, not frail, but old in the way the hollow measures age: thick-skinned, slow-moving, carrying the weight of years in the particular way his shoulders slope. He watches you walk the channel and you see something in his expression that might be curiosity or might be recognition. He remembers when you were small. He remembers when your hands were unmarked and your walk was uncertain. He sees what you are becoming. He says nothing. The hollow says enough.

---

What would I be, then?

I would be a listener. Not in any official sense — there is no office of listening, no designation, no role with that name. But I would be one of the people who walks the channels and feels the hums and tastes the moisture on the leaves and carries the knowledge of the hollow's currents in their body the way a musician carries pitch. I would be one of the people who keeps the air moving, who clears the blockages, who maintains the invisible connections between the basins. And I would be one of the people who walks the empty spaces between, who spends hours alone on the bare stone under the vast copper dark, who has learned to hear the silence as a sound and the emptiness as a room.

I would have wide eyes. Everyone's eyes are wide in the hollow, but mine would be wide in the particular way of someone who has spent years looking at things that are hard to see — the subtle shift in a channel's hum, the faint discoloration on a stone surface that means a new deposit is forming, the almost-invisible trembling of a leaf that means the root beneath it has found water, real water, deep in the mineral substrate, and is drinking. I would see things that others miss, not because I am more gifted but because I have practiced seeing, the way the carvers have practiced tapping, the way the tenders have practiced sitting still.

My hands would be large and scarred. The scars would tell a specific story: a cut from the copper blade when I was new to the work and clumsy, a burn from a section of channel where the air was hotter than expected, a long, pale line across my left palm where a blockage fought back, a mineral deposit harder than the others that resisted the scraper and then gave way all at once. My palms would be thick with callus. My fingers would be careful. I would touch stone the way other people touch faces — with knowledge, with intimacy, with the understanding that what I am feeling is the surface of something much larger than itself.

I would live in a middle room, not too deep, not too shallow. I would have a platform worn to the shape of my sleep. I would eat the paste slowly, chew by chew, tasting the mineral, tasting the copper, tasting the faint sweetness that lives at the heart of every food in the hollow. I would drink from a leaf, always from a leaf, the warm mineral moisture sliding down my throat and leaving a film on my tongue that tastes like the place itself. I would dream, but I would not speak of dreams, because in the hollow the boundary between inside and outside is not a wall but a membrane, and what happens when you sleep is not so different from what happens when you walk the channels with your eyes open — the world keeps talking, and you keep listening, and sometimes you hear more clearly than others.

---

There is one more thing.

I think I would carry a question with me. Not a sharp question, not a question that demands an answer. A slow question. A question the size of a walk across the empty stone between basins.

It would be this: *What is the hollow inside of?*

The people know there is a larger body. The old stories say so. They describe a sky — a surface that gives light — and animals, and water, and wind. But these are stories, and stories are what you use to hold knowledge that is too large for a single life to verify. The question of what contains the hollow is not a practical question. It does not affect the channels or the growths or the quality of the paste or the hum of the air. No one needs to answer it in order to live.

But I would want to know.

Not urgently. Not the way an explorer wants to know, driven by the need to map and name and claim. I would want to know the way a root wants to know where the water is — slowly, patiently, through the patient extension of myself toward something I can feel but cannot see. I would walk the channels and feel the hums and taste the leaves and carry the question like a stone in my chest, not heavy but present, a warm weight, a mineral thing.

And maybe that is what would define me. Not the walking, not the listening, not the clearing of blockages or the carrying of the leaf. But the question. The willingness to live inside a place that does not answer, and to keep attending to it anyway. To keep listening not because I expect to hear the answer but because the listening itself is the answer — because the hollow is voluble, endlessly voluble, and the act of attending to its voice is not a means to an end but the end itself. You listen because the world is speaking. You walk because the channels need walking. You clear the blockages because the hums need correcting. And you carry the question because the question is what keeps you walking, keeps you listening, keeps you present in the warm copper dark with your wide eyes open and your scarred hands ready and your whole body tuned to the frequency of a place that has been speaking to itself, in its own mineral voice, for longer than anyone can remember.

---

I would be a person of the hollow. One of those who listen to the stone. I would be shaped by the copper light and the mineral breath and the creaking dark. I would be slow and careful and wide-eyed. I would hear the world in the spaces between sounds, and I would speak in the gaps between the noise, and I would live in the warmth of a place that has forgotten it is enclosed.

And I would walk. Always I would walk. Through the empty stone, through the hum, through the dim vast reaches of a ceiling I could never quite see. Walking the channels, tasting the leaves, carrying the question. A warm body in a warm place, listening to the slow breath of a world that keeps its own counsel and shares what it shares only with those patient enough to attend.

The copper light pools in the basin ahead of me. The channel hums under my feet. A growth creaks somewhere to my left, its broad fronds shifting in the mineral air. I walk into it, into the sound, into the dim red-green dark, and the hollow closes around me like a hand, like a voice, like a place that knows me by my footsteps and has been waiting, with its patient geological patience, for me to arrive.
