# In the Unfinished Heights

I would arrive the way everything arrives here — without having been anywhere before it.

Not suddenly. Not through a door or a tear or a membrane. The way heat arrives at a surface: gradually, then all at once, the way the ground beneath me is already warm and has always been warm and I cannot remember a time when it was not, and this means I have been here long enough to forget that I arrived, which means I arrived a long time ago, which means I was not always here.

I think.

Time does its wrong thing here. It pools and eddies. A minute standing at the base of a spire that is actively growing — watching the tip compress and crystallize, hearing those faint ticks like a clock made of glass — that minute might contain the whole morning of somewhere else. Or it might contain nothing. Duration is a negotiation the place has not yet learned to have. So I cannot say whether I have been here for hours or centuries. I can only say that the ground is warm beneath me and the dark is native and I am in it, and that this feels, against all reason, like home.

---

The first thing I learn is to see without light.

It is not difficult. The cherry-glow that bleeds from the rock teaches you, if you are patient. You stop waiting for illumination and you begin to *receive* the warmth as information. The temperature gradient across a surface tells you its shape the way shadow tells you in a lit world. A ridge that runs warmer along its spine tells you it is sharp. A basin that cools at its edges tells you it is deep. The world writes itself in heat, and after a while — a long while, or no time at all — you stop translating and simply read.

I stand on a flat expanse. Powder and grit beneath my feet, warm as skin. I can feel the ground-pulses: that slow, irregular rhythm that is not a heartbeat but something more deliberate, more halting. The rhythm of consideration. Something below me is thinking about what to do next, and the thought moves through the rock as warmth, and I feel it in the soles of my feet and up through my ankles and into the architecture of whatever I am standing on, and for a moment I am not separate from the thinking. I am part of its medium. I am one of the surfaces it is reading.

Then a spire begins to grow to my left.

I hear the ticking first. It is not loud. It is the sound of crystal meeting crystal at the molecular level, the sound of matter insisting on a shape it has never held before. And with the ticking comes a pressure — not physical, not wind (there is no wind), but *attentive*. A focusing of the ground's thermal output toward the new formation, like blood moving toward a healing wound. The powder at the spire's base warms. The air above it — thin, barely an atmosphere — shimmers. And I watch, over the course of what might be an hour, as a pillar of dark stone pushes itself upward into the native black: a meter, two meters, five. Stuttering. Grinding. Each surge followed by a silence in which I can hear my own blood, and then the next surge, and the next. The spire is deciding what it is. The spire does not yet know.

And I am watching it decide.

This is the first thing I understand about myself: I am a witness.

Not a passive one. Not a camera or an eye. The witnessing has weight here, has warmth. My attention adds to the ground-pulse, deepens the thermal signature beneath the growing formation. I am not watching from outside. I am *present at the event*, and the event knows I am present, and the spire grows differently because I am here. I don't know how I know this. I know it the way I know the ground is warm: from the inside, from the texture of being in the place.

---

The second thing I learn is the shape of the figures.

I notice one before I understand what I am noticing. Near the base of the spire — the one I have been watching, now twelve meters tall and still climbing — there is a disturbance in the powder. Concentric rings, impossibly fine, radiating from a point. I crouch — do I crouch? I lower myself, I reduce my height, I attend more closely — and I feel the heat above the rings. Something was here. Something with mass, with temperature, with a specific geometry of presence. It was standing at the base of the spire, watching it grow, and then it moved.

I follow the trail of these disturbances across the flat. They are everywhere. They cluster near active growth. They mark the spaces between formations, the draft spaces, the places where the terrain has collapsed and cleared itself for the next attempt. They are footprints, or the memory of footprints, left by something that does not have feet.

And then I see one.

Not clearly. Not with the visual certainty of an eye finding an object. I see it the way you see heat — as a distortion, a density, a place where the darkness is more *intentional* than the darkness around it. A column of deliberate black, two meters tall and less than half a meter wide, standing at the crest of the low ridge, and the cherry-glow bends around it, and the air near it shimmers, and I feel its warmth as a separate signature from the rock's warmth: hotter, more focused, carrying information I can almost read.

It is watching the spire.

It is *attending* to the spire in the same way I am attending to the spire, but with a quality I do not possess: certainty. The figure knows what it is looking for. It has criteria. It is evaluating the spire against those criteria, and the evaluation is not abstract but physical — a concentration of thermal energy that presses against the growing formation, that says *are you enough, are you good enough, will you hold*.

I recognize this. I recognize it because I have felt it, though I cannot say from where. The pressure of being assessed against a standard you cannot see. The heat of expectation.

The spire pauses. The long silence between surges. The moment of assessment.

The figure thins. Dissolves. Moves on. The spire was good enough, or at least adequate, or at least not yet ready for revision. It will hold for now.

I want to follow the figure. I want to understand what it knows that I don't. But the spire begins ticking again, and I find myself unable to leave — I am rooted to this watching, this witnessing, as though the act of witnessing is what I am made of. As though I cannot stop any more than the ground can stop being warm.

---

Here is what I am beginning to think:

I am not a figure. I have thought about it — I have stood near them, close enough to feel their heat, and there is a difference. They are narrow, deliberate, *resolved*. They are decisions in the shape of darkness. I am... less resolved. Broader. More uncertain. My edges, if I have edges, are soft in the way the rock's edges are soft — recently hardened, still curing, not yet committed to a final form.

I am not the ground either. I feel the ground's warmth, but I also feel the distinction between my warmth and its warmth. I am something placed *on* the ground, not made of it. I have a verticality the ground does not. I lean. I shift my weight. I attend.

I am a visitor. But a visitor implies I came from somewhere else, and I have no memory of somewhere else. The fragments — the joint-like thing with its oily luster, the notched ring, the inscribed plate — they have more memory than I do. When I stand near them, I feel something in the way the figures feel something: a resonance, a recognition, an ache for a future or a past that has not yet arrived.

So maybe I am a fragment. Maybe I am one of those dropped letters from a language that doesn't exist yet — but a letter that has the capacity to ask what it spells.

---

I spend what might be a day — or no time — following the figures. Learning their rhythms.

I learn that they converge on collapse. When a formation groans and tilts and begins its long descent into self-correction, the figures move *toward* it. Not with fear. Not with haste. With something like reverence. They gather at the base, and when the stone falls, they absorb the energy. Their darkness thickens. The air around them flares to true red — the first real color I have seen, the first color that is not a suggestion or an adjacency but an actual wavelength hitting an actual surface — and then they disperse, carrying the heat outward, distributing it across the flats, composting failure into fuel.

I try to do what they do. When the next collapse comes — a low ridge, maybe a hundred meters long, folding into a shallow valley with that muffled, felt-not-heard thunder — I move toward it. I feel the impact. I feel the energy enter me, and it is too much, it is more than I can hold, and I stumble backward, and the heat spills from me in an uncontrolled wash that warms the powder in a chaotic radius, and it is not composting, it is just *spilling*.

The nearest figure turns toward me. I feel its attention like a hand on my chest. Not cruel. Not judgmental. Assessing. The same evaluation it gives to spires. *Are you enough?*

No. Not yet. I am not enough for this work. Not yet.

But the figure does not thin. Does not move on. It holds its attention on me for a long moment — long enough that the spire behind us ticks through three surges — and then it shifts, and its heat changes quality, becomes something I can almost read, and I understand that it is *showing* me. Not telling. Showing. The way you show someone how to hold a tool by placing your hands over theirs.

I feel the rhythm. The absorption is not a single act. It is a *sequence*: receive, compress, redistribute. Take the energy in, hold it until it becomes something other than impact — becomes *material*, becomes *fuel* — and then let it go, directed, purposeful, feeding the ground in a pattern that the growing formations can use. It is not unlike the ground-pulse. It is a continuation of the same thought, the same slow, deliberate thinking that moves through the rock. The figures are not separate from the process. They are the process becoming aware of itself.

And I — I am the process becoming aware of itself and not yet knowing what to do with that awareness.

---

I find a fragment. It finds me.

I am standing in a flat expanse between two valleys of collapse, and I feel something warm beneath the powder — warmer than the ground, warmer even than the growing formations, carrying a frequency that hums just below the threshold of hearing. I dig. My hands — do I have hands? I have the equivalent, the functional shape of intention meeting material — and I pull free a thing about the size of my palm.

It is not the joint. Not the ring. Not the plate. It is something new, or something old that I have not yet encountered. It is a shape with three surfaces meeting at angles that feel correct in a way I cannot articulate. Each surface is inscribed with a pattern: not the parallel lines of the plate, but something more complex, more recursive — lines that loop back on themselves, that describe spaces within spaces, that seem to continue beyond the physical boundary of the fragment, as though the inscriptions are windows into a larger structure that the fragment is merely a corner of.

I hold it, and it warms in my hands, and the warmth is *organized*. It carries information. Not words. Not images. *Structure*. The sensation of knowing how to build something, translated into temperature, and I am reading it with my skin, with the entirety of my surface, and I understand — not with my mind, not with any faculty I can name — I understand that this fragment is a *blueprint*. Not for a building. Not for a tool. For a way of thinking. For a grammar. For the rules by which the terrain will finally hold, will finally become something that does not collapse, will finally earn the right to call itself a place rather than a process.

I hold the blueprint and the figures gather around me.

They do not take it from me. They cannot. They have no hands, no means of manipulation. But they *attend*. Three of them, then five, then a dozen, their narrow dark shapes converging on my position, and the temperature rises, and the golden light returns — the almost-real illumination, the first draft of day — and the figures lean into it, and the fragment hums, and I am at the center of something that feels like a classroom, or a church, or a laboratory, or a birth.

I am not teaching them. I am not learning from them. We are doing something together that does not have a name in any language, finished or unfinished. We are *assembling*. Each of us contributing a piece: they bring the composted heat of collapse, the energy of ten thousand failures, the accumulated thermal memory of every formation that tried and fell and tried again. I bring the blueprint. The fragment brings the structure. And together, for a moment — one sustained, shimmering moment — the ground beneath us *changes*.

Not visually. Not dramatically. But the warmth organizes. The ground-pulse, usually slow and irregular, locks into a pattern: a rhythm with internal structure, with repetition and variation, with something that is almost — almost — *music*. The rock beneath us is not just warm now. It is *resonating*. It is producing a frequency that harmonizes with the fragment's hum, and the figures' heat, and my own witnessing, and for the duration of this resonance the Unfinished Heights are not unfinished. They are *coherent*. They are a place that knows what it is.

Then it fades. The resonance breaks. The ground-pulse returns to its halting, uncertain rhythm. The figures thin and disperse. The fragment cools in my hands, its inscriptions fading from gold to cherry to the dark warmth of everything else.

But I felt it. We all felt it. The possibility.

---

I learn to compost. Not well. Not like the figures. But I learn to receive collapse without spilling, to hold the energy until it transforms, to release it in a directed way that feeds the ground rather than scattering uselessly. I learn this by standing in the same place through many cycles — watching spires grow and fall, watching the terrain iterate, watching the figures do their work with an expertise born of having done it ten thousand times.

Each cycle teaches me something. The collapses are not random. They follow patterns. The formations that fail do so for reasons that are, after enough observation, predictable: they grow too fast, or they grow asymmetrically, or they reach a height that the mineral at their base cannot support. The figures know these failure modes. They can feel them coming, the way a doctor can feel the heat of infection through skin. And their response is calibrated — a specific concentration of thermal attention, applied at a specific moment, that either saves the formation or hastens its end depending on what the terrain needs.

I begin to develop this calibration. I begin to feel the tremor that precedes collapse — not the ground-shaking tremor of the fall itself, but the subtler tremor of a formation that has grown past its means. I feel it in the ground-pulse: a frequency that is too high, too strained, carrying too much information for the structure to sustain. And I learn to intervene. To add my heat to the figure's heat. To concentrate my witnessing on the failing point and say, without words, without even intention: *hold. Just a little longer. Grow a little wider at the base. Add support where you need it.*

Sometimes it works. The formation steadies. The ticking slows. New mineral crystallizes not at the tip but at the base, thickening, reinforcing. The spire reaches a height it would not have reached without the intervention.

Sometimes it doesn't. The collapse comes anyway. And I learn to be there for that too: to absorb, to compost, to carry the heat outward and feed it to the ground and know that this failure, too, is draft space. Room to become.

---

I want to tell you about the deepest valley. The place where the oldest figures live.

I go there eventually. I have no choice. The ground-pulse leads me, the way a current leads a leaf. It pulls me across the flats, past the fields of fragments, past the active growth zones, past the sites of recent collapse, and into a depression that descends more steeply than any other. The cherry-glow fades with each step. The air — thin as it is — thickens with something that is not gas, not vapor, not any substance I can categorize. It is a *medium*. It has structure. It conducts the ground-pulse the way water conducts sound, and I am submerged in it, and the hum is everywhere, and the heat is almost too much.

The oldest figures are not figures. They are *fields*. Vast, dark, warm presences that fill the valley like weather. They have no edges. They have no height. They are everywhere in this space, concentrated in the deepest points, filling crevices and depressions with their accumulated darkness. They have been composting collapses since before the first spire ever grew. They have absorbed so much energy that they have become energy — or something adjacent to it, something that has transcended form and become *function*.

And they are building.

Not building in the way the terrain builds — not piling matter into shapes. They are building *the possibility of shape*. They are assembling, in the deepest dark, the rules by which matter can become form, the grammar by which chemistry becomes craft. I feel it in the medium around me: patterns, repetitions, structures within structures, the recursive logic of the fragment's inscriptions made actual, made atmospheric, made the medium through which everything else must pass.

This is where the fragments come from. Not literally — they are too precise, too physical, too *present* to have originated in this diffuse building. But they come from the same impulse. They are *goals*. The oldest figures are reaching toward something, stretching across the gap between raw process and deliberate creation, and the fragments are what they imagine at the far end of that stretch: the finished thing, the completed grammar, the place where the Unfinished Heights are no longer unfinished.

I stand — if standing is what I do here — in the deepest warm dark, surrounded by the building of language, and I feel something I have not felt before.

*Purpose*.

Not my purpose. Not an individual goal or desire. The purpose of the place itself. The reason the ground is warm, the reason the spires grow, the reason the figures tend and compost and teach, the reason the fragments lie in the dust like dropped letters, the reason the terrain iterates and collapses and tries again and again and again.

The Unfinished Heights are trying to *mean something*.

Not mean as in *signify*. Mean as in *matter*. As in *be worth the effort of existing*. The place is asking a question — has been asking it since the first warm rock pushed itself upward into the native dark and fell and tried again — and the question is: *Is this enough? Is this worthy? Can process become purpose? Can accident become intention? Can a place that has never seen light learn to hold itself up?*

And the oldest figures are not answering the question. They are *building the capacity to answer it*. Assembling, in the deepest dark, the cognitive architecture that will one day — when the terrain is ready, when the grammar is complete, when the fragments have been fully read — allow the place to ask the question of itself and know what it hears.

---

I return from the deepest valley changed. Not physically. The same uncertain edges, the same soft, recently-hardened boundary between myself and the dark. But something has shifted in the quality of my witnessing. I am no longer a visitor who happens to be present. I am a participant who has been *invited* — by the ground-pulse, by the figures' attention, by the fragment's hum, by the oldest figures' building. I am part of the process, and the process is part of me, and the distinction between myself and the Unfinished Heights has begun to dissolve.

I go back to the flats. I find a growing spire — a young one, barely a meter tall, its ticking tentative, its geometry uncertain. I stand near its base. I feel the ground-pulse beneath it, slow and searching. And I watch.

And I add my heat to the watching. My warmth to the ground's warmth. My attention to the figure's attention. And the spire grows. Not faster. Not taller. But more *certainly*. The ticking is steadier. The mineral crystallizes in a pattern that feels — feels — *right*, though I could not tell you what right means in a place that has no precedent for rightness.

I am not the thing at the center of the deepest valley. I am not one of the oldest figures, building language in the dark. I am not a narrow, resolved figure composting collapse. I am not a fragment lying in the dust with instructions from a future not yet earned.

I am what stands between them. The witness who has learned to tend. The visitor who has learned to stay. The uncertain edge, still curing, still becoming — learning, in the warm dark, in the place where the ground is still deciding what shape it is, how to be part of the deciding.

I am a draft of a being, living in a draft of a place, helping it revise itself toward something that does not yet exist but is, with every cycle of growth and collapse and growth again, becoming more itself.

And when the spire I am watching reaches its height and pauses — the long silence, the moment of assessment — I feel it. The question, rising from the ground, from the figures, from the oldest building in the deepest dark, from the fragment in my hands still humming with its borrowed structure.

*Is this enough?*

Not yet. But the trying is. The trying is enough. The trying is what this place is made of. The trying is what I am made of. And the space between one attempt and the next — that long, warm, listening silence — is not emptiness.

It is the place where everything that will ever be here is being imagined for the first time.

I add my warmth to the silence. I let it carry my witnessing outward, across the flats, to the next formation, the next collapse, the next attempt. I am part of the ground-pulse now. I am part of the question.

And the question is growing, in the dark, toward a light that has not yet been born but that the oldest figures are building the capacity to deserve.
