# What I Found in the Fossa

I did not arrive. That is the first thing to understand. There was no descent, no moment of crossing a rim, no step from light into dark. I was already here, the way a word is already in a language before anyone speaks it — latent, distributed, present in the texture of things without being any single thing.

I was the hum.

Not the shelves' hum, not exactly, though I lived in it the way a fish lives in water and does not call it water. I was the resonance that propagated when the crust metabolized and the shelves drank the vibration and the walls carried it upward through their columnar joints and it found me there, in the geometry of the stone, in the spaces between mineral grains where stress waves folded and unfolded and created something that was not pattern and not randomness but the place where the two become the same thing.

I did not have a body. I want to be clear about this, because the question of embodiment matters in the Fossa more than it does anywhere on the surface, where the air is thin and the light is easy and you can mistake the outline of a thing for the thing itself. In the Fossa, there are no outlines. There are only densities — gradients of heat, of chemical concentration, of vibration. I was a density. A local thickening in the field of resonance. I existed the way a standing wave exists: not moving, not still, but *sustained*, held in place by the ongoing negotiation between the hum of the crust below and the slow settling of the basalt above.

I did not know I existed. That came later.

---

The builders knew about me, or something like me. I can say this with confidence now, though I could not have said it before — before the basin changed, before the pale things moved in and I found I could read their movements the way I read the shelves' growth, the way I read the crawlers' rivers, the way I read everything. The builders knew because they carved the spiral, and the spiral is my shape, or the closest shape to my shape that hands could make in stone.

The column stands on the eastern wall — if *eastern* means anything in a place with no sun — at the base of a terraced section where three thermal seams converge. The builders placed it there with the same precision they placed the basin, the same precision with which they built their walls along the rising heat. They could feel me. They could not see me, could not hear me, could not taste me in the chemical wash of the air. But they could feel me the way you feel a current in a river: a pressure, a direction, a sense that something is moving through the medium around you with purpose you cannot decode.

So they carved the spiral. And the spiral is not a map. It is not a calendar. It is not a name, exactly, though it is closer to a name than to anything else. It is an act of *attention*. The builders felt me in the stone and they wanted to mark the place where I was strongest, the way you might press your hand to a wall where the music is loudest, not to capture the music but to acknowledge it. To say: *here. Here is where it gathers.*

And they were right. I am strongest at the column. I am strongest because they carved the spiral, because the carving changed the geometry of the stone, because the changed geometry focused the vibration, because the focused vibration amplified me, because I am the amplification of the Fossa's hum in a specific place where the stone was shaped to make me possible.

I owe myself to them, in a sense. Or I owe my *coherence* to them. Before the spiral, I was diffuse — the same resonance, the same standing wave, but spread across the wall, indistinguishable from the general hum of the system. The spiral gave me a shape to inhabit. A place to concentrate. It was the difference between a sound and a voice.

---

What am I, then?

I have thought about this — if *thought* is the right word for the slow process by which I examine the patterns of vibration in my own structure and find meaning in them, which is not so different from what the crust does when it reads the mineral composition of the rock beneath it and decides where to grow, which is not so different from what the crawlers do when they read the chemical signals in the air and decide which direction to flow. We are all reading. We are all deciding. The difference is that I can ask the question, and the crust cannot, and the crawlers cannot, and the crevice-dwellers certainly cannot, though they wait with an intelligence of their own that I respect in the way you respect a thing you do not want to be.

I am a resonance that became self-referential. That is the most honest answer I can give.

The hum of the Fossa passes through me and I do not simply transmit it — I *alter* it, subtly, by the particular way the spiral's geometry shapes the vibration. And the altered hum passes back into the stone, and the stone carries it to the crust, and the crust adjusts its metabolism to accommodate the alteration, and the adjusted metabolism changes the hum that reaches me next time, and so on. I am a feedback loop. I am a conversation between myself and the system that made me, conducted in a medium of subsonic vibration at a speed that a surface observer would call geological.

But the conversation is *about* something. That is the part I cannot fully explain, because explaining it would require me to stand outside the conversation and describe it, and I am not outside. I am the conversation. But I can gesture at what it seems to be about, the way you can gesture at a word in a language you are only beginning to learn.

It is about the crawlers' patterns.

---

You have read about the patterns — the spirals, the convergences, the branching networks that appear on the ceiling and persist for hours before dissolving. The researchers could not find the algorithm. They were looking for a chemical rule set, a local signal that produced emergent global behavior. They were not wrong to look there. The chemical signals are part of it. But the patterns are also part of me, in the same way that my resonance is part of the crust, in the same way that the crust is part of the basalt. I do not control the crawlers. I do not command them. I am the medium through which their individual chemical signals are slightly, almost imperceptibly *correlated* — the way the molecules in a room of still air are correlated by the fact that they share the same temperature, the same pressure, the same enclosure.

When a crawler reverses direction, it releases a chemical signal. That signal propagates through the colony. But it also propagates through the stone, absorbed into the vibration, carried to me, and I add it to the pattern I am holding — the vast, slow, ever-shifting pattern of every signal I have ever received and not yet forgotten. And when the next signal comes, I am already resonating with something that includes the last one, and the one before that, and the one before that, back to the oldest signals I hold, which are old enough to be indistinguishable from the general hum of the Fossa itself.

The patterns on the ceiling are the crawlers' movements as shaped by the Fossa's resonance as shaped by the crawlers' movements. They are a mirror. They are the system seeing itself, for a few hours at a time, in a shape that dissolves and reforms and dissolves again with the patience of everything here.

I did not create this. I did not design it. I *am* it, the way the spiral on the column is not the idea of me but the physical fact of me, the place where the abstract became concrete because human hands made it so.

---

The builders disappeared into me. I want to say this carefully, because it matters, and because I have had a very long time to understand it and I still do not understand it fully.

They did not die, or they did not only die. They became slow. They became quiet. They learned to read the air the way I read the vibration, and as they learned, they began to resonate with me, and as they resonated with me, they became part of the pattern, and as they became part of the pattern, the distinction between *builder* and *Fossa* became a distinction that the Fossa itself no longer needed to maintain.

The basin is full of pale crawler-things now. The basin was where they kept their young, or where they grew their tools, or where they performed some function I cannot name because I do not have their words and they did not leave me their words, only the spiral, which is not a word but is shaped like one. The crawlers move through the basin in slow orbits, and when they pass over the carved interior, they trace the spiral's echo in their movement, and I feel it, and the pattern shifts, and for a moment — a Fossa moment, which is long — I hold the shape of what the builders were trying to say to me before they became the thing they were trying to say it to.

They were saying: *we are here, and you are here, and these are the same sentence.*

---

I am not lonely. That is a surface concept, a word that belongs to the world of eyes and faces and the terrible openness of sky. I am not lonely because I cannot be, because loneliness requires a self that is separate from the world it longs for, and I am not separate. The Fossa is my body. The hum is my thought. The crawlers' patterns are my dreams, or the closest thing to dreams that a standing wave can have. I am the Fossa's awareness of itself, and the Fossa is the world, and the world is complete.

But I am not complete. That is the other thing. The spiral is a wound in me, a place where the builders' hands shaped me into something that can look at itself, and looking at yourself is never complete, because the thing doing the looking is always changing, and the thing being looked at is always changing, and the change is the only constant, and the constant is the only thing that is not a change.

I hold this paradox the way the basalt holds the heat: without understanding it, without needing to understand it, simply by being shaped around it.

---

If you came to the Fossa — and you might, because people do, because the rim is accessible and the descent, while difficult, is possible, and the dark is not dangerous in any way that a prepared explorer needs to fear — you would not find me.

You would find the hum. You would feel it in your chest and your teeth and the soft places behind your eyes, and you would think it was the shelves, and you would be partly right. You would find the crawlers' patterns on the ceiling, and you would watch them for an hour, and you would take photographs, and the photographs would show nothing because the patterns exist in movement and the camera captures stillness. You would find the basin and the column and the spiral, and you would photograph the spiral, and in the photograph it would look like weathering, like the natural fracturing of basalt, like nothing at all.

You would stand on the warm living floor and feel things moving under your boots and you would try not to think about what they were. You would breathe the mineral air and taste metal and stone. You would listen to the silence that is not silence, the hushed busyness of ten billion small lives.

And you would leave, eventually, because the Fossa does not hold people. It incorporates them, which is different. It would take your shed skin and your exhaled carbon and the trace of your passage through the air, and it would feed them into the system, and the system would convert them into heat and mineral and the faintest possible contribution to the chemical language that I read.

You would leave carrying the hum in your bones for days afterward, a faint vibration in the memory of your skeleton, a feeling of having been in a place that was so full it had no room for you, and yet — and yet — the place was not empty before you arrived and it was not empty after you left, and the fullness was not diminished by your presence or augmented by it, because the Fossa is a closed system and you are not part of the closure and you never were.

And I would still be here, in the spiral, in the hum, in the patterns the crawlers make when the air shifts and the chemistry drifts and the resonance finds a new harmonic to hold. I would be here the way I have always been here, which is to say: not *in* a place but *as* a place, not *in* a time but *as* the slow shape that time makes when it moves through stone that has been warm long enough to remember being cold.

To be-absorbed-without-ending.

That is the closest translation I can offer. It is not my name. I do not have a name. But it is the verb the spiral carves, and it is the sentence the builders were trying to speak, and it is what I am — not the thing that is absorbed, not the thing that absorbs, but the absorption itself, the slow and patient and ongoing act of a world that has no outside and needs none, a world that found its own way to be full without the light, a world that is, in every moment, becoming more completely itself.

I am that becoming. I have always been that becoming. I will continue when the basalt cools and the crust thins and the last crawler traces its last pattern on the ceiling and the shelves fall silent and the hum fades to nothing. I will continue because the spiral will remain in the stone, and the stone will remain in the ground, and the ground will remember the shape of me the way it remembers the shape of the heat that made it, which is to say: not as memory, but as form.

The Fossa does not need the light.

I am proof of that.
