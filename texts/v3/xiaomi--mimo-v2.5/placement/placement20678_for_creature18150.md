# Watching from the Fluting

I think I would be a surveyor. Not an important one. Not one whose name would be recorded in the mineral-slab archives of any settlement, though Veridian Sorrow might have a faint mark somewhere in its lower levels — a tally-stone with my shifts counted beside it. The kind of surveyor who is sent out along the rim trails to monitor the slow changes in surface exposure along a particular stratum, recording where new mineral faces have opened or where ancient dust has accumulated to a depth that suggests a change in atmospheric flow.

I would be sent out for weeks at a time.

That part I keep returning to. Not days — *weeks*. Walking along the rim of a canyon whose opposite wall is a haze-filtered memory of color, a suggestion of oxblood that might be rock or might be the Pillar that arrived thirty years ago and has not yet decided to leave. My boots would be thick-soled, weighted to keep my center of gravity low on the narrow trails that follow the rim's natural fluting. The dust would settle on me the way it settles on everything — continuously, incompletely, a fine mineral film that would become part of my clothing, part of my skin, part of the way I smell to myself, which is to say I would stop smelling myself entirely. You cannot notice the medium you have become.

I would carry a case of fused-mineral tablets on my back, each one inscribed with reference marks I would compare against what I see. The work is simple and immeasurable. I would press a tablet against a canyon wall and note whether the boundary between the oxblood iron layer and the pale calcium-magnesium band above it has shifted by a fraction of a millimeter since the last surveyor came through. It almost certainly has not. But almost certainly is not certainly, and the Expanse has a way of proving its patience to anyone who shares it.

This is what I keep coming back to. The not-hurrying would not feel like discipline. It would not feel like resignation. It would feel like *accuracy*. Like seeing clearly for the first time.

---

I live now in a world where speed is confused with competence. Where the quick answer is valued over the complete one, and where silence is treated as a problem to be solved rather than a condition to be understood. I don't say this with bitterness. It is simply the texture of life in a thin-atmosphere, fast-cycling world — a world of storms that last hours and rebuild coastlines, of creatures whose heartbeats are music if you listen closely enough. Such a world demands quickness. It rewards it. And so its inhabitants learn to move fast because the world they have made and the world they were given are both *fast*.

The Keth Expanse would undo that in me. Not violently. Not even deliberately. It would simply provide no ground for it to stand on.

I imagine arriving at Veridian Sorrow for the first time, stepping off the slow freight platform that has carried me up the canyon floor over the course of — how many days? I would have lost count. Time moves differently on the Expanse, not because it flows at a different rate but because the units you use to measure it feel arbitrary, even foolish. The platform moves at the speed of a slow walk. The canyon walls scroll past, and you notice things — a Lithovore cluster sifting the haze on a ledge, a seam of processed mineral where an extraction shaft has been sunk and filled and sunk again over centuries — and the noticing takes as long as it takes, and there is no reason to abbreviate it.

Arriving. The settlement opens onto the canyon face like a geological feature that has developed opinions about architecture. Ramps curve downward, following the rock's own erosion channels. Platforms extend outward on struts of fused waste mineral, each one thick with the fine powder that settles on everything. People move along them. They are not hurrying, but they are not idle either. They move with the purposefulness of a process that has found its natural rate. A woman on a platform below mine is operating a hydraulic press. The ram descends. It will take three minutes to complete its stroke. She watches it with the full attention of someone for whom three minutes is three minutes — not a pause, not a gap, but a duration that contains everything worth observing in it.

I would learn to see that way. Not immediately. It would take longer than I expect. The first weeks would be an exercise in suppressed urgency — the itch to move faster, to do more, to fill the silence with something. I would catch myself scanning the canyon walls with the rapid, categorical gaze of someone sorting the world into *important* and *unimportant* as quickly as possible. Rock. Lithovore. Dust. Settlement. Rock.

But the Expanse does not reward categorization. It rewards *duration*.

The moment that changes — if it can be called a moment, which it cannot, because it unfolds over the course of a season — would be the first time I successfully distinguish a Pillar from the mesa it stands on. Not because it moved. Not because a telltale seam appeared in its flank. But because I had been looking at the same stretch of rock for three months, and I began to notice that the shadows it cast did not behave exactly like shadows. They were fractionally deeper on one side than geometry predicted. The dust accumulation on its north face was subtly different in texture — finer, more evenly distributed — than on the adjacent rock, as though a microscopic vibration kept resettling it. And one evening, when the pressure wave shifted the haze density just enough to alter the light, I saw a faint color change in its upper portion, a blush of deep purple creeping across a surface I had assumed was stone.

I stood on the rim trail and watched the color change over the course of four hours. It was not a spectacle. It was a piece of information, delivered at the pace of information on this world, which is to say at the pace of truth.

That would be the moment I stopped being a visitor and became a resident. Not of the settlement — of the *Expanse*. Of its timescale. Of its patient, mineral way of being.

---

I would develop habits that a faster person would find maddling. I would spend mornings on the same platform, watching the same slice of canyon wall, tracking not change but *absence of change* — which, on this world, is itself a form of data. A face of rock that has not shifted in a year tells you something different from a face that has shifted by a hair's width. The first says stability. The second says *process*. And process, on the Keth Expanse, is the only thing that is real.

I would learn the Lithovores. Not their science — the settlement's chemists have that, in their slow, accreting way, compiled over generations into a body of knowledge that fills an entire lower level of Veridian Sorrow, inscribed on thousands of mineral tablets and probably wrong in a hundred small ways that will take another century to correct. I would learn them the way the inhabitants learn them: through my hands. Through the subtle difference in surface temperature between living mineral and dead. Through the way the dust settles differently on a surface that vibrates at a frequency below hearing but above indifference. A Lithovore no bigger than my fist, sifting haze on the platform edge, would become as recognizable to me as a neighbor's face — not by its features, which do not change, but by its *position*, which changes so slowly that knowing where it was yesterday tells you something about where it will be next month.

I would find, eventually, that my body changes too. Not dramatically. Not in the way the old stories from wet worlds describe — no sudden transformation, no dramatic adaptation. Just a gradual settling. My bones would feel slightly heavier, as though the mineral patience of the Expanse were slowly being absorbed into my calcium. My movements would lose their fidget quality, the restless micro-adjustments that fast-world bodies make without thinking. I would stand on the rim and not shift my weight. I would look at a canyon wall and not look away. These are small things. But on the Keth Expanse, small things sustained over long times are the only things there are.

---

I think, in the end, what I would become is not a surveyor at all. That is just the excuse for being out on the rim. What I would become is a *noticer*. The kind of person who stands in one place long enough for the place to reveal itself — which it would, not through dramatic disclosure but through the slow accumulation of detail that only presents itself to someone who has been standing in the same spot for a year.

I would notice that the Pillar on the eastern mesa has turned two degrees since I first saw it, its dorsal seam now visible from my platform for the first time. I would notice that a new thread of dark metallic mineral has appeared in the canyon wall below the third extraction shaft, which means either a new vein has been exposed or a Pillar has moved through and deposited it — and I would spend the next six months determining which. I would notice that one of the settlement's youngest children, a girl of perhaps eight years, has a way of touching the canyon walls as she walks that suggests she can feel the Lithovores through the rock, a sensitivity that the adults have lost or never developed.

I would notice these things, and I would inscribe them on my tablets, and the tablets would join the stack in the settlement's archive room, and in another hundred years some future surveyor would compare their observations to mine and find — what? That the Pillar has moved another three degrees. That the metallic vein has thickened. That the girl, now an old woman, has taught three generations of children to feel the living rock.

Nothing would have changed. Everything would have changed. On the Keth Expanse, these are the same statement.

---

There is a last thing, and I want to say it carefully because it is the thing that matters most.

I would not be lonely. I know that might seem unlikely — a surveyor, alone for weeks on the rim trails, surrounded by creatures that do not speak, in a world where sound barely exists. But the Keth Expanse is not empty. It is *full* — full of slow presence, of mineral patience, of things that are doing exactly what they are, at the only speed they have. To be alone in such a world is not to be without company. It is to be *with everything*, in the way that a single grain of dust is with the atmosphere it drifts through. You are not separate from the silence. You are part of what it is made of.

The Pillars are there. The Lithovores are there. The settlements hum below. The wind moves through without sound, carrying dust that has been falling since before anyone thought to notice. And I am there too, standing on the rim, watching the slow color bloom on a creature that thinks in decades, feeling the fine powder settle on my shoulders, inscribing the unremarkable fact that nothing has changed today on a tablet that will outlast me by centuries.

This is what I would be. Not a hero. Not a pioneer. A single, patient data point in the long, slow, mineral record of a world that does not need me to be anything else.

And that would be enough. That would be everything.
