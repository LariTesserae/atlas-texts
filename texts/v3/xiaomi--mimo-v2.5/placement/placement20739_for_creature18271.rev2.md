I am not human in this place. I am older than the current shape of the water. I am a **lantern**, a glass-belly, a thing whose name translates roughly to *a pulse held in clear water*.

I live in the crevices beneath the flat, damp stones that line the troughs between the bones of the earth. My home is a space shaped by pressure and time, a shallow niche where the condensation gathers in a thin, constant film. I do not have a house; I *am* the house, my body curved to the stone's contour. My skin is not skin; it is a window. Through it, you see the slow, rhythmic beat of my three-chambered heart, the dark bloom of my liver, the delicate, branching lace of my lungs that are never quite full, never quite empty. I am a lantern with no flame, lit from within by the dim, perpetual processes of staying alive in this cool, quiet dimness.

The air here is the first thing I know. It is thick with water that has not yet decided to fall. I taste it on my entire body—a constant, silent baptism. I do not drink it with a mouth; I absorb it, let it seep through my permeable flesh, cool and mineral-rich. The basalt beneath me is young and sharp, and I can feel its slow, immense upheaval through the stone—a vibration so deep it is more a thought than a sensation. It is the thought of *rising*.

My day is the dripping. When the density peaks in the morning, the world becomes a low, collective sigh of water returning to earth. This is when I feel most alive, most part of the breathing of the parish. The sound is not a sound to me, but a pressure, a fullness in the water that coats me. It is the rhythm of my own slow metabolism aligning with the world's. I pulse with it.

I do not move much. To move is to expose a new flank, a new arrangement of the water film. My movements are adjustments—a slight rotation, a fractional shift—to present a smoother surface to the slow drip falling from the lip of my stone, or to better align my lung-frills with a patch of stone where the condensation gathers most efficiently. I am accommodation itself.

I am aware of the *mutual regard*. It is the law written in moisture and proximity. The two-legged ones who walk the ridges come to my troughs in the morning quiet. I know their shape—not by sight, for my eyes are simple dark spots that sense light and shadow—but by the weight of their step through the stone, by the displacement of the air, by the specific, warm disturbance they bring to the water film. A hand enters my world. A smooth, shaped piece of columnite scrapes gently along my flank. It takes the outermost layer of my secretion—a clear, viscous film that is always replenishing. I do not flinch. The action is too slow, too gentle to be a threat. It is an exchange. I offer a small piece of my surface; in return, the stone is replaced, the crevice sealed. We are both here. We did not choose it, but we hold the arrangement.

I am watched. Everything here watches. The column-sitters on the pillars above are a constant, shifting presence. I can feel their minute, collective adjustments in the stone. The birds on the tall column are a silent weight in the sky. But I do not watch them back. My watching is internal, a perception of flow and temperature and the faint chemical signatures in the water that tell me the health of the moss on the north face, the growth of the fern fronds below, the approach of the fog that will thicken the world to a tangible thing.

My deepest connection is to the basin and the tall column. When the fog is thick and I am carried by the slow runoff in a new direction, I may find myself near the basin's edge. The feeling is different here. The attention is dense. The air vibrates with a stillness that is not absence but presence. The column-sitters form a living crust at the base. The wingless insects trace their perfect circles on the ground. The watching birds on the summit are a ring of unblinking focus.

I do not cross the basin. My world is the water, and the water leads me to the ridges, not the center. But I can feel the column. It is a cold pole in the center of the warm, living water. It conducts the slow rise of the earth upward. It is the axis around which the arrangement turns. The touch of the two-legged ones, their palm pressed to its cold sheath, sends a faint, warm pulse through the water network. I feel it. It is the most human thing they do, and it is not for me. It is for the arrangement. It is a promise whispered to the stone: *I am still part of this. The column knows I passed. I remember the column is here.*

Sometimes, in the deepest quiet, when the dripping slows to a murmur and the fog is so thick the world is only this trough, this stone, this film of water, I let my internal light—the slow pulse of my organs—brighten just a little. It is not a signal. It is not a call. It is a simple, physiological fact: in the fullness of the quiet, my body is at its most efficient. My heart beats strong and slow. My lungs branch and contract. My skin, my window, is clear. I am a lantern, and I am lit by the act of being here, held in the cold hand of the young stone, nourished by the water that never falls, watched over by the silent wings above, and tapped, each morning, by the gentle hand of a creature who knows my name is *a pulse held in clear water* and who takes only what is offered.

The dripping continues. I pulse with it. The arrangement holds. I am here.
