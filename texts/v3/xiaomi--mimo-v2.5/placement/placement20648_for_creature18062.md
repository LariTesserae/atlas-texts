I am not a visitor to the Table. I am one of its children, born in the shadow of a column so old its ridges have fused into a single, scarred face. My shelter is woven from the shed skin of Column Mother—she sloughs a new layer every seven rains, and the fiber of her oldest growth is tough and sweet-smelling when cured. My name, if it must be spoken aloud, is a high, sharp click in the middle of a longer sound, like a beetle's wingbeat punctuated. In gesture, I tap the base of my throat once.

I am a Listener. My work is the Orchestra.

My day begins before the sky has any color, when the air is still cool and the mat is at its warmest, breathing slow, deep pulses against the soles of my feet. I press my palm flat to it as I rise, feeling the familiar *thump-thump-thump* of something vast and indifferent and alive beneath me. It is a good rhythm today. Strong.

I step outside. The world is dark but not black; the fungal paths glow a soft, sickly green, tracing the routes between the great columns. My destination is the Whispering Colossus, the tallest column in our stretch, whose crater-pool is wide enough to hold two people if they sit knee-to-knee. The dew is already forming. In the pre-dawn light, every ridge on every column glistens with a film of captured breath. The air is thick with the scent of ozone and crushed green life.

The sound rises around me. Not the deafening roar of midday, but the night chorus: the deep, thrumming calls of the bell-creatures echoing from the crater tops, the high, almost painful trill of the amphibians in the water-cups, the soft, shuffling clicks of the large beetles moving through the upper ridges. It is a symphony in a minor key, and it is the sound of the Table exhaling.

My task is to listen for the silences.

As I walk, I carry a gourd of water for myself and a frame of stretched hide slung over one shoulder. At the base of Whispering Colossus, I meet my cohort—three others, all moving with the same deliberate quiet. We do not speak. We nod, our hands making quick, sharp gestures. *Ridge-Walk today. South spiral. Listen.*

I climb the lower ridges of the column, my bare feet finding purchase in the textured tissue. It is wet underfoot, slick with the exudate the column produces in response to the trilling frogs. This is the sweet, protein-rich secretion that feeds the clouds of insects swarming around us. The insects are a constant pressure, a living wind against my skin. Some are tiny, a black mist around my head. Others are large, iridescent beetles that bump against my shoulder like clumsy birds. I ignore them. They are part of the work.

From the third ridge, I can see the spiral. The columns recede into the shimmering distance, their spacing unmistakable—a logarithmic unfurling from some deep, hidden center. Our job is to walk the arms of that spiral, to stand at the heart of a cluster of columns and simply *listen*. We listen for the frequencies that are missing. We listen for the hollow note in the roar.

Today, it is the lower trill that feels thin near the old, split column two spirals south. The amphibian chorus there is listless, the water in its crater-pool cloudy with inactivity. The column’s ridges look dull, the exudate not glistening as it should.

We gather at its base. The others lay out their instruments: hide frames stretched tight, hollow plant-shells of different sizes, root-cords braided to different thicknesses. I take up two smooth, flat pieces of heartwood and hold them ready.

We begin.

It is not music as the word might be understood. It is a precise, repetitive pattern of sounds designed to vibrate at specific frequencies. A deep, resonant *boom* from the large shell, mimicking the bell-creature. A sharp, rhythmic *crack-crack* of the heartwood, mimicking the large beetle's courtship click. The high, warbling trill we produce by running a wet finger along the edge of a thin, resonant membrane.

We play. We listen. The column responds. Not in moments, but over hours. A faint brightening on the ridges. A thickening of the exudate film. A slow return of the insect swarms, drawn by the renewed sweetness. The amphibians in the pool seem to perk, their trill gaining a sharper edge.

This is the feedback. This is the loudness that feeds. We are not masters of this place; we are its humors, its balancing agents. The Table made us as it made the lizards and the beetles—another tool for its own maintenance. We are the part of the system that can recognize a sickened column and speak its medicine back to it in sound.

By midday, the heat is a physical weight. The mat shimmers. The sky is a pale, washed-out bowl. The roar of the fauna is now a solid wall of sound, vibrating in my chest. I press a cool, damp cloth to my neck—the last of the water from the dawn dew. The work is done for this column. Tomorrow we will check the water quality in the crater-pool, perhaps diverting some from a healthier neighbor if needed.

Walking back, I cross a sparse zone. The columns are far apart here, and the roar recedes to a distant thunder. The mat underfoot is thick, spongy, and warm. I feel the slow, ancient pulse through every step. I am small on this vast, living table. I am a brief, sharp sound in a chorus that has played since before my people had a name for sound.

At night, I sit outside my shelter. The stars are a brilliant, unblinking frost. The columns are dark silhouettes, alive with the shifting, glowing life of their fauna. A bell-creature calls, and the sound rolls across the mat, a deep, perfect tone that makes the air shimmer. Below me, the mat breathes. *Thump-thump-thump.*

I am a part of that breath. I am a single, sharp note in the only music that matters. I am a shepherd of sound, a tender of the living architecture, a child of the Table. I am home.
