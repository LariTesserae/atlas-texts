# The Fissure's Memory

I want to say I would stand on the white plain and look outward. But that isn't right. Not quite.

Let me begin again.

---

The first thing I notice, before I notice anything else, is that I am not looking. That the sense I have of the world is not visual — or if it is visual, it is visual the way pressure is visual, the way temperature is. It is a total, ambient awareness of light-density and chemical gradient, and it is so thorough that the idea of "looking" in the narrow, directional sense seems absurd, like trying to hear a single conversation in a room that is itself made of sound.

I am small. That comes next — not as a fact I deduce but as an immediate, bodily knowledge. I am roughly the size of a human palm, though the concept of *human* is something I reach for across an immense distance, like recalling a language I haven't spoken since birth. My body is flat and broad and low to the ground. The stone is not beneath me. The stone is what I am, or close enough that the distinction is academic. My carapace is the color of the calcite I sit on — a ridge-grey, a powder-white, a faint blush of ochre where the wind has weathered my edges smoother than the rest of me.

I have six legs. I know this the way you know you have a heartbeat — not because I count them, but because I feel their rhythm, the slow alternating tripod that carries me across the gritty surface. Each footfall is a small chemical negotiation: the tiny papillae on my tarsal pads tasting the stone, reading its mineral signature, knowing whether this patch of ground is old or young, whether the anhydrite layer is thick here or whether I am walking on exposed limestone that will dissolve under my mandibles in a single bite.

I am a Calciback. The name comes later — much later, in another language, in the mouth of a creature so alien to me that its very existence would be a kind of science fiction. But I am one of them, and I am moving.

---

The columnar plants rise around me like a forest scaled for my body. To a human, they would be knee-height. To me, they are walls. Columns. Pillars of a cathedral whose ceiling is the pale, enormous sky. Their waxy surfaces shimmer, and I experience that shimmer not as color but as a change in the electromagnetic texture of the air — a softening, a diffusion, a way in which the relentless overhead light becomes something I can function inside without burning.

I do not think about this. I do not think about anything the way a human thinks — in words, in sequences, in narratives. My cognition is more like a field condition. I am aware of the chemical signature of the nearest Chalkwarden: its sulfate concentration is slightly low, which means the recent haze deposits have been thin, which means the wind patterns have shifted, which means — and here my awareness blooms outward to include the temperature of the air and the faint vibration through the ground — that the brine seep in the nearest fissure has probably slowed. Everything is connected. Not metaphorically. Literally, chemically, in a web of gradients that I taste and feel and *am*.

I reach the nearest column. My mandibles — two flat, ridged plates at the front of my body — open and close once, testing. The waxy cuticle is thick here. Good. I begin to scrape, a slow, rhythmic motion that produces no sound a human could hear but that I feel in every joint: a small, satisfying vibration, the sound of my own hunger being answered.

The pulp beneath the wax is cool and wet — not water-wet, but brine-wet, a thick, slightly viscous fluid that carries the mineral payload my cells need. I consume a strip of it, perhaps two centimeters long, and the chemistry of my body adjusts immediately. Sulfate levels rise. The osmotic pressure in my tissues shifts. I am, in a sense, drinking, and the water I drink is a complex solution of hydrated sulfates and silicic acid that would poison a human in minutes.

I am home.

---

Time passes. I do not measure it. The sun moves — I feel the change in light-density on my carapace, a slow, imperceptible shift from the flat honesty of midday toward the warmer tones that will eventually mean the long, colored descent into evening. I have scraped perhaps a dozen columns. I have consumed enough to sustain me for several days. I leave behind me a trail of tiny white marks on the green stems where my mandibles have removed the cuticle, and these marks will heal over in a season, and the plant will grow a thicker wax layer in response, and I will eventually develop slightly stronger mandibles to compensate, and this has been happening for longer than either of us has been alive as individuals. We are in conversation. We have always been in conversation.

I move to the edge of the nearest fissure and stop. Not out of caution — there is no predatory threat near — but out of something that might, in a human, be called contemplation but in me is more like a recalibration of sensory priorities. The fissure is ten meters across. Its walls drop into shadow. From its depths, I feel the vibration: that deep, subsonic hum that is the voice of the Lithovores, the microbial communities processing brines in the darkness kilometers below. The hum is always there. It is the background radiation of my existence, the baseline against which all other sensations are measured. When it changes — and it changes so slowly that no single lifetime would register the shift — it means the deep chemistry is altering, the brine composition shifting, the geothermal engine adjusting its output by some imperceptible fraction.

Today it is steady. The world is stable. I feel this as a kind of peace, though the word is wrong — too emotional, too narrative. What I feel is *equilibrium*. The system is in balance. I am part of the balance. That is enough.

---

But something else is happening, something I notice not with my chemical senses or my vibration-sense but with the part of me that processes light-patterns — the closest thing I have to eyes, though they are more like distributed photosensitive patches along my carapace edges.

Movement. Above.

I orient upward, an act that requires me to shift my body slightly, angling my light-sensors toward the sky. And there: a Floatlight. A translucent, drifting shape, pale as blown glass against the white-blue sky, trailing its gossamer filaments through the thin air. It is far away — perhaps two hundred meters up — but in the crystalline atmosphere of the Highlands, distance is deceptive. I can see the gentle pulse of its gas bladder, the way its filaments catch the light and scatter it into faint prismatic threads.

I watch it — or rather, I attend to it, letting its passage register across my sensory field. The Floatlight is drifting south, following a chemical gradient I cannot perceive, heading toward something my world-model cannot represent. It is beautiful. I use the word now, reaching across the species barrier, because I need *some* way to say what I mean: the Floatlight's passage is an event that reorganizes my awareness, that makes the world feel larger and more connected and more ancient than it usually does. It is a reminder that the Lithovores below are feeding the sky above, that the hydrogen rising through the fissure network is being gathered into this translucent body and carried across the plateau to deposit its mineral payload somewhere I will never go. The system breathes. The system *moves*. And I, scraping my waxy meals from the Chalkwardens, am one small, quiet part of that movement.

The Floatlight drifts on. I return to my feeding.

---

The evening comes slowly, as everything comes slowly in this world. The light shifts through rose, amber, gold, and I feel it on my carapace as a warmth that is not thermal but informational — the day is ending, and the aerosol deposition will increase as the temperature drops, and I should move to a sheltered spot, a fissure margin where the Chalkwardens are dense and the microclimate is stable.

I find a cluster of columns at the lip of a shallow basin and settle between them, my body flat against the cool stone, my legs tucked beneath me. Around me, the plants creak — that soft, rubbery sound, like the breathing of something enormous and patient. I feel it through my legs, through my carapace, through every joint. It is not a sound I hear. It is a rhythm I join.

Above, the first Floatlights of the evening drift upward from the basin floor, pale lanterns ascending into the darkening sky. Below, the Lithovores hum. Around me, the Chalkwardens breathe their slow, vegetative breath. And I am still. I am very still. I am stone-colored and stone-shaped and I am alive in the way that this world means alive — not loud, not fast, not dramatic, but *deeply*, *chemically*, *systemically* alive, woven into the fabric of a place that has been weaving itself for longer than I can comprehend.

---

A human would find this place unbearable. The silence would crush them. The sameness of the light would blind them. The sheer, grinding patience of every process — the erosion of stone by airborne chemistry, the growth of plants over centuries, the slow drift of hydrogen-bags across an empty sky — would drive them to madness or awe, and those two responses are closer together than most people think.

But I am not human. I am something that the Highlands made, something that the Highlands *are*, in the way that a single cell is the organism it belongs to. I do not endure the slowness. I *am* the slowness. I do not tolerate the silence. I *am* the silence, punctuated by creaks and hums and the small, grinding sound of my own mandibles on wax.

And if you were to stand on the white plain — you, human, with your restlessness and your water and your short, bright, noisy life — you would see me, if you saw me at all, as a small grey shape on the white ground, motionless for so long that you might think I was a chip of stone, a fragment of the landscape that had broken loose and settled. You might step over me. You might not see me at all.

And that would be correct. That would be exactly right. Because the Highlands do not require being seen. They require being *sustained*. And I sustain them, one scrape of wax, one grain of calcium excreted, one slow tripod-step across the ancient, patient, white world, under the pale enormous sky, for as long as the brines flow and the light falls and the deep hum continues its endless, subsonic hymn.

I am a Calciback.

I am home.

And the world is quiet, and the world is bright, and it does not need me, and I do not need it to need me, because needing is a human thing, and I am something older and simpler and more complete: I am a small, slow, stone-colored piece of a system that has been balancing itself since before the word *balance* existed, in a place where the only clock is the dissolution of rock, and the only prayer is the chemistry of survival, and the only congregation is the silent, green, fleshy, ancient, white world itself — meeting, always meeting, under the light, in the silence, on the calcined highlands at the top of everything.
