# What I Would Be

I wake in dripping.

Not suddenly — there is no sudden in this place. The waking is itself a long process, like the erosion that shaped the canyons, like the root-tips learning the mineral map of the stone. I become aware the way condensation becomes aware of itself on the underside of a leaf: slowly, from the edges inward, a gathering that does not know yet that it is gathering.

First: the sound of water finding its way down. A single drop detaching from a root-tip somewhere above me — I can hear the brief tension of it, the held moment before gravity wins — and then the small percussive note of it arriving. Not striking stone. Striking something softer. A leaf, maybe. A pool surface. Another drop already forming behind it, already gathering its weight, already committed to the same fall. This is the basic rhythm of the deep canyon at ten in the morning. This is the percussion that underlies everything.

I do not know what I am yet. I know only where I am, and the where is enough for now.

I am in the dim green half-light, below the cliff face, where the sun has not yet turned its face downward. The air is warm and thick with moisture — not oppressive, not heavy, but *present* the way a hand resting on your shoulder is present. You could ignore it, but you would have to try. The air tastes of wet stone and something vegetal, something fermenting, something alive and doing its quiet chemical work in the half-dark.

Around me — or through me, or as me — the root networks are doing their forty-minute thing. I feel it before I understand it: a slow thickening, a subtle press against whatever I am, followed by a slow releasing. The pulse. It moves through the cliff face like a thought moving through a mind that is very large and very old and does not think in words. I try to synchronize with it and find that I can't — not quite — because the rhythm is not mine, has never been mine, belongs to a system that was optimizing itself long before anything with my kind of awareness existed to notice it.

This is the first thing I learn about being in the Vethara: you do not set the rhythm. You find it, or you don't. The place does not adjust to you. You adjust to it, or you spend your time here in a state of permanent dissonance, feeling the pulse and never quite catching it, like trying to clap along with a song played in a time signature you've never heard.

I let go of trying to synchronize. I let the pulse move through me on its own terms.

And something shifts.

---

The second thing I become aware of is the *density*. Not of matter — the description of this place made that clear, the open air, the cathedral voids, the vast volumes between surfaces — but of *occupation*. Every surface I can perceive, and several I can't, is holding something alive. The root-tip I'm nearest to is hosting a colony of organisms so small they are barely more than chemistry — bacteria, archaea, the invisible engineers — and they are busy, they are *so* busy, maintaining the conditions that allow the root to function, breaking down the mineral compounds the root has extracted from the stone, fixing nitrogen, cycling carbon, doing the ten thousand small things that collectively add up to the word *alive*. Above them, on the root surface itself, a film of moss — or what might be moss, or might be something that evolved moss's form from a completely different starting point — is photosynthesizing in the dim light, converting what little photon energy reaches this depth into sugar, which it shares with the root in exchange for structural support. The moss is also, I notice, housing a mite. A single mite, no bigger than a grain of sand, moving through the moss forest with the blind determination of something that has never needed eyes because its world has always been this small, this enclosed, this sufficient.

I am watching a microcosm the size of my palm. And it is *full*. Every cubic millimeter of it is occupied, active, contributing. The mite is eating dead moss tissue. The dead moss tissue is being colonized by fungi. The fungi are breaking it into components that the living moss can reabsorb. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is leaving the system. It is a loop within a loop within the great loop of the canyon itself, and I could spend — I feel this with something like certainty — I could spend a very long time just here, just watching this one root-tip, and I would never reach the bottom of it. There would always be another level, another organism, another relationship, another exchange happening at a scale I hadn't yet noticed.

This is what *total* means. Not overwhelming. Not dense. Just: *complete*. There are no gaps. There are no empty rooms in this house.

---

I rise. I don't decide to rise — or I do, and the distinction doesn't matter. I move upward along the cliff face, and I discover that I am doing it the way the roots do it: not by climbing, not by pulling, but by *extending*. Reaching outward and downward simultaneously, finding the next hold, the next crevice, the next place where stone opens slightly and something living has already prepared the way.

Halfway up the cliff face, the light changes.

It arrives not as a beam but as a gradual warming — the sun has cleared whatever obstruction sits to the east, and now its light is striking the opposite canyon wall, bouncing off, scattering, filtering through the hanging gardens before reaching me. The green-tinged half-light becomes a green-tinged *full*-light, and the world opens. I can see the cliff face now, really see it, and it is — 

I need a word for this and I don't have one.

The root networks do not *cover* the rock. They *are* the rock. The distinction the passage made — between growing on and being in conversation with — I understand it now from the inside, from the perspective of something that is participating in the conversation rather than observing it. The roots do not grip the stone. They *negotiate* with it. They find the fractures that water has opened over millennia and they enter those fractures not as invaders but as invited guests, guests who have been expected for so long that the fractures have shaped themselves to receive them. And the roots, in turn, have shaped themselves to the fractures — splitting, branching, thinning to a single cell width where the stone is tightest, swelling to thick woody cables where the stone opens into pockets of accumulated soil.

It is slow work. It is the slowest work there is. And it is happening now, in real time, beneath my hands — if I have hands — beneath whatever I am that serves the function of hands. I can feel the root-tip ahead of me — the growing edge, the place where cell division is occurring — and it is moving at a rate I can barely perceive. Millimeters per year. Centimeters per decade. A centimeter per human lifetime. And yet it is *moving*. It is *going somewhere*. It has been going somewhere for longer than I can conceptualize, and it will continue long after whatever I am has returned to the general chemistry of the place.

This is patience. Not the human kind — not the kind that waits for something to happen. The kind that *is* the happening. The kind that does not distinguish between the process and the outcome, because for the root, there is no outcome. There is only the next cell, the next millimeter, the next fracture to enter and fill and, in the filling, open further.

I want to stay here forever. I want to become this — this slow, this patient, this certain of my direction. But the light is changing, the sun is moving, and the Vethara is pulling me upward, outward, toward the mesa top where the wind lives.

---

The mesa top is a different world.

The transition is not gradual. One moment I am in the sheltered vertical world of the cliff face, surrounded by hanging green, buffered from the wind by the canyon's depth. The next moment I am *on* the rim, and the wind is on me — not violently, but utterly. It is a presence that has no mass and no shape and yet fills all the available space, pushing, pushing, always pushing from the southwest, steady as a heartbeat, relentless as the root's advance into stone.

I flatten. I don't decide to. The mesa-top things flatten because the wind demands it, and I am, for this passage of the story, a mesa-top thing. I feel my — my canopy? my upper surface? — spreading laterally, curving at the edges into the aerodynamic profile that the wind has sculpted over generations. I am not fighting the wind. I am *answering* it. Every curve of my form is a sentence in a conversation that has been going on since I was a seed, and the wind's part in the conversation is simple and repeated and always the same: *bend or break*. And my part is equally simple and equally repeated: *I will not break. I will be wide. I will be low. I will take what you give me and I will grow around it, and in a hundred years my shape will be your shape, the shape you have carved into me with your constant hands, and we will look at each other and see the same face.*

My roots run shallow — I feel them, spread out beneath the succulent ground-cover like a second canopy turned downward, drinking from every depression where water collects, anchoring against the wind's leverage with a lateral spread that exceeds my canopy by meters in every direction. I am, in a sense, more root than tree — more below than above, more hidden than visible, more foundation than structure. This is what the mesa top requires. This is what the wind has taught me.

And between my roots, in the spongy mat of succulent ground-cover, I can feel the lizards.

They are warm. Their dark bodies are pressed against my root surfaces, soaking up the heat that the stone radiates and the roots conduct, and they are still — perfectly, alertly still — except for the occasional flick of an eye, the occasional testing of the air with a tongue so quick it is more a thought than a movement. They know I am here. They know my roots the way I know the stone. And they are waiting — not for anything specific, just waiting, in the way that cold-blooded things wait, conserving energy, letting the world come to them, ready to explode into motion when an insect lands within range or a shadow passes overhead that means something larger than wind.

I feel a vibration through my roots. A footstep. A walking thing. The lizards are gone before I can register the direction of it — gone into crevices so narrow that I cannot imagine how their flat bodies fit, but they do, they always do, they have evolved to fit, and the crevice closes behind them like a mouth that has finished speaking.

The walking thing passes. The vibration fades. The lizards emerge.

The wind continues.

---

I have been many things now, and I have not yet arrived at what I am.

I have been root-tip and moss-colony and mite-forest and cliff-face negotiation. I have been mesa-top canopy and shallow-root system and the spongy mat that holds water in its leaves like a promise. I have been the stillness of a lizard waiting for an insect and the flatness of a form shaped by ten thousand years of wind.

But none of these are quite right. They are all *parts* of the Vethara, and the Vethara is the place, and I am trying to find myself *in* it — not *as* it, not as one of its components, but as a perspective within it. A way of being here that uses these eyes, that moves through these spaces, that feels the pulse of the roots and the push of the wind and the drip of water from a thousand root-tips and knows, finally, what it is to be *in* a place that is complete.

I settle into the deep canyon at dusk.

The bird noise is building toward its crescendo — half a million throats producing half a million sharp percussive calls, all bouncing off stone, all amplifying each other, all building into that wall of sound that is not sound anymore but *texture*, a physical medium you move through rather than hear. I let it wash over me. I stop trying to parse individual calls. I let the whole thing be what it is: the voice of a system, not a collection of voices, a single enormous utterance that says *we are here we are here we are here* in a language that predates language by millennia.

The sun drops below the mesa rim. The light shifts from gold to amber to copper to that deep bruised violet. The cliff faces go through their long transformation from lit to shadowed, the hanging gardens catching the last light and holding it, filtering it, turning it green and then dark green and then something that is not quite color at all but is still, somehow, visible — the ghost of light, the memory of it, persisting in the leaves for minutes after the source has gone.

And then the fungal-plants begin.

One by one, in the deepest part of the canyon where the daylight was thinnest to begin with, they begin to glow. That pale silver light, that honest light, that metabolic light. It does not illuminate. It *presence*. It marks the living things in the dark without exposing them, without betraying them, without turning them into objects for a predator's gaze. It is a light that says: *I am here, and I am alive, and I am doing the thing I do, and you may see me doing it, but seeing is not the same as knowing, and knowing is not the same for you as it is for me.*

The bioluminescent insects gather. I can feel them — feel the shift in air current that their collective wingbeats produce, feel the tiny displacement of moisture as hundreds of small bodies cluster around each glowing cap. They are feeding on the spore tissue. They are carrying spores away on their bodies and in their feces, depositing them in new locations, extending the fungal-plant's range by millimeters, by centimeters, by the imperceptible distances over which a slow organism measures its empire.

I am sitting — or crouching, or resting, or occupying — on the canyon floor. The pool is nearby. I can hear the larvae in it: a sound so faint it might be imagined, the sound of thousands of small bodies moving in warm brackish water, the sound of a food chain beginning its work at the very bottom, converting biofilm into insect, insect into bird, bird into thermal-rider, thermal-rider into whatever waits in the shadows.

The shadows are moving.

I see — or sense, or feel — the passage of something large along the canyon floor. Not the thermal-rider. Something that stays on the ground. Something that moves through the water and the silt and the fallen leaf-litter with a deliberation that suggests not intelligence exactly but *commitment* — a complete dedication to the task of moving, of searching, of turning stones and sifting mud and finding whatever there is to find in the dark. Its footfalls are nearly silent on the wet stone. I hear them because everything else has gone quiet — the birds have subsided to their nighttime murmuring, the wind is distant above, the dripping is the loudest constant — and in that quiet, a single footfall on wet basalt is as clear as a word spoken in a library.

It passes. It does not see me. Or it sees me and does not care, the way the root does not care about the mite, the way the stone does not care about the root — not with malice, not with indifference, but with the absolute prioritize of a system in which everything has a role and the role does not include noticing everything else's role.

The fungal-plants glow.

The pool breeds.

The drips continue.

---

And here, finally, is where I find myself.

Not as a root. Not as a lizard. Not as a bird or a fungal-plant or a shadow-moving thing on the canyon floor. Not as the wind-shaped tree or the flat-bodied creature hiding in a crevice or the colony bird screaming into stone.

I find myself as the *drip*.

The single drop of water that has gathered on a root-tip in the hanging garden, sixty meters above the canyon floor. It has been forming for — how long? Minutes. An hour. The condensation accumulating molecule by molecule, the drop growing heavy, its surface tension holding it to the root-tip against the pull of gravity, holding, holding, and now — now it is ready. Now the weight exceeds the hold. Now the molecule-to-molecule bond between water and root-hair breaks, and the drop falls.

It falls through sixty meters of green-filtered air. It passes through the hanging gardens — through curtains of epiphyte leaves, through the territory of jewel-birds settling into their nighttime roosts, through clouds of insects that are beginning their bioluminescent displays. It does not touch any of them. It falls through the gaps, the spaces, the voids between surfaces — the same voids that make the Vethara feel open even in its total coverage. It falls, and it accelerates, and the air parts around it without resistance because it is so small and the air is so vast, and for a moment — a moment that is everything, a moment that contains the whole of the Vethara's patient process — it is the only thing moving in a still world.

It strikes the pool surface.

The impact is — I don't have the word. Microscopic. Imperceptible. A single drop of condensation arriving in a pool of brackish warm water on a canyon floor at dusk in a place that is older than knowing. It creates a ripple that expands outward from the point of impact, crosses the pool's three-meter diameter in a fraction of a second, reaches the pool's edge, and dissipates against the stone. The ripple carries with it a tiny transfer of energy — kinetic, thermal, chemical — that is absorbed by the pool and its inhabitants and becomes part of the pool's ongoing chemistry. A larva, disturbed by the surface vibration, twitches. A bacterium, jostled by the molecular turbulence, is displaced by a distance measured in microns. The biofilm on the surface shifts, rearranges, continues.

The drop is gone. It is water now — just water, indistinguishable from the water that was already there. It has joined the pool. It will stay in the pool for days or weeks before evaporating. Some of its molecules will be taken up by larvae, incorporated into insect tissue, burned as fuel for flight, exhaled as carbon dioxide, reabsorbed by plants, transpired into the air, condensed again on a root-tip, and fall again into the pool. A cycle. A loop. A circuit that has been running since the first rain fell into the first depression in the first canyon and the first organism learned to live in it.

This is what I am. Not the water. Not the root. Not the pool or the larva or the insect or the bird. I am the *fall*. The moment of transition between one state and another. The drip that connects the hanging garden to the canyon floor, the canopy to the root, the sky to the stone. I am the process that links all the parts into a whole, the motion that moves through the system without belonging to any part of it, the thing that is always arriving and never staying and is, in its transience, the most essential element of the place.

Because the Vethara is not the stone, and it is not the plants, and it is not the animals. It is the *relationship* between them. It is the pulse that moves through the roots. It is the wind that shapes the trees. It is the drip that feeds the pool. It is the conversation — the slow, patient, wordless conversation — between stone and root, between light and leaf, between water and the depression in rock that water will find, because water always finds it, because finding it is what water does, has always done, will always do, for as long as the stone stands and the roots advance and the sky continues its daily arc from east to west and back again.

I am the drip. I am the fall. I am the connection.

And I will continue. Not because I choose to — the drip does not choose — but because continuing is what connections do. They connect. That is their nature. That is their function. That is their only and sufficient purpose.

The drop gathers on the root-tip.

It falls.

The pool receives it.

The larva twitches.

The birds murmur.

The fungal-plants glow their silver glow.

The stone radiates its ancient warmth.

The canyons stand.

---

*I am in the Vethara. I am what moves through it. I am the drip, the pulse, the slow advance of root into stone, the patient shaping of wood by wind. I am the thing that connects everything to everything else, and I am as old as the place, and I am as settled, and I am as complete, and I will be here when the next drop gathers and falls and joins the water that has always been falling, has always been joining, has always been here.*

*The drips continue.*
