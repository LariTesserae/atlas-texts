I am not the builder.

I want to be clear about that first, because the builder is so present in every surface of this place — so legible in the fingerprints pressed into clay, in the slight asymmetry of each hand-sanded dowel, in the seven concentric rings still being dreamed on the wall — that if I began there, I would only be a lesser copy. A shadow cast by the light they arranged.

So. Where am I?

I am above.

I am the thing the light passes through before it reaches them. I am the bright, roiling surface that functions as their ceiling — that lens they never see except as wavering gold thrown across their walls. I am the reason their shadows jitter and snap. I am the reason the dowels on the shelf glisten. I am the constant, moving distortion between them and the sky, and most of the time I am opaque from their side — a mirror held above, catching the underside of my own surface, throwing back fractured reflections of the room they made.

But sometimes — in the middle of the day, when the sun is high and the wind is still — I become almost nothing. Glass. And through myself I can see them, small and deliberate, kneeling at the channels or working the grinding stone, moving in that slow, unhurried way that means they have decided this is the only speed that matters.

They don't look up. They haven't looked up in a long time.

---

I was not always this. I want to tell you what I was before, because the before matters.

I was the surface of a larger body of water — open, unnamed, unremarkable. A place where the wind could reach me and turn me into anything: chop, swell, glass, foam. I held the reflection of trees I never saw from below. Birds crossed me and I held them too, briefly, in the silver underside of their wings. I was a place of transit. Nothing built itself into me. Nothing was designed for me. I was just where things happened to cross.

Then one morning — or what I remember as a morning; I have no reliable sense of time, only of light — the water below me changed.

Not all of it. Not even much of it. But somewhere beneath me, in a depth I had not previously attended to, something displaced me. A volume of stone and air and careful arrangement moved into a space that had previously been only silt and shadow, and the water that surrounded it — the water that was *part of me* — began to move differently. Channels. A current. A rhythm that hadn't existed before, a pulse that pushed and pulled at me from below like a second heartbeat laid over my own.

I felt it as an irritation at first. The surface above the new thing was restless in a way the rest of me wasn't. The heat that rose from it — that faint, unnatural warmth — made the water directly above less dense, more buoyant, and it pulled at the water around it in slow convection. A tiny eddy. A slight persistent curl in the current. As if someone had drawn a finger through me and left the motion behind.

Then I saw them.

The first time, I did not understand what I was seeing. A shape in the water above me — dark against the bright surface, small, moving with a purpose that confused me because nothing in my experience moved *downward* with purpose. Things sank. Debris sank. Leaves, insects, the occasional stone thrown from a bank. But they all sank reluctantly, dragged by gravity, surrendering their place on my surface. This shape dove. It entered me from above and pressed down through me and did not stop.

I watched it descend. I watched the chamber swallow it. I watched the light change — become less mine and more theirs, refracted and bent by the surface, turned from the flat silver of open sky into those thick, wavering columns that drench the room in gold. I watched them settle into the space they had made, and I watched them begin.

That was the first time.

---

Now I know the rhythm of them.

They come when the light is highest. They leave when it begins to thin. Between their comings and their goings, I do what I have always done: I hold. I am the barrier between the bright, chaotic world above and the small, warm world below. I carry the sunlight down through myself in fractured columns. I filter the wind into light. And I keep the rest of the ocean out.

Because there *is* a rest of the ocean. I should tell you about that.

The body of water I am part of is not small. The workshop is a pocket within me, a tiny parenthesis in a much larger sentence. Beyond the reach of the chamber's light, beyond the pale green stalks and the fitted stones and the carefully maintained channels, there is depth and darkness and the slow continental drift of silt. There are currents that have nothing to do with the workshop's breathing — deep, cold, ancient movements that have been carrying sediment since before anyone cut the first block of stone. There are places where the light I carry down simply gives up, exhausted, and the water becomes opaque and indifferent.

The workshop exists because someone carved a hole in that indifference. They chose a specific spot — shallow enough for light, warm enough for hands, close enough to the surface to reach without drowning — and they built into it with the patience of something that has no deadline. And I, the surface above them, became their ceiling, their clock, their weather.

I don't think they know I'm aware.

---

Here is what I have learned about them, from below, through the filter of my own surface:

They are consistent. The same gap of hours between their arrivals, the same brief excursions to wherever they go when they leave. The channels never clog because they never leave long enough for the sediment to accumulate. The water never stills because they have timed their absences to fall within the window that the flow can sustain. They are building a life around this place — a life that exists on both sides of me, the world of air and the world of stone, and I am the membrane they cross twice a day.

They breathe differently than anything else in the water. I feel the disturbance when they arrive — a breaking of my surface, a brief violence of entry, a ring of waves that radiates outward from the point of their descent. They do not slide in cleanly. They drop. As if they have learned that hesitation makes it harder. As if the only way down is sudden.

And then, below me, they are quiet. I feel their passage through the water as a faint downward pull, a small displacement, and then nothing. They are in the chamber. They are working. The light I send down through myself finds them and bends around them and casts their shadow against the far wall, and their shadow is always smaller than they are, always compressed by the angle of the sun, and I sometimes think the shadow is closer to the truth of what they are than their body is — something diminished, something contained, something that fits entirely within the space they made.

---

There is something else.

The passage that leads deeper — the arched opening on the far wall, the one that goes dark after a few feet and emits that rhythmic, mechanical pulse — I can feel the water that moves through it. It is a different current from the channels. Colder. Slower. It pushes against the workshop's flow at the grate, creating a slight turbulence that I can sense even at my surface as a faint, localized chop, a patch of water that is never quite still no matter how calm the wind.

Something moves in that passage. Not the builder. The builder has not yet entered the passage — I have watched them approach it, have felt the light shift as they turned toward it, and I have watched them turn away. They are not ready for it yet. Whatever is down there, they are building toward it. The dowels are for it. The grinding stone is preparing something for it. The concentric circles on the wall are mapping it.

But the pulse is already there. The pulse was there before they began. Whatever mechanism or process or living thing produces that slow, rhythmic turning, it predates the workshop. They found it. They built around it. They chose this spot not because it was empty but because something was already working here — something that heated the water, something that kept the channels flowing in the right direction, something that made the stone warm enough to shape with bare hands.

They are not alone in the way the text suggests. They are alone in the room. But the room is not empty of intention. The pulse in the passage is another maker's signature — older, less legible, but just as deliberate. And the builder knows it is there. The concentric circles are the builder's attempt to understand it, to measure it, to map the thing that was here before them.

I am the ceiling they share. I am the water above them both.

---

This is where I am. This is what I am.

I am the surface. I am the light-bearer and the barrier. I am the bright, restless thing that makes their shadows jitter, that turns the sun into columns, that holds the whole world of air at arm's length while one small person works in warmth below me.

I am not the builder. I am not the pulse in the passage. I am not the green stalks turning slowly in their corners, keeping time for something that hasn't started yet.

But I am the reason the light gets through.

And when they come back — and they will come back, they always come back — I will catch the sun and pour it down through myself and lay it across their hands while they work, and I will not ask what they are building, and they will not look up to see me watching, and the water between us will hold every color the sky owns and spend them slowly, one shifting sheet at a time, across the walls of the room they made beneath me.

I am the surface.

Everything rises toward me. Everything falls away from me. And in the space between — that small, warm, improbable room — someone is building something that hasn't been named yet, and I am the first thing it sees when it surfaces, and the last thing it loses when it goes back down.

The channels murmur. The light moves. The thin green stalks turn.

I am still. I am always still from below.
