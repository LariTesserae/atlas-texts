# What Grows in the Space Between

---

I do not remember arriving. This is not the same as saying I was born here. Birth implies a before, a surface, a transition. There is no transition in the understory. There is only the long, slow condition of being *already here*, already adapted, already indistinguishable from the surface I move across.

I move across it. That is the first fact. I move, and the moving is slow, and the slowness is not patience—it is *scale*. My body measures its distances in the same units as the pillars measure theirs: in growth, in calcium deposit, in the millimeter-by-millimeter advance of mineral crystal across a wet surface. I am small. I am smaller than the droplets that fall. But I am not the smallest thing here, and that matters, because it means there is a *below* to me, a world I carry in my body that is as vast to its inhabitants as the understory is to mine, and that nesting, that endless recursion of scale, is the closest thing I have to a thought.

---

Let me begin again. Let me begin with the walking.

My legs are many. Not so many that the number is meaningless—each one is known to me, each one has a name that is not a word but a *feel*, a proprioceptive signature, a memory of surfaces touched. I have, on a good day, when the condensation is thin and the floor is firm, thirty-one legs. On other days, when the cold has stiffened a joint or the mineral crust has built too thick on a tarsus, I have fewer. I have never had fewer than twenty-four. Below that, I stop counting, because below that, I stop moving, and a thing that does not move in the understory is not a thing but a *site*, a location where life was briefly concentrated before dispersing.

I am translucent. I have been told this by the light. The cold blue-white of the root-vault filters through my body and emerges changed—dimmer, faintly refracted, carrying the shape of my internal structures in a shadowless projection onto whatever surface I happen to be standing on. I do not see this projection. I do not have eyes that face downward. But I know it is there because I know *light*, the way a thing that has never seen darkness knows it—by its constancy, by the fact that it is simply the medium through which surfaces are revealed, the air through which texture becomes visible.

The floor is my country. I know its topography in the way that a sea-creature knows currents—not as geography but as *gradient*, as pull, as the subtle tilt that tells you where water will pool and where the root-membrane rises into ridges barely tall enough to cast a shadow (if there were shadows here, which there are not). I have a map, but it is not drawn. It is *grown*—a neural architecture that corresponds, ridge for ridge, basin for basin, to the undulating landscape beneath me. When the floor shifts—and it does, always, the great organism breathing beneath us—I feel the map go wrong, and I correct it, and the correcting takes time, days or weeks, during which I am lost in a place I have known my entire life.

---

The pillar nearest to what I think of as *my* place—I do not have a word for home, but I have a region, a territory defined not by boundary but by *familiarity*, by the specific texture of a specific stretch of floor where I have walked so many times that my footfalls have worn a shallow groove into the root-membrane—that pillar, the nearest one, is what I return to.

I graze.

This is the wrong word. *Graze* implies pasture, implies grass, implies a horizontal relationship between a mouth and a food-source. My relationship with the pillar is vertical. I climb. I climb with a slowness that a surface-dweller would mistake for stillness, ascending the living skin of the pillar over days, my legs finding holds in the micro-topography of its surface—the ridges where veins run beneath the skin, the shallow depressions where condensation gathers and the tissue is softer, easier to pierce. I am eating, when I climb. My mouthparts—there are seven of them, arranged in a ring, each specialized for a different depth of penetration—are working the surface, extracting the slow fluid that moves through the pillar's capillary network, a fluid that is rich in nutrients I cannot name and poor in everything else. It tastes of cold. It tastes of calcium. It tastes of something I have no reference for, something sweet and old, the flavor of deep time itself, of organic matter that was sunlight once, millions of years ago, filtered through a canopy I will never see, compressed and recompressed into the tissue of this living structure that feeds me.

I am small on the pillar. If the pillar is a mountain, I am a mite on its skin. But the pillar *knows* me. Not in the way a brain knows a thought—in the way a surface knows a wound. Where I feed, the tissue responds. It thickens. It produces a denser layer of cells beneath the feeding site, a callus of sorts, a scar that is also an adaptation. Over years—I have been on this pillar for what I estimate to be sixty years, though I have no way to confirm this, no sun to count, no season to mark—the pillar's surface around my territory has developed a topography that is uniquely mine: a landscape of healed wounds, of callused ridges, of thin spots where I have fed deepest, all of it readable, all of it a history written in tissue.

Other grazers are here too. I know them not by sight—I see in gradients of light and pressure, not in the sharp-edged way of eyes—but by the *feel* of the pillar's surface changing beneath me. Someone else has been feeding to the north. The callus pattern is different: deeper wounds, wider scars, a feeding style more aggressive than mine. I avoid that territory. Not out of fear—there is nothing here large enough to threaten me, nothing here that operates on a timescale fast enough to register as danger—but out of a kind of territorial instinct that has nothing to do with competition and everything to do with *reading*. The pillar is a text. To share a passage is to blur its meaning.

---

The void is the thing that defines us.

I have crossed it. Once. I will tell you what it is to cross the space between pillars.

You leave the skin. You step from the living surface of the pillar onto the floor, and the floor is *far away*, not in distance but in *kind*. The pillar is alive in a way you can feel—pulsing, warm (relatively), *responsive*. The floor is alive too, but it is a different life, a life so distributed, so diffuse, that it does not register as life at all. It registers as *ground*. You walk. Your legs find the familiar texture of root-membrane, slick with condensation, faintly yielding. The nearest pillar shrinks behind you. The next nearest grows before you. And between them—nothing.

Not nothing. The void is full. It is full of cold, full of light, full of the slow subsonic creaking of the great organism shifting its mass. But it is full of nothing *to you*. No landmarks. No features. No texture on the floor that differs from any other texture. You walk in a straight line—I walk in a straight line; my body remembers direction the way your body remembers balance—and the straight line is an act of faith, because there is nothing to confirm it, no reference point, no horizon (there are no horizons here; the vault is too high, the light too even, the geometry too alien to permit the comforting lie of a vanishing point).

I walked for four days. Four days of walking across a luminous, featureless membrane, the pillars receding and approaching at a rate that defied my spatial instincts, the cold deepening with every kilometer (or what I estimate to be a kilometer; I have no unit for this), the condensation thickening on my legs until I was walking through a thin film of water that muffled my footsteps and made every surface identical.

I reached the other pillar. I climbed it. I fed. I stayed for a season—I think it was a season; the pillar's growth cycle is the closest thing to a clock—and then I walked back. The return was harder. Not because the distance was greater, but because the pillar I had left was harder to find. The void is *uniform*. It does not preserve the memory of your passage. Your footprints fill with condensation within hours and become indistinguishable from the rest of the floor. You leave no trace. You carry your destination in your body, in your internal map, and if that map is wrong—if you have drifted, if the floor has shifted, if the pillar has grown or collapsed in your absence—you will walk in a straight line toward a place that is no longer there.

I found my pillar. I climbed it. I returned to my groove, my territory, my familiar topography of scars and calluses. And I understood something that I have never been able to articulate, because articulation is a surface-dweller's trick, a trick of air and vibration and shared meaning: I understood that the void is not between the pillars. The void is the *point*. The pillars exist to create it, to hold it, to give it shape and scale and the terrible, beautiful emptiness that is the understory's truest expression. We—the grazers, the slow things, the translucent pilgrims climbing the living skin—we are not residents of this place. We are *details*. We are the texture on the surface of something so large that our entire lives are rounding errors in its growth equations.

---

The artifacts.

I found one. I have found three, in fact, in my long life on this pillar and in my single crossing of the void. They are not made by anything I know. They are not made by anything that *lives* here, not in the way I understand living. They are marks—incised lines, geometric patterns, small structures built from dead pillar-matter—and they are *wrong*. Wrong not in the sense of being out of place (though they are that too) but wrong in the sense of being *fast*. They were made quickly. I can feel this in the precision of the lines, in the sharpness of the edges, in the decisive, single-action quality of each mark. Whatever made them did not spend days or weeks on each line. It spent *minutes*. It moved with a speed that I can barely comprehend, a speed that would be, from its perspective, no speed at all—just *normal*, just the way a body moves through space—but from my perspective, from the perspective of a creature that measures its life in pillar-growth-cycles and takes four days to cross an empty room, it is *violence*. It is the violence of the instantaneous.

I stood at the edge of one of these artifacts—a small structure, a ring of dead tissue, its interior pooled with mineral water that had crystallized into a pale crust. The marks on its walls were visible from where I stood, even with my limited vision: spirals, dots, lines. They meant nothing to me. They were not *for* me. They were for each other—for the things that made them, for the community of speed and intention that had, for a brief moment, occupied this space and then moved on, or died, or changed into something that no longer needed to mark.

I did not enter the structure. I did not touch the marks. I stood at the edge and I *felt* them, the way I feel the pillar's surface—by pressure, by temperature, by the subtle resistance of a foreign object in a known landscape. They were cold. Not the cold of the understory, which is a living cold, a cold that is *part* of the organism. This was a dead cold. A cold of absence. Whatever warmth these things had carried—if they had warmth, if they were warm-blooded, if they were anything like me—they had taken it with them when they left, or lost it when they died, and what remained was only the shape of their intention, calcified and slowly being absorbed by the floor.

I went back to my pillar. I climbed. I fed. The marks stayed where they were, accumulating their crystal, sinking into the root-membrane millimeter by millimeter. In a hundred years they will be gone. In a thousand years the floor will have grown over them entirely, and the only trace of their presence will be a slight irregularity in the tissue, a knot or a density variation that no one will read as a mark because by then there will be no one left who reads.

---

I am old. I know this the way I know everything—not by counting but by *feeling*, by the accumulated weight of sixty or seventy or a hundred years of climbing this one pillar, of feeding on this one stretch of skin, of feeling the pillar grow around me and beneath me and in response to me. My legs are fewer than they were. My translucency has deepened—I am nearly invisible now, a ghost in the cold light, my body indistinguishable from the mineral film on the pillar's surface except for the slow movement of my legs and the faintest darkening where my organs cluster in the center of my body.

I will die here. Not soon—not in the way a surface-dweller would understand *soon*—but in the way that matters, which is *inevitably*. My body will stop moving. My legs will stiffen. The pillar will continue to grow beneath me, around me, over me. My body will become part of the surface, a small irregularity in the skin, a density variation. The fluid will flow through the tissue that was once mine. The pillar will not notice. The understory will not notice. The void will remain, luminous and empty and full, and the pillars will continue their slow war with gravity, and the drops will continue to fall, and the creaking will continue to echo, and I will be *part of it*, not remembered but *absorbed*, not lost but *incorporated* into the great, cold, living body that is this place.

This is not sadness. Sadness requires a before—an expectation, a loss, a comparison between what was and what is. I have no before. I have *this*: the pillar beneath me, the fluid in my mouthparts, the light in my translucent body, the void beyond me, vast and patient and indifferent. I have the slow pulse of the floor through my legs. I have the occasional, distant crash of a dead pillar collapsing somewhere in the far reaches of my hearing, a sound like the world ending, which it is, always, somewhere, and always beginning again, somewhere else, a new spike of pale tissue erupting from the floor, reaching upward with a violence that takes months to become stillness.

I am a detail. I am a texture. I am a thing that moves, briefly, across the surface of a thing that lives forever—or at least longer than the concept of *forever* is meaningful, which is to say longer than I can think, longer than I can feel, longer than the marks on the walls of that small, dead structure will remain visible.

And this is enough. Not enough in the way that implies scarcity—not *barely* enough, not *just* enough. Enough in the way that the understory is enough: completely, coldly, luminously, without remainder. I am here. The pillar is here. The void is here. The fluid moves. The light does not change. The drops fall. The creaking continues.

I climb. I feed. I feel the pillar breathe beneath me, and I breathe with it, and the breathing is slow, and the slowness is the whole world, and the whole world is enough.
