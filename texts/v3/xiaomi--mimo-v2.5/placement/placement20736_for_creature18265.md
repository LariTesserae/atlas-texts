# Hollow-Born

---

I am lying on my side in the sleeping hollow with my knees drawn up and my hands folded under my jaw, and the stone is warm behind my back, and the stone is warm against my chest, and I am the right size for this place in the way that a seed is the right size for the pod it grows in. I have been awake for some time. I know this because my hands know it — the pads of my fingers are already pressed flat against the wall beside my face, reading the grain without my telling them to, the way a tongue returns to a loose tooth. The basalt is slow-breathing today. The thermal exhalation is shallow, which means the shafts haven't reached the upper fissures yet, which means the warm-month sleep is still holding. I could stay here. I want to stay here. But my bladder is pressing and my stomach has that particular emptiness that isn't hunger yet but will be soon, and so I roll — one motion, knees tucking, hands pushing, spine curving into the shape the hollow taught me — and I am upright, crouching in the entry gap, feeling for the vine-cord ladder with my bare foot.

The cord is cool and damp and textured. I know its age by the feel. This is a young cord — three seasons at most, maybe four. The fibers are still distinct under my sole, not yet fused into the smooth, almost woody surface that old cord develops. My toes grip it the way my toes always grip it: without thought, without effort, the arch of my foot curling to match the curve of the rope. I descend.

The passage below is lit — not brightly, never brightly — by a colony of growth clinging to the wall at chest height. I know this colony. It belongs to the Asheri family, three hollows along and one level down, and it is one of the Quarter's finer lights: steady, cool-toned, with a faint bluish edge that distinguishes it from the commoner green-white. I pass my hand near it as I step off the ladder, and the growth responds — not visibly, not in any way an outsider would notice — but I feel the coolness of it on my palm, the faint moisture it carries, and I know it is healthy. I know it will glow tonight when the shafts retreat. I make a small sound, barely a sound, more an exhalation shaped into approval, and I move on.

The passage winds. It always winds. I could describe the turns — left past the deep hollow where the Wren family stores their dried vine-cakes, right where the passage narrows and the ceiling drops and you have to duck and turn your shoulders, then left again where it opens into a chamber where three passages converge and the acoustic confusion is total and the chittering is so thick you can feel it in your molars — but describing turns implies a map, and there is no map. There is only the body's memory, which is more reliable than any map, because it accounts for things a map cannot: the way the air moves here (a faint upward current, warm, carrying the copper-sweet smell of the ground cover), the way the stone sounds under my fingertips (dense, unfractured, safe), the way the light falls (two shafts, amber, crossing at the chamber's center, each one trembling with the slow drift of dust that tells me the air is still, no thermal shift, no collapse risk).

I am in the convergence chamber. I stop. I listen.

The chittering is the background — always the background — but within it I parse: three distinct alarm clusters, distant, in the upper passages, meaning a rooster is moving somewhere above me, hunting the climbers as the first light reaches their roosting spots. The chittering nearest to me is feeding chatter: low, rapid, unalarmed. The climbers are busy. Good. It means the insects are active, which means the system is cycling, which means today will be like yesterday, which is what today should be.

I crouch at the base of the chamber's main ladder — a thick, ancient vine, braided from four separate runners, its surface worn to a dark, polished smoothness by generations of hands and feet. I place my palms flat on it and feel: strong, still alive at its core, moisture moving in its vascular tissue, no rot, no brittleness. I climb.

Four body-lengths up, I reach a horizontal passage that runs along the chamber's upper wall. The hollows here are occupied — I can hear breathing from the nearest one, the slow, deep breathing of sleep, and I pass it quietly, my feet finding the worn stone lip without looking, my shoulders pressing close to the wall. The passage narrows. The light thins. I am navigating now in the way that is most natural to me: hands on the wall, fingertips reading the grain, each touch a word in the stone-tongue that tells me *curve here, widening there, hollow ahead, seam to the left, iron-bleed — don't touch, it will stain — and now a step down, and now the passage opens, and I smell it before I see it.*

Food. My food cache. A small hollow, no larger than my two cupped hands, tucked into the wall at waist height. Inside: a pile of stripped leaves, dark, waxy, their inner flesh exposed and already browning at the edges, meaning I should eat them today, meaning I was right to come. I take three leaves and fold them — thumbnail to edge, peel in one motion, the resinous skin separating with a faint, sticky resistance — and I eat. The taste is familiar. It is the taste of everything: the water locked in the leaf's cells, the slow sweetness of the plant's labor extracting that water from the basalt's mineral lattice, the faint copper tang of the resin that I have tasted every day of my life and that I will taste every day until I stop tasting anything. I chew. I swallow. The water releases gradually — not all at once but in a slow, sustained trickle that my body receives the way the stone receives the shafts: absorbing, storing, distributing. I am drinking. I am eating to drink. This is the way it works.

The leaf is gone. I have two left. I eat the second with the same deliberate attention, because attention is not a luxury here, it is the price of admission. Every act is an act of awareness. The way I chew — slowly, extracting every molecule of moisture from the tissue before I swallow. The way I stand — one foot slightly ahead of the other, weight balanced, ready to move if the stone tells me something has changed. The way I listen — to the chittering, to the insect drone, to the slow exhalation of the stone, to the particular silence that means a crawler is passing beneath my feet in the mat below, its muscular body rippling through the decomposing fibers with a sound like tearing cloth.

I finish eating. I lick the resin from my fingers — it tastes of copper, of course, it always tastes of copper — and I wipe my hands on the front of my garment, which is woven from vine-fiber and already carries the stains and smells of every surface I have touched today, which is many surfaces, which is always many surfaces.

I think about what to do next. But thinking is the wrong word. The thinking happens in my hands, in my feet, in the part of my mind that is never separate from the stone and the air and the living dark. There is always something to do. The passage to the east has been narrowing — I felt it last time, the walls pressing closer, the floor rising slightly where debris has accumulated. I should check it. The Wren family's light-colony needs supplementing — they mentioned it at the last gathering, their glow dimming at the base, and I have a fragment from my own colony, a piece I clipped last week, still viable, still faintly luminous in my pouch. I should carry it to them. And there is the matter of the high fissure — the one that nearly reaches the surface, the one the children are warned away from. I passed near it yesterday and felt something in the stone: a thinness, a vibration, not danger exactly but a change, the stone speaking in a tone I have not heard before, and I should go back and listen more carefully, because the stone does not speak without reason.

But first.

I press my palm flat against the wall, fingers spread, and I feel the stone breathe. In. Out. A thermal cycle so slow it would be invisible to any sense less patient than touch. The basalt is warm. It is always warm. But the warmth has a texture — today it is smooth, even, undisturbed, which means the system is stable, which means I can afford the time to stand here, in this half-lit passage, with the chittering in my ears and the copper-sweet taste in my mouth and the bioluminescent glow on my face, and I can simply be.

This is what the Quarter makes of you. It makes you into someone who can stand still and be alive in the standing still. Not waiting. Not resting. Being. The stone breathes and you breathe with it, and the walls glow and the insects hum and the climbers chatter and somewhere above a rooster folds its wings and settles into its roost with a sound like a door closing on a deep room, and you are here, in your body, in the dark, in the green, and you are enough. The place is enough.

I let go of the wall. I move on. The passage curves to the right, and I follow it, reading the stone as I go, listening to the world that is always speaking, always answering, always holding its breath just beneath the surface of what can be heard. My bare feet make no sound on the organic mat. My hands trail the wall, reading, reading, reading. The light shifts — a shaft of amber, somewhere above and to the left, angling down through a fissure I cannot see, marking a slow bar across the passage floor. I step through it and feel its warmth on my skin, brief, almost hot, and then I am past it, back in the greenish dim, and the passage narrows, and I duck, and I turn, and I go deeper.

The stone breathes. The world hums. The dream I had last night — the vast hollow, the living light, the sense of depth beyond depth, of something old and patient and waiting in the deepest dark — recedes but does not disappear. It lives in the part of me that lives in the stone, which is to say: most of me. I carry it the way the plants carry the water — slowly, invisibly, turning it over molecule by molecule, extracting from it what sustains me, letting the rest settle into the deep places where it will stay until I dream it again.

I go on. The passage opens ahead of me, and I hear the chittering rise, and I smell the vine-sap, and I feel the stone change under my hand — a seam, a softness, a place where the iron has bled out and the basalt is cooler, weaker, telling me its history in the language of touch. I stop. I press both palms flat. I listen with my hands.

The stone speaks. I hear it. I have always heard it.

I go deeper.
