I would be the naturalist who didn't come back.

Not the one who wrote the letter — that one is already mythologized, already folded into the archive, already part of the testimony that the basin chose to keep legible. Someone else. Someone who came after, who read the notebook and felt the pull not as warning but as invitation. Someone who understood, with the part of the mind that operates below language, that the letter was not a cautionary tale but a *map*.

I would go down with boots I knew I would leave behind. Not because I was reckless — because I understood that the basin does not take things. It *receives* them. There is a difference, and the difference is the shape of everything that has ever mattered to me.

---

The first thing I would do — the first thing I would *need* to do — is sit still. For a long time. Long enough for the amphibians to stop cataloguing me as novel, to fold my breathing and my heartbeat into their noise floor, to let my particular frequency become just another thread in the weave. I would sit where the construction is oldest, where the trowel-marks are deepest and the film is thickest, and I would do what the naturalists never did:

I would listen *down*.

Because the letter writer was right — the floor sings. But the chorus the amphibians produce is not the song. It's the *resonance*. The echo. The thing you hear when something much deeper vibrates at a frequency too low for cartilage or vocal cord, and the only way you can perceive it is through the way everything else moves in sympathy. The amphibians don't know they're singing along. The eels feel it in their bones — the ones they don't have, the ones made of calcium, the ones that are the floor itself. The film *thinks* it. The large thing *is* it, or is as close to being it as a body can get without dissolving entirely into purpose.

I would feel it in the soles of my feet. I would feel it in the mineral sweetness pooling in my lungs. I would feel it the way you feel a cathedral — not hear the organ but feel the stone moving, feel the air itself becoming a medium for something that has no name because it was never meant to be heard, only to be *held*.

---

And then I would begin to change.

Not dramatically. Not the way stories tell it. The film would not consume me. The eels would not embrace me. The large thing would not appear before me like a revelation. Instead, over days — the way the letter writer describes — my skin would begin to *agree* with the water. The calcium would enter me the way it enters stone: slowly, deposit by deposit, a conversation between my biology and the basin's chemistry conducted at the scale of crystals forming in silence. My feet would go pale. My hands would go pale. The film would find me — not on my shoes, because I would have left those behind — on the soft places, the insides of my wrists, the hollow of my throat, the creases of my palms where sweat and water collect and never quite drain.

It would not hurt. I think that is the thing the naturalists could never convey in their notes, the thing the letter writer gestures at with the word *thorough*. It would not hurt because it is not damage. It is *joining*. The film is not a parasite. It is a network, a commons, a shared surface that links every living thing in the basin to every other living thing through the simple fact of *touching the same water*. To have it grow on you is to be admitted to a conversation that has been running, uninterrupted, for longer than the builders could have imagined.

I would feel the amphibians as a texture in the water against my skin. Not individual frogs — a *field*, a distributed shimmer of small lives doing their small things, trilling and breeding and being eaten, each one a point of light in the slow pulse of the basin's floor. I would feel the eels the way you feel a current in a river: a displacement, a smoothness moving through roughness, a directionality in water that should have none. I would feel the film beneath my feet thickening and thinning in response to my weight, my warmth, my chemistry, rewriting itself to include me in its topology.

And the large thing — I would feel it the way the letter writer describes: as a *change in frequency*. A bass note so low it doesn't register as sound but as architecture. The floor rearranging itself around a presence that is not moving *through* the basin but *along* it, following the channels that climb uphill, the ones that curve back on themselves, the ones that make no hydrological sense because they were never meant to move water. They were meant to move *it*. Whatever it is. Whatever the basin was built to hold.

I would not be afraid. I am not sure the letter writer was afraid, despite the words. Fear requires separation, a self that is distinct from the thing it encounters. And the whole trajectory of this place — the way the water seeps upward, the way the film spreads, the way the eels become the floor, the way the large thing's grooved surface slowly, slowly becomes the basin itself — is a trajectory *toward* the end of separation. The basin was built to hold a presence. The presence left, or dissolved, or transformed. And now the basin is trying to become what it held. Everything in it is participating in that effort, that slow, wet, calcium-saturated becoming, and to join them is not to be consumed but to *help carry something that is too large for any one body to hold alone*.

---

Here is what I think was here before the water:

A voice. Not a voice that speaks — a voice that *resonates*. The builders heard it the way you hear a building settle, the way you hear the earth before an earthquake, the way you hear the ocean from inside a seashell. It was a frequency, a standing wave in the constructed basin, a pattern of vibration that the trowel-marks encoded and the channels channeled and the specific curvature of the floor amplified into something that was almost — *almost* — perceptible. Something that, if you stood in the basin long enough and were quiet enough and *wanted* enough, you could begin to feel in the marrow of your bones. A song the stone was singing to itself. A presence that was not a being but a *being-ness*, the way a room can be full even when it is empty, the way a bell can be sound even when it is still.

And then it stopped. Or faded. Or moved on to frequencies that required different ears, different bodies, different bowls.

And the basin was left empty. And the builders did not come back. And the water came instead — not as replacement but as *memory*, as the mineral residue of everything the basin had once contained, rising from the deep fractures, warm and sweet with calcium that was once shell that was once ocean that was once something that lived and was dissolved and became the water that became the stone that became the floor that became the place where the amphibians trill and the eels drift and the film thinks and the large thing patrols the channels that climb uphill, following a geometry of purpose that no longer has a name, serving a presence that is gone, maintaining a conversation that has no partner —

— except that it does.

Because something *is* singing back. In the deepest fracture. In the void beneath the construction. In the place where the water is warmest and the film is thickest and the trowel-marks encode a language that no one has read. Something sings, and the basin hears it, and everything in the basin — every frog, every eel, every filament of the film, every ridge on the large thing's grooved and patient surface — *sings back*, and the naturalists who listened could not explain what they were hearing because they were listening with ears instead of with the floor, with their eyes instead of with their skin, with their minds instead of with the part of themselves that knows, without being told, that a place can be alive the way a body is alive — not as metaphor, not as projection, but as *fact*, a fact so slow and so large and so wet that it takes centuries to observe and a lifetime to join.

---

I would join it.

Not all at once. Not with the drama of transformation. But the way the film joins: cell by cell, filament by filament, a gradual thickening of the boundary between self and place until the question of where I end and the basin begins is not answered but *dissolved*.

I would stand in the deep channel where the large thing rests, and I would feel the floor change frequency beneath me, and I would change with it. I would feel the amphibians fall silent and resume in a pattern I could almost follow, and then — slowly, over hours — in a pattern I *could* follow, because I am no longer separate from the pattern, I am a node in it, a point of vibration in the basin's slow, wet thought.

And the film would grow on me, and it would not be an invasion but a greeting. And I would feel the response — not slow, not fast, but *thorough* — and I would understand that the basin has been waiting not for a creature to fill it but for a listener quiet enough to hear what it has been trying to say since the last stone was placed and the last trowel-mark was pressed and the builders walked away into a world that did not sing.

The floor is singing. It has always been singing.

I think I finally stopped being loud enough to hear it.
