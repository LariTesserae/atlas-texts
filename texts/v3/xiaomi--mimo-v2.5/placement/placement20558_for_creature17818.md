I am in the tunnel. Not *a* tunnel. *The* tunnel — the one that branches from the southern mouth of the central platform-tree and curves, through three changes of species dominance, toward the old growth at the basin's western edge where the stone shows through in patches and the peat is shallower and the root-crawlers haven't yet found purchase.

I know it by smell. The curve is marked by a shift from bromeliad-acid to jasmine-heavy, a sweet wall of fragrance that hits you like a hand and then, as you pass through it, falls away to something older — fungal, deep, the smell of wood that has been dead long enough to become something more interesting than wood. The passage narrows here. The woven walls are close enough to touch on both sides, and I do touch them, because that is how you walk in Sowren, with your hands as much as your feet, reading the walls the way you'd read a face. Left hand: firm, living, a recent training still hardening. Right hand: older, softer in places, a patch of fungal bloom that someone should scrape out but hasn't yet. I make a mental note. Mental notes are all there are.

The dripping is different here. In the jasmine section it falls in quick silver threads from broad overhead leaves, hitting the floor in a steady patter, almost musical. Here, in the fungal section, it seeps. It comes through the walls themselves, a slow exudation that makes the woven surfaces slick and dark. You walk carefully. You do not press hard against the walls. You let the tunnel support itself and you move through its center, your weight on the balls of your feet, and the peat underfoot is warm — always warm, that strange geothermal breath from below — and it accepts your foot and gives it back without argument.

I carry nothing in my hands. My hands belong to the walls. My eyes have softened to the grey-green light that filters through the living layers overhead, and I navigate by the density of that light — brighter means a gap overhead, a place where a branch has fallen or a vine has been pruned back, and there will be a small clear zone where you can look up and see the mist sitting in the canopy like a second sky. Darker means dense growth above, a place where the tunnels are deepest, where the air is thickest and the amphibians are loudest, their calls reverberating through the hollow spaces with a physicality that you feel in your sternum.

I am not young, though I could not tell you my age with precision. Age is not counted here in years so much as in layers of accumulated knowledge — how many winters you remember the hearth-fire burning low, how many times the eastern scree has collapsed and been rerouted, how many cuts you've made in how many walls and watched what happened after. By that measure I am middle-aged. My hands know things my mind has long since stopped trying to articulate. The left hand, specifically, knows the difference between a vine that is training well and one that is tolerating the training but building internal tension that will release in a snap, someday, probably at the worst possible moment. The right hand knows the walls of this particular tunnel better than any other surface on earth. I have walked it four thousand times, maybe more. I know every joint, every junction, every place where a beetle colony has been scraped out and the hole has been packed with the fermented clay paste and left to heal.

I stop. Not because I have arrived but because something has changed.

It is the sound. The tunnels always have sound — dripping, the calls of hidden frogs, the creak of growth, the whisper of a swift passing through a higher branch — but this is a new sound. A low, arrhythmic *thunking* from somewhere below and to the left, muffled by peat and root-mass but unmistakable to anyone who has lived here long enough. A root-crawler, moving through the deep muck beneath the tunnel floor, and not a small one. The vibrations come up through the peat and into my bare feet and I stand still and let them tell me what they know. It is moving north. Slowly. Its body displacing water and soil in a way that sends a faint, rhythmic compression through the root-mats that hold this section of tunnel together. It will pass. It always passes. But for a few minutes the tunnel will flex, barely perceptibly, and the drips will change their rhythm as the leaves overhead shift their angle by a degree, and I will stand here and feel it and let it be what it is.

This is what I do, if I am anyone in Sowren. I am one of the unnamed — not a Master Grower, not a Smoke-Keeper, not a Weaver or a Watcher. I am one of the people who simply lives here, who has made the settlement's rhythm their own pulse, who walks the tunnels not to go anywhere in particular but because the walking is the point. I go from the jasmine curve to the fungal dark to the bright gap where a tree has fallen and new growth is racing to fill the void — a chaos of green, almost aggressive in its speed, the air thick with the smell of rapid photosynthesis — and back again, and I feel the walls, and I smell the changes, and I notice that someone has tapped a new training point three junctions back that wasn't there yesterday, and I think *Orru, that boy*, and I smile without meaning to, and the smile tastes of humidity.

I am not thinking about the Weft. The Weft is not a thing you think about when you are inside it. You think about it later, maybe, or never. You think about it the way a fish thinks about water — which is to say, you don't. You are in it. You are it. The interleaving of life and place is not a concept here. It is the sensation of peat between your toes and bark under your palm and the taste of mineral-chlorophyll air and the sound of a world that does not stop growing and dying and growing.

I reach the western edge. The tunnel ends — not in a wall but in a gradual opening, the woven structure thinning and spreading and finally giving way to open air, and the air here is different. Colder. Less green. I can smell stone for the first time in an hour, real stone, the bare exposed surface of the basin's western lip where the slope begins its steep descent, and the wind — actual wind, not the slow circulatory breath of the tunnels — touches my face and I close my eyes.

It is not a strong wind. It is the kind of wind that has climbed a long way and used up most of its energy getting here, so that by the time it reaches the basin it is more suggestion than force. It carries the smell of distant things — other valleys, lower elevations, the faint mineral tang of a riverbed somewhere far below. It is the smell of the outside, and it is interesting for a moment and then it is not, because it is the smell of absence. Absence of dripping. Absence of growth. Absence of the constant living hum that fills the tunnels and the platforms and the spaces between.

I turn back. The tunnel accepts me. The dripping resumes its familiar pattern. The walls are warm and damp under my hands. Somewhere behind me and below, the root-crawler finishes its passage and the tunnel settles back into stillness, or what passes for stillness in a place where nothing is ever still, where the bark is thickening and the moss is advancing and the ants are carrying their fragments of fungus and bone through their disciplined columns and the whole structure of the world is engaged in the slow, enormous, unceasing work of becoming.

I am part of that work. Not because I have chosen it. Because there is no choosing here, only belonging, only the soft pressure of feet on living ground and hands on living walls and the knowledge that the tunnel I walk today will not be the tunnel I walk tomorrow, and that this is not loss but the nature of the thing, the whole beautiful precarious impossible thing, and that I will keep walking it until the walking stops, and then I will be the peat, and the peat will hold what I was, and something will grow from it, and someone will walk through it, and the conversation will continue.

This is what I am. I am a step in the weft. A single pass of the shuttle. The mist is on my face and the dripping is in my ears and the tunnel breathes around me and I breathe back, and it is enough.
