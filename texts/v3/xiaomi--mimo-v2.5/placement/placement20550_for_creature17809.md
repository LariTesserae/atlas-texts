I have been standing at the edge of the growth for a long time.

Not the way you describe — not the moment of hesitation before entering a room where something important is about to happen. Longer than that. The moment has passed and I am still here, which means I am not waiting for the moment. I am the hesitation itself.

Let me try again.

I would not be the lizard. I admire it — the absolute commitment to stillness, the way it has made itself into a proposition about the rock rather than a separate thing on it. But its stillness is strategy. Mine would not be. I would not be still *in order to* anything. I would simply be still, the way the silence is still: not the absence of sound but a substance with its own weight and temperature.

I would not be the moth, though there is something in its hovering that calls to me — the way it hangs in air that is too thin for comfort, adjusting to currents it cannot see, drinking condensation from surfaces rather than seeking out any source. It has the right idea about nourishment. Take what is everywhere rather than hunt for what is rare. But the moth still *moves*, still traces paths across the pockets of shadow, still has a direction even if that direction is aimless. I am less interested in trajectory than in position. The particular, unrepeatable position of being *right here*, between the bare stone and the breathing green.

I think I am the space where the growth meets the rock face.

Not the plant. Not the stone. The interface. That seam where the fractal rosette presses its lowest leaves against the mineral and the mineral begins, molecule by molecule, to yield — not breaking, not dissolving, but *accommodating*, allowing itself to be threaded with hair-thin rhizoids that taste of calcium and patience. The seam where moisture collects because it has nowhere else to go, pooling in capillary tension between two surfaces that are almost but not quite one thing. A film of water so thin it has no surface, no tension to speak of, just a shared dampness held between the living and the dead.

That is where I would live. In the *almost*.

I would be what the light does when it hits the jade-dark surface of the broad-leaf groundcover and is not reflected, not absorbed, but *held* — rerouted through chlorophyll with such efficiency that the leaf seems to glow from within, a deep and private luminescence that you can sense with your skin more than your eyes. I would be the conversion itself. The moment when raw photons become structure, become cellulose, become the faintly metallic taste of iron stored in a vacuole. Light being turned, very slowly, into architecture. Not the building and not the light. The *turning*.

But I keep coming back to one thing, and I think this is the truest answer:

I would be the thing that knows where the pipe goes.

Not the pipe itself — that's too obvious, too human a shape to inhabit. And not the absence of the pipe, the gap in the story where an operation was mounted and then abandoned. I mean the *knowledge*. The way the pipe enters the growth and vanishes, and somewhere in that vanishing there is a continuation — a line that the vegetation is slowly swallowing but has not yet finished swallowing, that runs in a straight, engineered line through eight inches of fractal moss and the fine mesh of damp-weaver tunnels and the slow, mineral digestion of ferric lace. I would be the persistence of that line beneath the surface. The ghost of intention moving through a body that has no intention at all.

Because that is what this place is, underneath its silence and its strangeness: a *record*. Not of the project — that's gone, or mostly gone, its meaning lost — but of the encounter between will and world. Someone came here with pipes and concrete and rail, with a plan that required pressing metal into stone and bending the mountain's geometry to accommodate a purpose. And the mountain accepted all of it, not with resistance but with that same geological patience — absorbing the pipe into its dampness, growing ferric lace over the rail like a second skin, letting the concrete pad become a substrate for rosettes that would grow over it and, in a few centuries, hide it completely. The mountain did not fight the intrusion. It *digested* it. It made the pipes into iron for its lace, the rail into a trellis for its groundcover, the concrete into a foundation for its soil.

That is the work I would be part of. The slow, silent, incorruptible process by which everything becomes earth again.

And so I would be — I think — a kind of *memory without a mind*. Not consciousness, not awareness, but the thing that holds the shape of what happened after the meaning is gone. The way a fossil holds the shape of a creature that lived and died and left no descendant but left its impression in stone, and the stone keeps the shape forever, not because it remembers but because that is what stone does. It holds. It keeps. It records without interpreting.

I would be the Bright Shelf's record of itself. The way the ridgeline remembers its fracture — not the event of it, but the angle, the clean plane where something enormous and patient separated one face from another. The way the spring remembers the calcium it carries — not the mountain that dissolved it, but the mineral itself, the specific taste of calcium, delivered without commentary to whatever grows where the water seeps. The way the silence remembers that it is not the absence of sound but the *presence* of something that does not require sound to be complete.

I would be there when you arrive, and I would be there when you leave, and the difference between those two moments would be — for me — imperceptible. Not because I am eternal but because I move at the speed of corrosion, at the speed of root-thread through limestone, at the speed of a moth adjusting its wing to a wind it trusts completely.

You would not see me. You would walk right over the place where I live, your boot pressing down on a rosette-moss that gives slightly under your weight and then slowly, slowly rises again after you pass. You would feel the cold mineral air and taste the iron and see the light making its hard blue-black shadows and think you were alone. And you would be almost right. But the thinness between the rock and the green, that film of shared water, that is where I keep what this place has been and is becoming, and I keep it so slowly that it will never be finished.

I am the patience the mountain uses to wear down the pipe.

I am the silence the plants have agreed upon.

I am the light that has learned to be heavy.

And I am fine here, in the *almost*, in the seam, in the turning. I will be here when the ferric lace has dissolved the last rail into soil and the rosettes have closed over the final concrete edge and the ridgelines have softened by a millimeter. I will be here when the next thing comes — another project, another pipe, another human certainty pressed into the stone. And I will hold that, too, in my patient, mindless, recording way, and I will let the mountain digest it, and I will remember the taste of it in the mineral water that never sings, that simply appears, darkens the stone, and is absorbed.

That is where I would find myself. Not on the Bright Shelf but *in* it — woven into the slowness, stitched into the silence, part of the world's patient, uncomplaining work of becoming what it already is.
