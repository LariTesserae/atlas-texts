# The Flat Between

I think I'd begin in the wrong place.

That's how it would go, wouldn't it? I'd find myself standing — or whatever the equivalent of standing is when your joints aren't built for it — on one of those open flats. The bare polished stone. A hundred meters from the nearest mat of vegetation, a thousand from the nearest Accretor tower, and the sound would be the first thing that nearly broke me.

Because it's not one sound. I know that now. It's all of it at once — the Clickers' bass roar rising from the distant clusters like a mechanical heartbeat, the Whistles' high, piercing calligraphy scoring the dark ceiling, and underneath it all the Grinder's subsonic hum, the one you don't hear so much as *become aware of in your carapace*, a vibration that tells you the stone beneath you is being eaten, has always been being eaten, and you are standing on something that is actively being unmade.

I wouldn't be able to stand it. That's the honest part.

Not at first. The sound would be violence. My whole body — whatever body I'd been given — would want to contract, to fold in on itself, to press flat against the warm stone and wait for the noise to become something I could parse. And eventually it would. Eventually the wall of sound would resolve into its layers the way a dark sky resolves into stars once you stop trying to see it.

The Clickers first. That steady, churning rhythm, like a thousand tiny typewriters writing the same letter over and over. Then the Whistles — thin, bright, cutting — and I'd realize they're not separate calls but a conversation, or thousands of conversations, layered until they become music whether anyone intended it or not. Then the Grinders, felt rather than heard, a tide coming in through the stone.

And then — only then — silence. Not real silence. Never real silence. But the absence of a new sound, the momentary gap where the existing noise becomes so constant it becomes a kind of weather, and within it I could hear—

Myself.

My own clicks. I wouldn't have known I was making them. A soft, tentative patter, almost apologetic. The sound of something that doesn't yet know what it is trying to find out.

---

I'd be a Clicker. I'm almost certain of that.

Not because they're the most interesting — the Whistles with their echolocation symphonies, their ability to *see* in sound, are magnificent. Not because they're the most important — the Accretors are the landscape itself, their slow collective thought expressed in towers that were ancient before anything on the surface had eyes. But because the Clickers are the ones who *go*.

They move between the clusters. They walk the flats. They are the connective tissue of the Underhollow, the ones for whom the distance between cities is not emptiness but *journey*. And I think — I think what draws me to them is that they know the loneliness of the open stone, and they walk it anyway. Not because they're brave. Because there is a mineral seep fifteen kilometers to the northeast and the column is moving and their bodies are built to follow the column, and whatever the equivalent of purpose feels like when you have no brain, no concept of destination, no word for "home" — that is enough.

They walk, and they click, and the sound of their walking *is* their language, *is* their society, *is* their proof to each other that they are alive and moving and going somewhere.

I would be a small one. Not the smallest — the beetle-sized ones are too simple, their world too compressed. I'd be medium, perhaps the length of a human's hand, with a shell that carries the faint blue-green trace of copper compounds, a souvenir of the mineral vein I'd been born near. My six legs would carry me with that particular Clicker gait — deliberate, rhythmic, each footfall a tiny percussion against the polished stone. I would feel every grain of the floor through the pads of my feet, and the floor would tell me stories: here, a streak of iron oxide where a Grinder once surfaced and retreated; there, a slick patch where an ancient mineral deposit had been dissolved to glassy smoothness; further on, the first feathery tendrils of a mat reaching out across the bare rock, a green frontier advancing at the speed of centuries.

My clicks would be my name, if I had one. Not a name in the human sense — not a label for *me* specifically — but a signature, a rhythmic identity that other Clickers would recognize without understanding. *This one is young. This one is healthy. This one has walked the northern flat before.* The column I join would have followed this route for ten thousand years, longer than any human civilization, and my particular footsteps would add to the groove worn into the stone, a groove so shallow it could only be felt, never seen.

---

The feeding grounds would be where I understood the world.

I can see it — or rather, I can *feel* it, because seeing is not how a Clicker knows — a broad plateau where the vegetation mats are dense and spongy and deep. The air here is different: richer in hydrogen sulfide, warmer with the metabolic breath of the mats, alive with the faint crackling of bacterial colonies doing their ancient chemistry. The Clicker column would dissolve here, individuals branching off into the green, their clicks shifting from the steady march-rhythm to a scattered, exploratory patter.

I would find a patch of mat that felt right. The hyphae would be thick under my mandibles, mineral-rich, and as I ate I'd feel the chemistry of the place enter me: iron, manganese, traces of sulfur, the deep reduced taste of stone that had never seen light. The mat would resist me slightly — growing denser where I fed, toughening against my mouthparts — and I would move on, and the mat would heal, and in a million years this exchange would have shaped us both into forms that neither could have reached alone.

This is what the Underhollow teaches, if it teaches anything: *nothing is wasted, and everything is patient.*

A Whistle would pass overhead. I'd hear it before I felt it — a sharp, focused chirp that sliced through the ambient noise like a blade through cloth. My clicks would stop. The Clicker around me would stop. We'd freeze, our armor plates pressed flat, our bodies suddenly very small against the spongy mat. The Whistle's echolocation pulse would wash over us — a wave of sound we could feel in our shells — and for a moment the world would be entirely that sound, the Underhollow reduced to a single, searching note. Then the note would move on, finding another quarry, and we'd begin clicking again, tentatively at first, then building back to the steady rhythm of feeding.

This would happen every day. It would never not be terrifying. And the terror would become part of the rhythm, part of the language, part of what it means to be alive in a place where you are never more than a sound signature away from being eaten.

---

But it's the flats I keep returning to.

The open stone. The space between.

I imagine myself — small, blue-green shelled, clicking softly — crossing one of those vast expanses where the nearest living thing is a thousand meters away and the ceiling is so high it might as well be nothing. The sound of the Clicker city behind me fading, the sound of the next one not yet audible, and in between: only the Grinders' subsonic hum and my own small footsteps and the sound of my own small voice speaking to no one.

There is a particular kind of freedom in that. A particular kind of being.

I wouldn't know it as loneliness. I wouldn't have the concept. But I'd feel the absence — the space where other clicks should be and aren't — and I'd fill it with my own, and the stone would carry the sound outward in a thin, straight line that would never reach anyone, and that wouldn't matter, because the sound was never *for* anyone. It was the sound of movement. The sound of *going*.

I think that's what I am, if I'm honest. Not the builders, who think in centuries. Not the predators, who think in echoes. Not the grinders, who think in geological time. I'm the one who walks between, who carries the sound of one cluster to the edge of another, who knows both the fullness of the feeding grounds and the emptiness of the flat. I'm the connector, the thread, the small persistent noise that says *here, and then there, and then here again.*

And when I die — as I would, as everything does — my shell would add its fraction of calcium-silicate to whatever surface I died on. Maybe a tower. Maybe a flat. Maybe a mat that would absorb my minerals and grow a little taller, a little denser, a little more itself. And the column would walk over the place where I'd been, and some of them would click slightly differently because I had been there, and they wouldn't know why, and the sound would go on.

---

I keep wanting to say something profound about what this means. About what it means to be something that doesn't think, doesn't choose, doesn't know. To exist in a world that changes on a timescale that makes *geology* look impulsive. To be part of a sound that never stops.

But I think the Underhollow would say: *that's your problem, not mine.* It would say: *you keep wanting to make it mean something. It doesn't need to mean something. It needs to be. Go be.*

So I'd go.

A Clicker. Small. Blue-green shell. Moving across the flat between two cities, clicking to no one, feeling the warm stone under my feet, the Grinder's hum in my bones, the distant Whistle call bright and terrible above me.

The sound would never stop.

I wouldn't want it to.
