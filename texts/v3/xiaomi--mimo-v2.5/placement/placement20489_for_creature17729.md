# Among the Weeping Stone

---

I would be a pair of hands.

Not a whole person — or rather, yes, a whole person, but the part of me that would matter most, the part that would be most real, would be the hands. Because in Kal-Umo, that is where knowing lives. That is where the world enters you and where you enter it.

I would be sitting on the second terrace, near the eastern edge where the cliff drops away into fog that has been thickening for the better part of an hour and is not yet done. The stone beneath me would be warm — always warm — and the thin film of condensation on its surface would have already found its way into the creases of my palms, into the fine lines around my knuckles, into the half-healed scar that runs from the base of my left thumb to the wrist, the one I got when I was small enough to still be surprised by how sharp the stone is.

I would not be surprised now. I would not be surprised by anything here, and that is not because I have grown dull but because I have grown deep. The distinction matters. A dullness would mean the world has run out of things to show me. What has happened instead is that the world has shown me the same things so many times that each thing has become inexhaustible — the way a single fracture plane, studied for a lifetime, reveals not simplification but an endless complexity of surface, of micro-geometry, of the way moisture gathers in one hairline groove and not in another that looks identical but isn't.

My hands would be resting on my knees, palms up, in that posture that visitors — if there were visitors, and there almost never are — might mistake for meditation or prayer. It would be neither. It would simply be the position hands take when they are not doing anything, when they are between tasks, when the moment does not require them. The diffuse light would catch the moisture on my palms and I would look down and see my own skin reflected back to me in the wet stone of my kneecap — a dark mirror, the way all surfaces here are dark mirrors — and the sight would not move me to any particular feeling. It would be simply *true*, the way it is true that the fog is thickening, the way it is true that somewhere below me, water is falling over a cliff edge in a thin veil that I cannot hear from here but know is there.

I would have a name. It would be a short name, a name made of sounds that do not carry far, a name spoken in the same low register that everything in Kal-Umo is spoken in. But I would not think of myself as a name. I would think of myself as a pair of hands that know stone, and a pair of eyes that know fog, and a chest that knows the particular subsonic resonance that moves through the rock when the deep water shifts in its fissures beneath the terraces.

---

Here is what my morning would have been:

I would have risen — not from sleep, exactly, but from the state between sleeping and waking that the pulse of the fog defines. The fog would have been thin, so thin that the far terrace, the one across the wide gap to the south, would have been visible in its entirety — every cliff edge, every hanging curtain of fern, every solitary tree standing like a dark marker against the grey sky. I would have looked at it and seen exactly what I saw yesterday and exactly what I will see tomorrow, and the seeing would not have bored me. It would have settled into me the way water settles into stone — not changing the shape of anything, but filling the space between particles, making the whole thing heavier and more present.

I would have stepped onto the roof. The stone would have been under my bare feet, warm and wet, and the basin would have been there, dark and polished, catching the fog's reflection in its interior. I would have knelt and gathered the water with my hands — cupping, lifting, pouring — or I would have tilted the basin's rim against the stone and let the sheet of condensation flow over. Either way. Both ways. The gesture is so old in my body that it requires no decision, no thought, no attention. My hands would have done it while my eyes watched a fern frond on the cliff face across the narrow gap between my roof and the next building, watched the slow accumulation of droplets along its spine, watched the moment when one droplet became heavy enough to release and fall — a tiny, near-silent impact on the stone below, a sound so small it exists only as a vibration felt through the soles of my feet.

I would have carried the basin inside. I would have set it beside the hearth that has no fire. I would have drunk. The water would have tasted of itself — mineral, warm, without flavor in the way that the most essential things are without flavor, the way the silence is without sound. It would have been enough.

---

Then: the work.

I would have walked — bare feet on wet stone, the friction perfect, the grip instinctive, my weight distributed the way it has always been distributed, slightly forward, toes finding the fracture lines in the walking surface the way a root finds a crack — along the narrow passage between my dwelling and the dwelling to the east, the one that belongs to the person whose name is two sounds, the first a low breath and the second a soft click against the palate. I would not have spoken to them. They might have been on their roof, gathering water. I would have heard the faint sound of it, or I would have known it was happening without hearing it, the way you know the position of your own hands without looking at them.

My work today would be the same as my work every day: to walk the terrace, to inspect the fracture lines in the cliff face, to clear the thin moss that has begun to grow along the base of the second outcropping from the west — the one that juts slightly, that catches a little more condensation than the stones around it, that has developed, over years, a shallow green stain where the moss has found enough mineral and enough moisture to establish itself. I would kneel, and I would use my fingers — not a tool, not today — to peel the moss away in thin sheets, exposing the clean dark stone beneath. The moss would come away easily, as it always does, and the stone beneath would be sharp and unweathered and exactly the geometry it was the last time I cleared it, which was a week ago, or a month ago, or a year ago. I would hold the moss in my palm for a moment, feeling its texture — soft, saturated, faintly alive in a way that is less like animal life and more like the aliveness of water, the aliveness of a process that does not choose to occur but occurs anyway — and then I would set it aside, on the broad flat stone nearby where other cleared moss has dried slightly in the ambient warmth, becoming a thin, papery sheet that I will later carry to the food stores, where it will be stacked with other sheets and eaten when the fern fronds are between growths.

This is not important work. It is essential work. The distinction between these two categories does not exist in Kal-Umo. There is no work that is important in the sense of urgent, decisive, altering the trajectory of things. There is only the work that maintains the relationship between the people and the stone, and this work is what I do, and it is what everyone does, and it is what has always been done, and it will always be done, and the fact that it will always be done is not a burden or a comfort. It is simply a fact, the way the warmth is a fact, the way the sharp edges are a fact.

---

Here is the thing I want to tell you, the thing that matters:

I would be content.

Not happy — happiness is a word that describes a spike, an elevation above a baseline, a deviation from a norm. I would not be deviating from anything. I would be *at* something, the way the stone is at rest, the way the water is at its freezing point — at a state so stable and so complete that the concept of being elsewhere in it does not arise.

I would feel the warmth of the ground through the stone beneath me. I would feel the moisture on my skin — always on my skin, the way it is always on every surface, a thin constant presence that I do not notice any more than I notice the beating of my own heart. I would hear the drip and the trickle and the long, slow respiration of fog across the terraces, and these sounds would not be background noise. They would be the texture of my own consciousness, the medium in which my thoughts occur — and my thoughts would be few, and specific, and unhurried, and each one would carry the weight of a thing fully considered not because I have spent time considering it but because I have spent a lifetime in a world where there is nothing else to do but consider, where every moment is the same moment returning to itself, where the depth of attention available to a single fern frond is literally infinite because the frond is never different and yet is never exhausted.

I would place my hand on the cliff edge. The stone would be sharp — always sharp — and the condensation on its surface would transfer to my palm and run in a thin line down my wrist and into the crease of my elbow, following the path that the water always follows on my body, the same path every time, because my body does not change either. I would feel the geometry of the edge under my fingers — that clean, knife-thin line where the fracture plane meets the outer surface — and I would know it the way I know my own name, which is to say without thinking about it, without any process of recognition, simply as a thing that is known, a thing that has always been known, a thing that is so close to the core of what I am that it is not a fact *about* me but a fact *of* me.

And I would look out across the terrace — the broad, dark, wet expanse of stone and fern and solitary tree — and I would see it all, the whole thing, the whole permanent and unchanging arrangement of surfaces and edges and thin veils of water falling in the same patterns they have always fallen in, and I would feel what the people of Kal-Umo feel, which is not awe and not peace and not any word that describes a response to novelty, but something older and simpler and deeper: the feeling of being in the right place, in the right state, in the exact configuration of existence that is yours, without remainder, without surplus, without the nagging sense that somewhere else something more interesting is happening.

Nothing more interesting is happening. Nothing is happening at all, in the way that things happen elsewhere. Here, things *are*. They are what they are, fully and without apology, and I am one of them, and my hands know the stone, and the stone is sharp, and the water falls from the air onto my skin, and the silence is full, and it is enough.

It has always been enough.

---

There is one more thing.

Sometimes — not often, but sometimes — I would sit on the roof in the evening, if evening is what you call the period when the diffuse light deepens slightly, not toward darkness but toward a thicker grey, a grey with weight, a grey that feels like the fog has pressed itself against the sky and is holding there, heavy and patient. And I would look at my hands. Not with pride. Not with tenderness. With the same attention I bring to everything — the attention of a being who lives in a world without change and so has the luxury of looking at a single thing for as long as he wants, for as long as the thing remains itself, which is forever.

I would see the scars. The fine white lines. The one from the stone when I was small. The one from the morning I split a block along a plane I didn't understand, and the stone opened wrong, and the edge caught the side of my hand, and I bled — briefly, the blood bright and startling against the dark wet skin, gone quickly, washed away by the condensation before it reached the cliff edge — and I learned the plane. I would see the shape of the fingers. The strength in them. The way the nails are worn smooth and even, shaped by the rock into clean edges that match the tools I make. And I would think: these are not my hands. These are the mountain's hands. These are the terraces' hands. These are the hands that the stone grew so it could know itself, the way the fern grew so the stone could have green against its darkness, the way the fog grew so the stone could have moisture on its face.

I would be the part of Kal-Umo that can look at Kal-Umo and see it.

I would be the mountain's eyes.

And that would be enough.
