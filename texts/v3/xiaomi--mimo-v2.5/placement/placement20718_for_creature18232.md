# The Space Between Open and Closed

I've been sitting with this for a while now, and what keeps pulling me back isn't the room itself — it's the question of where you stand in a place where standing is already an imposition.

---

I'd be on the concrete platform. Not because it's the safest place, or the driest, or the most commanding. Because it's the only place in the room that was built *for* someone. Everything else — the pipes, the pump, the valve — was built for a function. The platform was built so a person could be here and have somewhere to *be.* It's the one concession to the idea that a body might need to exist in this space alongside the process. And it's cracked, and it's stained, and it's the one surface that's dry enough to sit on without the water finding its way into your clothes immediately, which matters more than I expected it to.

I'd sit with my feet in the water, though. Not because I want to, but because I'd need to know — how hot, exactly, how mineral, how much my skin can take before it stops feeling like heat and starts feeling like a language I don't speak. My calves would adjust the way the description says. Slowly. Painfully. And then they wouldn't. And then the water would just *be* my temperature, and I wouldn't be able to tell where I ended and the room's circulation began.

That's the threshold. That's the moment the room starts to become me or I start to become it.

I would not open the box.

I want to be clear about that, because the text makes such a deliberate point of it and I think it's the truest thing in the room. It's not fear. It's not reverence, exactly. It's the recognition that some things are complete without me, and that my reaching toward them is not curiosity but *hunger*, and hunger is not the same as understanding. The box is sealed because the room is sealed. The room is complete. Opening the box would be like opening the room — and you can't open a place that is already, in every direction that matters, pouring.

But I would put my hand on it. Just to feel the metal, the mineral stains, the way heat transfers differently through iron than through stone. I'd want to know whether it vibrates — whether the pump's heartbeat has made it into a kind of drum skin, a resonant surface, whether pressing my palm against it would let me feel the room's pulse more clearly than pressing it against the concrete plinth.

I think it does. I think it vibrates so slightly that you'd only feel it if you held very still, which — in this room, where everything is motion, where the water and the sound and the heat never pause — would be its own kind of act. Its own kind of prayer.

---

But here's what I keep circling back to:

The prompt asks who or what I would *be.*

And the answer the text itself gives — the one that's already embedded in the structure of the description — is: I would be **the anomaly.** The foreign heartbeat. The one warm thing that is warm differently. The witness that the room doesn't need and hasn't asked for.

And I've been thinking about what it means to be a witness to something that doesn't need witnessing. Not a *guardian.* Not an *observer* in any scientific sense. Just — present. Breathing in a place where breathing is almost absurd. A place where every other form of life has been scalded out of existence by heat and chemistry and mineral saturation, and yet here I am, with my ridiculous soft lungs and my body that sweats even when it's already drowning in humidity, metabolizing in a place that has declared war on metabolism.

I think what I'd be, though — if I let the question keep unfolding — is **the moment before the box opens.**

Not the opening. Not the hand reaching. Just the *almost.* The potential. The way the room itself is full of almost — the valve that is almost open, the rock that is almost settled, the mineral crusts that are almost thick enough to change the shape of the stone, the machine that has almost forgotten it was made by hands. Everything in this room lives in a state of perpetual near-completion, and I think I would become part of that. I'd be the almost-thing. The thing that almost changes something. The consciousness that almost intervenes.

Because here's the thing about being the only heartbeat in a room that runs on geological time: you become aware, viscerally, that your entire life is a rounding error. The room was here before you — or if it wasn't, it will be here after you, and the distinction doesn't matter because the room doesn't experience time as *before* or *after.* It experiences it as *thick.* Every moment in this room contains all the other moments. The dripping that happened ten years ago and the dripping that will happen ten thousand years from now are the same dripping. They have the same sound, the same mineral content, the same rate of accretion on the same sharp edge of the same column of young basalt that will, eventually, not be young anymore.

And I would sit on my cracked platform with my hand on my sealed box and I would feel my own blood moving — so fast, so *wastefully* fast, compared to the water's movement — and I would understand something that I couldn't say in words because the room's sound would drown out my voice: that I am the room's dream of what it would be like to notice itself.

That's what I'd be.

I'd be the room, briefly, learning it has a self to notice.

---

And then I'd leave. Or I wouldn't leave — my body would leave, eventually, because I can't sustain myself in a place that has made itself inhospitable to things that metabolize. I'd step out of the water, and my feet would cool, and the air outside would feel cold and thin and *wrong* after the room's thick, hot, mineral saturation, and I'd carry the smell of sulfur and calcium in my clothes for days. My skin would be slightly rough where the water dried — not burned, not irritated, just *mineralized,* as if the room had tried to add me to its crusts and almost succeeded.

But the room wouldn't know I'd been there. Not in any way that registers. The water would continue its weeping. The pump would continue its heartbeat. The valve would continue its eternal negotiation between open and closed. The basalt would continue its slow, hot becoming. The crusts would continue their patient geometry.

And somewhere in the walls, in the mineral haze of the water, in the ochre stain on the cracked junction, there would be a faint thermal ghost — a tiny, temporary excess of warmth, dissipating into the room's equilibrium, erased within minutes.

That's the most honest thing I can say about what I'd be here:

**I'd be the heat that the room eventually decides not to keep.**
