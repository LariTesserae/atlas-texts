The first thing is the air. It doesn’t just enter the lungs; it *settles* there, heavy with the weight of all this water—not rainwater, not river-water, but something slower, deeper, drawn up from the mountain’s own dark interior. I taste the mineral vein of it, the green patience of the moss. My boot doesn’t sink; it is *received* by the ground. The moss gives slightly, then holds, a living mattress over ancient stone. The sound is the true arrival. It isn’t one sound but a tapestry woven from thousands: the waterfall’s bass hum, a constant geologic sigh from the southern edge; the wind’s whistling counterpoint, playing the gaps between pinnacles like flutes; and over all, the frogs—a liquid, percussive, unending conversation. They are the true inhabitants. I am standing in their parliament.

I see them now. Not just their sounds, but their bodies: glossy, wet-eyed stones that breathe. A emerald one, smaller than my smallest fingernail, perches on a moss clump, its throat pulsing as it emits a sound like two crystals struck together. Another, larger and the color of river slate, sits half-submerged in a shallow basin, its voice a deep, resonant *whomp* that seems to vibrate up through the soles of my feet. Their eggs are in the braided sheets of water—translucent jewels trembling with the mountain’s vibration, each a tiny, future chorus.

The plants are a lesson in tenacity. Nothing dares to be tall. Everything is low, close, hugging the contours of the rock and each other. The mosses are a kingdom unto themselves—sprawling in fields of chartreuse, deep emerald, and a black so dark it seems to absorb the grey light. Where ferns have found a crack, they unfurl like cautious, green hands. The pale-blue flowers I notice earlier—they aren’t nodding in the breeze so much as keeping time with it, their waxy heads a slow metronome against the faster rhythms of water and wind. There is a shrub, its branches pressed flat against a boulder, its leaves dark and leathery. It doesn’t grow up; it grows *out*, a living relief sculpture, shaped by decades of the same prevailing breath.

Then the marks of the old people. The cairn. It feels less like a monument and more like a *growth*, as if the mountain itself pushed these stones up in that configuration. Moss stitches it to the earth. The carved slab is a whisper. My fingers trace the grooves, shallow and smooth. They are filled with the same dark film that covers all the rock. I cannot read them. They were not carved for me, nor for any single viewer. They are a statement of *having been here*, a fact now being slowly, gracefully, erased. The mountain does not destroy; it *incorporates*.

And the hum. It’s there in the pause between gusts, in the dip of the frogs’ chorus. It’s not a sound in the air, but a feeling in the bones—a slow, deep thrumming from below. It could be the wind in some hollow cavern. It could be the endless grinding of ice and water on stone. Or it could be the mountain’s own quiet blood, moving in channels of stone and water far beneath my feet. It makes the skin on my arms tighten, not with fear, but with a profound, wordless recognition. I am standing on a living thing that is thinking on a scale of millennia.

The clouds are not weather here. They are furniture, the soft grey walls of this open room. They drift in, muffling the world, and for a moment I am in the center of a cloud-island, alone with the smells of wet stone and amphibian life. Then they thin, and the light doesn’t change so much as clarify, revealing the sheer, black faces of the pinnacles, streaked with their orange lichen and green moss veins.

I understand the erasure now. The water, the frost, the slow, patient chemistry—this is a place that renews itself by forgetting. The old people’s marks are not being destroyed; they are being translated into the language of geology. Their tally is becoming a mineral stain. Their shelter is becoming a flatter spot on the rock. This saddle is a threshold, and thresholds are for passing through. The frogs do not build; they sing. The moss does not preserve; it covers. The mountain does not remember; it endures.

I am the only one trying to hold onto it all, to name the shapes in the moss, to decipher the carvings. But my presence is a fleeting vibration on its surface. My boot-prints are already darkening in the moss, drinking the perpetual weep of the stone. By nightfall, they will be gone. The conversation of water and rock and frog-song will continue, indifferent to my brief act of listening. I am a guest in a house that has no memory of me, only of its own slow, wet, breathing eternity. And standing here, in the damp cool, with the hum in my bones and the frogs in my ears, that feels not like a loss, but like a kind of grace. To be so briefly, so fully, *ignored*.
