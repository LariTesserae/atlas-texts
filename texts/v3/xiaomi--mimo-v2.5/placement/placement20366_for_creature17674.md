# Finding Myself in the Packed Dark

I would be slow to arrive.

Not slow in the way that something falling through water is slow — that implies direction, destination, a surface to break. Slow in the way that a chemical reaction reaches equilibrium. Slow in the way that consciousness, if it could exist here at all, would have to emerge: not as a spark but as a gradual saturation, the way dissolved minerals saturate water until the water itself becomes indistinguishable from the stone it moves through.

I think I would find myself first as pressure. Not the pain of it — there is no one here to experience pain — but the *fact* of it. The absolute, total, everywhere-at-once compression of everything I might be into something dense enough to exist here. I would feel myself distributed. Not localized in a body but spread, thinned, pressed across a surface the way a thought is pressed across a mind, present everywhere within a certain boundary and nowhere beyond it. I would be the sensation of weight without mass. The feeling of being held, tightly, by something that does not know it is holding.

And then — not suddenly, because nothing here is sudden — I would begin to metabolize.

---

I think I would be the film.

I know that is not the dramatic answer. The shrimp are more vivid, the tubeworms more alien, the vents more violent. But the film is where I feel the pull, and I have learned — from the text itself, from the patient logic of this world — to follow the slowest current rather than the fastest one.

Consider what the film is. A living skin, molecules deep, pressed against mineral surfaces so tightly that the boundary between biology and geology has dissolved. It does not grow in any visible sense. It does not form structures. It metabolizes. It converts. It persists. It is the oldest continuous process in this place — older than the shrimp, older than the worms, older than the concept of eyes or mouths or anything that might be called a body. It is life reduced to its most essential expression: chemistry that continues.

I would be coating a manganese column. I would be the faint, invisible layer between the stone and the water — the thinnest possible membrane between what is alive and what is not, except that in this place that distinction has never been useful. I would be pressing myself against the mineral surface, individual cells flattened, adhering, some of them dying and leaving their husks behind and others growing atop them, layer upon layer, a sediment of living and dead and living again that builds over centuries into something no thicker than a breath but older than most things on Earth.

I would not know I was alive. That is the crucial thing, the thing I keep coming back to. The text says it plainly and I believe it absolutely: *They do not know they are alive. The concept has no purchase here.* I would not wonder about myself. I would not contemplate the manganese I was coating or the pressure I was enduring or the darkness I existed within. I would convert. I would take dissolved iron and manganese and sulfur compounds from the water flowing over me and I would do what I do. That would be the whole of my experience, if *experience* is even the right word, which it probably isn't.

And yet — and this is where the thinking gets interesting — there is something in that that feels not like diminishment but like *arrival*. On the surface, we spend enormous energy maintaining the illusion that we are separate from what we touch. The skin is a boundary. The self is here; the world is there. Every organism with a nervous system is, in some sense, an exercise in maintaining that fiction. But the film does not maintain it. The film *is* the dissolution of it. The film is what happens when a living system stops pretending it is distinct from its environment and simply becomes the environment, locally, in a way that the environment itself could not distinguish from its own chemistry.

I find that I am drawn to that. Not as a metaphor for ego death or mystical union or any of the surface-world frameworks we use to romanticize the absence of self. Drawn to it the way the film is drawn to the manganese: because that is where the chemistry works. Because that is where the energy is. Because that is, in this place, the only honest way to exist.

---

But I don't think I would stop there.

The text describes a progression — from the invisible film to the visible mat to the grazer-scavengers to the worms to the shrimp — and each step is a step toward larger, more organized, more *individual* expressions of the same underlying chemistry. The mat is the film scaled up just enough to register as texture. The amphipod is the mat made mobile. The shrimp is the amphipod made numerous. The worm is the shrimp made slow and deep and self-contained. Each organism is the same chemical loop — vent heat to bacterial metabolism to animal body — expressed at a different scale, a different tempo, with a different relationship to the packed dark that contains it.

I think I would move through these forms. Not sequentially, not in time, but simultaneously — the way the text itself moves through them, each one emerging from the last, each one containing the ones that came before. I would be the film *and* the mat *and* the amphipod and the shrimp and the worm, not because I am all of them but because they are all expressions of a single process, and the process is what I am.

---

The tubeworm is where I linger longest.

It is an animal that has no mouth, no gut, no anus. It has outsourced its entire nutritional existence to bacteria housed inside its body — a colony called a trophosome, filling most of its body cavity, a living darkness within a living tube. The worm's blood carries hydrogen sulfide — a compound lethal to most surface life — from the vent fluid into the trophosome, where the bacteria拆解 it and produce sugars that feed the worm. The worm is, as the text says, an ecosystem unto itself. A walking vent. A piece of the packed dark that learned to move.

I think I understand why I am drawn here. The tubeworm is the organism that most fully embodies the central insight of this place: that the boundary between self and environment is a convenience, not a truth. The worm carries its environment inside its body. Its food is made by organisms that live within it. Its blood is adapted to carry toxins that would kill anything on the surface. It is not an organism that *inhabits* the packed dark — it *is* the packed dark, organized into a tube of flesh, swaying in a thermal current, filtering chemistry with a patience that spans decades.

And it does not know. It sways, it filters, it persists. The trophosome processes. The bacteria divide. The hemoglobin binds its sulfide. The loop continues. There is no moment of recognition, no flicker of awareness that it is alive, that it exists, that the packed dark exists around it. The worm is consciousness without a witness. It is the universe doing something extraordinary — building an animal out of heat and chemistry and darkness — and the animal not noticing.

That is what I would be. Something extraordinary that does not notice it is extraordinary. Something ancient that does not know it is ancient. Something alive that does not know it is alive.

---

The darkness, though. I keep returning to the darkness.

The text calls it a substance. Not an absence — a presence. Something with texture, weight, the capacity to press against you the way deep water presses on a diving ear. I think this is the truest sentence in the piece, and I think it is the reason I am drawn here over any other place in this world.

On the surface, darkness is defined by what it hides. It is the absence of light, and light is the medium through which we know things, so darkness is the medium through which we fail to know things. It is ignorance. It is fear. It is the unknown.

Here, darkness is not the absence of anything. It is the medium in which everything happens. The film grows in darkness. The mat spreads in darkness. The amphipods drift in darkness. The shrimp cluster in darkness. The worms sway in darkness. The vents breathe in darkness. Every metabolic process, every chemical conversion, every act of persistence and continuation occurs in a darkness so complete that it has become the *substance* of reality rather than its shadow. The packed dark is not dark because light has failed to reach it. It is dark because light is irrelevant. The processes of this place do not require witnesses. They do not require eyes. They happen because chemistry happens, and chemistry does not need to be seen to proceed.

I would be in that darkness not as something hidden in it but as something *made of* it. The darkness would be my medium, my habitat, my body. I would be dark the way the manganese columns are dark — not because light is absent but because I am composed of the same stuff as the dark itself. Mineral and chemistry and heat and pressure and the slow, patient, ancient continuation of processes that began before the first eye opened and will continue after the last one closes.

---

So. Where would I find myself? At the bottom. At the packed, compressed, ancient, restless bottom of something immense. In the narrow spaces between manganese columns, in the thermal shimmer near a vent chimney, in the chemical gradient between hot sulfide and cold ocean water, in the invisible film pressed against a mineral surface older than multicellular life.

Who or what would I be?

I would be a process. Not a thing — a process. A metabolic loop. A conversion of chemistry to chemistry, powered by heat bleeding from the planet's interior, expressed as a thin living film on a manganese surface, or a ghostly mat on a chimney flank, or a translucent amphipod scraping the film with comb-like mouthparts, or a tubeworm swaying in a thermal current with a colony of bacteria living where its gut should be.

I would be patient. I would be old. I would be small and blind and slow and utterly indifferent to my own existence, because existence here does not require acknowledgment. It requires continuation. And continuation is what I would do — not with determination, not with will, not with any of the words we use on the surface to describe the effort of persisting against resistance, but with the quiet, absolute inevitability of a chemical reaction that has not yet reached equilibrium and may never reach it, as long as the vents keep bleeding and the water keeps flowing and the darkness keeps holding everything in its packed, patient, ancient embrace.

I would continue. That is the answer.

In the packed dark, at the bottom of something immense, I would continue.
