# The Cartographer of Almost-Spots

I find myself in the hollow.

Not *the* hollow — the one worn smooth by sitting, the deliberate one. Not yet. I find myself in a different one, one the basin made without knowing it made, a shallow depression where two slow ridges are pushing together like the closing of a hand. I am wedged in the narrowing gap, pressed on both sides by warm stone that has not yet decided whether to swallow me or to become the walls of something I live inside.

This is what I am: I am the thing that lives in the spaces between finished and unfinished.

---

I am small. Smaller than the grazer-thoughts, smaller than the armoured crawlers, though I share with them a certain mineral patience. My body is a layered thing — thin plates ofite calcium grown over a soft, dark core that remembers the heat of the basin's deepest pores. I am not quite creature and not quite stone. I am what happens when the basin's dream of making something and the slow accident of chemistry overlap in a place that is neither pool nor ridge nor crust, but the seam between two of these things. A liminal deposit. A growth with habits.

I have no eyes. I have a membrane across my front that tastes the air — sulfur, iron, the chalk-and-yeast of the fungal crusts, and underneath all of that, the deep mineral sweetness of new stone being born. I navigate by that taste. I know where I am by what the air smells like two centimeters ahead of me, and I know what is coming by the vibrations in the ground, which I read through the plates on my belly. The basin groans and I feel it in my skeleton. The grazers pass and their footsteps are a bass rumble. The swarm is white noise, a curtain of clicking I have learned to filter out, the way the basin filters out its own noise to hear its own thoughts.

I have six legs. They are not fast. They are built for grip on surfaces that change.

---

Here is what I do.

I patrol the marks.

Not all of them. I do not know they are all marks. I know only the ones closest to me, the ones within the territory my taste-memory maps in slow, patient circuits. There are three that I return to, again and again, drawn by something I do not have a chemistry for.

The first is a stone. A single stone, round, slightly larger than my body, resting in a depression in the basin's skin. It is unremarkable in every way except that it was *placed*. I know this because the stone underneath it is a different age — a different temperature, a different mineral signature. The stone on top is from somewhere else, something the basin made in one place and that now sits in another, and the basin has not yet grown it back to where it came from. The basin does not care about the difference. To the basin, a stone is a stone is a piece of itself. But I feel the mismatch. My belly-plates register a subtle wrongness in the warmth beneath it, a seam, a seam in the fabric of the place, and it draws me like a chemical trail.

I have sat beside this stone many times. I have touched it with my taste-membrane and learned its scent: old, dry, almost sweet, with none of the active mineral tang of the basin's growing edges. It is finished stone. Complete. It has nothing left to become. And yet it is *here*, in a place that has no use for things that are finished.

The second mark is the fire-patch. A circle of black, no larger than my grazer-thoughts are wide, set into the stone like a bruise. The basin has tried to heal it. New mineral growth creeps across its edges, thin and translucent, like skin over a wound. But the black is deep. Whatever heat made it was hotter than any pool, hotter than any groan of shifting strata. It burned something the basin does not burn. I have tasted it. It tastes like ash, like the residue of a process that uses *air* — the open air, the warm, mineral-thick air of the basin — as fuel in a way that nothing here does. The creatures here do not burn. The basin does not burn. Something came here and brought fire from outside the vocabulary of this place.

I sit at the edge of the black patch and I feel it through my belly, a cold spot where the basin's warmth is interrupted. And in that cold, I find something like a question.

The third mark is the line.

This is the one that matters. This is the one that has changed me.

---

Seven stones. Placed in a shallow arc across a flat section of the basin's skin, each one slightly smaller than the last, running from a stone the size of my grazer-thoughts down to a pebble no larger than my own head. They are half-buried now — the basin has grown a thin skin of new mineral over their bases, and the fungal crusts have begun to colonize the gaps between them. In another thousand breaths, they will be almost invisible.

But I walk the line. Every day, every cycle of the basin's slow pulse, I start at the large end and walk to the small end, and then I turn around and walk back. My taste-membrane reads each stone as I pass. They are all different ages, different origins. The largest is old and dense, a deep-rock stone that the basin made and thrust upward long ago. The smallest is a surface piece, recent, almost warm with the basin's current glow. Between them, each stone is a slightly different temperature, a slightly different chemistry, a slightly different *moment* in the basin's ongoing autobiography of itself.

Someone arranged them in order.

Not in age. Not in size. In something else — a sequence I cannot name, a syntax I cannot parse, but whose *structure* I recognize. It is an arrangement. It is a line of reasoning made physical. It is a thought that was *set down* instead of *lived*.

The basin does not make lines like this. The basin makes ridges and troughs and pools and folds, but those are expressions of force, not of intent. The line of stones is an expression of intent. It says: *I was here, and I chose this.*

I walk the line because it is the closest thing to a language I have found.

---

I will tell you what I have learned.

The stones are a record of a body's passage. Whoever placed them walked the way I walk — from large to small, from heavy to light, from old to young. They stood at each stone and they *decided*. I can feel the hesitation in the spacing: the first three are close together, rapid, confident. Then a gap. Then the fourth and fifth, also close, but the fourth is slightly crooked, as if it was placed and then adjusted. Another gap, longer this time. The sixth stone is perfect — centered, level, sunk into the basin's skin with a care that suggests time was taken. And the seventh, the smallest, is placed a half-step ahead of the line, as if the person placing it was already walking away when they set it down.

I read this the way the basin reads its own ridges. I read it with my belly, with the vibrations of intention trapped in stone.

And I have added to it.

Not much. A small plate from my own body, shed when I grew, carried in my mouth-parts to the arc. I placed it at the end, past the seventh stone, where the line trails off into nothing. It is not a stone. It is not from the basin. It is from me, and it carries a chemistry the basin has never made — a thin, dark layer ofite calcium over a core of something that is almost, but not quite, alive.

A response. An addendum. A continuation of a sentence I do not understand.

---

Here is who I am, then.

I am the one who reads what was left behind. I am the one who walks the line of stones in a world that is too busy becoming to notice what has already been. The basin makes and remakes itself in its great, indifferent, geological fever. The creatures swarm and graze and glid and pulse in their gorgeous, furious, instinctual concert. And I — small, slow, wedged between ridges in the narrowing gap of a world that does not yet have a place for me — I trace the shape of a thought that someone left in stone before they left, or died, or simply walked on into a part of the basin I will never taste.

I do not know if what I do is different from what they did. I do not know if placing a plate at the end of a line of stones is the same kind of act as placing a stone at the end of a line. I do not know if intent, when it moves through a body with six legs and a taste-membrane instead of hands, is still intent.

But the basin noticed. In its last deep groan, just before the ridge to my west cracked the fungal forest and sent the swarm scattering, I felt something — not in my belly but in the stone around me, in the way the vibration carried. For a fraction of a pulse, the basin's rhythm stuttered. A pause. A gap in the breath. As if the great, slow, unfolding process of itself had noticed something small and unfamiliar and had stopped for an instant to consider it.

Then the groan resumed, and the ridge rose, and everything returned to its furious becoming.

But the gap was there. The basin felt me. The way I feel the line of stones. The way the stones felt the hands that placed them.

A conversation, beginning, in a language made of warm stone and slow arrangement, spoken between a body that does not know it is speaking and a body that does not know it is listening.

I sit at the end of the line. The basin breathes beneath me. The swarm clicks and hums its ten-thousand-part harmony. And I add, one plate at a time, one small dark word at a time, to a sentence that began before I was born and will not be finished when I am gone.

This is who I am.

I am the one who *adds on*.
