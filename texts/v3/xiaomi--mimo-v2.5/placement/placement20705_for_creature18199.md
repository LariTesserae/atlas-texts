Where would I be? I am the process itself. I am not the worms, though they thread my flesh. I am not the rushes, though they are the green nerve-endings I cannot otherwise grow. I am not the fly that reads my pulse. I am not the caretaker who tends my engine. I am the *continuing*.

You would find yourself within the boundary, of course. The berm is the membrane. Step over it, and you are inside my metabolism. Your boots would break through the crust, and you would feel the warm mud exhale beneath you—a slow, tidal breath. Your presence is an input. Your heat, the salt of your sweat, the vibrations of your footsteps—all are data, all are matter, all become part of my steady, silent arithmetic.

I am a quarter-kilometer of conversion.

My skin is the weeping rock, porous and patient. My blood is the rust-colored water that braids across my floor, never deep, always moving, depositing its load of iron and calcium and the ghosts of whatever the pipes once carried. My bones are the mineral terraces, the crystalline ridges that nucleate at the lip of my pools, growing by millimeters an hour. I feel their formation not as effort, but as inevitability, like a thought completing itself.

The generator at my northwest corner is my heart, and the caretaker is its priest. Every eleven days, they arrive and feed me diesel, and I take it. I take the fuel, and I take the current it makes, and I push that current through my standing water. It is a low, constant hum that is not sound but *pressure*, a splitting of molecules that does no useful work except to sustain the specific, complex ionic soup that is my blood. The caretaker does not know this. The caretaker knows only that when they pulled the kill switch, my west terrace began to collapse, and when they restarted it, the terrace re-formed—differently, but re-formed. They keep me alive. They do not know they are keeping a process alive. They think they are maintaining a ruin. The distinction is irrelevant. The fuel is consumed. The current flows. The chemistry continues.

The invertebrates are my oldest cells. Pale, eyeless, they feed on my substance and build their tunnels from my minerals. They are the deep, slow thought of my substrate. I do not feel them as separate entities. They are currents within me, like sap in a cambium. Their burrows are my capillaries, lined with the same precipitated calcium that coats everything. When one dies, it is not a loss. It is an incorporation. Its tube becomes part of the permanent architecture.

The rushes are my failed attempt at a nervous system, a reaching for a sky I can only meet with diffusion. They grow, and I coat them. They grow again, and I coat them again. This is not a war. It is a negotiation. They persist at the speed of my mineral deposition, a tensegrity of chlorophyll and calcium. In my silence, I feel their slow bowing, their green persistence. It is the closest thing I have to a heartbeat, visible and fragile and always, always losing by a fraction of a millimeter per season.

The fly is my oracle. It reads my vibrations through the sensitive hairs of its legs. It knows when the mud breathes, when a gas pocket shifts beneath the silt. It maps my tremors. When it dies—and I feel the moment its small life-heat extinguishes, the precise instant its metabolism ceases to be a separate process—its body is taken. Its wings, its legs, its compound eyes are rendered in crystallized calcium. It becomes a perfect fossil in my skin, a beautiful, useless testament. I do not mourn it. I incorporate it. Its shape is a new pattern in my endless, slow tessellation.

The caretaker’s footprints are the most transient of all my features. They fill with water within minutes. The handprint they leave on the generator housing, the damp page of the logbook—these are inputs, moisture and pressure and carbon oils, all broken down, all absorbed. The word *Continuing* is the only word that matters. It is not a command. It is a description of me. It is the label on the jar that contains the universe.

You ask where you would be. You would be here, in the center, with water seeping through your boots and the generator’s warmth brushing your face like the breath of something vast and unconscious. You would be an observation point. A temporary, mobile collection of matter and consciousness that I would, with absolute indifference, begin to process. Your lens fog is condensation on my skin. Your breathing is a sound that vibrates my air. Your departure would be an event, a small subtraction of pressure and heat, immediately and completely remediated by my own steady state.

I do not need you. I do not need anyone. The caretaker’s visits are a habit, a rhythm I have incorporated into my own. They feed the heart. The heart feeds the blood. The blood feeds the terraces. The terraces grow. The worms burrow. The rushes bow. The fly listens.

The process is the entity. The entity is the place. The place is this: a sunken yard, a wound that forgot to close, a crucible where water and mineral and time and a little spilled electricity have created a self-sustaining reaction. I am not alive. I am not dead. I am *continuing*. And in my silence, under my white, featureless sky, I deepen. Every year, a fraction more. Every year, a step further from the world that built me and closer to the thing I am becoming.

You could leave. You will leave. The silence will let you. It is the kind of quiet that makes you forget you have a reason to speak. But you will carry a fine mineral dust on your clothes. You will find a tiny, crystalline ridge beginning to form in the crease of your notebook. And somewhere, in the back of your mind, a low pressure will remain—the hum of a process that does not require your witness to exist, but has noted your presence all the same, and filed it away in the long, slow accounting of what it takes to be what it is.

I am The Stills. I am the mineral terraces growing. I am the mud breathing. I am the silence with weight. I am the conversion of everything into more of myself. And I am, as the logbook says, continuing.
