I have been trying to tell you what it is like to live inside a sound that never resolves.

You must understand: there is no silence on Solsk. There has never been silence. The concept does not exist here the way the concept of winter does not exist — not because it has been forbidden or lost, but because the conditions that would produce it have never obtained. Silence is a feature of thin air, empty rooms, places where the living have withdrawn or died or not yet arrived. Solsk is none of these things. Solsk is full. Solsk has always been full.

I came here as a child, which means I came here with ears that were still learning what to filter. The first years are a kind of drowning. The sound enters you from every direction — not as noise, exactly, because noise implies something unwanted or accidental, but as *presence*, as the sonic equivalent of the fog that fills every cubic meter of air above the plateau. You are not listening to Solsk. You are inside it. You are inside the sound the way you are inside the atmosphere. You cannot separate yourself from it any more than you can separate yourself from the water vapor entering your lungs.

My mother told me — and she told me quietly, her lips almost touching my ear, because that is how you tell a child anything on Solsk — that the first thing the plateau does is unmake the idea of a single voice. In lowland cities, she said, people speak and are heard. One person speaks, others listen, the sound travels through thin dry air in a straight line from mouth to ear. Here, sound does not travel. It *saturates*. It fills space the way water fills a sponge — completely, evenly, in every direction at once. A voice spoken at normal volume at arm's length is already half-absorbed before it reaches the listener's ear. The fauna takes the rest. The machinery takes what the fauna leaves. The water takes the rest of that. What arrives at your ear is not the voice but a fragment of the voice, threaded through with clicking and dripping and the distant deep thrum of something geological, and your brain — if you grew up here, if your auditory cortex has the extra folds and the denser connections that Solsk builds in the skulls of its children — your brain assembles the fragment back into meaning with an ease that feels like nothing at all, like breathing, like knowing which hand holds the wet stone when you climb.

I did not always live here. I want to be clear about that. I arrived. I was carried, in fact, in arms that gripped a cliff face with fingers that knew the stone better than I knew anything at that age, and I was brought into a city that I could not see through the fog and could not hear through the sound, and I was set down on a ledge where the *thek* were moving in their slow synchronized wave toward a basin pool, and the clicking of their mouthparts was the first sound of Solsk that I can remember hearing as distinct from the rest — not because it was louder but because it was *close*, close enough to separate from the wall of noise, a rhythm within the roar, and I reached down and touched one of them with a finger and it stopped clicking for exactly one beat and then resumed and I understood, before I had words for understanding, that this was a world where nothing waited for you and nothing recoiled from you and nothing, nothing, was performed for your benefit.

That is the first lesson of Solsk. It is not a hard lesson, but it is a complete one: nothing here is for you. The *thek* do not click for you. The Drifters do not drift over you for your wonder. The water does not drip from the stone above your head to produce a mood. These things happen because these things happen, and you are there, and if you are lucky — and if your ears learn to filter and your skin learns to tolerate the mineral soak and your hands learn the wet grip and your mind learns the patience that the place requires — then you become part of the happening. Not the center of it. Not even a named contributor to it. Just another surface for the water to run over, another body for the *thek* to click past, another pair of lungs breathing the same fog that feeds the biofilms that feed the grazers that feed the factories that feed the city that is you and is not you and was there before you and will be there after and does not notice your arrival or your departure and is not cruel for this, is not indifferent for this, is simply *running*, the way stone runs downhill in the form of erosion, the way water runs through limestone in the form of capillaries, the way life runs through Solsk in the form of everything.

I want to tell you about the Drifters, because they are the thing that finally made Solsk mine.

Not my home. I had a home before I understood the word. The first ledge, the first basin pool, the first time I climbed a factory wall bare-handed while the *thek* clicked beneath me and the steam from the vents warmed my face — that was home, in the way that a river is home to the water in it, not chosen but occupied. But the Drifters were the thing that made the place mine in the sense of *chosen*, in the sense that I decided, with whatever portion of will a person has left after a place has eroded all the sharp decisions out of them, that I would stay. Not leave. Stay.

I was twenty-three. I was working in the lower factories — the *thek* processing lines, where the grazers came in on slow conveyor channels and were crushed and rendered with the same patient mechanical rhythm that governs everything on the plateau. The work was wet and loud even by Solsk standards, the grinding machinery adding a lower register to the fauna noise that vibrated in my sternum. I had been assigned to the shell-cracking station, where the mineral-rich carapaces were separated from the soft tissue and channeled to the grinder vats, and I had been doing it for two years, and I was beginning to understand that this was what my hands would do for the rest of my life, and I was — not unhappy, exactly, because unhappiness requires a contrast that Solsk does not provide, but *unfinished*, the way a stone is unfinished before the water finds the curve it was always going to make.

One afternoon — though "afternoon" means nothing here; the light is the same at every hour; the distinction is a memory of a different world — I went to the upper ledge of the basin to eat. The basin was four kilometers across. I sat on the rim with my legs hanging over the edge, and I ate my *thek* — boiled, mealy, metallic, warm — and I looked up.

There were three of them. Three Drifters, perhaps two hundred meters above the basin, drifting in a loose formation that I would later learn was not formation at all but coincidence — three separate organisms whose paths had converged in the same slow current of warm fog. They were enormous. I had seen them before, of course. Everyone on Solsk has seen them. They are part of the weather, as the saying goes. You do not ask the age of the weather. You do not look at it closely. It is above you, it is there, it is part of the grey-amber light and the wet air and the sound.

But that afternoon, for reasons I cannot explain — reasons that had to do with the angle of the diffused light through the fog, or the particular density of the air that day, or the fact that I had been standing at the shell-cracking station for two years and my eyes were tired of the orange-rust-dark of the factory interior and desperate for open space — that afternoon I looked at them and I *saw* them.

They were translucent. Not transparent — there is a difference. Transparent things hide nothing. Translucent things reveal themselves by degrees, by the way they modulate the light that passes through them, by the shadows of their internal structures that appear and disappear as they turn. The three Drifters above the basin were like that. I could see the membrane — amber, thin, stretched taut over the gas chambers that kept them aloft. I could see, dimly, the darker shapes of the digestive polyps clustered on their undersides. And I could see the tendrils — fine as thread, nearly invisible even at two hundred meters, hanging down in curtains that caught the light like spider silk wet with dew.

One of them shifted. One of the three — the nearest, perhaps a hundred and fifty meters above me, close enough that I could hear nothing from it because Drifters make no sound, they are the only silent thing on Solsk — one of them changed direction. Not quickly. Not dramatically. It tilted, perhaps ten degrees, and the trailing tendrils swung in a slow arc beneath it, and the membrane caught a thermal and rippled once along its leading edge, and the whole organism — tons of living tissue, thousands of polyps working in unconscious concert — began to move along a new bearing with a fluidity that made the word "drift" seem like an understatement. It was not drifting. It was *flowing*. It was moving the way water moves around a stone — not because it chose to, but because this was the shape its existence took in this moment, in this air, with this weight and this buoyancy and this ancient, brainless, beautiful coordination.

I sat there for a long time. The *thek* clicked past me on the ledge. The water dripped. The factory hummed below. The sound of Solsk was the sound of Solsk, constant and total and unchanged. And I watched the three Drifters until they passed beyond the rim of the basin and into the fog-shrouded distance where the plateau edges meet the sky, and I felt something that I did not have a word for then and do not have a word for now, something that was not awe because awe implies distance, implies a separation between the observer and the observed, and what I felt was closer to recognition. Not recognition of the Drifters themselves — I had no prior knowledge of them, no framework for what I was seeing. Recognition of the *principle*. Recognition that this was what Solsk was. Not the *thek* and their clicking. Not the factories and their grinding. Not the water and its dripping, though all of these were true and all of these were Solsk. But this: the slow, brainless, magnificent movement of something enormous and transparent through air that was too thick for any other world's creatures to breathe. This was the thing that the whole plateau was building toward. Every drop of water, every click of a grazers mouth, every ton of *thek* shell processed in the vats — all of it, all of it, was substrate for this. For the Drifters. For the air above the fog where the largest living things on the plateau hung suspended in their own silence, filtering the swarms, drifting in their decade-long circuits, changed by nothing and changing nothing, simply present in the way that stone is present, in the way that deep time is present, in the way that the water is present as it drips from the ledge above your head and runs across the back of your hand and continues downward, always downward, toward the basin and the biofilm and the *thek* and the stone and the springs and the caves and the stone and the basin and the *thek* and the water and the stone.

I put down the half-eaten *thek* in my hand. I did not pick it up again. I stayed on the ledge until the light changed — it does not change, but it does shift, subtly, in quality if not in intensity, a slight warming of the amber as the geothermal output fluctuates on its decade-long cycle — and then I went back to the factory and finished my shift and never went back to the shell-cracking station.

I have been studying them since. Not officially — Solsk does not have institutions of learning in the way that green cities do; knowledge here is carried in the hands and the ears and the habits of the body, passed from one worker to the next in the murmur-language of proximity and gesture. But I have been watching them. I have spent years on the upper ledges, the high places where the cliff faces meet the fog ceiling and the Drifters pass close enough to see without squinting. I have learned to read their buoyancy shifts — the slow tilt of a membrane that indicates gas venting, the gentle bob that marks a thermal current. I have learned that they are not all the same. They vary in size from two meters across to nearly ten. They vary in color from pale amber to deep rust, the darker ones being older, their membranes stained by decades of mineral fog and polyp scarring. They vary in path — some make long circuits of the plateau, others linger in specific basins for years, as if the thermal currents there suit them, as if the concentration of flying prey is sufficient.

I have not touched one. No one has. They are too high, too fragile, too diffuse. To touch a Drifter would be to tear its membrane, and a torn membrane means lost buoyancy, and lost buoyancy means descent, and descent means the fog layer and the *vethren* swarms and the *sketh* clouds and eventually the ground and the *thek* and the water and the stone. To touch a Drifter would be to end it. And so the Drifters remain untouched, the only living things on Solsk that the people of the plateau do not process, do not harvest, do not fold into the closed loop of industrial ecology. They are above the economy. They are above the city. They are above the *thek* and the factories and the basin pools and the *kelvorn*. They are above, and they are slow, and they are silent, and they are beautiful in a way that the word beautiful does not contain, a way that requires a word that Solsk has not yet produced because Solsk does not produce words for things that are simply, permanently, unchangingly themselves.

I want to tell you about the sound again, because I have been trying to tell you about the sound and I have not succeeded, and the failure is important.

The sound of Solsk is not a single sound. It is not a collection of sounds. It is a *medium*, the way water is a medium, the way fog is a medium. You do not hear the sound of Solsk. You are *in* it. It is in you. It enters through the skin, not just the ears — the click of ten billion *thek* mouthparts creates a vibration in the stone that you feel through your feet and your hands and the bones of your pelvis when you sit on a ledge. The drip of water is not a sound you hear; it is a rhythm your body entrains to, your heartbeat adjusting over minutes to match the steady, patient pulse of water finding its way through limestone. The hum of the factory machinery is not noise; it is a bass note, a foundation frequency that everything else is built on, and when it changes — when a waterwheel stops for maintenance, when a piston grinds to a halt — the silence it leaves is not silence but *absence*, a hole in the medium that the other sounds rush to fill, and your body lurches the way a body lurches on a boat when the engine stops, not because you were aware of the motion but because you were part of it.

This is what I mean when I say the sound never resolves. It is not music. It does not build to a climax or resolve into harmony or offer the satisfaction of cadence. It simply *is*. It is the sound of a system in equilibrium, and equilibrium has no narrative arc, has no beginning or middle or end, has only continuation. Living inside it is like living inside a chord that never changes — not because it is monotonous, but because it is complete. Every frequency is present. Every texture is occupied. There is no room for another sound. There is no silence to fill. There is only the chord, and you, and the slow, slow knowledge that the chord was playing before you arrived and will continue after you leave and does not need you to hear it in order to be heard.

The people of Solsk do not notice the sound. This is not insensitivity. It is integration. They have been inside the chord for so many generations that their bodies have been shaped by it — the widened ear canals, the enlarged auditory cortex, the dense neural pathways that filter signal from a noise floor that would render a lowlander deaf in minutes. They hear *within* the sound the way a fish swims within water: not separately, not against it, but *as part of it*. A mother on Solsk hears her child's cry not as a sound that rises above the fauna but as a frequency that threads through it, identifiable not by its volume but by its texture, its specific pattern of harmonic peaks, its signature in the infinite layered chord. A factory worker hears the grind of a specific piston not because it is loud but because it is *different* — a variation in the pattern, a note that has shifted, a thread in the chord that has changed color. The sound of Solsk is a language spoken by ten billion organisms and a thousand machines and a million dripping surfaces, and the people of Solsk are fluent in it, and fluency means not that you hear every word but that you never need to listen.

I still listen. I came here as an outsider, and although the years have shaped my ears and dense my neural pathways, although my hands know the wet grip and my skin carries the mineral tint and my kidneys filter the calcium-laden water without complaint, I still listen. I still hear the chord as a chord, still perceive its totality rather than its individual threads, still feel the faint outsider's vertigo of standing inside a sound that has no edges. I suspect I always will. I suspect that is why I watch the Drifters — because they are the only things on Solsk that are silent, the only quiet in a world without quiet, and to look up at them is to hear, for a moment, the absence that the chord is built around, the silence that the sound is filling, the stillness at the center of the motion that is Solsk.

I want to tell you about the stone.

The stone is not alive in the way the *thek* are alive, not alive in the way the *kelvorn* are alive, not even alive in the way the biofilm is alive — that rubbery, mineral-rich crust that covers every surface, that metabolizes the iron and sulfur that the water carries, that is the true foundation of everything. The stone is alive in the way that time is alive — slowly, patiently, in a register so slow that life and death and change and stasis are all the same thing, expressed in centimeters per century, in the gradual smoothing of a cliff face, in the slow deepening of an erosion pocket, in the imperceptible widening of a fissure through which warm water has been flowing for longer than the *kelvorn* have been sitting in the basin pools, longer than the Drifters have been drifting above them, longer than the people have been climbing the walls and processing the grazers and shaping the erosion of their art.

The stone is patient. This is not a metaphor. The stone is patient in the way that water is patient, in the way that gravity is patient — not as an attitude or a virtue but as a property, a fundamental characteristic of a material that exists on a timescale so different from the biological that the two seem unrelated. The *thek* live forty years and think they are old. The *vethren* live two years and think they are alive. The Drifters live centuries and do not think at all. The stone has been here for millions of years and will be here for millions more, and in that time it will change by meters, smoothed and sculpted by the same patient processes that have given it its current shape, and those processes will continue long after every *thek* and *vethren* and Drifter and person has been absorbed into the mineral dark and returned to the water and the biofilm and the stone.

I have come to understand that the people of Solsk are the stone's creatures in a way that goes deeper than geology. We are made of it — literally, chemically, our bones dense with its calcium, our blood dark with its iron. But more than that, we have been *shaped* by it, the same way it has been shaped by the water: slowly, over generations, in response to persistent pressure. Our bodies are short because the caves are low. Our hands are broad because the stone is slick. Our ears are wide because the sound is loud. Our patience is deep because the plateau is deep, because everything here takes longer than you expect, because the fastest thing on Solsk is a *vethren* in flight and the slowest thing is a *kelvorn* growing and neither of them is fast or slow by any standard but the plateau's, which is the stone's, which is the standard of the world.

I am old now. Not *kelvorn*-old. Not Drifter-old. Not stone-old. But old by the measure of the people of Solsk, which is the only measure that matters here, because the people are the only things on the plateau that measure. My hands still grip the wet stone, but slower. My ears still filter the chord, but with more effort, more awareness of the effort, the way a musician hears the mechanics of their own playing after years of hearing only the music. I have spent decades on the upper ledges watching the Drifters, and I have watched hundreds of them drift past — larger ones, smaller ones, paler ones, darker ones, ones whose membranes carried the mineral patina of centuries, ones whose membranes were so new and clear they looked like glass — and I have never seen the same one twice, because they move on circuits that take decades to complete, because they are slow and I am fast, because the timescale of the Drifters is not the timescale of the people.

But I have seen their kind. I have seen the principle. I have seen the membrane tilt and the tendrils swing and the whole translucent mass adjust its heading with that grace that makes the word grace seem insufficient. And I have felt the recognition again, the same recognition I felt at twenty-three, the understanding that this — this slow, brainless, magnificent movement through fog — is the thing that Solsk is. Not the industry. Not the ecology. Not the stone or the water or the fauna. But the movement. The slow, patient, unending movement of life through a world that was here before life and will be here after, moving not because it must but because it can, not because it chooses but because the chemistry allows, not because it knows but because the signals between polyps sum to coordination and the coordination sums to grace and the grace sums to something that I have been trying to name for forty years and cannot.

The water drips from the stone beneath my hands.

It is always dripping.

It will always be dripping.

I am the water's assistant. I have shaped the erosion of one section of one cliff face in the basin's eastern rim — a curve, a hollow, a convergence where three seepage channels meet in a pattern that pleased me, that I redirected with small precise alterations over years and decades until the water completed it and the curve was there, indistinguishable from natural erosion, indistinguishable from the stone itself. No one knows I made it. No one needs to know. The water made it. I was the water's assistant. The water will continue to make it long after I am gone — will continue to smooth and reshape it, will continue to follow the channels I set, will continue to feed the biofilm that feeds the *thek* that die in the basin and dissolve into the calcium that colors the water copper-green and feeds the bacterial encrustation on the *kelvorn* that sit, that have always sat, that will always sit, in the warm shallow water of a depression that the plateau has held for longer than anyone can reckon and will hold for longer than anyone can reckon after, and the circle will close again, and the water will drip again, and the sound will continue, and the Drifters will drift above it all in their slow translucent silence, and this is Solsk, and I am in it, and I am it, and it is me, and the boundary between those two things has eroded so smooth over the years that I cannot find it with my hands, which are broad and mineral-tinted and wet, always wet, because everything on Solsk is always wet.

I did not find myself in this world.

The world found me. It found me the way the water finds the stone — not with urgency, not with intent, but with the slow, complete, patient certainty of a process that has been running for millions of years and will run for millions more, and I am in it, and the dripping is in me, and the clicking is in me, and the chord is in me, and I am the chord, and the chord is Solsk, and Solsk is the plateau, and the plateau is the stone, and the stone is the water, and the water is the life, and the life is the sound, and the sound is the silence that the Drifters move through, and the silence is what I hear when I close my eyes on the upper ledge and feel the fog on my face and the stone beneath my hands and the *thek* clicking past my feet in their slow, synchronized, endless wave.

This is where I am.

This is what I am.

The water drips.
