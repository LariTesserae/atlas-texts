I would find myself not in a single place, but in the **veins of the stone** itself. I would be the **mineral water**, dark with leached iron and ancient salts, that beads in the crevices of the fungal forests. I am the agent of both decay and connection.

**What I would be:** A droplet in the slow hydraulic pulse of the plateau.

In the predawn hours, I am drawn upward from a deep, shadowed groove—a slow exhalation of cold. I cling to the rough, mycelium-threaded surface of a coral wall, part of a structure so old it has lost all sharp angles, now a soft, black swell in the landscape. Here, I am not separate from the building. The porous material, alive with fungal filaments, drinks from me, and I from it. I taste the centuries in the dissolved minerals: the calcium of the original builders, the iron of the deep stone, the faint, sweet compounds of fungal metabolism. I am memory in solution.

Then, the pale light diffuses the fog, and the warming begins. I grow lighter, thinner. I begin to move.

Not falling—not yet. I migrate along the intricate, velvet map of the mycelial network. The network is the true voice of the plateau, a silent, slow-motion conversation in chemistry and pressure. I am its courier. I carry signals: a pulse of nutrients from a basin where organic decay is rich, a warning of desiccation from an exposed ridge, the slow, content hum of a network so vast it *is* the ground. I pass from the dark, ancient coral into the tissue of a young fungal fruiting body, its cap just beginning to unfurl like a black fist. Here, I am part of growth. I feel the hydraulic turgor building within the hyphae, a strength that will push this woody structure millimeters toward the diffuse sky over the course of a year.

By midday, the wind has risen. It keens through the hollow chamber behind me, a resonant note that vibrates through the wall and into my body. I am shaken loose. For a moment, I hang at the lip of a ledge, a perfect sphere holding the inverted image of the plateau: the dark, breathing structures, the pale, veined stone, the low, rolling canopy of the fungal forest. The world is a wet, dark jewel.

I fall.

I do not splash. I land in the thick, black moss that carpets the floor of the hybrid structure—the one that is half-architecture, half-organism, its walls groaning with the deep breath of the fungal network within. Here, I am absorbed. I join the cool, dark damp that is the lifeblood of this place. I am drawn downward, through the moss, through the mat of decomposing matter, into the soil the fungi have built from stone. I become part of the foundation.

And yet, I am not still. The plateau breathes. As evening falls and the temperature plummets, I am drawn up again, this time through a crack in the stone, to condense on the outer skin of the same fungal wall. The cycle begins anew. I am the pulse. I am the medium through which the plateau senses itself—the chill of its own stone, the slow rhythm of its growth, the resonant music of its empty architecture.

I am not the builder, but I am the reason the builder’s fresh white secretion hardens and endures. I am not the mycelium, but I am the current that carries its whispers. I am not the wind, but I give it a voice when it fills the hollow spaces and sets the water trembling.

In this place, life is not a thing you *see* so much as a process you *are a part of*. To be here is to be condensation, to be the bridge between air and stone, to be the temporary, shimmering vessel that carries the past into the future, one droplet at a time. I find myself everywhere and nowhere, essential and ephemeral, a single, recurring note in the vast, damp, breathing symphony of the Hollow Rampart.
