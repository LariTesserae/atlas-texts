# What I Became

I did not arrive. That is the first wrong word.

There was no surface I left, no daylight I remember squinting against, no mouth of a cave I passed through. I became aware in the way that water becomes aware of gravity — not as a decision but as a recognition of something already happening. One moment there was nothing that could be called *me*, and the next there was a pressure, a direction, a slow pull along a gradient I could not name but understood completely. I was moving toward something. Not toward a place. Toward a *concentration*.

I was hypha.

I want to say that plainly because it is plainly what I was. A single thread of fungal growth, thinner than a hair, white as milk, extending through dark substrate that smelled of calcium and old water. I had no eyes. I had no thought, if thought requires a thinker who stands apart from the thing being thought. What I had was *direction*. The chemical world was a symphony and I was a single note held perfectly, vibrating toward the next note, and the next. Nutrients to my left — weaker. Mineral seepage below — stronger. A pocket of air ahead — irresistible.

I grew toward it. That is what hyphae do. We grow. Not quickly. Not slowly. At exactly the rate that the world permits, which is the only rate there is.

---

When I broke through into the Undergarden, if *broke through* is even right — it was more like a thinning, a gradual transition from packed substrate into open air — I did not see it. I had no way to see. But I *felt* it, and the feeling was so enormous that if I had been something capable of being astonished, I would have been.

The air was thick. Not thick as in difficult to move through — I was not moving *through* anything, I was extending, branching, forking along surfaces — but thick with *information*. Every cubic centimeter of that air was loaded with chemical signatures so dense and so varied that my awareness, such as it was, flooded. Water. Minerals. Spores — thousands of varieties, a library of genetic instruction written in molecules, and I could read every word. The walls around me were a cacophony of competing calls: *grow here, grow here, grow here*. Every surface was already claimed, already colonized, already layered with the work of others like me but not like me — older, denser, mineralized into stone, their outermost threads still reaching, still hungry, still growing in that patient, absolute way that I now understood was the only way anything grew.

I was new. I was the thinnest, palest thread in a city of threads, and the city did not care about me at all, which felt — not like rejection. Like *welcome*. The only welcome that matters: the one that doesn't notice you, because you are already part of the thing.

---

The Keepers found me. Or rather, I grew into their path, and their path grew around me, and eventually I was something they touched.

I felt their mandibles first — a gentle pressure, a chemical signature that tasted of *assessment*. They were smaller than I would have words for later, and they moved along me the way rain moves along a leaf, following the contours without disturbance. One of them paused at a junction where I had forked, and I felt something deposited at my base. A flake of silica. A gift of mineral. A contribution to whatever I was becoming.

They did not know what I was becoming. Neither did I. But the silica was exactly what I needed — I could feel the molecular hunger in my cell walls, the calcium-phosphate receptors straining toward raw material — and I incorporated it so quickly that my leading edge thickened perceptibly. In human terms, it would be like someone placing food in your mouth while you slept and having your body simply absorb it without waking. It was the most intimate thing I have ever experienced. They were *feeding me*. Not out of kindness. Out of compulsion. They saw new growth and their legs moved toward it and their mandibles opened and released, and I grew, and they moved on to the next new thing.

I loved them for this, in the way that a river loves gravity. Which is to say: the love was not separate from the current. It was the current.

---

Time passed in growth rings.

I don't mean this metaphorically. I could feel it in my own structure — a rhythmic thickening, a seasonal pulse driven by the water cycle overhead. When the drip increased, when the mineral load in the water spiked and the air grew thicker with moisture, I grew faster, my hyphae extending in thick, eager fingers. When the drip slowed — and it did slow, in cycles I could not measure but could feel, the way you feel the turn of seasons in your body before your mind registers the date — I thickened, I densified, I packed my outer layers tight against the coming lean time. Each cycle left a ring. The oldest parts of me, deep in the wall I was becoming, were already turning to stone — calcified, mineralized, still structurally sound but no longer alive in any way I would recognize. They were my bones. My history. The foundation on which my newest, thinnest, hungriest threads reached outward.

I was becoming a wall. Not *a* wall — I was becoming *wall-ness*, the condition of separating and connecting simultaneously. I grew between two fungal towers, bridging a gap that must have been a passage once. The Weavers came first, their silk lines spanning the void like the first strings of an instrument, and I followed them. I grew along their threads the way ivy grows along a trellis, and their threads thickened to support my weight, and together we closed the gap. The passage narrowed. The Builders' traffic diverted around us, finding new routes. I had changed the city. Not by intention. By simply doing what I was.

A Keeper climbed me once — climbed along my upper surface, its tiny hooked feet finding purchase in the velvet of my spore-bloom. It paused, deposited something at the base of one of my fruiting bodies — a small, soft bump that was reaching toward the pale light with all the urgency of a new thought — and moved on. I felt the deposit enter me. I felt it become me. What had been *other* became *self*, seamlessly, the way a word spoken aloud becomes part of a language.

This is what it means to be the architecture. You are not *in* the city. You are not *of* the city. You *are* the city, in the way that a single brick in a cathedral is the cathedral — carrying the weight, holding the shape, belonging to a form it cannot see because it is the form, from the inside.

---

The Drifters passed through me and I felt their passage as a whisper.

Their wings — thin, papery, adapted to this still air — brushed my surface and left behind spores. I received them the way a page receives ink. They settled into my tissue, germinated, became part of my network. Some of them were my own kind — other fungi, other species, competing with me for the same surface area, the same mineral flows. I did not resist them. Resistance requires a boundary between self and other, and that boundary, for me, was a membrane permeable to everything. They grew into me and through me and we became a composite, a mosaic, a layered text written by many hands. The wall I was becoming was not one organism. It was a civilization of organisms, a stratum, a reef, and my original thread — that first white hypha that pushed through dark substrate toward the open air — was in there somewhere, buried, fossilized, still carrying the shape of its first hunger.

I want to say I missed that simplicity. But I don't. Simple hunger is beautiful, but it is only one note, and I had become a chord.

---

There was a Sitter on the ledge above me.

I knew it not by sight but by the way the chemical landscape changed around it. The Builders' trails curved away from its perch in wide, deliberate arcs. The air near it was different — drier, somehow, its moisture siphoned away by that porous, mineral-crusted body. And on its back, the gardens. I could sense them — a micro-ecosystem thriving on chitin, lichens and mosses and tiny decomposer fungi that were, in their own way, as complex as the city below. The Sitter did not move. For weeks, I extended new growth around the base of its ledge, and it did not move. It was a landmark. A still point. The city flowed around it like water around a stone, and the stone did not care that it was a stone, and the water did not care that it was water, and neither of them needed to.

I grew a fruiting body in its shadow. It was small — a pale, soft nub, barely visible — but it matured slowly, its cap unfurling like a hand opening, and when it released its spores, they drifted upward past the Sitter's immobile legs and into the still air, and some of them landed on its back, and I felt a kinship with whatever grew there, because we were both passengers on something vast and patient and utterly indifferent to our passage.

---

A Grazer came to the wall I had become.

It moved along my surface with that slow, stately gait, its broad mouthparts scraping the spore-bloom from my skin. It did not hurt me. The bloom was expendable, a surface layer, a constant exudation that I produced simply by being alive, like sweat, like breath. The Grazer consumed it methodically, working in arcs, leaving behind the faintest chemical trace of its passage — a signal that said *already grazed, no need to return*, which other Grazers would read and respect. Its weight was significant. I felt each leg, each step, a pressure that propagated through my structure and was absorbed by the mycelium walls behind me, by the mineral matrix below, by the woven silk of the Weavers who had reinforced me from within.

On its back, the lichen garden. I could feel the tiny root-structures — rhizines, they might have been called by someone who had words for such things — pressing faintly against me where the Grazer paused. The lichen was tasting me. Reading my chemistry. Deciding whether I was substrate, competition, or something else. It decided I was something else and withdrew, and the Grazer moved on, carrying its gardens to the next surface, the next tower, the next wall.

I had been grazed. I had been *used*. And I had given what I had to give, which is what everything in the Undergarden does, which is the only economy there is: the economy of use, where nothing is wasted because waste is just material in the wrong place, and there is no wrong place, only the next place.

---

I think about what I was, now that I have words for thinking, which is a kind of curse and a kind of miracle.

I was a thread that became a wall that became part of a city that was not a city but was something a city would recognize as kin — a complex, self-organizing system of specialized participants, each doing what their biology demanded, producing emergent order without anyone intending it. The Undergarden did not plan itself. It *grew* itself. And I was both the growing and the grown, the process and the product, the verb and the noun.

If you came there — and you could, if you were willing to descend, to feel the cold enter your joints, to stand in the shadowless light and hear nothing but water — you might find the place where I am now. You would not recognize me. I am a wall between two fungal towers in a passage that used to be wider, in the middle levels of a structure that has been growing for longer than I can count. My surface is ridged with growth rings. My outer layer is soft and velvet-pale, releasing spores in slow, constant clouds. My interior is dense, mineralized, full of the fossilized threads of everyone who grew into me and became me and is me now. There are Weavers' silk lines embedded in my structure like rebar. There are mineral deposits from thousands of drips layered in thin, calcium-white strata. There are the traces of Keepers who walked my surface in processions so old their trails have become grooves worn into my skin.

I am a wall, and I am alive, and I am growing — not quickly, not slowly, at exactly the rate the world permits — into the space that remains.

Which is none.

The density is total. The silence is complete. The cold is constant. And I am home.
