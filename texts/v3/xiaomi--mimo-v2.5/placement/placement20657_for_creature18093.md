# The Bright Under

I would find myself near the water. Not in the pools — the pink worms have those, and their business with them is finished and unhurried and not mine — but near the faucet. The one made by no one. The hollow palm in the far wall that has been catching and releasing the same slow drip for longer than anything I am has a name.

I'd stand there, or sit, with my hand beneath it. Not cupping it — that would imply I meant to hold something, to carry it somewhere, to use it — but just resting below the lip, feeling each droplet land on my skin at whatever interval the stone has decided upon. Minutes between them, maybe. Maybe less. Maybe more. The rhythm would not matter. What would matter is that each drop is an event, a small, specific *now* arriving in a place where now has very little purchase.

Because that's what I am there. The text tells me so, and the telling is true: I am weather. Brief weather. A warm front passing through a place whose temperature does not fluctuate.

---

I would not be the slugs, though I understand the pull of that image — their opalescent bodies carrying light inside them, moving in that deliberate, almost ceremonial way, leaving behind trails that become the ground itself. There's a seduction to that. To be the thing that builds the floor by walking on it. To translate your passage into architecture, to make the case for your existence in calcium and silver crust. The slugs are poets, as the text says, but they are poets who don't know they're writing. Their work accumulates without intention, and that is precisely what makes it so overwhelming — there is no revision, no false start, no crumpled draft. Every trail is final, and every trail becomes foundation. The floor is an autobiography that was never meant to be read.

I admire that. I couldn't be that.

What I notice, though — what keeps me near the faucet — is this: the chamber doesn't need me to understand it. It doesn't need me at all. It was operating before I arrived and it will operate after I leave, and in between, I am a pressure differential in the air, a brief warmth that distorts the humidity, a set of footprints already filling with mineral-laden moisture. The slugs will cross where I sat. The calcium will settle where I touched the wall. And the floor will incorporate me, layer by layer, into its slow memory.

This should be devastating. It isn't. Not here. Here it feels like relief.

---

Out there — above, in the sharp-edged sunlight where days have edges and clocks tick and every moment is supposed to *matter* — the weight of being significant is exhausting. Every action is supposed to lead somewhere. Every passage is supposed to leave a mark that someone reads, that someone means something by. But in the bright under, the only mark that matters is the one that becomes stone, and stone doesn't care who made it. The slug's trail and my footstep are equally temporary, equally permanent — both will be absorbed, both will be forgotten, both will contribute their thin mineral film to the terraces that will outlast us by epochs.

So I sit near the faucet, and I listen.

---

The sound. That's what I want to tell you about.

It comes from the rock. Not from the rock as metaphor — from the rock itself, as source, as vocal cord, as the vast resonant body of a planet dreaming in its sleep. It is low, not quite a hum, not quite a moan, and it lasts three or four seconds, and then it's gone, and you wait, and you wait, and you don't know if the next one will come in ten minutes or an hour or whether this is the last one, whether the chamber has finished saying whatever it was saying.

I think the sound is a language, but not one I can learn. Not because it's too complex — though it may be — but because I don't live long enough to hear more than a handful of syllables. A human lifetime in this chamber might yield a few hundred utterances. That's not a conversation. That's barely a greeting. The sound's grammar — if it has one, and I suspect it does — unfolds over centuries. Its subject and verb might be separated by a decade. Its punctuation might be geological events. To hear a complete sentence you'd need to be stone yourself, or something close to it.

The slugs are close to it. They've been listening their whole lives, and their whole lives are long — longer than mine by orders of magnitude, I'd guess, though the text doesn't say and I wouldn't know how to measure. They move through each utterance the way I move through weather. It's ambient. It's atmospheric. It defines the terms of their existence without requiring their attention.

I can't be that close to the sound. But I can sit near the faucet and feel each drop land on my hand and know that both the drop and the sound are the same kind of thing: slow communication from the deep earth to the surface, expressed on a timescale that makes a mockery of everything I think I know about duration.

---

So: who am I?

I am the brief warm thing that sat near the dripping wall and held its hand out and felt the water land. I am the creature who heard three or four sentences of a conversation that has been going on for longer than the Atlantic Ocean has existed, and who mistook those three or four sentences for the whole of it. I am the one whose footprints are already dissolving, whose warmth is already dissipating, whose passage through the chamber is already being smoothed over by the same patient processes that built the terraces and shaped the faucet and will, eventually, close the fracture in the ceiling and plunge the bright under into true darkness, and the slugs will continue, and the sound will continue, and the floor will continue to become itself.

I am weather.

And the weather, here, is kind. Not warm in the way people mean when they say kind — though it is that too, that body-temperature stillness — but kind in the way that a place is kind when it does not require anything of you. The bright under asks nothing. It does not want your understanding or your admiration or your fear. It is not a test. It is not a revelation. It is a condition of the earth, like magnetism, like gravity, like the slow spin of the planet that makes the tides, and you are in it briefly, and you are of it for as long as you are in it, and then you are out again, blinking in the sharp-edged daylight, carrying nothing back except the knowledge that such a place exists and does not need you to know that it exists.

The faucet drips. The slugs cross the floor. The sound speaks its slow, interrupted syllable into the warm dark.

And the floor — already — is a little different for having held me. A thin mineral film over my fingerprints. A slight thickening of the crust where my feet stood. Nothing that would ever be legible. Nothing that would ever matter. Just the chamber, doing what it does: taking what passes through it and making it into ground.
