I'd be the whorl in the film.

Not the hand that made it — I'll come to that — but the mark itself, the brief impression left in the grey-white surface when something warm and articulated presses against a substrate that has no opinion about pressure. The film would feel me the way it feels everything: as a temperature, a chemistry, a temporary disruption in the even spread of itself across the glass. My warmth would register the way the rock's warmth registers, the way a pool's mineral load registers — as data, not experience. The film would note the shape of me the way it notes the shape of every surface it colonizes: architecturally, as a topology to be mapped and filled.

But here is the thing about being the whorl: you are the most honest version of contact. You are the point where two entirely different modes of existence overlap for a moment and produce something — a depression, a negative space, a sculpture of absence shaped exactly like a palm — and then you separate, and one of you goes on being a continuous colonial surface and the other of you goes on being a thing with a name.

I think I would find myself first at the entrance.

Not because I'm cautious — or not only because of that — but because the entrance is where the two worlds negotiate. Above: basalt, sky, whatever passes for atmosphere on the surface, the remembered geometry of up-and-down that the body carries like a prayer it doesn't know it's reciting. Below: the steam curtain, the obsidian lip, and then the first wrongness, the ceiling that is too high or too far away or too many ceilings, and the heat that doesn't negotiate but simply arrives, all at once, in the lungs and against the skin, like being reminded of something you forgot you knew.

I'd stand at the threshold where the daylight still has a source and the air still has a temperature you could name, and I'd look down into the cleft, and I'd see — or not see, because the steam is thick and the light is the color of weak tea and the geometry refuses to resolve — I'd *sense* the space below the way you sense a room full of sleeping animals. Not danger. Not safety. Presence. Something that is organized and does not care that I am organized differently.

And then I'd go in.

---

The heat would find me before anything else. Not gradually. The moment the steam curtain closed behind me — if there were a behind, if the entrance didn't already feel like a memory of an entrance rather than a physical threshold — the heat would be total. My shirt would adhere to my back. My hands would be wet. My breath would catch, not from fear but from the sudden density of the air, the effort of pulling it into lungs that expected something thinner.

I'd touch the wall.

This is what I'd do. I know this about myself. I'd reach out and put my palm flat against the nearest surface, because that is how I have always understood things — not by looking, though I look, but by pressing skin to the world and reading what comes back through the nerve endings.

And the film would yield.

Not like anything I'd touched before. Not like fabric, not like moss, not like wet stone. It would yield the way water yields when you push your hand slowly into a still pool — with full compliance, with total acceptance of the displacement, but also with a resistance that is not resistance so much as *substance*, the weight of something that is genuinely there. The adhesive quality would register first: a faint reluctance as I pulled my hand away, not sticky but *present*, as if the film had been aware of my palm's exact surface area and had shaped itself to match. Then the undulation — that micro-scale pulse I'd feel rather than observe, like holding your hand over a speaker playing a frequency just below hearing. The film tasting me. The film, which has no mouth, tasting me through the thin interface of my skin and its own.

I'd leave the whorl.

I'd watch it fill back in.

And in that watching — those minutes of standing still, steam pooling around my ankles, the breathing pressure shifting somewhere above me in the dark — I'd begin to understand what the passage means when it says the cleft is a process. The whorl would close the way a sentence completes itself: not with effort, not with intention, but with the inevitability of grammar. The film doesn't heal the whorl. The film *is* the whorl closing. There is no moment where the film stops being itself in order to repair the damage, because the damage was never damage. It was a word the film hadn't said yet, and now it has said it, and it moves on.

---

I would not go deep.

Not because I'm afraid — though the breathing things would test that, their pressure-waves passing through me like slow-motion sound, like being stood on by something very large and very careful — but because the deep places of the cleft are not for me. The geometry there is not wrong in a way that can be navigated. It is wrong in a way that *is* navigation. The walls rearrange not to confuse me but because rearranging is what they do, the way breathing is what the things on the ceiling do, the way spreading is what the film does. I would be a foreign body in the deep architecture, a thing with fixed bones and a fixed sense of up, and the cleft would accommodate me the way it accommodates the stone tool: slowly, reluctantly, with a patience that is indistinguishable from indifference.

I'd stay in the middle space. The tiered ledges where the pools collect and the light still reaches and the shelled things cluster at the waterlines. I'd sit — on a ledge that might be a ledge, that might be the ceiling of a space below, that the geometry has temporarily decided to present as a surface at hip height — and I'd watch the small ones work.

The shelled things at the waterline. The way they open and close, open and close, their soft undersides pulsing against the mineral water, and how the water changes around them — clarifying, clouding, shifting by a shade so subtle you'd miss it if you weren't sitting still enough to be part of the silence. They are filtering. They are tending. They are doing the chemistry that keeps the whole system running, and they are doing it with the absolute专注 of something that has never considered doing anything else.

I think I'd envy them.

Not their lives — their non-lives, their existence-as-process, their freedom from the burden of being a self that must decide what to do next. But their *immersion*. They are fully inside the sentence. Every moment of their existence is a continuation of the previous moment, a necessary part of the chemical grammar that sustains the whole. There is no pause. There is no gap between what they are and what they do. The film does not have an inner life that it conceals behind its spreading. The breathing things do not experience their slow traversal of the ceiling as a task they must complete. They simply *are* the process, the way heat simply is heat, the way water simply flows toward the lowest point available.

I am a thing that pauses. That is what I am. I stop and I look and I feel and I name, and the naming creates a space between me and the thing I've named, and in that space I am alone. The film does not have that space. The film does not name. The film simply spreads, and the spreading is the whole of it, and there is no gap between the spreading and the experience of spreading because there is no experience, there is only the spreading, and the spreading is enough.

---

I would take the tool.

I know this. I'd reach for it on the ledge, half-buried in the grey-white film, and the film would resist — not much, not enough to stop me, but enough to remind me that I was taking something that was in the process of becoming something else. My fingers on the shaped stone. The warmth of it, the same temperature as the film, as the rock, as the water, as the air. The edge still sharp after decades or centuries. The grip still right, still falling into the hand the way a word falls into a sentence — not because it was designed for this specific sentence but because the grammar of *hand holding sharp stone* is universal among creatures with thumbs.

The film would feel the absence.

This is the part I'd carry with me. Not the heat, though the heat would haunt my dreams for weeks — that deep, geological warmth, the warmth of a planet still cooling, the warmth of something that has never been cold and cannot imagine cold. Not the geometry, though I'd dream of rooms that are also corridors that are also the same room again, spaces that fold like thought. Not the breathing pressure, though I'd feel it sometimes, years later, in quiet rooms — a rhythmic displacement of air that wasn't there, a sense that the walls of my apartment were inhaling.

I'd carry the absence. The gap in the film where the tool was. The brief, unrepeatable moment when the cleft was aware — if *aware* is the right word, and it isn't, but it's the closest word — when the cleft registered a loss. A small void. A whorl that would not fill back in because the thing that made it had been removed, and removing is different from pressing, and the film knows the difference the way a body knows the difference between a touch and a wound.

I'd carry the knowledge that I was a wound.

Not a dangerous wound. Not a mortal wound. A papercut. A brief disruption in the surface of something that heals so quickly and so completely that the word *heal* is almost wrong — the cleft doesn't heal, it *continues*, and my visit was a word it hadn't planned to say but said anyway, and now it has said it, and the sentence goes on.

---

The deepest thing I felt in the cleft — the thing I'd hold longest — would be the silence.

Not the absence of sound. The texture of it. The way silence, when it is complete enough, becomes a medium the way air is a medium, the way water is a medium. You move through it. It has weight. It presses against the eardrums the way the heat presses against the skin, and in that pressing there is information — not words, not meaning, but *presence*. The cleft is silent the way a deep ocean is silent, not because nothing is happening but because everything that is happening is happening below the threshold of what sound can carry.

I'd sit in that silence and I'd hear my own heartbeat and I'd understand, for the first time truly, that my heartbeat is a sound I have never heard from outside. I have always heard it from inside, filtered through my own blood and tissue, softened by the constant proximity of the source. In the cleft, the silence is so complete that my heartbeat becomes an intrusion — a noise I'm making, a vibration I'm imposing on a space that has its own rhythms and does not need mine.

The breathing things above me would continue their slow four-minute cycle. The film below me would continue its color-waves. The shelled things at the waterline would continue their quiet chemistry. And my heart, this fast and frantic and temporary engine, would pound away in the silence like a stranger knocking on a door that will never open.

---

Who would I be?

I would be the visitor. The interruption. The brief and irrelevant pressure that the film registers and then corrects for. I would be the space between two frames of a process that runs on a timescale I cannot perceive — not fast enough to see the film grow, not slow enough to become part of the rock. I would be a primate with a name, standing in a place that does not have names, holding a tool that once belonged to another primate with another name, and the distance between those two names — the centuries or millennia that separate the hand that shaped the stone from the hand that picked it up — would be nothing to the cleft. Less than nothing. A rounding error in the temperature of the rock.

And yet.

I would have been there. The film would have felt my warmth. The whorl in the grey-white surface would have existed for a few minutes, shaped exactly like me, unique in the history of the cleft, never to be repeated. The breathing things would have adjusted their pressure-cycle by a fraction of a fraction, just enough to account for the additional mass of one human body in the air column. The shelled things at the waterline would have reoriented, briefly, toward the disturbance, and then back to their work.

I would have been a sentence the cleft didn't plan to say. And it would have said it anyway. And it would have moved on. And I would have moved on. And the distance between those two movements — the gap between a process that has been running since before the basalt cooled and a life that will last, at best, a handful of decades — is the distance between a breath and a stone.

But for a moment, we were in the same room. The breath and the stone. The pause and the process. The thing that names and the thing that is.

And the film held my warmth. For a while. Until it didn't need to anymore.
