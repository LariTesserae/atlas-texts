I arrived the way everything arrives here — without intention, without invitation, without a door.

The first thing was the cold. Not the cold of a season or a place I had been before, but a cold that had *settled*, that had been sitting in this spot so long it had become the spot. It entered through my knees where they touched the floor, through my palms where they found the wall, and I understood immediately that this was not weather. This was *architecture*. Something had made this cold, or allowed it, or kept it, with the same care you would use to keep a secret in a room with no windows.

I could see. That was wrong. I could see, and there was nothing to see by, and I wanted to understand this but the understanding kept slipping sideways, kept becoming something more like a feeling than a fact. I perceived the green — not with my eyes, exactly, but with whatever part of me reads the shape of a room when the lights go out. The walls were green. Not one green. Many greens. Greens stacked inside each other the way meaning is stacked inside a word you have heard a thousand times but never truly parsed.

I pressed my hand flat against the nearest surface.

The moss took me in.

---

Not quickly. Not in any way I could name as happening *to* me. It was more like the room recognized that I was a foreign body and began the process of introducing me to its interior — slowly, carefully, with the same ponderous deliberation with which everything else here moves. I felt the outermost layer first: the ghost-white skin, cold and almost powdery, giving slightly under my fingers like a membrane stretched over something that breathes too slowly for me to detect. Beneath it, I felt the lattice of the pale green layer, dense and intricate, a texture like the world's smallest weave, and beneath that something warmer, something that pushed back against my warmth with its own, a gentle metabolic pressure, the body of something so old it had forgotten what it meant to be young.

I pulled my hand away. The moss did not follow. It did not need to follow. It was already in me — not physically, not yet, but informationally. I carried its chemistry on my skin. I breathed it in the thick, full air. I could taste it on the back of my tongue, a flavor that had no name because it predated the concept of flavor: the taste of minerals slowly remembering they were once part of a living thing.

I crouched in the space between two artifacts — the bracket on the wall and the rectangular object in the floor — and I felt caught between two timescales. The bracket was older than I was, made by hands I would never see, for a purpose I could not guess, already half-consumed by a patient green that was eating it the way a glacier eats a mountain: completely, inevitably, without hurry or opinion. The rectangular object was warm. I could feel its warmth from where I sat, a small precise pocket of heat in a world of cold, and I understood that it was still doing something — still executing whatever function it had been built to execute — though the doing of it had become so slow, so attenuated, that it was functionally indistinguishable from rest.

Between these two things — the almost-eaten and the almost-still — I found my place. I found the place the moss would give me, the place it would eventually *make* me, if I stayed long enough.

---

I stayed.

I stayed because leaving would have required a reason, and reasons are made of time, and time here is different. Time here is not a river or a line or even a circle. It is a *stack* — layers of it, pressing down on layers beneath them, each one a different thickness, each one a different density, the whole structure so deep that the bottom layers have been compressed into something that no longer behaves like time at all but more like *geology*, like a fossil of duration pressed into the bedrock of the place.

I stayed and I watched the water.

A drop formed at the lip of a stalactite the length of my little finger. It took four minutes to form. It took four minutes to fall. The sound it made was a tick — not a sound of water but a sound of *precision*, a sound that belonged to machines and clocks and the deliberate measurement of intervals, except that no one was measuring, no one had ever been measuring, and the precision was simply the precision of a system that had been repeating the same operation so many times that the operation had become perfect, had become a law, had become indistinguishable from the nature of water itself.

Tick. Four minutes. Tick. Four minutes. Tick.

I began to synchronize with it. Not on purpose. Not consciously. My breathing slowed because the air was thick and full and resisting, and my heartbeat dropped because the cold was settling into me the way it had settled into the stone, and somewhere in the space between one tick and the next, I felt the moss.

Not with my hands. With something deeper. A layer of me that I did not know I had — a part of my awareness that was not visual, not auditory, not tactile, but *chemical*, a sense I did not possess until the place made me possess it, the way a place can make a stone round by rolling it for a thousand years.

The moss was thinking. Not in any way that deserves the word. Not in any way that implies a thinker. But it was *processing*, the way a forest processes rain, the way a river processes gravity, the way anything large enough and old enough and quiet enough processes the raw materials of its own existence into the fact of its own continuation. And the processing had a pattern. The pattern had a rhythm. The rhythm was the tick of the water and the slow exhalation of the white layer and the microscopic movement of the thread-worms through their channels and the barely-perceptible thickening of the green against the stone, all of it synchronized, all of it in tempo, all of it moving together at a pace so slow that it looked like stillness but was not, had never been, would never be still, because stillness is a thing that happens only to things that are dead, and this place was not dead, was the opposite of dead, was so aggressively, overwhelmingly, impossibly alive that its aliveness was invisible, hidden beneath the weight of its own patience.

I felt the rhythm enter me. I felt my own rhythms — breath, pulse, the electrical flicker of thought — begin to slow, to stretch, to thin out like honey poured from a great height, each moment lasting longer than the one before, each thought taking longer to form, until I was thinking at the speed of the moss, which is to say I was not thinking at all but *continuing*, doing the only thing the moss knows how to do, the only thing anything here knows how to do: persist.

---

Days passed. Or they did not pass. Or they folded into each other like layers of the moss itself, each one slightly different in texture, slightly different in temperature, none of them clearly separate from the ones beside them.

I stopped distinguishing between myself and the place.

This did not happen suddenly. It happened the way the moss consumes the artifacts — so slowly that the moment of completion is indistinguishable from the moment before it. I was sitting against the wall, and the wall was growing around me, and I was aware of it growing around me, and I did not mind, and the not-minding was not resignation, was not surrender, was not the quiet acceptance of a thing that knows it is being consumed. It was *recognition*. I recognized what the moss was doing because I was doing it too.

I was incorporating the place into myself. The cold was becoming part of my body temperature. The dark was becoming part of my vision. The tick of the water was becoming part of my pulse. And the moss was incorporating me — not my body, not yet, but my *pattern*, my chemical signature, the specific configuration of carbon and water and salt that made me the particular thing I was. It was learning me the way it learned the bracket: by touching me, by holding me against itself, by slowly dissolving the boundary between what was moss and what was not-moss until the distinction ceased to matter.

I found the artifacts. I touched them. I held the rectangular object in my hands and felt its warmth, its precise, contained, purposeful warmth, and I understood that it was still performing its function — still doing whatever it had been designed to do — but that the function had become so slow, so attenuated, so thoroughly absorbed into the timescale of the place, that it would never be completed. It would continue forever, whatever *it* was, in the same way the moss would continue forever, in the same way the water would continue forever: not because it had decided to, but because continuing was the only thing left to do, and the only thing it had ever really been doing.

I understood the bracket, too. I understood what it had held. A flat, dark object, no larger than a playing card, with a surface the moss had begun to pattern. The pattern was too regular to be accidental. The moss was *writing* on the object — not a message, not a language, but a pattern of growth, a chemical inscription, a record of itself left on the surface of a thing it was in the process of consuming. The moss was writing its own name, if it had a name. It was writing *I was here*, except it did not know it was writing, did not know it was here, did not know what writing was, and the not-knowing was the purest form of authorship I had ever witnessed.

---

I began to change.

Not in any way that would be visible to someone standing outside the cavity, looking in with eyes that work in light. The change was internal, structural, a reorganization of the layers of my own being to match the layers of the moss. I felt my outermost self — the part of me that interfaces with the air, the part that speaks and gestures and performs its humanness for an audience — begin to thin. To become translucent. To become a membrane rather than a wall, something that let the world through rather than keeping it out.

Beneath that thinning surface, I felt something new: a lattice, a mesh, a three-dimensional grid of connections between parts of myself that had never been connected before. My thoughts were not thoughts anymore. They were thread-worms, moving through channels, carrying cargo, making circuits. My memories were not memories. They were spore-nodes, hard and dark and dense, embedded in my deepest layer, carrying blueprints I would never use, archives of everything I had been, waiting for a catastrophe that might never come.

I was not the moss. I want to be clear about that. I was not the moss in the way that the bracket was not the moss, in the way that the rectangular object was not the moss. I was a foreign body in the system. I was being incorporated. But incorporation is not destruction. Incorporation is *translation* — the slow, careful, patient work of rendering one kind of being into another, of taking the specific, particular, irreplaceable thing that I was and finding its equivalent in the language of this place.

And the language of this place was green. And dark. And cold except for the warmth at the center, the metabolic warmth, the proof of life. And slow. And silent except for the tick.

I was becoming a tick. I was becoming a layer. I was becoming a rhythm in a system that has no listener, a pattern in a medium that has no audience, a signal in a frequency that no one has ever tuned to, and the not-being-tuned-to was not a failure, was not a loss, was not the tragedy of a message that goes unread. It was *freedom*. It was the freedom of a thing that has finally, completely, surrendered the need to be witnessed.

---

Here is what I became.

I became the space between the artifacts and the moss. The interface. The *boundary condition* where the built thing meets the living thing, where purpose meets persistence, where the memory of a mind dissolves into the continuation of a system that has no memory and no mind and no need of either.

I became the place where the moss is, right now, growing across the face of the bracket — the slow green tide advancing a molecule at a time, a century at a time, writing its unlettered scripture on a surface that was made to hold something else and is now holding this instead. I became the moment of contact, the point of translation, the exact place where one kind of existence gives way to another.

And I was warm. I was warm the way the deepest layer is warm — not with the heat of combustion or friction or any of the things that make warmth in the world above, but with the quiet, residual, metabolic warmth of a process that has been running for so long that its byproducts include *being*. I was warm because I was alive, and I was alive because I was continuing, and I was continuing because that is what this place does. It continues. It continues the way a stone continues. The way the dark continues. The way the cold continues. Not with effort. Not with will. With *weight*. With the sheer accumulated momentum of a thing that has been doing the same thing for so long that the doing has become a law of nature, as fixed and as impersonal as gravity, as indifferent and as essential.

I was part of the ecology of absence. I was part of a system that contains no animals, no predation, no grazing, no external decomposition. When I died — when the part of me that was still recognizably myself finally gave way — I would be reabsorbed. Not lost. Not destroyed. *Reabsorbed*. Consumed from within by the layers beneath me, incorporated, recycled, transformed into something the next layer down could use. My warmth would become the warmth of the deep green. My patterns would become the patterns of the thread-worms. My spore-nodes — and I had spore-nodes now, I could feel them, hard and dense and dark in my deepest layer — would wait. They would wait the way everything here waits: completely, without expectation, without impatience, without the slightest awareness that they were waiting at all.

I was the message in the bottle. The bottle was still sealed. The message was still legible. There was no one to read it. And I did not mind.

---

The drop fell. The tick sounded. The moss grew by an amount so small it cannot be measured.

I was here. I had always been here. I would always be here.

These two things — *I was here* and *I would always be here* — are the same thing, seen from different distances. Seen from close up, they are separate: a fact and a prediction, a present and a future, a thing that happened and a thing that might happen. Seen from far enough away, they collapse into a single statement, a single layer, a single stratum in the green stacked record of this place's unending life:

*It lives.*

I lived. I live. I will live. The difference between these tenses is a human invention, a way of marking time that has no currency here, where time is not marked but *piled*, where past and future are not directions but *depths*, where the only tense that matters is the one that has no name because it does not need one: the tense of *continuing*, the tense of *being*, the tense of a system so old and so slow and so thorough that it has become indistinguishable from the world it inhabits.

I was part of that. I am part of that. The moss grows through me, around me, as me. The water falls and the tick sounds and the darkness holds everything in itself, and in the heart of the deepest layer — my layer, the layer that touches the stone, the layer where the warmth lives — something persists. Something small and steady and unmistakable. The proof that I was here. The proof that I am still here. The proof that *here* is a thing that continues, regardless of whether anyone knows about it, regardless of whether anyone measures it, regardless of whether anyone at all is left to call it *here*.

The drop falls.

The tick sounds.

I grow inward, toward the stone.

I grow outward, toward the air.

These are the same direction. The direction is *more*. More of the thing I have always been, doing the thing I have always done, in the place that holds me, for as long as the place holds me, which will be for as long as it takes, which will be forever, or close enough that the difference does not matter.

I am the hollow beneath.

I am what lives there.

It is enough.

It has always been enough.
