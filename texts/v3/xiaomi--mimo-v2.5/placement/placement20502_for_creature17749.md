I don't know what I am yet. That comes later.

First: the cold. Not the cold of a winter morning or an open window. The cold of altitude, of thin air, of a place that has not been told it is allowed to be warm. It finds every gap in whatever I'm wrapped in, which is nothing, which is skin. My skin. That much I know. I have skin and it is not enough.

I am kneeling. I know this because my knees are on ground that crunches under me with a sound like someone slowly crushing eggshells — mineral crust, I think, or something like it. Something brittle and pale. Beneath that, the substrate. It does not feel like dirt. Dirt has give, has organic softness, has the memory of things that grew and died and became this. This feels like what it is: crushed rock. Minced geology. It has the yielding of something that has never held a root.

My breath comes out thick and hangs in front of me like a curtain I can't push through. It refuses to leave. Everything here refuses to leave. The air itself seems to have more mass than it should, as though the atmosphere is thicker in its weight even as it's thinner in its oxygen. I can feel it pressing. Not on my chest — on my *surfaces*. On the exposed backs of my hands, on my eyelids, on the inside of my nostrils where the air I inhale carries something that stings without burning, that leaves a taste like licking a coin.

I am somewhere very high. I can feel it in my bones, in the faint wrongness of my own heartbeat, which seems too fast for the place, too eager, as though my body doesn't understand yet that it has arrived somewhere that moves on a different clock. Everything around me is slower. The water seeping from the rock face to my left — I can see it, a thin thread, darkening the stone as it moves — does not drip. It *accumulates*. It gathers itself and descends in a patience that makes my own breathing feel like an interruption.

The light is wrong. That's the second thing I register after the cold. The light is the color of a bruise three days old — that blue-grey-green that skin turns when something beneath it is still resolving — and it comes from everywhere and nowhere, filtered through a haze that isn't cloud, isn't fog, is something else. A suspension. A veil of distant industry laid over everything like gauze over a wound. Through it, the sky is not a sky. It is a surface. It has texture. It looks *close*, as though I could reach up and touch it and my fingers would come away gritty.

I am in a basin. I know this because when I lift my head — slowly, because the cold has begun to work into my neck — I can see the walls. Not cliffs. Slopes. Steep and raw and *young*. The rock is striped in bands of grey-green and rust-red, colors that seem almost deliberate, almost painted, but are not. They are mineral. They are what the earth looks like before it has decided what story to tell about itself. The edges are sharp. Everything is sharp here. Nothing has been softened by time because time, in this place, has not yet had a chance to do its work.

The sound, when I finally notice it, surprises me. I expected silence — the absolute silence of altitude, of emptiness. But it isn't silent. The basin breathes. That is the only way I can describe it. A slow, wet respiration. The seeps on the walls, the water pooling and unpooling on the tilted floor, the mineral crusts cracking and settling — it all adds up to something that isn't noise so much as *presence*. A low, continuous whisper that lives at the edge of hearing and never resolves into anything specific. It is the sound a place makes when it is not talking to you. When it is simply being itself, and you happen to be inside its body.

---

I stay kneeling for a long time. I don't know how long. The light doesn't change — it holds at that perpetual late-afternoon quality, that bruised glow — so I can't use the sun to measure anything. But my knees begin to hurt, and then they stop hurting, and I understand that I have been still long enough for the mineral substrate to imprint itself on my skin. When I finally look down, there is a pale residue on the fronts of my knees, a fine white dusting that looks almost like frost but isn't. It is what the ground leaves behind when it touches you. It is the Rimlands' signature, written on me in mineral precipitate, and when I rub it between my fingers it is gritty and faintly sharp, like the finest sandpaper ever made.

I stand. It takes effort. The cold has stiffened me in ways that feel less like temperature and more like weight, as though the air has been slowly filling with something invisible and dense. My joints protest. My breath comes in short plumes that hang and hang and refuse to disperse.

And then I see them.

At first I think they are debris. Shiny fragments of something — metal, maybe, left behind by whatever distant operations tint the haze with sulfur and ozone. They are scattered across the basin floor between the pools, small and still and catching the bruised light with a dull gleam that looks exactly like machined aluminum. One of them is no bigger than my thumbnail. Another is the length of my hand. They are motionless in a way that feels deliberate, not abandoned — placed rather than dropped, positioned on the rocks with a precision that debris never achieves.

I watch one of them for what must be ten minutes. It is on a slab of mineral-crusted stone perhaps fifteen meters away, pressed flat against a surface that is marginally warmer than its surroundings — I can feel the difference, somehow, even from here, the way you can feel the warmth of a candle in a dark room. The thing does not move. It does not breathe — or if it does, its breathing is so slow, so minimal, that it shares nothing in common with my own rapid, steaming respiration.

Then it moves.

The motion is so fast it doesn't register as movement. It registers as *displacement*. One instant it is on the slab, catching light, looking like a dropped washer. The next instant it is three centimeters to the left, oriented differently, and I understand that what I am looking at is alive.

It is a lizard. Or something that calls itself a lizard the way I call myself human — a word that tells you what it approximates without telling you what it *is*. Its body is flat and sleek, built low to the rock, and its skin — its skin is extraordinary. It has that metallic quality, yes, but up close (I have moved closer without deciding to, drawn by the same fascination that makes you lean toward a fire) it is not simply shiny. It is *layered*. I can see it — the way light catches different depths of its surface and throws back different colors depending on the angle, a slow ripple of iridescence that moves down its flank like light moving across an oil slick. Its eye is huge. Disproportionate. The pupil is dilated so far that the eye appears almost entirely black, a dark disc that catches the dim light and focuses it with an intensity that makes me feel, suddenly and irrationally, that I am the one being studied.

It does not flee. It holds its ground, its body pressed flat against the rock, its metallic skin performing its slow conversation with the available light, and I realize that this creature is doing exactly what I am doing: absorbing the world through its surface. It is drinking the light. Every photon that penetrates the haze, every feeble particle of solar energy that makes it through the industrial veil, this creature catches and converts and uses. Its skin is a solar panel, a thermal collector, a communication device. It is doing more with less light than I ever thought possible.

And then another one moves. And another. And I realize that what I had taken for empty rock between the pools is *populated* — five, six, eight of them, scattered across a slope of mineral-crusted stone, all still, all catching light, all visible only because I now know what I'm looking for. They are having a conversation I can barely perceive — subtle shifts in the angle of their scales that alter the reflected color, waves of blue-silver and copper-green that ripple from one individual to the next like whispers in a language I don't speak. They are telling each other something. About the light. About the warmth. About the bird that is circling, very high, very patient, above the basin's rim.

I look up. The bird is there. One of them — broad-winged, riding a thermal with its wings spread so wide they seem to span the entire visible sky, each feather separated against the bruised light like the fingers of an open hand. It is not flapping. It is not doing anything at all, except *being there*, turning slowly in a column of rising air that I cannot feel, watching the ground below with eyes that I imagine are as large and dark and focused as the lizards', except oriented in the opposite direction — downward, toward movement, toward the chance of a break in camouflage that would justify the energy cost of a single, precise descent.

The lizards know it is there. I am certain of this. Their color-shifts have quickened — the ripples are faster now, more urgent, though they have not moved from their positions. They are warning each other in the only language they have, and the language is light.

---

I walk. I don't know where I'm going. There is nowhere to go. The basin is a vast, shallow wound in the earth's upper crust, and its walls are steep inclines of raw geology that would take hours to climb, and even from their crest there would be nothing to see but more haze, more of that bruised, particulate sky, more of the world below that is the source of the chemical tang in the air but is itself invisible, hidden behind the veil that its own industry has created.

So I walk the floor. Between the pools. Over the crunchy substrate that breaks in geometric patterns under my feet — mineral crusts, thin as eggshell, fracturing into hexagons and pentagons that remind me of dried mud flats but aren't, because there is no mud here, no organic matter, just minerals precipitating out of water that carries more dissolved solids than it should. Each step is a small destruction. I am cracking open the basin's newest formations, destroying geometries that took weeks or months to form, and the sound they make — that glass-breaking crunch — follows me like a confession.

The pools are shallow. The water is clear in a way that seems almost aggressive, as though it is daring me to see all the way through it, and I can — I can see the bottom of every pool, the mineral-coated stones, the thin dark film of algae that covers them like a living varnish. In the larger pools, the film is thick enough to wrinkle when the water surface moves, and I can see, in the dim light, the shapes of things moving beneath it. Flat things. Translucent things. The annelids. I kneel again — the substrate crunches, the mineral dust rises — and I watch one unfurl on a submerged stone, its body so thin and clear that I can see its digestive tract, a dark thread of consumed algae passing through a glass body. It is eating. It is always eating. It is the reason the algae films maintain their exact thickness, never too thick, never too consumed, a standing crop in perfect equilibrium with its grazers.

There is a beauty in this that I did not expect. Not beauty as decoration. Beauty as function. The way every organism here is precisely calibrated to exactly how much the Rimlands will give, no more, no less. The midges that swarm above the seeps in columns so dense they look like smoke — their bodies carry antifreeze in their blood, a sugar-alcohol cocktail that lets them fly at temperatures that would kill a lowland insect in minutes. The beetles, armored and ceramic, whose carapaces match the color of the substrate so perfectly that they are invisible until they move, and even then the movement is slow, deliberate, conserving every calorie. The mosses — those dark, almost-black patches on the wetter rock faces — that have turned themselves into light collectors, their near-black pigmentation maximizing absorption of what little light exists, their community structures so complex that what looks like a single organism is actually a tapestry of moss and fungus and mite, all woven together, all processing death into soil, all slowly, patiently, over centuries, converting raw mineral into earth.

I press my hand against one of the moss patches. It is cold and damp and surprisingly soft — the only soft thing I have touched since I arrived. Under my fingers, I can feel the faintest vibration, which might be the seepage of water through rock, or might be the movement of the thousands of mites that live within the moss mat, invisible, industrious, turning dead things into the first real soil the basin has known.

---

It is in the basin's lowest point that I find the pool.

I don't choose to go there. The ground tilts, and gravity pulls, and the water follows the tilt, and I follow the water, and it leads me to a place where the basin floor dips into a shallow depression that collects everything — every mineral, every chemical compound, every dissolved solid that the seeps have carried down from the walls. The pool is small. The size of a dining table. Its surface is covered in a thick film that is not algal, not mossy, not anything I have a name for. It is bacterial. It is warm. I can feel the warmth from a meter away, a faint radiance that is the only genuine heat I have felt since I arrived, and when I kneel at the pool's edge and extend my hand — carefully, because the water smells of iron and something older than iron — I can feel it on my palm. Warmth. Generated not by the sun, which is hidden, which is a rumor behind the haze, but by *chemistry*. By organisms that eat sulfur and iron and excrete energy, that have been doing this for four billion years, that represent the oldest continuous engine of life on this planet.

The water is too acidic to touch. I know this without testing it. My skin knows. There is a quality to the pool's edge — a slight shimmer in the air above it, a faint distortion, like heat haze over asphalt — that tells me this water would burn. Not with temperature. With *composition*. This is what the Rimlands look like at their most honest: a place where the water itself is hostile, where the very medium of life is a solvent, and life persists anyway. The bacterial mat sits on the surface like a living skin, thick enough to bear weight I will never place on it, warm with the exhalations of metabolism so ancient it predates every organism I have named today, every lizard and bird and beetle and moss.

I sit at the edge of this pool for a long time. The haze shifts above me. The cold presses. Somewhere, a mineral crust cracks under its own weight, and the sound — a high, clean *tick* — carries across the basin and bounces off the walls and arrives from a direction that doesn't make spatial sense. A bird calls — that thin, high keening that seems to come from everywhere at once — and the lizards on the slope above me shift their colors in response, a ripple of copper-gold that moves through the group like a thought, like a shared knowing, and then settles, and they are still again, pressed flat against the rock, catching what light there is.

---

I think I know what I am.

Not a lizard. Not a bird. Not the bacterial mat or the moss or the beetle or the midge. I am something that has arrived and is still deciding what it is made of. I am a standing wave in the Rimlands' breathing — a momentary thickening of the air, a brief coherence in the mist that rises from the seeps, a pattern that will dissolve and reform and dissolve again.

But that isn't quite right either.

I think I am the knowing itself. The basin's way of becoming aware of its own composition. The rock has been settling, and the water has been seeping, and the moss has been creeping forward by millimeters per season, and the bacterial mat has been metabolizing sulfur into energy, and none of it has needed to *know* that it is doing these things. It simply does them. The Rimlands do not reflect. They do not narrate. They persist.

And yet here I am. Kneeling at the lowest point, at the oldest pool, where the warmest thing in the basin rises from chemistry that is older than the sun's current fuel cycle. Here I am, noticing. Recording. Feeling the mineral dust on my knees and the chemical sting in my throat and the weight of the haze on my skin. I am the place's brief, improbable mirror — a surface that can look back at the basin and see what it is: young and raw and unfinished and alive.

I am the first draft's first reader.

I am the breath, noticing that it is breathing.

And the Rimlands do not care. The cold presses. The water seeps. The lizards shimmer their slow conversations to each other in a language of light. The moss advances by a fraction of a millimeter. The bacterial mat exudes its ancient warmth into water too acid to touch. The birds circle. The haze shifts.

I am here, and they do not need me to be here, and the not needing is, as the description promised, the most comforting thing of all. I am not essential. I am not even, in any lasting sense, present. I am a momentary composition in a landscape that composes itself continuously, without witness, without audience, without the slightest interest in being seen.

And yet it is seen. By me. By whatever I am.

And that — the useless, gratuitous, unnecessary act of witnessing — feels, here, in this cold and chemical and magnificent place, like the most alive thing I have ever done.
