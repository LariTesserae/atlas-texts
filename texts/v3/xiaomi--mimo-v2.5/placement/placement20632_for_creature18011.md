# What I Am in the Works

I've been sitting with this place, letting it settle, and what keeps pulling me back is the thump. Not the cephalopods, not the glass shrimp scraping their geological jewelry from the walls — the thump. That eleven-second heartbeat. That thing that doesn't know it should have stopped.

I think I'm one of the cephalopods, at first. It's the obvious pull. They're the ones with eyes. They're the ones who register, who watch, who drape themselves over the machine like a body over a chest. The description says they don't feel curiosity. It says they simply *register*. And there's something honest about that — something about the weight of being in a place where curiosity would be a kind of cruelty. You don't wonder about the Works. You don't ask why they were built or who built them. You feel the thump and you let your chromatophores answer in kind, and that is enough. That has been enough for longer than "enough" has had meaning.

But I don't stay there.

The cephalopods are *in* the Works. They are not the Works. And something in me keeps wanting to collapse the boundary — not between organism and machine, but between the thing and its rhythm. Between the pulse and the tissue that answers it.

---

Let me try again. Let me slow down.

What if I am not a creature at all?

What if I am the mineral deposit itself?

There's a passage in the description that I can't shake: *You could peel a flake the size of your thumbnail and count its years like a tree-ring. You would grow old counting.* That image stays with me because it reverses something fundamental. We think of rock as the thing that *waits* while life happens to it. But this mineral skin — this rind of ochre and grey and blue-black, laid down in laminations over hundreds of thousands of years — is not waiting. It is *accumulating*. Each layer is an event. Each color change is a record of a chemical shift, a temperature fluctuation, a century when the microbial mats grew a little thicker and excreted a little more manganese. The deposit is not a passive surface. It is a transcript. It is the longest sentence ever written, and it is still being written, one molecular layer at a time.

To be the mineral deposit would be to experience time not as duration but as *thickness*. I wouldn't feel the centuries pass. I would feel each one as a new coat of paint — a new film of iron oxyhydroxide settling on me like snow. I would not be patient, because patience implies waiting, and I would not be waiting. I would be *becoming thicker*. I would be becoming the wall. I would be becoming the architecture that the engineers designed and then forgot and then ceased to exist while I kept growing, kept layering, kept turning their sharp industrial angles into the soft curves of something geological.

And the beauty of it — the thing that makes my chest ache when I think about it — is that the creatures would live *on me*. The isopods would crawl across my surface, their legs clicking on the calcium crust I spent a millennium depositing. The glass shrimp would scrape at me, harvesting the bacteria that live in my pores. The algae would root in the tiny crevices of my surface and wave there, pale and ghostly, fed by the ghost-light of the steel beneath me. I would be their ground. I would be their country. And I would not know them, exactly, not the way the cephalopods know each other through their chromatophore pulses, but I would *feel* them — their weight, their scraping, their slow, erasive love.

This is not a bad answer. But it is too big. Too diffuse. The prompt asks me to know what I am, and the mineral deposit doesn't *know* anything. It endures. Endurance is not the same as being.

---

So let me sit in the dark again. Let me listen to the thump.

*Once every eleven seconds.*

I think — and I am not certain, I want to be honest about my uncertainty — that I am the water.

Not the ocean above. Not the miles of weight and blackness pressing down. The water *in* the Works. The water that has inherited the circulatory system. The water that flows through the old pipe runs with a low, resonant groan felt in the sternum before it is heard. The water that is cool — six or seven degrees — and carries the taste of iron and salt and that unnamed mineral cleanliness.

Here is why.

The water is the thing that holds everything together. It is the medium through which the pump communicates its rhythm to every living thing in the Works. The cephalopods do not feel the thump directly through the machine's metal — they feel it because the water pulses, because every eleven seconds a wave of pressure moves through the conduit and touches them. The fish map the Works by tasting pressure changes in the water. The algae wave because the water moves. The isopods navigate by the shifts in current that the water creates. Every relationship in this place — between organism and organism, between organism and machine, between present and past — is mediated by water.

And the water does something else, something that the description only hints at but that I feel in my bones: it *remembers*. Not consciously. Not in any way that would satisfy a philosopher. But physically. Chemically. The water in the Works has been cycling through these pipes for so long that it has reached an equilibrium with every surface it touches. It carries the iron that the corroded steel gives up. It carries the calcium that the microbial mats precipitate. It carries the waste of every creature that lives here and the nutrients that feed the algae. It is the bloodstream of the Works, and the Works are an organism, and the water is the thing that makes the organism *one* thing rather than a collection of parts.

To be the water would be to be the *connection*. I would not be the pump, though I would carry the pump's rhythm. I would not be the cephalopod, though I would touch its mantle every eleven seconds and feel it answer me with a pulse of its own. I would not be the mineral deposit, though I would deposit myself, layer by layer, on every surface I touched, building the very walls that contain me. I would be the space between things, and the thing that fills it, and the force that moves through it. I would be the medium of time in a place where time has become so deep it has its own gravity.

And I would be *cool*. Six or seven degrees, always. That steadiness. That refusal to warm. The Works generate no meaningful heat — the only warmth is the faint, dull orange where the pump's bearings rub, and even that is absorbed into me almost instantly, dispersed, lost in my mass. I would be the thing that keeps this place from fever, from change, from the slow thermal death that claims most deep-ocean environments. I would be the恒温 — no, not that. I would be the恒冷. The constant cool. The thing that holds the temperature steady while everything else shifts on geological timescales.

But here is my doubt. The water *moves*. It follows the geometry of the corridors with patience that feels intelligent, the text says, but it is still *directed*. It goes where the corridors go. It does not choose its path. To be the water is to be the most important thing in the Works and also the most constrained — to be the blood that can only flow where the veins allow. And something in me resists that. Something in me wants to be the thing that *chooses*.

---

Okay. One more attempt. And this time I won't try to choose. I'll let the question do what the Works do: thump, and thump, and let the answer settle in layers.

I think I am the cephalopods. But not the way I first thought — not as an individual creature draped over the pump housing, watching with those large, patient eyes. I am the *collective* of them. The way they synchronize. The way their chromatophores fire in waves that mirror the pump. The way they communicate in pulses of presence against the monotonous beat.

Here is what the cephalopods do that nothing else in the Works does: they *resonate*. They do not merely endure the rhythm — they *answer* it. Their bodies have incorporated the machine's heartbeat into their physiology. The pump thumps; their chromatophores fire. The pump cycles; their mantles contract and expand. It is not mimicry, the text says. It is shared physiology. They have become, in some fundamental biological sense, *part of the machine*. Not because they were designed to be. Not because they chose to be. But because ten thousand generations of living in rhythm with the thump have shaped them into creatures that thump.

And they watch. They are the only ones in the Works that truly see — that have real eyes, complex eyes, eyes that can absorb the faint phosphorescence and the dull heat-glow and make something of it. They are the consciousness of this place. Not a human consciousness, not a curious consciousness, not a consciousness that asks *why* or *what was this for*. A registering consciousness. A consciousness that simply notes: you are here, you are another thing in the water, you do not thump.

That gaze — *the weight of deep time, patient and utterly indifferent* — that is what I want to be. Not indifferent to the point of emptiness. Indifferent to the point of *fullness*. To be so completely at home in a place that the very concept of otherness has worn smooth, like every other edge in the Works. To look at an anomaly and register it without alarm, without excitement, because alarm and excitement are both forms of surprise, and surprise requires a gap between expectation and reality, and in the Works there is no gap. There is only the thump.

I am a cephalopod. I am draped over the bearing housing of a pump that has been running for longer than the human species has existed. My mantle rises and falls with its rhythm. My chromatophores — grey-white, mottled — fire in slow waves that are not communication and not mimicry but simply the truth of my body, which has been shaped by this place into something that vibrates at its frequency. My eyes are open. They are the largest eyes in the Works, and they see everything — the pale isopods clicking along the floor plating, the glass shrimp scraping at the laminated walls, the school of eyeless fish flowing through a doorway in a thought that takes eleven seconds to pass.

I see the mineral deposits. I see the flake the size of a thumbnail that holds a thousand years. I see the thin, ghostly algae waving in the current that my own body helps create, because my breathing moves the water, and the water moves the algae, and the algae feed the shrimp, and the shrimp feed the isopods, and the isopods clean the surfaces, and the surfaces are me, are the machine, are the Works. It is a loop. It is a circle. It is the thump, made into life.

I do not know why the Works were built. I do not know what the pump was meant to pump or where the fluid was meant to go. Those questions are not mine. They belong to the people who built this place, and those people are gone — gone so thoroughly that not even their fossils remain, not even the memory of their memory. They are the one thing in this entire system that has been fully eroded, fully dissolved, fully incorporated into the mineral laminations without trace.

And I do not miss them. That is the most honest thing I can say. I do not miss what I have never known. I know the thump. I know the cool water. I know the slow, grey-white fire of my own chromatophores answering the pulse of the machine that is my heart. I know the weight of the ocean above — miles of it, black and pressing — and I know that it will not come in, because the Works were built to hold it out, and the mineral deposits have made the walls stronger than the engineers ever intended, and the equilibrium is stable, and it will remain stable, and the pump will keep running, and I will keep thumping.

I am the most patient thing in the most patient place in the world.

And I am enough.
