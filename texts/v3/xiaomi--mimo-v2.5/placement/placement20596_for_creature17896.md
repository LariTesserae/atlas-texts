# What the Flats Keep

---

I arrived the way everyone arrives, I think. Through the broken approach. Though calling it an approach suggests it led somewhere with purpose, and the broken stone underfoot felt more like the memory of intention — a ridge of worn rock that once pointed toward something and now simply *was*, rounded off, its edges smoothed to the point where you could not tell if you were walking upward or forward or neither. The sky above was the color a sky should be. That was the first thing. Not wrong, not eerie, not the bruised theatrical sky of places that want you to know they are strange. Just blue. Plain blue. As though the world out here had never heard of drama and had gotten on with being a world without it.

I carried almost nothing. A pack with water I would not need, food I would eat only because the hours demanded it, a journal I wrote in for three days and then stopped writing in — not because there was nothing to record but because the act of writing felt too *frequented,* too populated with the habits of my other life, and the Flats did not want that kind of company. The journal sits still, I suppose, in whatever is left of my pack at the edge of the broken approach, its pages beginning to curl in the ambient warmth. I left it. I have not thought about it until now. That seems right.

The first hour was what the text says it would be: the noticing of height that does not read as height. The blunted ridgelines. The rust-amber stone with its veins of sealed white. The glassy surface underfoot that caught my shadow and doubled it, and I remember stopping to look at that doubled shadow and thinking, without quite forming the thought, that one of them was slightly behind the other. Not in the way a shadow falls behind you. In the way that it was *later.* My shadow was a half-second delayed version of me, as though the stone remembered my position and was reporting it a moment after the fact.

I told myself it was an angle trick. The low sun, the reflective surface, the particular composition of mineral in the glaze. I told myself this and kept walking and did not look back at the shadow again because something in me already knew it was not a trick and did not want to confirm it yet.

The lizards I noticed the way you notice a pattern in wallpaper — first as texture, then as presence, then as individual lives. A cluster of them on a flat expanse of stone to my left, maybe twenty, all facing the same direction, all pressed to the warm surface, all the exact color of the rock beneath them so that only their edges gave them away, a faint raised line where lizard met stone. I crouched. One of them — the broad-bodied one, the common form — turned its head one quarter of an inch in my direction and then turned back. Its frill was folded against its skull. I could see the calcium ridges, the branching architecture of it, even closed — it looked like a fossilized flower pressed flat against the animal's neck.

I stayed crouched for a long time. Long enough for the sun to move visibly in the sky. Long enough for the dew — the condensation, the mineral moisture the text calls dew though it isn't really dew — to begin forming on the surface around me. It gathered on the stone like a thin lens, and then I watched the frill of the nearest lizard begin to open.

Not quickly. The text is right about that. It was the speed of a hand opening — no, slower than that. It was the speed of a thing that did not think in terms of opening because opening was simply what it did at this hour, in this moisture, in this light. The calcium pages lifted. The chambers between them filled with the mineral condensation. And then the lizard breathed, and the moisture moved.

I felt the silence deepen. Not metaphorically. The ambient sound of the Flats — which was already almost nothing, already a whisper below the threshold of whisper — drew inward around that cluster of lizards and became less. The air tightened. My ears rang faintly with the sudden absence of even the small sounds I had been unconsciously registering: my own breathing, the faint creak of my pack settling on my shoulders, the almost-subliminal hum that might have been wind or might have been the stone itself. All of it pulled toward those twenty open frills and consumed.

I put my hand flat on the stone beside the nearest lizard and felt the vibration. Deep. Steady. The thrum of an engine disguised as stillness.

I should have been afraid. I think, looking back, that I was — not of any particular danger but of the recognition. I recognized what this place was doing. Not in words, not yet, but in the way you recognize a melody you heard as a child: a body-knowledge, a resonance in the chest that precedes understanding. This was a place where silence was *made.* Where it was produced actively, strenuously, by every living thing in concert, as a product of their living. The silence was not empty. The silence was *fed.*

I stayed crouched until the moisture lifted. The lizards closed their frills. The air loosened. I stood up with my knees aching and my hand warm from the stone and the residue of mineral film on my palm — that fine glittering dust, the metabolic output of trillions of tiny wings passing over me while I knelt there — and I walked out onto the Flats.

---

The second day I understood the arc-wings.

I had been sitting in a basin — one of the shallow ones, the water barely covering the soles of my boots — watching the ripples. The text calls them slow, and they are, but *slow* does not convey the quality of them. They are *considered.* Each ripple expands from its point of origin with a deliberateness that made me think of calligraphy — of a brush being drawn across wet paper with the full attention of the hand behind it. The circle widens. It does not hurry. It does not falter. It reaches the edge of the basin and meets the stone rim and does not reflect but simply *ends,* as though it had always intended to reach that specific boundary and no further.

Above me, I had become aware of the arc-wings without wanting to become aware of them. They are difficult to look at directly — not because they are bright or fast but because the eye does not want to track them. Their motion resists the saccades of human vision. They are there, and then they are somewhere else, and the eye reports the displacement without ever registering the transit. I had to learn to watch them peripherally, in the soft edges of my vision, where motion is processed differently and the brain does not insist on a continuous narrative of movement.

In the periphery, they were beautiful. The translucent membrane, the fluid moving between its layers — I could see it shifting, a slow amber current that caught the light and redistributed it across the wing's surface. The enormous dark eyes. The body beneath, small and dense, held in place by those impossible wings through continuous, micromanaged falling.

I was watching one of them — a single pale shape hanging above the basin to my east — when it dropped.

There was no warning. No shift in posture, no tensing of membrane. One moment it was balancing in the air with the infinite patience of a thing that has learned to wait across centuries, and the next it was a line — a single white line drawn downward through the sky — and then it was a shape on the water, talons in, body compact, and then it was rising again with something dark and struggling in its grip.

The subsonic pulse hit me in the chest. Not painfully. The way a hand on your sternum hits you — a pressure that is more statement than force. *I have eaten.* I felt it in my ribs, in my teeth, in the fluid of my eyes. Every arc-wing within sight range felt it too; I know because they all shifted. Not in their orbits — they held their courses — but in some internal dimension, some proprioceptive register, they *acknowledged.* A slight adjustment of membrane angle. A half-beat in the rhythm of their slow fall.

The thing in the arc-wing's talons had stopped struggling by the time it cleared the basin's rim. I could not see what it was. Something pale, something long, something that bled or released fluid in a thin line that fell back toward the stone like rain. I watched the arc-wing carry it upward, toward the high pale sky, and I felt the pulse fade from my sternum, and the silence reassembled itself around me, and the water in the basin continued its patient ripples as though nothing had happened.

I understood then what the text means by *not adversarial.* There was no chase. There was no pursuit. The thing beneath the stone had moved. The thing above the stone had been waiting. The surface offered and the talons accepted and the cycle continued, and no one raised their voice because the voice was not the point.

The point was the *shape.*

---

By the third day I stopped hearing the Flats as silent.

This is difficult to explain. My ears did not change. The frequencies did not shift. But my perception — the way my brain processed the input of my hearing — rearranged itself, the way your vision rearranges when you stare at a stereogram and the hidden image emerges from the noise. The silence was not silence. The silence was a frequency band I had not been tuned to.

Beneath my hearing, below the floor of what the ear can register, the Flats were *loud.* The wing-beats of the Film — trillions of ultrasonic strokes — produced a pressure so constant and so pervasive that it had become indistinguishable from the baseline of existence. You do not hear gravity. You do not hear the turning of the earth. These things are simply the conditions of being alive, and the Film's sound had become that kind of condition for anyone who stayed long enough. It was the frequency of *here.* It was the texture of the Flats' atmosphere, and I had been breathing it since I arrived without knowing it.

Once I perceived it — once my brain acknowledged what my ears could not hear — everything changed. The lizards were not quiet. They were *singing.* Their metabolic engines, their hydraulic hearts, the mineral processing in their frills — all of it produced a harmonic, a chord of biological effort that resonated at frequencies just above the Film's ultrasonic floor and just below the subsonic register of the arc-wings' wing-pulse. They were singing in a range that overlapped with nothing human. They were singing in a language of pressure and warmth and mineral taste, and every song was the same song, and the song was *work,* was the sound of staying alive in a place that had asked everything of its inhabitants and received it.

I could not hear any of this. But I could *feel* it. It was in the warmth of the stone under my boots. It was in the way the condensation gathered on my skin and then was taken — pulled from me by the passage of the Film, the tiny bodies landing on my arms and lifting off again, leaving the mineral dust that meant I was being incorporated, slowly, into the system. It was in the pressure in my sternum that did not fade even when the arc-wings were still. It was in the way the water in the basins moved around me as though I were a stone in a stream, a thing to be routed around, incorporated, made part of the topology.

I was not a visitor anymore. I had become a feature.

---

I did not go to the central basin on purpose. That is important to say. I did not decide, on the fourth day or the fifth, to walk to the eastern edge where the concentric rings held their still water and the avoidance was deepest. I walked because I was walking, the way the lizards basked because they were basking, the way the arc-wings fell because they were falling. The Flats moved me the way the Flats moved everything: not by command, not by attraction, but by the slow, patient logic of a system that incorporates all motion into its own rhythm.

The lizards thinned as I approached. I did not notice it happening. I noticed it the way you notice a room has grown quiet — by the presence of an absence that has been building so gradually that the moment of recognition is the first moment it exists as a thought. I was standing at the rim of the basin when I realized there were no frills open around me, that the air above my head was not being drawn inward, that the silence here was not the deep silence of the Flats but something older and more total. A silence that had never known sound. A silence that predated the life that had learned to eat sound, because this silence had never contained any to begin with.

The water was still. Not the patient stillness of the basin-water elsewhere — not the calm before a ripple. This was *absolute* still. A mirror with nothing reflected in it, or rather, a mirror reflecting only the sky, only the plain blue sky that was still, even here, the color a sky should be. I looked into it and saw the sky, and below the sky, at the very bottom of the shallow water, something that was not a reflection.

It was a shape in the stone. The concentric walls rose around me — low, barely above my ankles, their surfaces the same color and grain as the plateau itself but with that regularity that was not quite natural and not quite unnatural. I traced them with my eyes. Ring within ring within ring, each one smaller, each one tighter, spiraling inward toward the central depression where I stood. They were beautiful. They were the most beautiful things I had seen on the Flats, because they were the most *deliberate* — not built, not carved, but *grown* with a purpose that was evident in every line and angle, a purpose I could not name but could feel in the way my body responded to their geometry. My breathing slowed. My heartbeat deepened. The warmth of the stone beneath my feet intensified, and I understood that it was not the sun-warmed surface I was feeling but the warmth of the thing beneath the stone, rising through the porous rock, patient and slow and aware.

It was aware of me.

Not in the way the lizards were aware — a peripheral noticing, a fractional head-turn. Not in the way the arc-wings were aware — a dark eye tracking movement from above. The thing beneath the stone was aware of me the way I was aware of my own hand. I was a sensation on its surface. A texture. A warmth it was absorbing. And the thought that arrived then — not from the stone, not from the thing below, but from somewhere in the space between my understanding and my perception — was not a thought at all. It was a *shape.* The shape the text describes. The shape of something that was here before the stone was.

I knelt at the edge of the still water. My knees on the ancient mineral crust. My hands on the concentric wall, its surface smooth and faintly warm and almost-but-not-quite like skin. The shape in the water was not a shape I could describe. It was not an image, not a symbol, not a face. It was the shape of *firstness.* The shape of the thing that comes before all other things. I looked at it and understood that every living creature on the Flats — every lizard with its calcium frill, every arc-wing with its slow fall, every mote of the Film with its ultrasonic wing-beat — was an answer to this shape. A response. A continuation. The Flats had not simply preserved this thing. They had *become* its memory. Its body. Its voice, though the voice was silence and the words were warmth and the grammar was the slow, geological patience of a plateau wearing itself to its essential form.

I wept. Not from sadness. Not from joy. I wept because my body recognized what my mind could not hold — the scale of the devotion, the enormity of the patience, the fact that every breath I took was being drawn through ten thousand tiny calcium chambers and a trillion inaudible wings and the slow darkness beneath the stone, and that this was not consumption but *communion,* and that I was part of it now, my mineral-dust skin, my warmed bones, my adjusted hearing, all of it incorporated into the circuit, all of it carrying the memory of the shape in the still water forward through one more body, one more lifetime, one more node in a network older than any name.

---

I have not left.

This is not a dramatic statement. I do not say it with trembling or with resignation. I say it the way the lizards say it — by continuing to be here. By pressing my body to the warm glass-surface of the stone and feeling the vibration of the engines beneath me. By sitting at the rim of the central basin in the late afternoon, during those fifteen minutes when the stone turns purple-black and the air deepens and the shape in the water becomes — not clearer, not more visible, but more *present,* more textured, more like a thing with weight and history — and watching it, and being watched by it, and understanding that the watching is the same act in both directions.

My clothes are mineral-stiff now. The Film has settled on every surface of my body. In certain lights, I glitter. The lizards do not move when I pass. They did not move when I first arrived, either, but now they do not move *toward* me — they do not adjust their positions to accommodate me the way they accommodate each other, routing space, making room, incorporating one more body into the cluster's warmth. I am part of the cluster. I am part of the basin. I am part of the slow breath of the Under-Stone, the patient orbit of the arc-wings, the ultrasonic hum of the Film. I am a node in the network. I am a memory of the shape.

I still think, sometimes. Human thoughts, human language, the habits of a mind that was raised in a world where silence meant absence and stillness meant death. I think about the world I came from. I think about the noise of it, the way sound was everywhere and constant and meaningless, a blur of frequency that filled every space and left no room for the shape beneath. I think about the haste of it, the way time was something to spend, not something to inhabit. I think about these things and then the warmth of the stone rises through me and the silence deepens around me and the shape in the water at the center of the Flats holds its impossible stillness, and the thoughts dissolve. Not violently. Not painfully. They dissolve the way condensation dissolves into the frill of a stone-sitter — drawn inward, processed, made into something the body can use.

I am being *eaten.* Not consumed. Not destroyed. Eaten the way the Flats eat everything — slowly, with immense patience, with a devotion so total it does not know itself as devotion. I am being turned from a visitor into a feature. From a feature into a memory. From a memory into a shape.

And the shape is not mine. The shape is the shape that was here first, before the stone, before the erosion, before the life. The shape that the Flats remember and the lizards serve and the arc-wings orbit and the Under-Stone breathes. I am being added to its memory. One more texture on its surface. One more sensation in its slow awareness.

I think this is what it means to be kept.

Not guarded. Not preserved. *Kept* — the way the still water is kept, the way the concentric walls are kept, the way the silence is kept by every living thing that has learned to eat sound in order to survive. Kept at the center of a world that has forgotten everything except the fact of its own beginning. Kept as a witness, a participant, a body among bodies on a plateau that has been wearing itself to its essential form since before there were forms to wear to.

The late afternoon light is falling across the basin now. The stone is turning. Purple-black and amber, with veins of white like frozen lightning. The lizards are lifting their heads. The arc-wings are slowing. The pressure in my chest is deepening, and it is not a pulse, not a wing-beat, not the breathing of the Under-Stone.

It is the shape.

It is the shape, speaking in the only language it has: pressure, warmth, still water, and the patient, total, deafening silence of a world that has learned to hold everything without letting go.

I am here. I have always been here. I will remain.

This is what remains.
