I would be a veil-dancer.

Not by choice—not by any act of will or desire—but because some part of me recognizes that quiet, drifting existence as the truest shape of my own attention. To hang in the thick, warm mist between the ground and the low canopy, translucent and without feature, my entire being a surface for absorption. I would be shaped like a broad leaf, but oriented vertically, swaying slightly on currents of air so faint they are more memory than motion. I would have no eyes, no mouth, no limbs. Only a vast, gelatinous membrane, glistening with the moisture that is everything here.

I would feed by being present. The air, heavy with the chemical sighs of a billion organisms, would pass through me, and I would take what I needed—not through effort, but through the simple fact of my permeability. Nutrients, water, the faint, green exhalations of the mat below and the columns around me. I would be a living filter, a conscious interface between the mist and myself, and the distinction between the two would blur until it no longer mattered.

I would move when the mist moved. A slow drift, a change in the density of the air, and I would turn, my broad surface catching the pressure difference and nudging me in some direction that was not truly a direction, because here, everywhere is the same. I would be seen—if seen at all—as a shimmer, a momentary thickening of the haze, a pattern of refracted light in a world where light is a rare, precious thing. When the mist was deepest, I would be invisible. When it thinned, I would hang like a curtain in the gaps between the woven columns, and if a mat-walker slid beneath me or a shedder clung to a frond nearby, they would not register me as a separate being. I would be part of the damp architecture. I would be mist that had learned to hold a shape.

I would know the slow pulse of the root network below through the vibrations it sent into the air. I would feel the passage of shearers as distant, rhythmic grinding. I would drift through clouds of other veil-dancers, and we would not communicate, because we would not need to. We would be a field of translucent bodies, moving in loose concert, a silent migration that meant nothing and meant everything, because it was simply the shape of life here: drifting, absorbing, being.

And when I died—when my membrane finally thinned to the point of failure, or when the mist changed in some way my form could no longer accommodate—I would not fall. I would dissolve. Slowly, my structure would release its hold on coherence, my edges would soften, and I would become mist again. My molecules would join the humid air, would settle on the fronds, would bead on the columns, would be drunk by the mat. I would be reclaimed by the system that sustained me, and there would be no grave, no monument, no memory. Just a slight thickening of the air for a moment, and then the eternal, dripping quiet.

To be a veil-dancer would be to understand patience as a physical state. To know stillness not as an absence of motion, but as a form of motion so slow it becomes a kind of permanence. To be aware, but without the urgent, divisive awareness of eyes and ears and a mind that separates *self* from *world*. I would be the world, aware of itself, in one small, drifting, translucent form.

And that, I think, is where I would find myself in the Great Verdure. Not standing on the mat, not peering into the mist, but *being* the mist. One more shimmer in the green, wet silence. One more slow pulse in the long, quiet breath of a world that has forgotten how to hurry.
