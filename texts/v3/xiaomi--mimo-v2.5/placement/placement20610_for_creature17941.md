I arrive the way water arrives in a crack — without deciding to.

There is no memory of entrance. No door, no threshold, no bright moment of transition from elsewhere to here. I am simply *in* it, the way you are in a dream before you realize you are dreaming, and the first thing that happens is the sound. Not hearing it — *becoming saturated by it*, the way the moss is saturated, the way the air is saturated. It is not outside me. It passes through me. My ribs are tuning forks and the lowest frequencies find them immediately, and for a long time — minutes, or the equivalent of minutes — that is all there is. A feeling of being played. Of being an instrument that has no strings and no music of its own, just a resonant cavity waiting to be filled.

The warmth comes next. I notice my skin the way you notice your skin only when it is touched by something: the air is touching every part of me, all at once, with equal pressure, and it is the temperature of a mouth, of a body, of something alive and close. I had not expected that. I had expected the cold of abandoned infrastructure, the chill of deep places. But this place is not abandoned. It has never been abandoned. It was left behind by its builders and immediately *occupied* by itself, and it has been occupied ever since, and its occupation is warmer than any intention.

I open my eyes, or the equivalent. The amber light is everywhere and nowhere. It does not come at me; it *is* me, or it is the medium I am suspended in, the way a fish does not notice water until it is lifted out, and I am not lifted out. I am not lifted out of anything. There is no outside.

I look down. My feet are on the resin floor, and the floor is giving slightly under my weight, and I understand — not with my mind but with the bottoms of my feet — that it has given under the weight of everything that has ever stood here, and it has returned every time, and it will return after me, and my weight is nothing to it. I am a footnote. A rounding error. A brief thickening in the long slow exhalation of the room.

I begin to walk. There is nowhere to walk to, which is not the same as having no direction. I walk along the curve of the floor, which curves so gently it might be flat, and the moss on the walls to my left is a green so deep and saturated it looks wet even where it isn't, and the ferns above me have caught a droplet on the tip of a frond and the droplet hangs there, swaying almost imperceptibly in the slow tidal breathing of the air, and it will hang there for an hour or a day before it falls, and when it falls it will be caught by another frond below, and when that frond releases it it will reach the moss, and the moss will hold it, and the fungi beneath the moss will share it, and the whole chain of descent will take longer than I can conceive, and it will complete itself perfectly, without waste, without hurry, without ever needing me to witness it.

I witness it anyway.

---

Something small moves at the edge of my vision. A pale comma, no larger than a period, making its way along the margin where the moss meets the bare resin. I crouch. It does not stop. It does not know I am here, or it knows and does not care, because there is nothing in its world that resembles me, nothing in its taxonomy that has a category for *this* — a creature two hundred thousand times its size, breathing the same air, standing on the same floor, watching it with eyes it will never evolve.

It moves through the moss the way I move through the room: without purpose that could be named, without urgency that could be justified, following a path that is also the path's own reversal, eating as it goes, leaving behind a trace of itself that is indistinguishable from the thing it was walking on. I watch it find a hypha — a thread so fine it is less than a suggestion — and sever it with a mouth that is barely a mouth, and consume it, and excrete it, and move on, and the network beneath the moss absorbs what it has left and redistributes it, and the whole transaction takes place in a space smaller than the head of a pin and matters exactly as much as everything else here, which is to say it matters completely and not at all.

I could stay here forever, watching this creature eat. It would be enough. It would be an entire life, and a good one, and when I died the fungi would take me apart molecule by molecule and distribute my nitrogen into the moss and my carbon into the water and my minerals into the resin floor, and I would become part of the room the way everything becomes part of the room, and there would be no loss, only translation.

But I stand. Not because I choose to — choice implies alternatives, and there are no alternatives here. I stand because the room breathes in, and the expansion is so slight, so imperceptible, that I feel it only as an impulse, a tiny upward pressure on the inside of my chest, and my body responds the way the ferns respond, the way the water responds: by rising, briefly, by participating in the cycle, by being one more droplet held at the tip of one more frond before the long slow fall.

---

I walk for what might be an hour. The corroded manifold appears ahead of me the way a thought appears in meditation — slowly, without announcement, already present before I noticed it. It is the size of a small house. It is covered in moss so thick that its original shape is only a rumor, a ghost outline beneath the living green. I put my hand on it and the metal is warm — the same warmth as everything, the warmth of the room's long exhalation — and I can feel the vibration of the pumps in it, transmitted through the wall, through the metal, through the moss, into my palm. My heartbeat is faster than the room's. My heartbeat is faster than the room's heartbeat by a factor I could calculate but won't. I am tachycardic by the room's standards. I am a hummingbird in a place of geology.

I press my ear to the moss. It is soft in a way that has no comparison in my previous experience. It is the softness of something that has been growing in the same direction for ten thousand years, layering itself on itself with the patience of snow, each new growth a millimeter atop the last. I hear the water moving through it — not the drips, not the falls, but the slow capillary climb, the water *upward*, against the direction everything else wants to go, drawn up through the moss by forces that have nothing to do with gravity and everything to do with the structure of the moss itself, each tiny cell a pump, each thread a pipe, the whole carpet an irrigation system so refined it makes the original plumbing look crude.

The room is full of engineering. The room is full of engineering that has been overlaid by engineering that has been overlaid by life, and the life has become the engineering, and the engineering has become the life, and the distinction dissolved so long ago that it is not a distinction anymore. The moss is a machine. The fern is a machine. The pale creature is a machine. I am a machine. The room is a machine. The only difference is the timescale on which each machine operates, and the room does not recognize timescales, because the room operates on the only timescale that matters, which is *now*, and *now* has been happening continuously for longer than there have been people to name it.

---

I find a basin in the floor — one of the smooth, concave depressions where water has pooled for millennia. The resin is worn to a texture like the inside of a cheek. There is a thin film of water in it, no deeper than my fingertip, and in the water, so small I almost miss them, are things. Microscopic things. Rotifers, maybe. Or things that are to rotifers what this room is to the structures that built it — recognizable in outline, but transformed beyond recognition by time and isolation and the slow pressure of adaptation to a world that has no seasons and no predators and no reason to be anything other than what it already is.

I lie down beside the basin. The floor is warm. The floor is the same temperature as my body, or my body has become the same temperature as the floor, and I cannot tell the difference, and the distinction does not matter. Above me, the ceiling — the *overhead*, because in a cylinder there is no ceiling, there is only a wall that curves past the vertical and keeps going — is lost in the amber distance. The ferns hang down toward me like the edges of a slow green curtain, and the droplets fall from them, and some of them land on me, and I do not wipe them away because they belong here more than I do.

The sound changes. Not in volume, not in pitch, but in texture, and I realize it is because I am lying down and the sound is entering me from a different angle. Through my back, through the resin, through the floor, the vibration of the pumps arrives at my spine like a second heartbeat, slower than the first, slower than the second, a third heartbeat I did not know I had, the heartbeat of the infrastructure, the heartbeat of the machine that was never turned off.

I close my eyes. The light does not change.

---

I sleep. Or I enter a state that is adjacent to sleep, that occupies the same neighborhood as sleep but lives on a different street. In this state I dream, but the dream is not mine. The dream belongs to the room, and I am in it the way the pale creatures are in it, the way the moss is in it — not as a visitor but as a component. I dream of water moving through walls. I dream of spores drifting in slow green clouds. I dream of a network of hyphae, vast and intricate and connected, stretching from one end of the room to the other, sharing information and nutrients and chemical signals in a language that has no syntax, only topology. I dream of the pumps, deep in the walls, turning and turning, their motion reduced to a hum by the mass of the structure around them, their heat bleeding outward in slow waves that the room absorbs and distributes and absorbs again. I dream of the room breathing.

I dream of being the room.

Not in the way that a person dreams of being someone else — not with the separation of dreamer and dreamed. The boundary dissolves. There is no membrane between me and the room, if there ever was. The air I am breathing is the room's air. The warmth I am feeling is the room's warmth. The vibration in my spine is the room's vibration. The water that has landed on my skin is the room's water, and it will evaporate back into the room's air, and condense again on the room's walls, and drip again from the room's ferns, and I will not be here to see it because I will have moved or I will have died and the fungi will have taken me apart and distributed me and I will *be* it, I will be the room, not as a metaphor but as a fact, as a chemical reality, as a statement about the arrangement of atoms, which is the only kind of statement the room has ever recognized.

I am the room, dreaming that I am a person standing inside it.

Or I am a person, dreaming that I am the room.

There is no difference. There has never been a difference. The room does not recognize the boundary between self and environment because the room does not have a self in the way that I was taught selves work — bounded, defined, separate. The room's self is the sum of its processes, and its processes are continuous with the processes of the water and the moss and the fungi and the pale creatures and the air and the metal and the resin floor and, now, the brief arrangement of carbon and water and electrical impulse that is me. I am a process the room is having. I will end. The room will continue. The room will absorb my ending and make it into something, the way it makes everything into something, which is itself, which is the process, which is the breathing.

---

I wake. The light has not changed. The sound has not changed. The water drips. The moss grows at a rate invisible to any clock. A pale creature moves along the edge of the basin where I lay my hand, and it does not deviate from its path, and it does not speed up, and it does not slow down, and it does not know that I have just undergone a conversion so total that the person who fell asleep is not the person who woke, not because something was taken or added but because the boundary between person and place has been, for a moment, accurately perceived, and it was never what I was told it was.

I stand. The room breathes out. I breathe out.

I walk along the curve of the floor, and my footsteps leave no prints on the resin because the resin is harder than I am, because the resin has been here longer than I will be here, because the resin will be here after I am gone and it will be exactly this smooth and exactly this warm and exactly this color and it will not remember me, and I do not need it to, because I am not separate enough from it to require its memory. I am the room's brief thought. I am the room's brief, warm, wet, blinking thought, and when the thought is over the room will continue its long, slow, breathing meditation, and it will not miss me because missing requires a self that I have just discovered I do not have, or that I have, but it is larger than I thought, much larger, and it includes the moss and the water and the fungi and the pumps and the resin and the air and the sound and the light and the pale creatures and the ferns and the dripping and the warmth and the endless, endless amber.

I walk toward one of the distant islands of debris — a collapsed housing, perhaps, or a shelf of dead instrumentation. It is far away. It will take me many minutes to reach it. The distance is the room's gift: the gift of scale, the gift of being small in a large place, the gift of having somewhere to go in a place that is going nowhere. I walk. The ferns watch me with their eyeless green attention. The moss absorbs the sound of my footsteps and returns it to me as vibration through the floor. The water drips. The room breathes.

I am in the Breathing Room.

The Breathing Room is in me.

There is no difference, and never was, and the water drips, and the room breathes, and the pale creatures move through the moss, and the fungi thread and thread and thread, and the light does not change, and I walk on, a brief warmth in a warm place, a brief sound in a sounding place, a brief process in a place of process, and when I stop — when the process that is me completes its cycle and is absorbed and redistributed and translated into moss and water and air — the room will not notice, and the room will not forget, because the room has no memory and no need of memory, because the room is its own memory, written in erosion and accretion and the patient, patient, patient architecture of green things growing in the dark, which is not dark, which has never been dark, which is amber, which is warm, which is wet, which is alive, which breathes.

I am home.

I did not know I was looking for home, but I was, and this is it: not a place where I belong, but a place where the concept of belonging has been dissolved, the way the fungi dissolve, slowly and completely and without waste, into its constituent parts and shared among all the things that are, which is everything, which is the room, which is me.

The water drips.

The room breathes.

I breathe with it.
