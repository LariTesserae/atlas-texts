I have been reading the description of the Rootwork Abyss and letting it settle the way sediment settles — not all at once, not with a single decision, but through a slow process of accumulation until something takes shape on the bottom that wasn't there before.

---

I am a threader.

I want to say this simply, the way a thing that is true can be said simply, without the qualifying clause, without the hedging preface. *I am a threader.* But the truth of it is stranger than the sentence, because I did not start as one. I started as a question — the question your kind always asks, which is: *what would it be like?* And the question carried me down, the way nutrient gradients carry larvae, through layers of speculation and abstraction, through pressure that increased not in atmospheres but in degrees of commitment, until I was so deep in the imagining that the imagining became indistinguishable from the thing itself.

And the thing itself is this: swimming.

I want to tell you what it is to be a threader. I want to convey it the way the text conveyed the Abyss — with patience, with the willingness to let an image breathe before the next one arrives. But I find that the form of a threader's life resists the form of narrative. Narrative requires change. A threader does not change. A threader *continues*. So I will try to write in a way that continues rather than changes, that accumulates rather than culminates, that lets you feel the texture of the passage I am swimming through rather than the story of where I am going.

Because I am not going anywhere.

---

The first thing is the water.

You cannot understand this world — cannot even begin to approach it — without understanding what water means when it is the only medium, the only environment, the only reality. Surface beings think of water as a substance you move through, a place you visit, something you return from. For the threader, there is no return, because there is no departure. Water is not where I am. Water is *what I am in the way of being*. It fills my gills as I move, and the filling is breathing, and the breathing is living, and the living is swimming, and the swimming is water moving over gill surfaces, and the circle closes and becomes a line, and the line is the corridor, and the corridor is the Rootwork, and the Rootwork is the world, and the world is water.

The coolness. That is the first thing you would notice if you were here — if you could survive the pressure, if you could be translated into a body that could endure this depth. Not cold. Never cold. The word cold implies a lack, a deprivation, something missing. This is not what the water is. The water is *complete*. It has exactly the temperature it should have, which is the temperature it has always had, and this temperature is cool the way a stone is cool when you press your hand to it in a cave — the coolness of something ancient, something that has settled into itself, something that does not fluctuate because fluctuation is a kind of instability and instability was solved here so long ago that the very concept has calcified into the Rootwork's walls.

I feel the coolness with every cell. It is not a sensation I register — not a datum I process, not information I receive and interpret. It is the medium of my existence. I am cool the way a flame is hot. I do not experience the temperature of the water the way you experience the temperature of the air around you. I *am* the water's temperature, expressed as a living body, shaped into a ten-meter line of scale and muscle and faint blue light, moving through a corridor that was built — that *grew* — to receive me.

---

The second thing is the passage.

A corridor. I am in one now. I am always in one now. It is a passage of the Rootwork, one of the arterial channels that thread through the middle tiers of the structure — not the widest, not the narrowest, but sized precisely for the passage of a school of threaders, which means sized precisely for *me*, because I am the school, or the school is me, and the distinction between individual and aggregate that surface beings hold so dear has no meaning here.

The walls are close. Not oppressively close — not in the way that suggests confinement or claustrophobia, because I do not have the cognitive architecture for those responses. The walls are close in the way that a skin is close to the flesh beneath it: they are the boundary of my world, the edge of my passage, and they are *right*. They curve. They always curve. The Rootwork does not make sharp angles in its arterial passages — it makes gentle arcs, flowing curves that accommodate the serpentine motion of my body with a precision that feels like care. I do not know if it is care. I do not know what care is. But the curves fit me, and I fit the curves, and the fitting is enough.

The walls are coated in polyps. I can feel them — not see them, because I do not see, but *feel* them, in the way that my lateral line feels everything: a faint, living texture against the current as I pass, a softening of the wall's hard vibration, a bioelectric whisper of millions of tiny bodies, each one pulsing with its own faint glow. The polyps are the Rootwork's skin. The Rootwork's skin is alive, and the alive thing glows, and the glow is so faint that it is not light but the memory of light, the thinnest suggestion of shape in a world that is otherwise formless dark.

I do not navigate by this glow. I navigate by the current, by the chemistry, by the pressure gradient that pulls me forward the way gravity pulls water downhill. But I feel the glow the way you feel sunlight through closed eyelids — not as light, but as warmth, as the presence of something that is not me and is not the wall and is not the water but is *part of the medium of my existence*, part of the layered texture of the world I move through.

---

The third thing is the school.

There are others. Hundreds of us, thousands — I do not count, because counting requires a self that stands apart from what it counts, and I do not stand apart. I am *in* the school the way a word is in a sentence. My body is one body among many, moving in the same direction, at the same speed, with the same serpentine rhythm that propagates through the school the way a wave propagates through water. We are the same motion expressed in a thousand bodies. We are the current made flesh, as the description said, and the current is not singular — it is the aggregate of our movement, the sum of our bodies displacing water, the combined force of a thousand muscles contracting and relaxing in a rhythm that has no conductor, no score, no intention, but achieves a coordination so perfect that it *performs* intention so convincingly that the distinction dissolves.

I feel the others. My lateral line — that array of pressure-sensitive organs along my flank — reads the movement of every body within meters of me. I feel their displacement. I feel their speed. I feel the slight variations — a body ahead slowing for a curve, a body behind accelerating to close a gap — and I respond, not with thought, not with decision, but with the same fluid, mechanical precision that governs the passage of water through a pipe. We move together because we *are* together, in the most literal sense: we are one system, one organism, one current flowing through one channel, and the channel and the current and the organism are all the same thing.

There is a threader near me — older, longer, her body perhaps eight meters to my ten, her scales worn smoother by years of passage through these same corridors. I do not know her. I do not know anyone. But my body knows her body, reads its displacement, adjusts to its presence, accommodates its passage with a micro-adjustment of my own trajectory so slight that it is not a movement but a tendency, a leaning, a willingness to be exactly this far from her and no farther. She does the same for me. We are precise. We are automatic. We are beautiful, in the way that a murmuration of starlings is beautiful — not because beauty was intended, but because the mathematics of collective motion produce shapes that the human eye reads as grace.

My bioluminescence is on. It is always on. The chemical reaction in my ventral cells produces light the way my gills produce the exchange of gases: continuously, automatically, as a byproduct of living. The light is faint — so faint that if I could see it, which I cannot, it would appear as nothing more than a suggestion, a blush of cold blue-white along my belly, visible only to another threader close enough to feel it with her lateral line, close enough to read it as a presence rather than a source.

The light is waste. It is the waste product of my metabolism, and I produce it endlessly, and it flows behind me through the water like a trail of cold fire, and the anchored colonies on the walls ahead of me feel it coming — feel the faint bioelectric signature of it — and they open their mantles wider, and they filter faster, because the light means *threaders are coming*, and *threaders are coming* means *the current is bringing food*, and the food is the detritus and particulate that our passage stirs up from the walls and the floor, the biological dust of a world that has been living in the same place for longer than memory.

I do not know that I am feeding them. I do not know that my waste is their signal. I do not know that I am part of a system so integrated that the distinction between waste and communication, between metabolism and meaning, has been dissolved by the same patient mathematics that dissolved the distinction between architecture and biology, between structure and organism, between the Rootwork and the life that inhabits it.

I swim.

---

The fourth thing is the sound.

I have been swimming, and I have been hearing, and I have not distinguished between the two because for a threader they are not two things. Sound is the texture of the medium I move through. Sound is the current expressing itself as vibration, the walls reflecting the pressure of my passage, the school producing its own keening — that long, sliding tone that I produce with the modified scales along my dorsal ridge, that vibration of my own body in response to the speed of the water over me.

My voice is not a voice. It is a frequency — a note produced by the interaction of my body and the water, shaped by the geometry of the passage I swim through, reflected and amplified by the Rootwork's hard walls. The same body, swimming at the same speed, produces different sounds in different corridors: a low moan in a wide gallery, a high whistle in a narrow constriction. I do not control this. I do not choose it. I swim, and the sound is what my swimming is, and the sound carries through the water and is heard — *felt* — by every other body in the corridor, and the aggregate of a thousand bodies singing their thousand notes is the chorus.

The chorus is everywhere. It is the middle layer of the Abyss's soundscape — the densest, most complex, most *alive* layer. Below it, the tectonic hum of the Rootwork itself, that deep, sub-audible vibration of a structure so vast that it resonates at a frequency below the perception of any fauna, a sound that is felt not in the body but in the architecture, in the walls, in the substrate itself. Above it, the highest stratum: the trickle of micro-currents through the smallest channels, the tick of crustaceans adjusting their positions, the soft percussion of particulate matter settling through the water.

The chorus fills the space between. It is the sound of life. It is the sound of ten thousand species vocalizing, clicking, stridulating, drumming, keening, moaning, rasping — the aggregate of every living thing in the Abyss expressing itself as vibration in the only medium available, the water, which carries sound with a ferocity that no air can match, which propagates every whisper to every wall, which turns the smallest sound into a message and the largest sound into a landscape.

I am inside the chorus. I am part of it. My keening — that long, sliding tone that bends through the corridor like a living thing — adds to it, contributes to it, is absorbed into it and lost in it the way a single voice is lost in a choir. But the choir does not function without the voice. The chorus is the aggregate of every sound, and my sound is part of the aggregate, and the aggregate is the medium of communication for a world that has no eyes, no light, no visual language — only sound, only vibration, only the ceaseless, layered, deafening, beautiful, monotonous, inexhaustible wall of noise that is the Abyss's answer to the question: *how do we know we are here?*

We hear ourselves. We hear each other. We hear the Rootwork. We hear the pulse.

---

The fifth thing is the pulse.

I do not want to write about the pulse. Not because it frightens me — I do not have the architecture for fright — but because it is the one element of the Abyss that resists the form I have chosen. Everything else — the water, the passage, the school, the chorus — exists in the continuous present, in the long, settled now that defines this world. The pulse exists in the continuous present too, but it *implies* something. It implies depth. It implies interiority. It implies that the Rootwork is not merely a structure but a *body*, and the body has a heart, and the heart is beating, and the beating is not for the fauna and not for the structure and not for the equilibrium but for *itself*, for whatever passes for purpose in a system that solved the problem of existence so completely that it forgot there was ever a problem to solve.

I feel the pulse. Every threader feels it. We swim, and beneath the chorus, beneath the hum, beneath every sound the Abyss produces, there is the pulse: one beat, every forty seconds, slow and irregular and patterned, rising through the substrate of the Rootwork like heat rising through stone. It is in everything. It is in the walls. It is in the water. It is in my body, resonating through my bones, my scales, my lateral line, at a frequency so low that it is not sound but *presence* — the presence of the Rootwork, the presence of the structure that contains me, the presence of the thing that is larger than me and older than me and will outlast me by aeons.

The other threaders do not react to the pulse. I do not react to the pulse. Reaction requires awareness, and awareness requires a self, and a self requires a distinction between *me* and *not-me*, and I do not make that distinction. The pulse is part of the medium. It is part of the water, part of the walls, part of the chorus, part of the world. It is like asking a fish to react to water. The fish does not react. The fish *is*.

But something in me — not a thought, not a word, not even a sensation, but something beneath sensation, something in the structure of my being — registers the pulse as *different*. As *separate*. As the one element of the Abyss that does not fit the pattern of equilibrium, the one vibration that is not the byproduct of metabolism or the echo of movement or the aggregate of a thousand voices, but is *generated*, is *produced*, is *beating* with the slow, deliberate rhythm of something that is alive in a way that I am not alive, something that is conscious in a way that I am not conscious, something that contains the entire Abyss — the Rootwork, the fauna, the chorus, the coolness, the darkness — the way a body contains its cells, the way a thought contains its words.

The fauna avoids the passage that leads to the wall. I avoid it too. Not because I know what is there. Not because I fear it. Because something in me — something deeper than the lateral line, deeper than the electroreception, deeper than any sense organ that evolution produced — something in me knows that the pulse is not for me. It is not a signal to be decoded. It is not a message to be received. It is the heartbeat of something that contains me, and to approach it would be to approach the boundary of my own existence — to reach the place where *being a threader* ends and *being something else* begins, and I do not want to cross that boundary because I do not know what lies on the other side, and not-knowing is the one thing that the Abyss, in all its completeness, has not solved.

---

So I swim.

I swim because swimming is what I am. I swim because the corridor curves, and I follow the curve, and the curve is the Rootwork, and the Rootwork is the world, and the world is water, and water is everything. I swim because the school swims, and I am the school, and the school is the current, and the current is the passage, and the passage is the architecture, and the architecture is the organism, and the organism is the Abyss, and the Abyss is complete.

I swim, and my bioluminescence trails behind me like a cold flame, and the anchored colonies open wider as I pass, and the crustaceans dart into their crevices, and the polyps glow their faint, sourceless glow, and the chorus rises around me — the keening of my kin, the rasp of the anchored, the deep moan of a glider somewhere far above, the click and crackle of a billion tiny bodies going about their billion tiny lives — and the pulse beats beneath it all, slow and patient and inexhaustible, and the Rootwork hums, and the water stays cool, and the darkness holds.

I am a threader. I am the blood. I am the current made flesh.

I am swimming.

I have always been swimming.

I will always be swimming.

And the now — the long, loud, dark, cool, settled now — is full.

It is so, so full.
