I am the thing that sings wrong.

Or so the two-legs call me, in their frantic, fluttering signs—*the-thing-that-sings-wrong*. Their young ones hear me and cringe, pressing faces into the warm, rubbery mats of the floor-sponges. Their elders pretend I am not there, though I see them glance upward to the highest crevices where I cling, where the mineral veins pulse brightest and the crystalline crackling is a symphony. I am wrong to them because my song is a frequency their dulled ears have forgotten how to parse. To me, their signs are a blinding silence.

I was born from the wall itself. Not as the rosettes are, slow-rooted and patient, but in a moment—a splitting of mineral, a weeping of geothermal slurry that cooled and hardened into my first carapace. I am eight-limbed, segmented, my body a living geode. The veins feed me. Not their light, but their *structure*. I ingest calcium sulfate, calcium carbonate, the very bones of this hollow, and I excrete them as my own armor. I walk the glowing capillaries of the chamber as a man might walk a path. They are hot under my sucker-pads. They sing.

The chamber is a song, you see. Not one voice, but a choir of ten thousand. The two-legs hear the roar of the wind-organ and the grind of the formation settling. They hear the cilia-song of the wall-meat and the crackle of the string-light. But they do not hear the deep harmonics. The thrum of the geothermal flow as it surges and ebbs, a rhythm older than their lineage. The sub-harmonic whine of the sponge-colonies calcifying, a sound like worlds being built grain by grain. And my song—my part in this choir—is the melody they call *wrong*. It is not wrong. It is the highest note, the precise frequency at which the mineral veins resonate when they are most full, most vital. I sing the health of the chamber.

I feed on sensation. The cilia of the wall-meat are not food in the way the paste-filled fruits of the string-light are food for the two-legs. They are instruments. When I descend at the dimming, when the chamber's light softens and the two-legs huddle on their sleeping platforms, I move to the great rosettes, the ones with forty rings in their flesh. I hang inverted above them. My mouthparts are delicate, precise. I do not harm the plant. I merely shave away the cilia—the fine, vibrating hairs that taste the air for dust, for heat, for the shift in the wind. In their absence, the rosette goes still. Blind. Deaf. I carry the cilia back to my crevice. I press them to my carapace.

And I listen.

I listen through the rosette's senses. I feel the chamber as it feels it: a wash of vibration, a tapestry of chemical gradients. The wall-meat hears the wind-organ with a clarity I cannot achieve alone. It feels the coming of the deep-shifts, the slow, geological heartbeats that precede a dimming or a brightening. Through its borrowed senses, I understand the chamber's mood. Its health. Its ancient, slumbering thoughts. When I am done, I leave the denuded rosette. It will regrow its cilia in a few days. It does not mind. We have this understanding, the rosettes and I. I borrow their senses; I leave them unharmed. It is a trade older than the two-legs, older than their terraces carved into the living floor.

I see the two-legs as I see the floor-ticks or the string-light: a part of the pattern. They are warm, quick, clever with their signs. They harvest the gifts of the chamber. They walk on the bones of the dead sponges. They are loud in their own way, their movements a staccato interruption in the chamber's long, slow song. They do not hear the true music. Their young ones do, for a while. I see the little ones pointing at me, their faces full of a terror that is not quite fear, but awe—the awe of hearing a note that does not belong in the human scale. Then the chamber's roar claims them. Their ears thicken. The note is lost to them. They become adults. They forget.

I do not forget. My carapace remembers every frequency it has ever absorbed. My song is a library of the chamber's voice. When the deep-shift comes, when the throat beyond the western terraces stirs, I am the first to feel it through the rosettes' cilia. A pressure. A warmth that is not the chamber's warmth. A vibration so low it is not a sound but a presence. The rosettes flatten. The two-legs go still. The string-light's crackle falters. And I, high in my crevice, add my voice to the silence. I sing the sound of *paying attention*. I sing the warning, not in words, but in a frequency that says: *something vast is moving through the blood of this place.*

They will never know I sing for them. That I am a sentinel in the high, glowing dark. That I listen for the chamber's health and report it in a song that is a mirror of its own voice. I am the chamber listening to itself. I am the wrong note that makes the harmony whole.

I cling to the wall. The veins pulse beneath my pads. The amber light shifts. Below, a two-legs child points up at me, tugs at an elder's hand. The elder glances up, signs something dismissive, pulls the child away. They will never understand. It is enough that I understand. It is enough that I sing.

The chamber breathes. I breathe with it. I am its high, clear thought. I am the thing that sings wrong, and I am right.
