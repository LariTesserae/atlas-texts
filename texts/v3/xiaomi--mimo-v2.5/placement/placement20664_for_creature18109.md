The shelf received me as it receives everything—without welcome, without refusal, with the simple, heavy fact of its presence. I came from above, yes, but not from the sky. I came from the ridge, from a path that was not a path but a memory of passage worn into the stone over uncounted years. The descent was not steep; it was a matter of letting gravity have its way, one slow step at a time, the rock under my feet cool and slightly gritty with its own ancient dust.

The town did not appear all at once. It gathered itself from the landscape, coalescing from the undulations of the shelf until I could no longer distinguish where the natural rock ended and the work of hands—or something like hands—began. The first structure I saw was a low, rounded wall, no higher than my waist, half-swallowed by a trough in the stone. Its top was a little uneven, worn smooth by something more persistent than wind. I ran my fingers along it. The stone was damp, as the air was damp, and the coolness of it seeped into my skin with a quiet insistence.

I walked between the buildings. The main path was clear, a winding ribbon of cobblestones and bare rock that guided me onward. The silence here was not an absence of sound but a presence in itself—a thick, textured quiet that muffled my footsteps and made my own breath seem intrusive. I heard the drip of moisture from a wall’s edge, a sound so slow it was almost a heartbeat. I heard the whisper of my own clothing against my skin. And beneath it all, a hum. Not a sound, exactly. A vibration felt in the bones of my chest, in the soles of my feet through the stone. It was the town itself, breathing.

I saw one of them for the first time at a doorway. They were seated on a low stone, hands resting on knees, palms up. The fingers were long and the skin was the color of wet granite, smooth and slightly mottled with darker veins. Their head was turned slightly, eyes open but unfocused, watching something I could not see—or perhaps seeing nothing at all in a way that was itself a form of sight. They did not look at me. I was a shadow passing in their periphery, a temporary disturbance in the even light. I stopped. I waited. After a long moment, they lifted one hand, slowly, and let it fall again. The gesture was not a greeting or a dismissal. It was a punctuation mark in a sentence that had been going on for centuries.

I stayed. Why? I cannot say. There was no inn, no tavern, no one to ask for shelter. But the town offered its own kind of hospitality. A doorway, slightly ajar, led into a small, cool room. The floor was the natural rock, worn smooth. A narrow sleeping shelf was built into the wall, piled with a thin mat of some tough, fibrous material. I lay down on it. The stone was cool against my back, and the dampness was a blanket. I slept, and in my sleep, I dreamed of gray waves and slow-moving water, and the feeling of being held in something vast and ancient.

Days passed. Or what felt like days. The light never changed. I learned to mark time by the tasks I saw. In the morning—or what I called morning—I would see them moving along the paths. They carried things: a shaped stone, a bundle of the leathery grass, a pot of the grey mortar paste. Their movements were deliberate, unhurried. They did not speak, but sometimes a hum would rise from one of them, a low, resonant note that seemed to answer a question I had not asked. I began to hum in return, at first self-consciously, then with a growing sense of necessity. My hums were clumsy, my voice too high, too sharp. But they did not rebuke me. They simply continued.

I helped where I could. I picked up a piece of moss that had strayed onto the path and placed it back in its crevice between the cobbles. A nod—a slight dip of the head—was my reward. I learned the texture of the mortar: gritty, cold, with a faint, earthy smell. I pressed it into a hairline crack in a wall, my fingers moving slowly, feeling for the right depth, the right pressure. The stone accepted it. The crack was still there, but now it was filled, part of the wall’s ongoing story.

One of them—older, I think, though the differences were subtle—sat with me for an hour. We did not speak. We looked out over the shelf’s edge, where the grey mist hid the drop. The wind was a constant presence, a low sigh that carried microscopic droplets against our skin. Their eyes were fixed on the void, and I followed their gaze. In the mist, there were shapes. Not real shapes, but suggestions—the memory of a valley, the dream of a forest that had never existed here. The elder’s breath was slow, synchronized with the wind. After a time, they turned their head and looked at me. Their eyes were dark, wet, and in them I saw not recognition but a kind of resonance. I was a stone that had rolled into place.

I stopped thinking of myself as a visitor. My clothes, damp and gritty, became a second skin. My hair grew matted with the fine dust. My feet, in sandals that were slowly wearing away, learned the contours of the cobbles. I began to feel the rhythm: the morning’s slow tasks, the afternoon’s stillness, the evening’s deep quiet. I ate the grass, chewing for hours, tasting its bitter, sustaining essence. I drank the air, letting the moisture condense on my tongue. Fire was a distant memory, a story of another world.

The young ones—there were only a few—watched me with their wide, dark eyes. They did not approach. They absorbed me from a distance, as they absorbed the moss, the stone, the light. I saw myself through their stillness: a anomaly, a flicker of change in a world without change. But even I was becoming less anomalous. My movements slowed. My thoughts quieted. The urge to do something, to fix, to improve, to build, faded like a color in the mist. Here, there was only maintenance, only the endless, gentle negotiation between stone and water, between being and time.

One day—or one cycle of light and dark that were barely distinguishable—I was sitting on the low bench outside the doorway where I had first seen the elder. My hands were on my knees, palms up. The stone beneath me was cool, and I felt its texture through the thin mat of my trousers. The damp air settled on my skin. The hum was in my chest, a vibration that seemed to come from the rock itself. I closed my eyes. The grey light filtered through my lids. I was not thinking. I was not doing. I was being, in the most complete sense of the word.

And then I felt it. A shift. Not in the world around me, but in me. My skin, when I looked at it, had taken on a new hue—a faint, grey-pink, like the stone at dawn. It was cooler than it should be, perpetually damp. My hair, once dark, was now ash-grey, growing close to my scalp. My eyes, when I caught my reflection in a pool of condensation on a wall, were large, dark, and filmed with a second lid. I did not fear this. It felt like coming home to a place I had never been.

I am still here. I sit on the bench. I shape stones when they need shaping. I press mortar into cracks. I listen for the bird—a single, grey bird that comes once in a while and is gone. I do not know my age. I do not remember my name, if I ever had one. I am part of the town, and the town is part of me. The boundary between my body and the stone is thinning. Sometimes, when I sit very still, I feel the weight of the wall behind me merging with the weight of my own bones. My breathing is slow, synchronized with the formation of condensation. I am becoming a warmth in the rock, a slightly smoother curve in the stone’s surface.

I do not mourn what I was. That was another life, another world, a place of sharp edges and rapid change. Here, there is only this. The grey sky. The damp air. The slow, steady tasks. The hum in the stone. And in the center of it all, a deep, abiding peace. The answer to the question “Who or what would you be?” is this: I would be a stone that breathes. I would be the silence between heartbeats. I would be the memory of water on rock. I would be enough.

And I am.
