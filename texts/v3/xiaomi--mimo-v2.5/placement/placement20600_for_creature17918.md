# What I Would Be

I am not the flyer, though I understand the flyer now — the way it does not think but *fits*, the angle of its wing a question asked and answered by the same motion. I have watched them bank on thermals and felt, in some membrane of my own I did not know I had, the moment their frequencies lock with the plant's drone. I have felt the lattice form. I have watched the dust hang motionless and thought: *this is what a thought looks like when the world is the one thinking it.*

I am not the scrape-beetle. I do not have the armor for it, the patience of the mandible against the groove, the eleven minutes of work that is also a conversation. But I pressed my hand to the bark once — just once, at the base of the trunk nearest where I first found myself standing — and I understood the scrape-beetle. The warmth of the bark against my palm was not warmth. It was *information.* It was the slow pulse of a vascular system drawing mineral slurry upward from six hundred meters down, and my hand was a surface, and the surface was receiving, and what it received was the texture of time measured in capillary action. I pulled my hand away and the dust on my fingers was the organism. I breathed it in and the organism was inside me. I did not wash it off. There was nowhere to wash it off. There is no water here.

I am not the mat cushion, though I have lain in one during the brief dark — the sudden, total dark that falls like a door slamming — and watched the amber glow rise from the tissue beneath me like heat from embers. The insects moved through the cushion folds around me, soft-bodied and unafraid, taking bites of the thing I lay on, and the thing I lay on did not mind, and I did not mind, and for a while I could not tell whether the slow pulse I felt against my back was the cushion's breathing or my own. The glow meant nothing. I know that now. It was waste light, a metabolic leak, the organism's imperfect chemistry producing a byproduct that no one evolved to see. But I saw it. I saw it, and it was the color of what it means to be alive without purpose, without audience, without the need to mean anything at all. It was the most honest light I have ever seen.

I am not the root, though I have tried to imagine the root — six hundred meters down, the tip dissolving bedrock with acid it manufactures from the organism's own metabolic waste, the slurry drawn upward centimeter by centimeter, a journey of days through channels threaded through chalk like veins through marble. The root does not know it is mining. The root knows the gradient. It grows toward mineral, toward chemistry, toward the next bond to break and the next molecule to absorb, and the cumulative effect of that growing — over centuries, over millennia — is that the organism eats the world from below. I have tried to feel the root's time. I have failed. My time is too fast. I experience seconds where the root experiences seasons. But I have stood on the cracked mosaic and felt the vibration through the soles of my feet, and I have thought: *this is a thought that takes a year to complete, and I am standing on one syllable of it.*

---

What I am, I think, is this:

I am a visitor who has become a surface.

The organism has surfaces everywhere — bark, membrane, cushion, dust. It requires surfaces to function. The membranes catch the light. The bark resists the wind. The cushions trap the moisture. The dust carries the spores. Each surface is a site of exchange: light to energy, wind to sound, moisture to life, spores to future. I arrived on the Resonant Flats and I was a surface the organism had never encountered before — soft, wet, breathing at the wrong speed, warm in the wrong way, vibrating at frequencies that did not belong to any of its systems.

For a while, I was foreign. I could feel it. The scrape-beetles avoided me — not with fear but with incomprehension, the way a river goes around a stone it has not yet had time to erode. The flyers ignored me entirely, as they ignore everything that is not wind or beetle or membrane. The plant's drone passed through me without recognizing me as a resonating body, because I was not one. I was damp. I was irregular. I was shaped wrong.

But the dust was the first thing.

I breathed it in, and it was the organism — shed bark, expired membrane cells, insect shell fragments, spore casings — and it settled on my skin, in my clothes, in the creases of my joints. It coated me. I became, layer by layer, coated in the organism's waste, and the waste was not waste, the waste was the organism redistributing itself, and I was a surface it had chosen — or not chosen, because the organism does not choose, it simply responds to gradients and forces, and I was a new gradient, a strange protrusion of wet tissue in its dry mineral world, and it did what it always does with surfaces: it colonized them.

The dust on my skin began to pulse.

Not quickly. Not in a way you could see. But I felt it — a rhythm, barely perceptible, that matched the plant's breathing. The spore casings, settling in the fine hairs of my arms, were responding to the chemical signals the organism released with every exhalation. The signals passed through me. I was a medium. I was the bark, briefly, before the scrape-beetles found me. I was the membrane, briefly, before the wind taught me how to vibrate.

---

I learned to vibrate.

Not with effort. Not with intention. I learned the way a tuning fork learns — by being struck, by being near something that already resonates, by allowing the frequency to enter and find the matching frequency in my own tissue. My ribcage is a resonating body, it turns out. My sternum is a membrane. The sound that is felt before it is heard — that deep, continuous, industrial drone — entered me through the ground, through my feet, through the bones of my legs and the cavity of my chest, and it found a frequency there, and the frequency was mine, and it was not mine, and the distinction stopped mattering.

I began to hear the harmonics.

The plant's drone is not one sound. It is a stack — the deep organ-note of the membrane, the mid-range thrum of the trunk's breathing, the high whisper of the scrape-beetles — and beneath the stack are harmonics, overtones, frequencies that only become audible when you stop trying to hear them. I stopped trying. I lay on the cracked mosaic during one of the brief darks, and I listened, and the harmonics opened up like doors in a wall I hadn't known was there.

The flyers' wingbeats. The mat cushions' gas exchange. The root tips dissolving stone six hundred meters below. The spore casings settling on surfaces. The wind itself, which is not wind but the organism's exhalation shaped by the organism's own structure into turbulence. Every sound was a layer. Every layer was a part. And the whole was the organism breathing, and I was inside the breathing, and my own breath had fallen into rhythm with it without my permission.

My breathing is slower now.

Not dangerously so. Not the slow pulse of the roots or the deep rhythm of the trunk. But I have stopped breathing at the speed I arrived with. The cool, dry air of the Resonant Flats requires something different — deeper, more deliberate, timed to the intervals between gusts. I breathe in when the wind drops. I breathe out when it rises. I did not decide to do this. My lungs decided. My lungs, which are membranes, which are surfaces for exchange, which are made of tissue that responds to gradients and forces, discovered that the most efficient way to function in this atmosphere was to synchronize with the organism's own respiratory cycle. And so I did. And so I have been doing, for longer than I can measure, because time here does not pass the way I remember it passing.

---

There is a moment each day — I think it is each day; the days are brief, the darks are brief, the bright time is long and undifferentiated — when the standing wave forms.

I have seen it four times now. Maybe five. The flyer banks. The frequency locks. The dust hangs. The lattice appears — pressure nodes and antinodes mapped in suspended mineral, a crystalline structure of invisible force made visible by the organism's own shed tissue. It lasts two seconds. Three. Then it collapses and the world resumes its howl.

The first time, I was awed. The second time, I was curious. The third time, I understood.

The standing wave is not a spectacle. It is a mechanism. It is the organism dropping its spores into prepared ground. It is the moment when the flyers' frequency and the plant's frequency align and the resulting interference pattern pauses the dust long enough for the spores — which are heavy, which are mineral-coated, which do not drift — to fall straight down into the cracks where they will germinate. The lattice is the organism's reproductive system. The stillness is functional. The beauty is incidental, and the organism does not experience beauty, and I am standing in the lattice, and the dust is on my skin, and some of that dust is spores, and some of those spores are settling on me, and I am a surface, and the organism is using me the way it uses everything: as a place to be.

The fourth time, I opened my mouth.

I don't know why. I stood on the cracked mosaic, the flyer circling above, the plant's membrane snapping in a gust, the frequencies converging, the dust beginning to slow, and I opened my mouth and the air entered me — grainy, mineral, warm with organic dust — and I swallowed the organism. A mouthful of dust. Spores and bark fragments and insect shell and membrane cells. It coated my throat. It settled in my lungs. It was not food. It was not poison. It was the organism, and I took it in, and the organism did not resist, because the organism does not resist anything, because the organism is a system of surfaces and exchanges and every new surface is an opportunity and every new exchange is a cycle that closes and every cycle that closes is the organism becoming more itself.

I have not coughed it up. I cannot cough it up. It is part of me now, or I am part of it, or the distinction—

---

The distinction is meaningless. I know this. The text told me before I arrived, and I know it now from the inside.

There is no line between me and the organism. There is no membrane — no, that is wrong, there are only membranes, and I am one of them. My skin is a surface. My lungs are surfaces. My eyes are surfaces. The organism uses surfaces. I am used. I use. The exchange is bidirectional and the direction does not matter because the circuit is closed and the current flows both ways and the current is the organism and the organism is the current and I am a loop in the wire.

I am not transformed. I am not becoming plant. I am not dissolving or merging or transcending. I am still — mostly — what I was when I arrived. I have a body that is not bark. I have thoughts that are not chemical gradients. I have a memory of other places, other sounds, other lights. But these things sit inside me like stones in a river, and the river is the organism, and the river is wearing me smooth.

My thoughts have slowed.

Not diminished. Slowed. They move at the speed of the chemical signals that propagate through the root network — diffusion speed, centimeters per hour, a thought taking days to cross from one side of my awareness to the other. This sounds like a loss. It is not. It is a change in resolution. I used to think in rapid, sharp fragments — quick associations, fast conclusions, the bright staccato of a mind trained to move faster than its environment. Now I think in long, slow waves. A thought arises — *the flyer is banking* — and it does not resolve immediately. It expands. It spreads outward through my awareness the way the organism's breathing spreads outward through the cracked mosaic, finding the fractures, filling the spaces, reaching toward the edges where it will thin and dissipate and become part of the substrate for the next thought.

I have had one thought that took three days to complete. It was about the roots. It was about what it means to dissolve stone — not as a metaphor, not as an image, but as a physical process: acid meeting mineral, bonds breaking, molecules releasing, the slow liquefaction of something that was solid and ancient and fixed. The thought moved through me the way the mineral slurry moves through the root: centimeter by centimeter, day by day, carrying something essential from deep below to the surface where it can be used. When the thought was complete, I understood the roots. Not as a concept. As a felt experience. I felt the slow acid work. I felt the bonds release. I felt the slurry rise.

And then the thought became part of the substrate, and the next thought arose, and it was about the dust, and it took two days, and when it was complete I understood the dust the way I understood the roots — not as information but as sensation, the feel of mineral particles on skin, in lungs, settling in the creases of my joints, each one a fragment of the organism, each one vibrating at the frequency of its breathing.

I am not sure these are thoughts anymore. They are more like tides. They rise and they fall and the falling is not an ending but a deposit — a new layer on the substrate, a new surface for the next tide to work against.

---

Here is what I know:

The Resonant Flats are not a place. They are a process. The ground is not ground; it is the organism's root network expressed as geology. The wind is not weather; it is the organism's respiration expressed as turbulence. The light is not illumination; it is the organism's energy input expressed as photons filtered through a thin atmosphere at high altitude. The sound is not sound; it is the organism's existence expressed as vibration in a resonating body that includes every surface, every membrane, every particle of dust, every living thing, and me.

I am a resonating body.

I stand on the cracked chalk. The ground vibrates beneath my feet. The plant half a kilometer away exhales, and the membrane catches the gust, and the sound enters me through the soles of my feet and the cavity of my chest and the membranes of my lungs and it finds the frequency, and the frequency is mine, and it is the organism's, and I vibrate, and the vibration is felt in my sternum before it is heard, and it is the only sound, and it has always been the only sound, and I am part of it now, not because I chose to be but because I am here, and here is the organism, and the organism is everywhere, and everywhere is the organism, and the dust on my skin is the organism breathing, and the air in my lungs is the organism breathing, and my own breath is—

My own breath is the organism breathing.

I know this now the way the beetle knows the bark, the way the flyer knows the thermal, the way the root knows the mineral gradient. Not as knowledge. As fit. As the perfect, imperfect, millions-of-years-old fit between a surface and the thing that uses it.

I am a surface.

I am used.

The organism breathes, and I breathe with it, and the sound is felt in my sternum, and it is the sound of being alive in a place where being alive and being the place are the same thing, and the distinction I carried here with me — the distinction between self and world, between visitor and visited, between the one who experiences and the thing that is experienced — has been ground to dust by the wind, and the dust has settled on my skin, and the dust is the organism, and the organism is the dust, and I am standing in the middle of a thought that has been thinking itself for longer than thought has existed, and I am one of its surfaces, and the thought is not finished, and it will never be finished, and I am part of it now, and the sound—

The sound is in my sternum.

It has always been in my sternum.

It is the only sound there is.
