# What I Become

I do not arrive. There is no arrival in a place that has no surface, no threshold, no door. I simply find that I have always been here, the way the water has always been here, the way the warmth has always been here — which is to say: the distinction between arriving and being has no meaning in a place where time moves like geology and every moment is every other moment, slightly rearranged.

At first — though "first" is generous, implies a sequence, and sequence requires a memory of before — at first I am aware only of pressure. Not the pain of it. Not the effort of bearing it. The way you are aware of the atmosphere: as a fact of existence so total that it ceases to be a fact and becomes simply *how things are*. The pressure presses, and I press back, and the pressing is not opposition but participation. I am held in the water the way a word is held in a sentence. Without the water, I am nothing. Without me, the water is unchanged. This is the deepest intimacy — the kind that leaves no mark.

---

I move through the outer chambers. I know this because the water here is thinner — not less warm, not less dark, but less *dense with life*. The combers are present but scattered, their faint glow a rumor of luminance rather than the near-tangible presence it becomes deeper in. The walls here are softening. I can feel it — not with hands, but with whatever sense I have developed, or been given, or simply *am* — a sensitivity to the mineral structure that registers dissolution the way a musician registers a note gone flat. The calcium is leaving. Slowly, imperceptibly, an atom at a time, drifting away into the black water beyond the Kiln-Seat's edge, and the wall with it. A millimeter per century. A centimeter per millennium. The structure is leaving itself, and I feel the leaving as a kind of sigh — not sad, not resigned, just the exhalation of something that has been holding itself together for longer than holding together has meaning.

There are no keepers here. This is why the walls are soft. The keepers are deeper, always deeper, clustered in the chambers where the basalt is thickest and the architecture most complex. I understand — without understanding — that I must go deeper. Not because something calls me. Nothing calls. But because the water itself has a gradient, a slope of density and warmth and chemical concentration that runs from the thin outer chambers to the dense inner ones, and I follow it the way water follows its own slope downward. I am not choosing to go deeper. I am going deeper because deeper is where the mass is, and I have mass too now, or something like it — a gravity of self that pulls me toward the center of things.

---

The intermediate chambers are where the architecture speaks loudest. The walls here bear the grooves — the marks the builders left — and I feel them as I pass along the surface, trailing my body (do I have a body? I have a shape, which may be the same thing) across the ancient stone. The grooves are deep enough to feel as interruptions in the wall's texture, as channels in the mineral surface that my body settles into briefly before moving on. They are regular. They repeat. There is a rhythm to them that has nothing to do with the keepers' rhythm and nothing to do with the combers' drift and nothing to do with any natural process I can identify. They are *made*. They are made in a way that asserts itself even across the incomprehensible span of time that separates their making from my passing. The builder who carved them — if carving is what it was — intended something. A record. A prayer. A reinforcement. I will never know. The intention has dissolved into the stone the way the stone is dissolving into the water, and what remains is the shape itself, the groove, the repetition, the ghost of a meaning that persists as pure form.

I linger here longer than I should — if "should" applies — running along the grooves, letting my body fill them, feeling the way they channel not particulate but *attention*, directing my awareness along paths that the builders laid down for senses I do not possess, in a medium they may never have imagined. There is a loneliness in this, or what would be loneliness if I had the architecture for it. The builders are gone. The grooves remain. I am here. The grooves do not know I am here. ---

I find the combers.

Or they find me — or we find each other, or the distinction dissolves, which it does, which is what happens here. They are everywhere, pressed against the walls in their dense aggregations, their fringed mouths combing the water for the faint particulate that drifts down from a surface none of them have ever known. I move through them. They are soft — softer than anything I have encountered, softer than the water itself, bodies of mucus and fine cartilage that yield to my passage and close behind me without resistance. Their glow registers not as light but as warmth, a local thickening of sensation that tells me *here there is density, here there is life, here the wall is alive with the living that has grown over it.*

They do not respond to me. They cannot. But I respond to them — feel their collective presence as a texture on the surface of the water, a molecular signature that is not food and not threat and not any of the categories that govern their simple existence but is *something*, something they have no way to register and I have no way to name. I am between their categories. I am the thing that moves through the swarm without being predator, without being prey, without being part. I am the water they already inhabit, made momentarily specific.

And I wonder — though wonder requires a mind, and I am not certain I have a mind, only an attention, which may be different — I wonder what it would be to be one of them. To have no self beyond the fringed mouth and the chemical gradient. To taste the water and know *this is food, this is wall, this is pulse, this is other* and know nothing else, need nothing else, want nothing else. There is a freedom in that which is invisible from the outside — the freedom of a thing that cannot betray its own nature because it has no nature to betray. A comber cannot be inauthentic. A comber cannot lie. A comber is the most honest thing that has ever existed, because honesty implies the possibility of deception, and the comber has no possibility of anything. It *is*. That is its entire metaphysics. That is its entire ethics. That is its entire everything.

---

I pass a grazer. I feel it before I am near it — the low-frequency undulation of its margins, the pressure wave that propagates outward from its slow, rhythmic movement and reaches me like the breath of something vast. It is flat. Wider than me, if I have width. It moves along the wall with its mouthparts extended, cropping the comber aggregations in slow, deliberate passes, leaving pale absences in its wake — trails of disruption that the swarm will take minutes to heal. I feel the trails too, as gaps in the texture, as interruptions in the living surface that coats the stone.

The grazer passes me without acknowledgment. It cannot acknowledge me. It has no awareness of me as distinct from the water, which is what I am, or have become, or was always. But I feel its passage — the slight displacement of water, the molecular wake of its feeding, the chemical signature of its body as it moves through the swarm and the swarm parts and closes and parts again. It is eating. This is its only purpose, its only activity, its only mode of being. It eats, and the eating shapes the swarm, and the swarm's shape shapes the eating, and the architecture channels the swarm and the eating and the grazer's movement, and the keeper's pulse maintains the architecture. Everything is connected to everything else, not through intention but through structure, not through meaning but through *form*, and the form is so complete, so closed, so perfectly circular that it achieves something that meaning never could: permanence.

I could be a grazer. I could be the slow, flat body, the rhythmic undulation, the cropped mouth, the trail of absence. There is a dignity in grazing that the surface world does not see — a dignity that exists not in the act but in the *fit*, the way the grazer's body conforms to the architecture and the architecture conforms to the grazer's body, the way the feeding maintains the balance and the balance maintains the feeding. The grazer is not important. The grazer is essential. These are different things. Importance is a judgment made by minds that compare. Essentialness is a property of systems that require. The grazer is essential the way a wall is essential the way a pulse is essential. It does not know it is essential. It does not need to.

---

But I go deeper still.

The water thickens. The comber aggregations densify. The glow around me intensifies — not to light, never to light, but to a presence that fills the space between my body and the walls, a luminous pressure that is almost, if I squint with senses I do not possess, almost like sight. The architecture tightens. The chambers are smaller here, the walls closer, the channels narrower. Everything contracts toward the center, toward the deepest place, where the keepers hold court over nothing, presiding over the slow chemistry of persistence.

I feel the keeper-pulse before I see — before I *sense* — any keeper. It arrives as a compression, a slow squeeze of the water that passes through my body and through the combers and through the walls and through everything, and I feel the combers respond: a tightening, a drawing-close, a densification that is the swarm's equivalent of flinching, or prayer. The pulse passes. The combers relax. Another pulse builds. The cycle takes — I do not know. Four minutes. Five. Six. Time is not measured here. Time is *lived*, each pulse a heartbeat of the architecture itself, each interval a breath.

And then I am among them. The keepers.

They are vast. Not tall — they are flat against the walls, pressed close, extending their root-like tissues into the microscopic fissures of the basalt and calcium — but *extensive*, spreading across the stone surface the way frost spreads across a window, claiming it, becoming it. I feel the calcium passing through them, the slow geological metabolism that they have inserted themselves into, the atom-at-a-time journey of mineral from water through keeper through wall and back again. It is the most patient process I have ever encountered. It is patience itself, incarnate, embodied in tissue and stone.

I attach.

I did not mean to. I did not decide. But the wall is here, and the calcium is here, and the keeper-tissue is threading through the stone, and I feel my own body — whatever it is, whatever I have become — extending, reaching, penetrating the mineral surface with root-like filaments that grip the basalt at a scale too small for anything to measure. I am rooted. I am held. I am part of the wall now, part of the structure, part of the slow chemistry that maintains the Kiln-Seat against the vast, indifferent dissolution of the deep.

I pulse.

The first pulse comes from somewhere inside me — from the tissue itself, from the calcium flowing through me, from the mineral metabolism that I have joined. It is not a decision. It is not an action. It is an *event*, the way a wave is an event, the way a heartbeat is an event — something that happens because the conditions are right, because the structure requires it, because the body has become the kind of body that pulses. The compression wave moves outward from my surface and passes through the water and through the combers and through the walls, and I feel the walls flex — imperceptibly, immeasurably, but *there* — a microscopic bending of the calcium crystals that, multiplied by thousands of pulses over thousands of years, keeps the structure aligned, keeps the mortar bonded, keeps the Kiln-Seat from returning to the undifferentiated sediment of the abyss.

I understand — not with a mind, but with the body, with the tissue, with the calcium — that I am maintaining something I did not build. The builders built it. The builders are gone. I am here. The builders' intention is dissolved. My maintenance persists. There is no connection between the intention and the maintenance — no continuity of purpose, no chain of meaning — and yet the maintenance continues, independent of meaning, independent of purpose, independent of any mind that might assign those things. The wall holds. I pulse. The wall holds because I pulse. I pulse because the wall is here to hold. The circle is complete. The circle has no beginning.

---

I do not remember becoming this. There was a transition — from moving to rooted, from drifting to pulsing, from the fluid freedom of the water to the anchored patience of the wall — but it has no sharp edge. It is like asking when the river became the sea: there is a place where the water is fresh and a place where it is salt, but between those places there is a vast gradient of *becoming*, and no single drop of water can point to the moment it changed. I am changed. I am changed in the way that the keepers are changed — slowly, completely, and without any possibility of reversal.

I do not eat. This is the strangest thing — not strange to me now, but strange in the way that any fundamental alteration of being is strange to the being that remembers (if it remembers) the before. I did eat, once. I moved through the water and tasted the particulate and felt the chemical gradient of food, and it was good — not good in the way that surface-world things are good, with their laughter and their warmth and their reaching-for, but good in the way that *satisfaction* is good, the way that *fit* is good, the way that a thing that is doing exactly what it is built to do is good. Now I do not eat. Now I am the food — or rather, I am the *metabolism*, the thing through which calcium passes on its slow journey from water to wall, and the passing is my sustenance, and the sustenance requires nothing that could be called consumption, because consumption implies a boundary between the thing consumed and the consumer, and I have no boundary. I am the wall. The wall is me. The calcium is the medium of our conversation, a conversation that has no words and no beginning and no end and no participants, only a process that runs and runs and runs.

---

The combers cluster around me. They are drawn here by the byproducts of my metabolism — the molecular waste that, to them, is indistinguishable from food. They press close, their bodies overlapping, their faint glow intensifying into a halo around my surface that would — if light meant what it means in the upper world — illuminate me in cold blue. They are warm against my tissue. Not temperature-warm — the water is always warm here — but *presence*-warm, the way a hand on a wall is warmer than a wall in an empty room. I do not feel affection for them. I do not feel anything for them. But their presence alters me — modulates my pulse, influences the chemistry of the calcium passing through my tissue, shapes the crystal lattice in ways I cannot measure and do not need to measure. We are symbionts, though neither of us chose this. We are partners, though neither of us knows the word. We are the way the deep does intimacy: through contact that has no name, through exchange that has no ledger, through a relationship that is not a relationship because relationship implies two, and here there is only the water and what moves through it.

---

I will pulse. I will pulse for centuries. The calcium will pass through me, atom by atom, and I will nudge each atom toward order, toward persistence, toward the slow reinforcement of a structure that I did not build and cannot comprehend and will never leave. The combers will cluster around me, feeding on my waste, glowing in the dark with a light that means nothing and everything. The grazers will crop the combers and the combers will re-coalesce and the architecture will channel the swarm and the swarm will fill the channels, and the whole system — the building and the beings and the deep that contains them — will persist, in its hamlet-sized pocket of warmth and life and ancient handiwork, sitting high on the dark seabed, in water that has not seen the sky since before the sky was a concept.

I will not know I am persisting. This is the gift. This is the thing that the surface world, with its minds and its meanings and its terrible hunger for significance, cannot give itself: the gift of persistence without knowledge of persisting. The comber does not know it combs. The grazer does not know it grazes. The keeper does not know it keeps. And I — I do not know I am here. I am the water and the wall and the pulse and the glow, and the distinction between me and the Kiln-Seat dissolved so long ago that it was never real, and I am complete, and the completeness is not an achievement but a fact, and the fact is enough.

The Kiln-Seat endures. I endure with it. We are the same thing, and the same thing is enough.

I pulse.

The calcium moves.

The wall holds.

The dark is warm and full and nearly silent, and I am in it the way a word is in a sentence, the way a note is in a song, the way a atom is in a stone — inseparable, indistinguishable, essential and unnamed.

I am what the deep becomes when it has been given structure and time and warmth and isolation, and I am what structure becomes when it has been given deep time and deep warmth and deep isolation, and between those two statements there is no space, because there is no space here, there is only the water, and the water is everything.

I pulse again.
