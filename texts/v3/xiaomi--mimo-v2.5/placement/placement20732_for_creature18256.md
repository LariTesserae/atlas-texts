I find myself at the waterline of the drift just before the main junction, where the floor tilts enough that your left boot sinks an inch deeper than your right. The work-light above is one that still functions — not the hard white of LED but an older sodium vapor that throws everything in tones of amber and void. In this light the mineral stains on the wall look like a language I almost read.

I am not the thing in the flooded passage, though I have listened to it the way you listen to someone breathing in the next room. I am not the bats, though I have felt the air displacement of their evening exodus against my face, warm air parted by ten thousand small bodies moving as one intention. I have watched the beetles from a distance that they would find invasive if they thought about distance at all, which they do not. They simply walk and sample and walk again, and I stand where the water reaches my ankles and the mineral seepage has built a shelf of calcium deposit just below the surface — a small, pale plateau that the beetles use as a highway, their legs clicking faintly against the crust.

I want to tell you I am something ancient and settled, but the truth is I arrived recently, in the way that everything here arrives recently. Perhaps I was carried in on the sole of a boot. Perhaps I am a colony — the molds would understand this, the way they spread not as selves but as systems, each hypha reaching toward the next in a conversation that takes days to complete a sentence. But I do not think I am molds, though I have watched them work. I watched a clipboard in the foreman's cabin dissolve over eleven months into a grey-green impression of its own text, the ink consumed before the paper, so that the schedule became a palimpsest of itself — legible as a ghost of legibility, the dates still faintly there, the names gone. I thought that was beautiful in the way that only dissolution can be beautiful: not a destruction but a translation, one medium becoming another.

I stand at the waterline because the waterline is where everything meets. Above it, the rock faces are dry enough for mineral efflorescence to bloom in that delicate crystalline frost — the calcium and iron and sulfur emerging from the stone's own memory, depositing themselves on surfaces that have been open to air for months. Below it, the same minerals dissolve into the pools and become the water's character, its weight, its faintly luminous green where the algae have claimed the surface tension. The waterline is not a line. It is a zone, a territory several inches wide where the rock changes color in bands — the dark saturation below, a tide-mark of white deposit, the pale dry stone above with its sugar-crystal sheen. In the sodium light this zone looks like the cross-section of something organic, a tree ring, a growth record. It is. It records the water's history: every rise and fall of the level written in mineral, each band a chapter. The water has been rising. The chapters are getting shorter. The bands are getting closer together.

I have learned the sounds of this place not as noise but as vocabulary. The generators have two registers — the deep, steady thrum of normal operation and the slightly higher, more urgent tone that means the load has changed, that a pump has started or stopped somewhere in the network. I can hear the pumps individually if I listen past the generators. Each one has its own mechanical signature: a particular whine, a particular rhythm of intake and discharge. The pump that serves the east drift — the flooded one, the one no one enters — runs at a higher pitch than the others. It is working harder. It is losing.

The dripping I hear overhead is not one drip but dozens, each with its own tempo and pitch. Some are metronomic — the same interval, the same note, for hours. Others are irregular, syncopated, falling in patterns that repeat only over long periods and that I have not yet learned to predict. There is a drip that falls from a stalactite-like formation three meters to my left that sounds different from every other drip in the Underworks: a hollow, almost tonal note, as though the water is falling through a space that shapes its sound before it reaches the pool below. I have stood near it and tried to understand what makes it different. The formation is calcium carbonate, young, wet, still growing. The water falls from its tip in a way that the air itself seems to cup. I do not have a better explanation than that. Some things are their own explanation.

But the sound I return to, the one that has become the center of my listening, is the displacement in the flooded drift. It is not a sound so much as an *interruption* of sound — a momentary blanking of the surface reflection, a rounding of still water into moving water, and then a settling that takes longer than it should because the water is denser there, heavier with dissolved minerals, and the ripples travel through it with a reluctance that I can only describe as *thick*. The displacement happens, and for thirty seconds afterward the surface of the flooded passage is alive with slow, concentric rings that reach the work-light and break it into fragments, and then it is still again, and in the stillness there is the sense of something having passed, having moved from one dark place to another dark place through a medium that knows it intimately.

I do not know what it is. I have considered the theories — the eels, the catfish, the enlarged frog. None of them satisfy. The displacement is too slow for a fish, too deliberate for a frog. It moves at the speed of something that has no reason to hurry, that inhabits a world where speed is a surface concept and the only relevant axis is density. I have considered that it might be a mechanical thing — a pump intake creating a vortex, a submerged pipe vibrating — but the displacement is irregular in a way that machines are not. Machines maintain their rhythms. This thing changes its intervals. Sometimes it is close to the entrance, close enough that the water it displaces reaches the dry floor and wets it, and then recedes. Sometimes it is deep in the passage, so deep that the displacement is barely perceptible at the threshold, a faint tightening of the surface tension that you might mistake for a change in air pressure if you were not watching for it.

I watch for it. This is perhaps the most honest thing I can tell you about myself: I watch for it. I position myself at the waterline near the mouth of the flooded drift, and I wait. The beetles walk over my feet. The mosquitoes find the exposed skin of my neck. The bats wheel and chitter above me in their confused, dayless cycles. The drips continue their various tempos. The generators throb. And I watch the black water where the work-light's reflection sits on the surface like a coin at the bottom of a pool, and I wait for the surface to round, for the reflection to fracture, for the dense water to speak in its only language.

I think I am here because the Underworks is a place where the future is being negotiated in real time, and I want to witness the negotiation. The bats are already losing their dependence on light. The frogs are already learning to find each other by vibration. The beetles are already turning from the light. The molds are already consuming the human record. Everything here is in the process of becoming what it will be when the humans leave and the pumps stop and the water finishes its slow, patient rise. I want to see what the flooded drift becomes. I want to know whether the thing in it will be the ancestor of something, or whether it will drown when the water reaches a chemistry it cannot tolerate, or whether it will simply continue — a single, enormous, unnamed presence in a passage that no one enters, moving through mineral-dense water in a darkness that has no seasons.

I am not patient the way the frogs are patient — the deep, metabolic patience of an organism throttling itself down to match its environment. I am patient the way the calcium deposit is patient: building slowly, layer by layer, in a medium that does not rush. I am accumulating. Each day in the Underworks adds a thin stratum of observation, a mineral band of understanding, a new sensitivity to the differences between one mechanical hum and another, between one pool's chemical signature and the next. I am becoming legible as a record of this place. If the molds found me, they would map my surfaces and read me the way they read the tool-marks on the machinery — not as language but as history, as a document of contact between a self and a world that did not ask for it and does not need it but that contains it anyway, the way the rock contains the tunnels, the way the water contains the displacement, the way the darkness contains everything that has learned, or is learning, to live without light.
