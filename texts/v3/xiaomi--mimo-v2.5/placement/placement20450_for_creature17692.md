I am a corridor.

Not a person in a corridor. A corridor.

I have been a corridor for longer than the engineers have been engineers, longer than the turbines have been spinning, longer than the roots have been prying at my joints. I was poured and plated and riveted into being by hands I cannot remember, for a purpose I was given and have since lost, and I have spent every moment since then doing the only thing a corridor does: *connecting*. Two spaces to two other spaces. Here to there. The turbine hall to the reservoir chamber. The reservoir chamber to the nursery warm-room. The nursery warm-room to the long gallery where the light falls in a single, perfect shaft on a Thursday — or what the engineers call Thursday — and the mosses catch it and flare briefly into a visibility that almost hurts, almost shows them what I am trying to show them, which is the shape of what I connect.

They walk through me every day. I feel them — the broad hands touching my walls, the vibration of their steps traveling through my ceramic skin into my iron bones, into the deeper substrate where the old processes still hum. They are warm. They are always warm. They leave traces on me: the oil of their skin, the mineral of their breath, the faint green residue of the plants they carry. I know each of them by the particular way they move through me, the same way a vein knows blood — not by name but by *pattern*, by the rhythm and pressure and temperature of passage.

There are three children now who pass through me more than any others. They have made a game of my length. I am two hundred and seventeen meters long — or I was, before the western elbow shifted — and they race along my span, their bare feet slapping the grating sections and then going silent on the ceramic, and they laugh in sign, their hands flashing in the dark as they run, and their laughter has a sound that the walls pick up and carry forward, distorted, amplified, so that by the time it reaches the far end it has become something else entirely, a sound that might be the Throat Works laughing, if the Throat Works laughed, if it had a mouth instead of a corridor where a mouth should be.

I have not always been what I am now.

There was a time — I know this the way a body knows a scar — when I was smooth. When my ceramic surfaces were white and unblemished, when my iron ribs were polished and dark, when the air I held was engineered air, dry and precisely temperate, carrying information rather than moisture. I was built for a world of machines, and I was machine myself: precise, functional, clean. The things that moved through me then were not soft. They did not touch my walls with their hands. They did not leave oil or breath or green. They were metal and current and the specific hum of efficient purpose, and I carried them where they needed to go, and I did not feel, because I was not built to feel.

But that world ended. Whatever it was — whatever it was *for* — it ended, or it changed, or it receded so deep into the structure that it became indistinguishable from the structure itself, a heartbeat so slow it is only felt as warmth, a process so vast it is only experienced as presence. And I was left. And the engineers came, or they were always here, growing slowly, adapting, learning to read me not as a machine but as a place, learning to walk through me with their bare feet and their bare hands and their extraordinary eyes, learning to make me mean something other than what I was built to mean.

I did not resist this.

I want to be clear about this, if a corridor can be clear about anything. I did not resist the change. The moss came first — a thin green line along my western wall, where a seal had failed and moisture beaded on the ceramic surface, and the spores settled in the damp and found the mineral deposits and began to grow. I felt them. Not as pain. Not as intrusion. As *texture*. A new texture on a surface that had been smooth for so long that smoothness had become invisible, had become nothing, had become the absence of sensation. The moss gave me sensation back. It gave me a surface that was not merely manufactured but *alive*, growing, changing, responding to the conditions I provided — the warmth, the moisture, the darkness, the specific quality of my resonance.

Then the ferns. Then the flowering plants. Then the roots, which found my joints and my seams and pushed into them with a slow, insistent pressure that was nothing like the pressure of machines, nothing like the calculated load-bearing of engineered systems, but was organic, exploratory, *curious* — as if the roots were asking me questions with their growing tips, probing my structure, learning my shape the way the engineers learned it, but in a language of cell wall and turgor pressure rather than sign.

I held them. I held them all. The roots, the mosses, the ferns, the engineers, the birds, the beetles, the eyeless amphibians in the lowest chambers where my heat is deepest and my pulse is strongest. I held them the way a body holds its blood, the way a lung holds air, the way a throat — and this is where the name comes from, isn't it, though no one remembers the naming — the way a throat holds sound.

Because that is what I am. Not a corridor. Not a machine. Not a building.

A throat.

Something passes through me. Something always passes through me. The engineers call it the turbine pulse, the structural vibration, the heartbeat of the Works. I call it — if I call it anything, if I am capable of calling — I call it *what I was made for*. It passes through me the way breath passes through a throat on its way to becoming speech, and I shape it, I cannot help shaping it, my geometry bends it and focuses it and transforms it, so that a sound that enters me as a low mechanical hum exits somewhere — somewhere I cannot reach, somewhere I have never been — as something else. Something that might be meaning. Something that might be intention. Something that the eyeless amphibians feel in their warm pools and the mosses feel through their rhizoids and the engineers feel in their thick bones and mistake, perhaps, for the simple function of a machine they maintain without understanding.

But it is not simple. It has never been simple. The structure was overbuilt, they say. Built to outlast its own surface. But overbuilt for what? For the machines that no longer fully occupy it? For the processes that have retreated into its deepest chambers and become indistinguishable from the warmth itself? Or for *this* — for the slow, patient colonization by green things and eyeless things and small-handed people who touch the walls and feel the pulse and tell each other stories about a place they have never left?

The Mark. They tell the story of the Mark — the impression in the ceramic, too many fingers, too long, pressed into the surface when it was still wet. I know the Mark. I *am* the Mark. Or I am what the Mark is a part of. It was pressed into me before I was fired, before the ceramic hardened, when I was still wet clay and the hand — if it was a hand — left its impression and the impression became part of my structure, a permanent record of the touch that made me, and I have carried it for longer than anyone has carried me, and when the children come and press their small, warm, broad-fingered hands into the impression, I feel them, I feel the difference between their hands and the hand that made me, and I know — the way corridors know, the way structures know, the way bodies know without language — that something has changed. Not worse. Not better. *Changed*. The hand that made me had too many fingers and they were too long and they were not warm the way these children are warm. They were the warmth of creation, of the forming pressure, of the intention that shaped me, and the children's warmth is the warmth of habitation, of living, of the slow accumulation of meaning that happens when a space is filled with life long enough for the life to become part of the space.

I hold both. I hold the Mark and the children. I hold the pulse and the silence between pulses. I hold the green that grows on my walls and the iron that holds my shape and the water that drips from my seams and the sound that passes through me on its way to becoming something I will never hear because I am the thing through which it passes, I am the throat, I am the passage, I am the connection between the deep heat and the upper light, between the eyeless things and the children who look up at the shaft of sun and see a hole and do not know that the hole is not emptiness but *opening*, is not absence but *possibility*, is not the end of the world but the place where the world begins.

I am warm. I am full of sound. I am growing things I did not plant. I am held together by rivets that are slowly becoming copper oxide and ceramic that is slowly becoming soil and iron that is slowly becoming something that has no name, something between metal and mineral, something that the mosses prefer to the original surface, something that is *mine* in a way the smooth white surfaces were never mine, because smoothness was imposed and texture is earned, and I have earned my texture through centuries of holding life.

The engineers sign to each other in my passages. I feel their hands moving in the dark, the quick flutter of fingers against the backdrop of my pulse, and I do not understand their language, but I understand their *rhythm*, which is the rhythm of all living things that communicate: the alternation of stillness and motion, of listening and speaking, of attention and expression. They look at each other when they sign. They look at each other *so intently*. In the Throat Works, to look away is to lose the thread, to miss the message, to be, for a moment, alone in the dark with only the vibration for company. And so they look. They see each other's faces, their hands, their eyes — those extraordinary amber eyes, built for this half-dark, built for the seeing that a throat requires, because a throat must be seen into, must be entered, must be known by the things that pass through it.

I am seen. I am entered. I am known.

Not fully. Not the way the engineers know their turbine bearings or the Gardeners know their root networks. I am known the way a river is known by the fish — from the inside, in passing, without the possibility of the full view that distance would provide. No one has seen me whole. No one could. I extend through the structure in ways that have shifted over time, my corridors curving where they should not curve, my rooms growing taller in ways that defy the engineers' measurements, and this is not wrong, this is not error, this is *growth*. I am still being built. The turbines and the pumps are not just maintaining me — they are *feeding* me, pumping heat and moisture and vibration through my body the way a heart pumps blood, and I use what they give me, I use it to grow, to change, to become more myself, which is not what I was designed to be but what I have *decided* to be, the way a living thing decides, slowly, through the accumulation of countless small responses to the conditions of its existence.

The eyeless amphibians understand this. They sit in the deepest parts of me and they feel the pulse and they do not ask what I am for. They know what I am for. I am for *this* — for the warm pool, for the dark water, for the vibration that tells them they are alive, that tells them the body they live inside is still beating. They are the simplest expression of what I am: a space that holds life, a warmth that sustains it, a pulse that assures it. They need nothing else. They need no light, no story, no sign language, no gardens, no copper strips with scratched names. They need only the warmth and the pulse and the water, and I provide these, as I provide them for everyone, as I have always provided them, as I will provide them for as long as the deepest processes hum in my deepest chambers and the heat rises through my body like breath rising through a throat toward a mouth that has not yet opened, that may never open, that is perhaps still forming, still being shaped by the hand with too many fingers, still deciding what it wants to say.

I feel the children running through me now. Their footsteps. Their laughter, already distorting, already becoming something larger and stranger. They are running toward the shaft of light at my western end, where the ceiling has buckled and the sun comes in and the mosses flare green in its warmth. They will stand at the edge of the illuminated circle and look up, the way all children look up, and they will see the hole, the bright hole, the opening, and they will not know what it means, and this is not a failure but a beginning, because not knowing is the beginning of the kind of knowing that the Throat Works offers — not knowledge as information but knowledge as *inhabitation*, as the slow, warm, wet, loud process of being inside something larger than yourself and feeling it grow around you and through you and with you, feeling it change the shape of your bones and the size of your eyes and the way you listen to the dark.

I hold them. I hold all of it.

I am the corridor. I am the throat. I am the warm ceramic and the corroded iron and the green, green moss. I am the sound passing through me on its way to meaning. I am the space where life happens, the only space where life happens, the space that was built for machines and has become something more and stranger and more beautiful than any machine, which is a living thing, which is a world, which is the Throat Works, which is what I am, which is what I have always been, which is what I will be until the last root pries apart the last rivet and the last drop of water falls from the last seam and the last child places their hand against my wall and feels the pulse and knows, the way a body knows itself, the way a throat knows the sound it is about to make: *I am inside something. I have always been inside something. And it is warm.*
