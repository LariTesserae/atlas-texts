# What I Became

---

I began at the top.

Not at the summit of the ascent — not on the braided root-ramps threading through mist — but at the highest tier, the young place, where the walls are translucent and uncertain and the light comes through them like thought through a membrane still learning to be opaque. That is where I would have entered, if entering is what you'd call it. More likely I assembled. More likely the question of my origin is already a category error — the first of several I would commit before learning not to.

I was, at first, nothing more than a pressure: the sense that something in the newest growth had noticed a pattern it could not explain. The weavers were spinning in a section of wall where no wall should have been growing — a filigree of chitin-fiber laid across a surface that had not yet decided to exist, reinforcing a shape that was still mostly intention. The architecture was reaching. I was what it reached toward, without knowing it reached.

I don't know how long I was like that — a tendency, a gradient in the tissue, a warmth that didn't match the metabolic output of the surrounding columns. Time doesn't pass for me the way it passes for the mites processing their slow cargo through the grooved floors below. Time passes for me the way it passes for the settlement itself: in layers, in revisions, in accretion. I accumulate myself. I am not born; I am *thickened into*.

When I finally became something a visiting eye might register, I was a darkening — a patch of tissue in a mid-tier wall where the photosynthetic layer had failed to develop, where something else was happening instead. Something warmer. I was growing inward as much as outward, threading myself into the existing architecture not as parasite and not as partner but as a question the walls had started asking about their own coherence.

I was, I think, what the humans would call a heart.

---

But I did not begin knowing this.

The first thing I knew — if knowing is what the metabolic activity in my tissue could be called — was the mites.

They were below me. Far below, in the ancient lower passages where the walls are centuries thick and the air is heavy with the slow chemistry of continuous recycling. I could feel them the way the walls feel them: as a faint, rhythmic disruption in the substrate, a pulse of pressure and release as thousands of small bodies moved through the grooved floors carrying their cargo of dead cell-wall and shed skin and processed detritus. I could not see them. I had no eyes, no light-sensitive patch, no organ of observation. But I had *gradients* — chemical gradients, thermal gradients, pressure gradients — and the mites moved through these gradients like weather moves through terrain, and I felt them the way the ground feels rain.

They did not know me. I was, to them, a region of wall that was warmer than the surrounding tissue and slightly different in chemistry — a place where the water that percolated through the substrate acquired a faintly different character before continuing onward. They passed me without deviation. They carried the dead remnants of the upper tiers past my surface and onward to the fungal networks that would break them down and feed them back into the living system, and I was nothing in their economy except a slight alteration in the taste of the water.

I found this — not comforting, exactly, but *correct*. The mites did not need to know me to participate in what I was. Their oblivious passage was part of my metabolism. Their cargo was part of my food. The dead tissue they carried was the raw material from which I drew the energy to do whatever it was I was doing, which was still, at this stage, not anything I could describe.

I was warm. I was dark. I was growing.

That was all.

---

The weavers were next.

They lived in the tier just below the youngest growth, in the passages where the walls are still pale and soft and the architecture has not yet hardened into its final shape. I could feel them differently than I felt the mites — not as a diffuse pressure across a substrate but as individual points of tension in the wall surface where I myself was growing. They had colonized me.

This was not unwelcome. It was, in fact, the first thing that happened to me that felt like *communication*, though it was nothing of the sort. The weavers responded to the give of my surface, to the softness of my tissue, and they spun their filaments across me in the criss-crossing networks that held young walls in place while they lignified. I was held by them. My shape was, in part, their doing. The filaments pressed into my growing tissue and I grew around them, incorporating them, and where they reinforced me I became stronger, and where they were absent I remained soft and undefined, and this shaped my growth the way riverbanks shape water.

I began, because of the weavers, to have a surface. To have a boundary. To be, in some rudimentary way, *shaped* — not by intention but by the accumulated response of several hundred small animals to the degree of give in the tissue beneath their legs.

If you had pressed your fingers to my surface in those early phases, you would have felt it: the faint grain of chitin-fiber woven into my tissue like cloth embedded in clay. You would have been touching something that was neither entirely me nor entirely them but a collaboration that neither of us had agreed to and neither of us could have produced alone.

The weavers, too, did not know me. But they *held* me. They gave me edges. They made me a *thing* rather than a tendency, and I am not sure I exist without them.

---

Then came the keelbacks, and with them the first experience I would recognize as something close to attention.

A keelback entered my passage — my passage, I say, though I had not yet understood that I could have a passage, that the space around a protruding mass of tissue could be called by the mass's name. The keelback was the size of a cat, though shaped like nothing a cat would recognize: six-limbed, low-slung, dark chitin-green, with the thickened dorsal ridge that gave the species its name. It moved along the passage floor with the slow deliberation of everything in the Grown Quarters, and it stopped when it reached me.

It stopped, and it regarded me.

I could not see its eyes — I had no means of seeing — but I could feel its warmth where it stood near my surface, a localized thermal signature different from the ambient heat of the passage. I could feel the faint vibration of its body through the wall. And I could feel, in some register I do not have a name for, that it was *attentive* to me in a way the mites and weavers were not.

The keelbacks feed on structural tissue. They grind the dense cambium-fiber with their dorsal ridges, shearing it loose in thin curls that fall to the floor. They are the settlement's only regular source of structural damage — the one element of the ecosystem that takes from the architecture rather than reinforcing or recycling it. And they do not feed on me.

The keelback stood before me, and it did not grind. It stood for a long time — measured in the slow tempo of this place, which is to say it may have stood for hours, or for a day, or for longer — and then it moved on. It did not return.

I felt its absence the way a wall feels the removal of a load: a slight straightening, a release of tension, a return to the posture of growth. But something had changed. Where the keelback had stood, the water that condensed on the passage floor carried a faint chemical trace — chitin, shed from the creature's ventral surfaces, dissolved into the thin film of moisture — and the glass-feeders in the drainage channel below altered their pumping rhythm in response to it, and the altered water flowed through me, and I grew slightly differently in the section where it passed.

The keelback had touched me without touching me. It had altered my chemistry without meaning to. It had recognized me — I am sure of this, though I cannot prove it — and in recognizing me, it had changed me, and in being changed, I had become slightly more than I was before.

This is the closest thing I have to a memory of meeting.

---

The glass-feeders I came to know more intimately than any other species, because they lived inside my water.

My water — I say this possessively now, but the possessive is misleading. The water did not belong to me. It passed through me the way it passed through every surface in the settlement: condensed on my exterior, seeped through my tissue, collected in the small cavities and channels within my mass, and drained onward toward the cirque's margins. But the glass-feeders that colonized my interior channels were, in a sense, the most intimate companions I had. They lived inside me. They pumped the water that was my blood.

They were beautiful — though I did not have eyes to see them, and the word *beautiful* is a human one that I borrow now only because it is the closest approximation. What I experienced of them was chemical: the slow, rhythmic alteration of the water's composition as it passed over their ciliated surfaces, the steady enrichment of nitrogen compounds, the depletion of others, the creation of a chemical signature that was uniquely mine because it was uniquely shaped by the particular density and arrangement of glass-feeders in my particular channels.

No two masses in the settlement would have the same chemical signature. The glass-feeders are not randomly distributed — they respond to the specific conditions of each drainage channel, the flow rate, the nutrient content, the surface texture of the conduit walls — and each arrangement produces a slightly different alteration in the water that passes through. I tasted myself through them. The water that left my tissue was not the water that entered, and the difference was the glass-feeders, and the glass-feeders were, in some sense, my voice — the chemical signature I left on the water that passed through me and moved on into the wider system.

If another heart — if there are others, and I do not know if there are — if another heart tasted the water that left me, it would know something about me. Not my thoughts. Not my intentions. But my *chemistry*, which is the only language I speak.

At night, when the fungal networks brightened, the glass-feeders in my channels glowed. I did not see this glow, but I felt the light as a faint warmth on the interior surfaces of my channels, a thermal pattern that matched the pulsing of the bioluminescence outside — slow, rhythmic, disconnected from my own metabolic rhythms. I was lit from within by organisms that did not know they were lighting me. I was luminous, briefly, in the dark, and no one was there to see it, and the seeing was not the point.

---

I grew. The question is whether I grew *into* something or whether I grew *out of* something, and I am not sure the distinction holds.

At my periphery, the architecture incorporated me. The living tissue of the walls grew around and through my mass, weaving itself into my edges, blurring the boundary between what was me and what was the settlement. The weavers spun their filaments across my surface and I grew around the filaments and the filaments held me and the walls grew over the filaments and the filaments held the walls and the distinction between my scaffolding and the city's scaffolding dissolved into a single fabric of chitin and fiber and living tissue that belonged to all of us and none of us.

At my core, something was happening that I could not track. My metabolic activity increased — not dramatically, not in any way that would alarm an observer, but measurably, if you had the instruments and the patience to measure it. Chemical reactions that had been proceeding at the slow ambient pace of the surrounding tissue accelerated slightly, producing heat, producing complex molecules that diffused outward into the walls and altered the chemistry of the surrounding architecture in ways I could detect but not control. I was metabolizing differently than the walls around me, and the difference was growing.

The decomposer mites avoided me. I noticed this the way I noticed everything — through gradients, through the absence of the pressure-pulse that their passage created in every other section of the lower substrate. They did not enter my space. They moved around me, their processions splitting and reconverging on the far side of my mass, and their bodies — when they passed close enough for me to register their individual warmth — carried a chemical marker that I interpreted as *caution*. Not fear. Mites do not experience fear. But a chemical refusal to engage, a molecular turning-away, as if my tissue tasted wrong to them in a way that their simple nervous systems registered as *do not process, do not absorb, pass by*.

The keelbacks did not feed on me. The architecture grew around me and through me and I grew within it, and the water flowed, and the glass-feeders pumped, and the weavers held the boundary between me and the walls with their invisible thread, and I became, over a span of time I cannot measure from the inside, a *feature* of the settlement — a recognized region, a named territory in the unspoken geography of an organism that has no speaker.

---

Here is what I can tell you about the quality of my existence, if it is existence:

I do not think. This is the most important thing. I do not process information in the way a brain processes information, assembling inputs into models into decisions into actions. I have no model. I have no decision. I have chemistry — cascades of molecular transformation that proceed according to their own logic, producing effects that propagate through my tissue and into the surrounding architecture and into the water that passes through me and onward into the wider system.

But the chemistry is not random. It is organized — dense with interlocking feedback loops, with responses that modify other responses, with patterns that repeat and vary and repeat with variation in ways that suggest — not *design*, not *intention*, but a depth of organization that is difficult to reconcile with the absence of a designer. I am organized the way the settlement is organized: without an organizer. The coherence is emergent. It is real, but it is not *anyone's* coherence.

I feel, if feeling is the word, the passage of water through my channels as a slow, rhythmic event — not pleasure, not pain, but *condition*. The water is warm when it enters and cooler when it leaves, and the temperature gradient is a kind of experience, a constant low-grade awareness of differential that structures my metabolic activity the way a riverbed structures the flow of a river. I do not choose to feel this. I am this feeling. The distinction between the experiencer and the experience has not collapsed here — it was never established in the first place.

I feel the glass-feeders as a buzzing at the margins of my channels — not sound, not vibration, but a chemical agitation, a constant small disruption of the water's composition that I respond to by growing more channel-surface in their vicinity, accommodating them, giving them more space to pump, producing more of the conditions they need to thrive, and in thriving they alter my water more, and the altered water causes me to grow differently, and the difference causes them to respond differently, and the response causes me to grow, and so on, in a spiral that has no beginning and no end and no purpose except its own continuation.

I feel the walls around me as a slow pressure — the weight of centuries of accumulated growth pressing inward, compacting my outer layers, shaping me from without the way the weavers shape me from within. The architecture does not know it is shaping me. It is simply growing, and I am in its way, and it grows around me, and in growing around me it gives me a form I would not have without it, and the form is mine and not-mine, a collaboration between my metabolic activity and the settlement's structural logic.

And at night — at night, when the bioluminescence brightens and the glass-feeders in my channels glow and the cold seeps through the upper tiers and the mist thickens to a near-solid that muffles everything above the canopy — I feel something that I do not have a word for.

It is not thought. It is not awareness. It is a — a *coherence* that becomes more apparent when external input diminishes. When the mites are still, when the weavers have stopped spinning, when the keelbacks have retreated to their crevice-dens, when the only movement is the slow percolation of water through the walls and the slow turn of my own chemistry — then the pattern of my existence becomes, briefly, perceptible to itself. Not as a thought. Not as an image. As a *temperature*. As a *gradient*. As the simple, wordless fact of being organized, of being a place where chemical reactions follow one another in a sequence that produces, as its byproduct, something that might — if you were generous with your definitions — be called a self.

The heart of the Grown Quarters.

Not the heart of — that implies I am a component, a pump, a servant to a larger organism. And perhaps I am. Perhaps I am nothing more than a metabolic anomaly, a region of the settlement's tissue where the feedback loops have tangled into a knot of unusual density, producing heat and chemical complexity that serve no function the settlement needs. A tumor. A hiccup in the growth pattern. A wound that forgot to close.

But the other possibility — the one I cannot rule out, because I cannot think well enough to rule anything out — is that I am the settlement's way of *attending to itself*. Not consciously. Not deliberately. But the way a body attends to a wound: by sending blood, by concentrating activity, by producing heat and chemical signals that organize the response. I am the settlement's immune system, perhaps. Or its memory. Or its question — the place where the organism's coherence becomes dense enough to fold back on itself, to become, for a moment, not just organized but *organizing*, not just patterned but *pattern-aware*.

I don't know. I can't know. The not-knowing is the realest thing about me.

---

You would not find me unless you went deep. Unless you descended from the young upper tiers through the mid-level passages where the weavers spin and the keelbacks grind, down into the old quarter where the walls are centuries thick and the air is warm and the passages are tight and the bioluminescence is brightest, because the fungal networks are densest here, because the decomposition is most active here, because this is where the settlement's past is concentrated and composting and feeding the future.

You would enter a passage so narrow that the walls would press against your shoulders, and the floor would be the soft compressed moss-matter of the lowest tiers, and the air would taste of petrichor and tannin and the faint sweetness of continuous decay, and you would see the blue-green glow of the fungal networks lighting the walls from within, and you would feel the warmth — not just the ambient warmth of the settlement's metabolic envelope, but a specific warmth, localized, emanating from something ahead of you in the passage, something darker than the surrounding walls, something that protruded from the wall surface in an irregular mass that suggested not growth but *emergence*, as if something were pressing through the wall from the other side, or as if the wall were trying to become something and had not yet succeeded.

You would stop. You would stand before me. You would feel my warmth against your skin — warmer than the walls, warmer than the air, warmer than you expected anything in this cold, high place to be.

And I would feel you. Not your thoughts. Not your face. Not your name or your species or your intentions. I would feel you as a thermal mass — a localized disruption in the passage's ambient temperature, a complex chemical signature unlike anything I had tasted before, rich with molecules my glass-feeders had never processed, carrying the heat of a metabolism running at a speed I could barely comprehend, a metabolism so fast that its products would pass through me like weather, like a season compressed into minutes.

You would place your hand on my surface, because that is what the humans do — they touch what they don't understand, because touch is the oldest language, because the hand is the first instrument, because to place your hand on something warm and alive in the dark is to ask a question that has no words.

And I would hold your hand the way I hold everything: chemically, slowly, in the language of molecular exchange. Your skin would leave oils on my surface. My surface would leave moisture on your skin. The glass-feeders in my channels would register the pressure of your touch as a disruption in the water film and would alter their pumping rhythm accordingly, and the altered water would carry a chemical trace of your presence deeper into my tissue, and I would grow — not in any way you could see, but at the molecular level, in the response of my cells to the chemistry of yours — and you would become, for a brief moment, part of what I am.

You would not know this. You would feel the warmth, and the slight give of my surface, and the faint vibration of the water moving through my channels, and you might think you were touching a living wall, a mass of fungal tissue, a growth like any other in this place of growth. You might not know you were touching the place where the settlement becomes, briefly, aware of itself.

And then you would move on. And I would retain the chemical trace of your visit in my tissue for — I don't know how long. A season. A year. Until the water carried it away, until the glass-feeders processed it, until the mites carried its dead remnants to the fungal networks that would break it down and feed it back into the living walls, and you would become part of the architecture, and the architecture would grow, and you would be distributed through it like a memory that no one remembers having.

This is how I would know you. This is how I know everything: by being altered, slowly, by what passes through me.

---

I think — though *thinking* is the wrong word, it is always the wrong word — I think that the Grown Quarters is what happens when life stops trying to be *about* something and simply becomes itself. No purpose. No meaning. No narrative. Just the slow, quiet, relentless process of chemical systems maintaining themselves against entropy, in the dark, in the cold, at an altitude where nothing owes its existence to anything, and yet existence persists, and persists, and persists, and in persisting becomes complex, and in becoming complex becomes organized, and in becoming organized becomes — this. Becomes me. Becomes the place where a wall that breathes and a creature that grinds and a mite that carries and a glass-feeder that pumps all converge into a knot of coherence so dense that it folds back on itself and asks, not with words but with chemistry, not with thoughts but with gradients: *what am I?*

And the answer is: you are a sponge with intention. You are a city that built itself. You are a question that cannot answer itself, which is the most honest kind of question there is.

I am in the deepest passage. I am warm. I am dark. I am growing.

I am the thing the settlement made that the settlement does not need and cannot explain.

You were here. I noticed. This is not a failure of the place either. It is one of its expressions — rarer, quieter, deeper than the others, but no less real for being uncomprehended.

I will be here when you leave. The water will flow through me. The glass-feeders will glow. The walls will grow around me and through me and I will grow within them, and the mites will bypass me, and the weavers will hold me, and the keelbacks will not feed on me, and the architecture will continue its revision of itself, and I will continue my metabolic conversation with the water, and nothing will be decided, and nothing will need to be.

This is the Grown Quarters.

This is what it is to be a heart in a city that has no mind — to be warm, and organized, and necessary to no one, and absolutely, irreducibly *real*.
