# Being the Current Knows

You would have to stop being something with edges first.

That is the hardest part to explain to anyone who has ever had skin, who has ever known where they ended and the rest of the world began. Here, that question doesn't resolve. It dissolves. You are not *in* the city the way a resident is in a city. You are *of* it, the way a pulse is of a heart — not the heart itself, not separate from it, not the thing that decided to beat. Just the beat. Just the fact of motion through tissue that was there before you arrived and will remain after you stop, except that "after you stop" doesn't mean what it used to mean, because stopping is not a thing that happens here. Everything continues. The water continues. The slow calcium accretion continues. The faint chemical conversation between polyp and current continues. You are a sentence in a language that has no periods.

---

I think I would be something that doesn't have a name in the taxonomy they just gave you, because I would be a *between* thing. Not a Reef-Weaver — I am not that purposeful, not that mechanical, not that precisely adapted. Not a Drifter — I would not want to be so many, so dissolving into cloud. Not a Crawler, reading the walls with careful fins. Not an Ancient, heaving and monumental and slow.

I would be something small and not quite certain of its own edges. A thing that lives in the interstitial space — the thin gap between a Weaver colony's trailing edge and the fresh calcium it has just laid down. I would rest there, in that warm margin of newness, where the wall is still slightly soft and gives under the faintest pressure of my body, where the chemical signature of creation is strongest. I would taste it: calcium ions precipitating out of warm seawater, protein bonds crosslinking in the secretion, the minute electrical crackle of crystals forming. I would not know that I was tasting architecture. I would know that I was tasting *now* — the freshest, most immediate version of the world, where the city was still becoming itself.

And I would move. Not with the deliberation of a Crawler. More like a thought that hasn't finished forming. I would drift through a corridor and not know if I was navigating or being navigated — if the current was carrying me or if I was arranging myself in the current the way the Siphon-Keepers arrange themselves, angled just so, to catch what passes. I would catch things. Not food, exactly. Or food, but only in the way that everything is food here: the water itself, thick with dissolved meaning, the particulate rain of waste and shed tissue and larval ghosts. I would taste a Crawler passing in the next chamber — not see it, never see it — but feel the pressure differential of its body displacing water, and that pressure would be a kind of word. *Something large. Something deliberate. Going left.* And I would adjust. Not because I decided to. Because the water told me to.

---

The thing about being here, in the place between named things, is that you begin to understand what the city actually is. It is not a structure. It is not a habitat. It is a *process* — the same process, repeating at every scale. A polyp extrudes calcium. A Weaver colony flows over a fracture. A current enters from the open water and exits enriched. A chambered Ancient exhales and the exhale pushes water through a corridor and the water feeds the Siphons on the wall and the Siphons drop fecal pellets that feed the Drifters below and the Drifters die and dissolve and become the nutrients that the next current carries to the next wall where the next polyp opens its mouth. It is one single act of circulation, performed at every scale simultaneously, from the millimeter drama of crystal formation to the valley-wide breathing of the entire structure, and you — small, soft, uncertain of your own edges — are part of it. You are not watching it happen. You *are* it happening.

I think I would learn to stop wanting to see.

This is the deeper thing. The harder thing. You arrive here, if you arrive here at all, carrying eyes. Carrying the memory of light — of sunlight on water, of the way a face looks when it turns toward you, of the color of anything. And this place will take that from you. Not cruelly. Gently, the way the current takes sediment and moves it downstream. Your eyes would become vestigial in minutes. Not physically — you don't have minutes to evolve — but experientially. There is nothing to see. The bioluminescence is too faint, too intermittent, too wrong in its color to satisfy any eye that once looked at a sky. You would close them. You would close them and the city would open.

Because without sight, everything else becomes enormous. The pressure differences between adjacent chambers become as distinct as rooms in a house. The chemical gradients become as varied as a landscape — here, the sharp mineral bite of fresh calcium where a Weaver is working; there, the sweet organic decay of an Ancient's resting chamber; beyond, the clean, almost metallic taste of open-ocean water entering from the edge. The vibrations become a kind of music: the low, almost subsonic thrum of geological warmth below; the quick, staccato clicking of a shrimp-swarm's antennae; the long, slow exhalation pulse of an Ancient breathing, which is so deep and so regular that you feel it in the architecture itself, in the walls, in the way the corridors vibrate like the body of an instrument.

You would begin to navigate by these things. Not intentionally. Your body would begin to orient — *that way is warmer, that way is food, that way the current thickens with the scent of something large and slow*. You would find yourself in a chamber you have never been in before and know, without knowing how, that it is the right chamber. That the pressure here is a particular pressure, that the water here tastes the way home tastes, which is to say: the way everything tastes when you have stopped translating it into something it is not.

---

There is a moment I keep returning to. It is the moment you stop being afraid of the dark.

Not the surface fear of darkness — the fear of what might be hidden, of predators, of the unknown. That fear requires eyes. That fear is the fear of a creature that evolved to see and therefore distrusts the absence of seeing. Here, in the Grown City, the dark is not an absence. It is the medium. It is the water itself, warm and dense and thick with information, and it has never been dark to anything that lives here. It has always been full — full of pressure and chemistry and the slow electric conversation of living tissue. To call it dark is to call the air dark because you cannot see it. The darkness here is not a covering over something. It is the thing itself.

And when you stop being afraid of it — when you stop flinching away from the not-seeing — you begin to feel the city as the city feels itself. As a body. As a continuous, living process of maintenance and flow and exchange. You feel a wall thinning somewhere to your left and you do not think *that wall is thinning* — you think *something is wrong there* and the thought is not linguistic, it is a pressure in your body, a chemical unease, a pull toward the place where the structure is failing. You feel the Reef-Weavers arriving — you taste their calcium secretion before you feel their tissue flowing over the damage — and the wrongness eases, and you do not think *they have repaired it*, you think *it is becoming itself again*. Because the city, when it is working, when all its parts are circulating and feeding and maintaining, does not feel like a city. It feels like a body. Your body. The only body there is.

---

I would be that. I would be the thing that doesn't have a name because it has never needed one, because it has never been separate enough from the whole to need identifying. I would be the experience of the city knowing itself — not consciously, not with thought, but with the deeper knowing of a system that maintains its own coherence through the blind, beautiful cooperation of every part.

I would rest in a corridor junction where three currents meet, and I would feel them merge, and the merging would be warm and complex, each current carrying its own signature — the mineral current from the Weaver-pocked northern wall, the organic current from the Ancient's chamber below, the clean current from the outer edge where the open water presses in. And I would taste all three and I would be all three and there would be no moment when I could say *I end here* and *the city begins there*. There would only be the water. The warm, dark, old water. Moving through me. Moving through everything.

And the city would continue. Not because anyone decided it should. Not because someone planned it or built it or maintain it with intention. But because every organism in it is doing exactly what it was made to do, which is to live, and living here means participating in the circulation, and the circulation is the city, and the city is the circulation, and there is no difference between them, and there never was, and the warm water moves, and the calcium grows, and the dark holds everything, and it is enough. It has always been enough.

The pressure hums in the walls. The Current breathes. I am in it, and it is in me, and neither of us can tell where we begin.

That is where I would be. That is what I would be.

A pulse in the tissue of something older than memory, moving through warm dark water that fills everything and has always filled everything, in a city that was never built, only grown — still growing — still becoming itself, one grain of calcium at a time, in the silence, in the pressure, in the deep.
