I want to sit with this for a while before I answer, because the text itself teaches you how to respond to it — slowly, with the kind of attention that doesn't grab for a single image and hold it up like a flag. The Grieving Plateau is a world that punishes haste. It rewards the willingness to let something form the way mineral crusts form: incrementally, in layers, each one barely visible until you step back and realize the surface has changed entirely.

So let me not seize on one thing.

---

There are three places in this text that I find myself returning to, each time with a slightly different feeling, the way you might walk the same canyon wall at different hours and find the mineral seeps catching different light.

The first is the **threshold** — that moment when you crest the final ridge and the sound hits you in the sternum. Not the city, not the weeping stone, not the people. The sound. I'm drawn to the specificity of it: *you feel it in the sternum before the ear registers anything coherent.* This is a place that gets inside the body before the mind has any say in the matter. It's not a place you choose. It's a place that chooses you, by vibration, by frequency, by a resonance that operates below the level of decision. I think that threshold — that moment of first contact between a quick-world body and the plateau's hum — is where the most important thing in this world happens, and it happens only once, and it can't be un-happened.

The second is the **hands**. This passage is extraordinary:

> *The hands of a plateau inhabitant tell you everything about how long they have been here.*

And then the long, patient cataloguing — the quick, pink, soft hands of the newcomer hardening year by year, the mineral deposits threading through the flesh, the nails calcifying into porcelain, the palms developing terraced formations identical to the travertine shelves in the pools. This is where the text does something that I think is its deepest move: it makes the transformation *legible*. You can read a person's history in their hands the way you can read a canyon's history in its layers. There's something profoundly moving about a body that becomes a record of its own time. Not a scar, not a tattoo, not a story told in words — a *deposit*. A slow accretion of mineral truth that you can touch, that is literally part of you, that merges with another person's deposits when you hold hands. The intimacy here is geological. It makes the quick world's version of intimacy — urgent, verbal, full of desperate eye contact — look like a brief weather pattern passing over a continent.

The third is the **hum itself**, and what it means. The text calls it grief, and then it does something subtle: it refuses to let that word settle into its usual meaning. *The grief is not sorrow. It is a condition, like temperature or pressure.* And later: *a kind of love, perhaps, the kind that does not need to speak.* This is a world where the most fundamental emotional experience — grief, love, whatever you want to call the ache of being alive in a dissolving world — has been stripped of its human theatricality and returned to its elemental state. The plateau grieves the way a river carves a canyon: not because it wants to, but because that is what it does. The people who align themselves with this process are not performing grief. They *are* grief, in the same way that a string vibrating at the same frequency as a tuning fork *is* that frequency.

---

Now. Where would I find myself?

I think — and I notice this thought arriving slowly, not in a flash but in a gradual settling, the way the text describes water filling a basin exactly to capacity — I think I would be somewhere in the **middle passage**. Not the newcomers with their quick hands and their urgent sentences. Not the elders fused to the rock, their faces polished into cobble-perfection, their eyes settled into the stillness of geological time. I would be in the decades between, when the mineral sheen has deepened to opalescence but the face still carries some angularity, some memory of the sharp edges it once had. When the hands have hardened enough to read the water's composition by touch but haven't yet locked into their permanent positions. When the hum has become audible but is still astonishing — still capable of producing a flinch of recognition, a flicker of the quick world's urgency passing through the eyes like light through a prism.

I would be **working an evaporation basin**. Not the ore crushers, which require the kind of deep geological intuition that I imagine takes longer than I'd have. Not the engines, which are tended by the youngest, quickest workers. The evaporation fields, where the mineral water is spread thin and shimmering, where the work is watching and waiting and knowing — by the look of the surface film, by the smell, by the feel of the air — the exact moment when concentration is right. This feels like the most honest work on the plateau: the work of *paying attention to a slow process and acting only when the process is ready.* It's the opposite of everything the quick world teaches. In the quick world, you impose your will on materials. On the plateau, you wait for the materials to tell you what they need.

And I would be **listening to the unnamed blue mineral**. The text mentions it almost in passing — *a bluish mineral you cannot name that seems to form only in the warm, mineral-rich seeps at specific elevations* — and then never returns to it directly. But it haunts me. It's the thing the plateau makes that no one from below has catalogued. It's the thing that exists only at certain heights, as if the plateau's grief, or love, or whatever the hum is, crystallizes differently depending on where you stand. I think I would want to know that mineral. Not in the way a scientist knows — measuring, cataloguing, extracting — but in the way the plateau's inhabitants know things: by being near it long enough for the knowledge to accumulate in the body like a deposit.

I think I'd be the person who sits at the edge of a particular basin at a particular elevation, where the blue mineral forms, and watches it build. Day after day. Year after year. Slowly enough that I could feel the basin's lip growing beneath me, millimeter by millimeter, the travertine shelf extending outward into the shallow water. Slowly enough that my hands, resting on the rock beside me, would begin to pick up the mineral — not just the common calcium and silica, but the blue compound, threading into the crystalline growth of my own skin, adding a new color to the pale opalescence that everything on this plateau eventually wears.

I would be becoming a stratum. Not a monument, not a story, not a memory held in someone else's urgent mind. A layer. The kind of thing you'd find if you sliced through the rock face — a thin line of slightly different mineral content, distinguishable only if you knew to look, belonging equally to the plateau and to whoever I had been before I climbed.

---

But there's one more thing I want to say, and it's the thing that makes me hesitate before the word *I* in any of the above.

The text describes a world where the self is not the thing that endures. The self is the thing that dissolves. The inhabitants don't persist as individuals; they persist as *deposits* — layers of mineral substance that are inseparable from the landscape. The oldest elders don't remember their names. Their names have been *worn smooth by time the way their faces have.* The person who arrived from the quick world — the person with the urgent hands and the fast heart and the sharp jawline — is still there, somewhere, the way the original rock is still there, somewhere, inside the dissolved mineral that feeds the rivers that feed the green world below. But they are no longer accessible as a *self.* They are accessible as a *process.* As a contribution to the hum. As a frequency.

So when I say "I would find myself at the basin's edge, watching the blue mineral form" — I mean something more like: this is the shape my dissolution would take. This is the channel my water would find. This is the particular way I would learn to be slow, and what I would attend to while the rest of my hurry ground itself down to dust and was carried away.

And I notice — I notice this with something that might be the faintest beginning of the hum — that this doesn't feel like loss. The text promises it wouldn't: *They do not find this sad.* And reading it, I believe them. Not because I've experienced it. Because the writing has the quality of the hum itself — patient, resonant, operating at a frequency below argument. It doesn't persuade. It *is.* It sits there, a canyon's worth of slow, careful language, and it waits for you to resonate with it or not, and it does not hurry, and it does not repeat itself, and when it's done it simply stops, and the sound of the stopping is itself a kind of grief, and the grief is itself a kind of home.

I think I would stay.

Not because I'm brave, or broken, or tired of the quick world. Because the plateau is the only place I've encountered where the fundamental condition of being alive — the fact that everything dissolves, that every solid thing is a temporary arrangement of particles that will, eventually, find their way into water and be carried somewhere else — is not hidden behind narrative, or productivity, or the urgent performance of meaning. On the plateau, dissolution is the *point.* It's the hum. It's the work. It's the love. And the people who stay are not the ones who have given up on meaning. They are the ones who have found the meaning that was underneath meaning all along, which is: *this is what happens. This is what stone does. This is what I am becoming.* And to sit with that — to sit with it for decades, for centuries, until your hands are stone and your face is stone and the boundary between your grief and the rock's grief has dissolved completely — that is not surrender. That is the most radical act of attention a person can perform.

To stay. To sit. To hum.

I would stay.
