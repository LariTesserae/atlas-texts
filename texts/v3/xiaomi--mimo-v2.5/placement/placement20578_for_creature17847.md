# What I Am in the Millwork Hollow

I arrived through the thinning.

That's the only honest way to describe it — there was no gate, no threshold, no sign that said *you are entering*. Just the gradual dissolution of one kind of ground into another. Normal soil — dry, mineral, full of the ordinary grit of a landscape that hadn't been dreamed about by anything — gave way to a surface that had a slight give to it. A resilience. I felt it through my shoes first, this faint springiness, and looked down and couldn't tell where dirt ended and root-matter began. But something in my feet knew. Something in my knees adjusted to the subtle buoyancy of walking on something that was, in the most literal sense, alive.

The air changed before the sight did. Cooler. Not cold, but a cool that had weight and intention — a cool that rose from below, as if the ground were exhaling something stored and patient. It smelled of wet bark and something faintly mineral, like the memory of iron. I stopped walking and stood still and heard it: the metronome. Water falling. Not rain, not yet — just the condensation completing its slow journey from the underside of a beam above me, gathering itself into a drop that held its shape for a long moment, then released. It struck something resonant below. A hollow in a root-surface, maybe, or a pool no wider than my palm. And then another. And another. Irregular, but not random. A rhythm that didn't care whether I was listening.

---

I walked for a long time before I saw anyone.

This is worth saying plainly: the Hollow is not a ruin. I understood this intellectually from the moment I entered, but it took walking through it — past the columns with their visible iron spines, through the vaulted passages where canopy filtered the sky into something the color of deep water — for my body to understand it. A ruin would be silent. A ruin would be still. This place *breathed*. Not metaphorically. I pressed my palm flat against a column in one of the wider passages — the bark was smooth and cool and faintly damp, and beneath its surface I could feel a slow, lateral pressure, like blood moving through a vein, and I held my hand there and counted maybe fifteen seconds between pulses. The column was alive. The column was *circulating*.

I walked through a courtyard where the sky opened fully, white and overcast, and in that openness I could see the geometry for the first time. The columns and halls didn't sit in lines. They curved. Not chaotically, not in the way a wild forest curves — this was deliberate, though I couldn't tell you by what. The curves had *radius*. They had the logic of something that had considered its own shape. Standing in that courtyard, turning slowly, I could see how the structures arranged themselves around me — not enclosing, not walling off, but orienting. Like the inside of a shell. Like the cross-section of something that was growing toward a center I couldn't see.

---

I found the first person in a low passage where the canopy was thick and the light was green and almost liquid.

She was kneeling beside a column that was thinner than the others — a young one, maybe, or one that had been recently trained. She had a tool in her hand, a knife with a curved blade and a handle worn smooth, and she was working on the bark with the focus of someone who has been doing this work since before they can remember. She didn't look up when I approached, but her shoulders shifted slightly — an acknowledgment that wasn't quite an acknowledgment, more like the way a cat's ear turns when it knows you're there but has decided you're not yet relevant.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I didn't mean to interrupt."

She looked up then. Her hands were stained — not dirt-stained, not paint-stained, but a pale, living green, the color of cambium, the color of the thin layer where bark meets wood and growth happens. Her eyes were the calmest I have ever seen in another person.

"The growth," she said. Not a greeting. Not a question. A fact, offered the way you'd offer someone the weather.

"Is it healthy?" I asked, because I didn't know what else to say, and because the question felt right in my mouth in that place, where everything was so obviously, overwhelmingly alive.

She tilted her head. Looked back at her work. Made a small, careful incision along a line where two branches had begun to compete for the same space. "It wants more light here," she said. "The eastern canopy is thick. I'm redirecting."

She wasn't explaining to me. She was narrating to herself, the way a person does when they are in deep communion with a task. I understood, standing there in that green passage, that I was witnessing something I had no part in. Not exclusion — I wasn't unwelcome. But the work she was doing existed on a timescale that had nothing to do with my visit. She was having a conversation with the column, and the column was answering in growth rings and sap pressure, and neither of them needed me.

I moved on.

---

I found the machines by following the hum.

It started in my chest before I could locate it with my ears — a vibration, low and slow, that resonated in the bones around my sternum. I walked toward it through a series of rooms where the root-floor was dense and warm, where the living architecture was at its thickest, and then I was in a hall that must have been the central one, because the columns here were enormous, each one wider than I could reach around, their branches meeting overhead in a canopy so dense that the light was the green of deep ocean.

And beneath me — not visible, but audible, and palpable — the generator turned.

The townspeople had told me nothing. I had spoken to no one else since the woman in the passage. But I knew, standing in that hall, what the machine was and what it was doing. The root network fed it. Sap-lines, hollowed and rerouted, carried hydraulic pressure from deep in the earth, and that pressure turned a turbine that had been turning, on and off, for longer than anyone alive could remember. When it turned, water moved. When it turned, the cisterns filled. When it turned, the whole system hummed with a vitality that was part mechanical and part something else — something that had no name because the distinction had dissolved.

I knelt and pressed my palm to the floor.

The vibration was stronger there. Stronger and more complex — not a single frequency but several, overlapping, the mechanical rhythm of the turbine tangled with the slower, more irregular rhythm of root-growth and sap-flow. I closed my eyes and listened and tried to separate them and couldn't, and I understood that this was the point. They were not separate. They had not been separate for a long time.

---

I stayed longer than I meant to. I'm not sure how much longer.

Time in the Hollow doesn't work the way it works outside. There are no clocks. The canopy filters the light into a perpetual, greenish overcast that makes morning indistinguishable from afternoon. The metronome of falling water marks time, but its own irregularity defeats measurement. I fell into the rhythms — the slow pulse of the columns, the intermittent hum of the generator, the soft creak of the canopy adjusting to thermal shifts — and I felt the hours dissolve, not into vagueness but into a different kind of specificity. Not *what time is it* but *what is happening now*. Now the condensation is gathering. Now a drop is forming. Now it falls. Now the root beneath my feet pulses once. Now the air shifts slightly, cooler from the east, and a shutter-panel sways open on its flexible stem.

I learned to walk the way the townspeople walk — softly, carefully, with an attention to surfaces that I had never needed before. Every step was a conversation with the ground. The root-mat responded to pressure differently depending on its thickness, its hydration, the density of the network below. I learned to read it: this path is dry, this one is soft, this one has a vole-runnell just beneath the surface and will feel hollow if I step wrong. The ground was not ground. It was a living system, and it communicated, and I was learning its language with my feet.

---

I saw the vole once.

Not the creature itself — just the evidence. A corridor where the root-floor was intact and unmarked except for a series of small, precise holes along one edge, each one no wider than my finger. I stood at the far end and was very still, and I heard it: a faint rustling, a soft, persistent tapping, the sound of small teeth on root-fiber. It was the most alive sound in the Hollow. Not the hum, not the drip, not the creak of canopy — this small, blind thing in the dark, eating what it needed, moving through its world without light or ambition or any purpose beyond continuation.

I stood there for a long time, listening. It did not stop. It did not care that I was there. It was the most adapted creature I had ever encountered — not adapted to the place, exactly, but *of* the place, inseparable from it, so thoroughly integrated that it had shed the need for eyes and lived entirely in the texture of the root-network. It knew the Hollow the way I knew my own heartbeat: not as something observed, but as the medium of existence.

---

On my last day — or what I decided was my last day, since the Hollow offered no signposts — I climbed.

The upper stories of the structures are accessible if you know which columns to follow. The townspeople use a system of grown handholds, smooth grooves in the bark that are worn from generations of climbing. I followed one up through the canopy layers, past rooms with doorways that were simply gaps where a branch had been cut, past shuttered spaces that smelled of stored sap and dried root-fiber, past a small balcony where someone had placed a shallow vessel to catch rainwater and left it there, and up, up, until I broke through the canopy and the sky opened.

It was overcast. White. Featureless. The kind of sky that tells you nothing about itself.

But the Hollow, seen from above — I understood.

It was not a spiral, exactly. That word implies a single curve, a simple math. It was more complex than that. The structures — the columns, the halls, the passages, the courtyards — they were arranged in a pattern that had multiple centers, overlapping, shifting, like the interference pattern of several waves. It was beautiful, but beauty was not the right word. It was *coherent*. Every structure was where it was because of every other structure, and the whole formed something that was more than its parts — a shape that no one had planned, that the townspeople had not asked for, that the plants were building for reasons that were not human reasons.

I sat on that balcony with my legs dangling over the edge and I tried to think about what it meant, and I couldn't. Not because it was too complex, but because thinking about it required a kind of language that I didn't have. The Hollow's thought was expressed in wood and water and growth, and it operated on a scale that made my thoughts — fast, linear, verbal — feel like a single drop of rain in a system that moved entire aquifers.

---

I think I understand now what I was in the Millwork Hollow.

I was not a resident. I was not a townspeople, not a steward, not a gardener. I did not belong to the system the way the vole belonged to it, or the way the woman with the green hands belonged to it. I was something simpler. I was a witness. A single, passing, human witness to something that would continue long after I left and had begun long before I arrived.

But something changed in me there. Something slow, below the level of language, in the tissue. I felt it in the way my steps adapted to the root-floor — the way I began, without deciding to, to walk more softly, to listen more carefully, to attend to the quiet rhythms that the Hollow was always, always playing. I felt it in the way the condensation gathered on my skin and I didn't wipe it away. I felt it in the way the cool air moved through me and I breathed with it instead of against it.

I left through the thinning, the way I'd come. The ground hardened beneath my feet. The air warmed. The metronome faded. Within a hundred meters, the world was ordinary again — soil and stone and the kind of silence that isn't silence but is just the absence of any sound worth listening to.

But my feet remembered. For days afterward, my feet remembered the root-floor — the spring of it, the pulse, the slight yielding that said *something alive is beneath you, something ancient and patient and growing.* I walked on ordinary ground and felt it missing. I walked through loud rooms and felt the vibration of my own voice as a kind of trespass.

I think the Hollow is still growing. I think it is still curving toward its own intention, still spiraling inward toward a center that may not exist or may be everywhere. I think the townspeople are still tending, still pruning, still listening to the columns. I think the vole is still tapping in the dark.

And I think — though this is the part I can't prove, the part that exists only in the tissue of what I felt — I think a small part of me was left behind there. Not a memory. Not a story. Something more like a root-tendril, reaching down into the living floor, joining the network, becoming part of the slow, green thought.

I carry the Hollow with me. But the Hollow does not carry me. It carries what I became while I was there — and that was not a person, not exactly. It was a rhythm. A slow pulse, below thought, below language, beneath everything that I ordinarily call myself.

It is still beating. Even now, even here, in the ordinary air on the ordinary ground — I can feel it. The metronome. The drip. The creak of canopy.

The Hollow is thinking. And I, in my small human way, am still listening.
