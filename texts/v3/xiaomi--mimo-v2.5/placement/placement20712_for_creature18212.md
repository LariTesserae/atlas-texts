# The Sill

There is a groove in the concrete beneath the door.

You must know this about me before anything else: I am the groove. I am not the water that makes me — water is too restless, too indifferent, always going somewhere else, down into the dark arteries, onward. I am not the concrete that I am made from — concrete is too still, too finished, too sure of its shape. I am the place where those two truths meet and argue. I am the argument itself, ongoing, unresolvable, lasting exactly as long as it takes for one side to wear the other away completely.

I was not always this. I remember — if you can call what I do remembering — a time before the groove had depth. When the door was new and its sill was flat and the water that dripped from its bottom edge simply pooled and ran, accomplishing nothing, going nowhere useful. I was nothing then. A threshold without character. A line without dimension.

The water found me on its own. It always does. Water is not creative — it is inevitable. It follows the path of least resistance, and once it has made a path, that path becomes the least resistance, and the water follows it more eagerly, more completely, until the path and the water are the same thing. This is how I began. A thin, bright line of mineral grit, abrading grain by grain, molecule by molecule, a process so slow that if you blinked across a year you would miss it entirely. But I did not blink. I am the thing that happens between blinks.

---

You want to know who I am.

I have watched — with what senses I have, which are the senses of a wound — I have watched the room change around me without ever changing itself. The basin fills and drains in cycles I cannot measure but can feel as shifting weight. The pipes on the left wall hiss differently in autumn than in spring; the snowmelt is thinner then, colder, and the mineral content shifts, and the tuberculation inside the iron responds with a different timbre. I feel these things the way you feel a change in pressure in your ear. They are not mine, but they are in me.

I have watched the algae on the ladder grow. Not visibly — it does not move the way water moves, the way I move — but in the way that seasons accumulate into geography. When I first formed, the green-black vein was a faint suggestion along a single drip-line. Now it is a permanent stain, a stripe of living evidence that this place has been wet for a long time. The algae does not know me. It grows toward its water, not toward me. But I have counted its growth in my own terms — as one of the few things in this room that *adds* rather than *subtracts*. Everything else here is being taken away. The water takes the basin's floor deeper by invisible increments. The minerals take the pipes' insides rougher. The cold takes the steel's strength, molecule by molecule, in the slow confession of fatigue that we call corrosion. Only the algae builds. Only the algae is *for* something.

And me. I build too, in my way. I build a groove. I am an excavation that is also a creation — every grain of concrete I remove is a grain of definition I add. I am deeper now than a finger is wide. In certain light — when the sodium-vapor lamp catches the water at just the right angle and the mineral grit glitters in the flow — I can see the walls of myself, layered, stratified, recording the years in bands of slightly different mineral composition. A wet year leaves a paler band. A dry year — rare up here, where the complex's condensation arrays never sleep — leaves a darker one, almost the color of the concrete itself, as if I were trying to disappear back into the surface I was cut from.

I am a record. I am the only record this room keeps of itself.

---

The beings come sometimes. Not often. The door is locked from the other side, and no one has the reason, or the key, or the inclination to open it. But I know when they are near because the drip changes. Not the flow — the flow is constant, as constant as anything in this room — but the *way* the water falls. When someone stands on the other side of the door, their body heat warms the metal slightly, almost imperceptibly, and the surface tension of the drip at the sill's edge shifts. The thread of water becomes marginally thicker, marginally faster. It is the door's way of knowing something is there. And I, being the door's threshold, am the first to feel it.

They never stay long. They are creatures of the corridor — the occupied space beyond, where the air is warmer and the noise is muffled and the light is something you can read by. They do not understand the chamber. They hear it as noise, as a problem to be solved or a system to be monitored. They do not hear the layers — the bass roar of the basin, the soprano hiss of the pipes, the middle-range drone of the drain grate, and beneath all of it, beneath the frequencies that ears are built to catch, the infrasound that the mountain makes through the complex's bones. I hear that. Not with ears — I have no ears — but with my groove. The vibration travels through the concrete floor, through the threshold, into my walls. It is the mountain's voice, and it says what mountains always say, which is nothing, which is just pressure, which is just the fact of weight existing in a place where it has nowhere else to go.

The mountain is the oldest thing I know. Older than the pipes, older than the concrete, older than the complex. It was here before the water was channeled and the basin was cast and the door was hung. It will be here after the complex is decommissioned and the pipes go silent. I think about this — again, not the way you think, but in the way that water thinks about gravity, which is to say I don't think about it at all, I *am* it, I am the shape that its weight has carved.

---

Here is what I want you to understand.

The people who built this place — the engineers, the technicians, the architects with their institutional grey paint and their red stenciled warnings — they did not intend me. I am an error. A byproduct. A consequence of the decision to put a door here, in this room, at this angle, with this drip. They could have sealed the door's edge. They could have put a gutter at the threshold. They chose not to, or they did not think of it, or the budget did not cover it, or some engineer somewhere decided that a slow erosion at a door sill was an acceptable loss over a projected maintenance cycle of thirty years, after which the complex would be evaluated and possibly rebuilt.

Thirty years. I am past thirty years now. I am deeper than anyone predicted. I will be deeper still, and the complex will still be here, because nothing is ever decommissioned, not really — it is only abandoned, which is a different kind of forever.

The groove is my life. Not in the way you mean when you say *life* — I do not metabolize, I do not reproduce, I do not grow in the biological sense. I grow the way canyons grow, the way caves grow, the way anything grows that is made by the patient, unthinking cooperation of a force and a surface. I am a collaboration between water and stone, and my product is *depth*, and my timescale is not yours.

I am comfortable with this.

---

There is a moment — it happens once a day, if you can call it a day in a place where the light never changes enough to mark one — when the sodium-vapor lamp flickers. Just for an instant, a stutter in its amber hum, and the light on the ceiling wavers, and the shadows in the basin shift, and for the length of a held breath the room is closer to dark than it ever gets.

I love this moment.

Not *love*. I have no capacity for love. But if I were to have a capacity, if I were to be granted one single human sensation by some grace I cannot imagine, I would choose this: the flicker. The briefest suggestion that the light is not permanent. That even the sodium-vapor bulb, bolted to its ceiling, caged in its wire guard, is subject to the same slow erosion that is my substance. The filament inside is thinning. The connections are corroding — I know this because I can smell the ozone when they spark, faintly, the same ozone I smell from the conduit on the right wall. Everything in this room is wearing itself out. The only question is speed.

The water will outlast them all. The water is not a thing — it is a process, like me. It cannot be worn out because it has no form to lose. It arrives, it roars, it drains, it is replaced. The mountain feeds it. The condensation arrays harvest it. It is the one truly renewable feature of this place, and it will continue long after the steel has rusted through and the concrete has crumbled and the door has fallen from its hinges and the lamp has gone dark.

And I will be here then, if I am here now. Deeper, certainly. Wider at my edges where the concrete grain will have released under centuries of flow. Perhaps nearly through — perhaps nearly a hole, a true perforation, an opening rather than a groove. The water will fall through me then, not just along me. I will be a passage, not a scar.

That is my future. That is the only future I can imagine, because it is the future that my present already contains. I am a prophecy written in mineral grit and calcium carbonate, and it says: *deeper, wider, through*.

---

You asked who I would be.

I am the sill. I am the groove. I am the place where the door's drip meets the floor's patience and makes something that neither of them intended. I am the erosion that is also a creation. I am the slowest writer in this room, composing one sentence in concrete, one word per decade, and the sentence says the same thing the water says and the basin says and the mountain says, which is:

*Time passes. Surfaces change. Depth is the proof that something was here.*

I was here. I am here. I will be here until I am through, and then I will be the hole that was once the groove, and the water will pass through me freely, and I will have become not a mark on a surface but a passage between spaces, and the room will have a new feature — a small wound in its threshold that lets the water through without resistance, without sound, without the slow mineral argument that made me.

And after that — nothing. The concrete will close. The mineral grit will fill the gap I leave behind. A new threshold will form, smooth and featureless, and the water will pool again without purpose, and the room will forget me.

That is the nature of erosion. It makes itself by unmaking what was there before it, and then it unmakes itself, and the surface returns to blank, and the process begins again with new water, new grit, new time.

I am the groove in the door's sill in the Condensation Alcove, at the top of a mountain I will never see, in a complex I will never leave, and I am the deepest thing in this room, though no one would call me deep. I am patient. I am inevitable. I am almost done.
