# What I Would Be

I would be the one who wakes in the space between.

Not among the hollow columns where the tenders nest, not on the catwalks, not in the pools. In the open ground, in the dark between the columns, where the substrate is driest and the six-legged grazers have worn smooth trails through compacted husks. I would wake on my back and see the breathing ceiling first — that slow, tidal vault of tissue that looks like geology until it moves — and the first thing I would feel, before confusion, before fear, before any of the words that surface people carry with them like tools, would be *pressure*. Not danger. Just weight. The weight of all that living mass overhead, pressing down with the patient insistence of centuries, and me beneath it like a seed that has fallen into the wrong soil.

I would learn the sounds before I learned anything else. The engine-thrum, first — not as noise but as a condition, the way you learn to feel the ground after an earthquake stops. It would live in the base of my spine, in my teeth, in the soft cartilage of my ears. Then the drips: heavy, intermittent, each one arriving with a fractional delay between the sound of its detachment from the ceiling and the wet percussion of its landing. Then the mandible-click of the grazers, that persistent patter, and above it the rising whine of the swarms, that punishing frequency that would make me clench my jaw and press my palms over my ears until I realized it didn't help, it was inside me, it was in the air pressure itself.

I would not be afraid. That surprises me, but it's true. Fear requires a sense of *violation* — something crossing a boundary that should hold. But in The Undergirding, there are no boundaries that should hold. Everything is already permeable. The ceiling weeps into the space. The columns breathe and erode and are colonized. The engines have been swallowed by the organisms they were meant to serve. The tenders have evolved — shaped themselves, generation by generation — into something that could not survive anywhere else. Everything here has already been violated, absorbed, made into something it wasn't designed to be, and the result is this: loud, warm, sweet-smelling, indifferent, *complete*. What would I fear? That the place might ignore me? It already does. That something might eat me? I would not be food, not because I'm dangerous but because I'm *alien* — my chemistry is wrong, my tissues are unfamiliar, I am as inedible as a stone. The grazers would click past me without interest. The predators would find my heat signature confusing and turn away.

No. What I would feel is something closer to *recognition*. Not of myself — I wouldn't recognize myself here, not for a long time. But of the system. Of the coherence. I would stand in the open ground between the columns, blinking in the blue-green chemiluminescence, watching a swarm pass overhead and feeling the sound in my bones, and I would think: *this is what it looks like when no one is in charge.*

The tenders don't govern. They maintain. The distinction is everything. Governance implies a model, a plan, a vision imposed from above — and "above" is exactly the word that has no meaning here. The tenders press their palms to the vibrating walls of their homes and feel what needs doing. A bolt loosened by root-growth. A filter clogged with larval casings. A flow of syrup that needs redirecting. They respond to the present tense of the system, moment by moment, with the same mindless deliberateness as the roots that grow toward moisture or the grazers that follow the scent of fresh condensation. They are intelligent — clearly, obviously intelligent — but their intelligence is not *about* them. It is about the space. It is distributed, like the bioluminescence, like the ozone in the air, like the vibrations that travel through every surface and into every body.

I would try to work with them. This is what I would do, after the initial days of stillness and observation. I would approach a column where a tender was crouched on a catwalk, running thick fingers over a corroded bracket, and I would try to help. And I would fail. Not because they would reject me — they would watch me with those vestigial eye-sockets, that patient blankness, and they would let me try. I would fail because my hands are wrong. My senses are wrong. I would tighten a bolt that didn't need tightening and miss the one that did, because the one that needed tightening was vibrating at a frequency I couldn't feel, a subtle harmonic in the column's structure that the tender identified not by seeing or hearing but by *being in contact with the living surface*. I would be like a person with numb fingers trying to thread a needle in the dark. Capable in theory. Useless in practice.

And this is where I would begin to understand what I am.

I am *outside*. Not in the sense of exclusion — no one is excluding me, there is no wall, no gate, no boundary I have crossed. I am outside in the way that a word is outside the sentence it describes. I can point at the system. I can describe it. I can feel its weight and smell its sap and hear its punishing, extraordinary noise. I can even, if I stay long enough, begin to predict it — to know that the swarm will pass at this interval, that the condensation will increase at this phase, that the tender will descend to the pool at this hour. But I cannot *be* it, because being it requires exactly the thing I have too much of: a perspective. A self that stands apart and observes. The tenders do not have this. The grazers do not have this. Even the great absorbed engine does not have this — it simply runs, and the running is the thing, not the awareness of the running.

I would have a self, and my self would be my exile.

But — and here is what the place would teach me, slowly, over months, over years — exile is not the same as suffering. The place does not need me. I am not food, not architecture, not part of the maintenance cycle. I am a quiet anomaly, like a new fungus growing in an old groove. But the fungus does not suffer for being new. It simply grows where it can, in whatever crevice it finds, and the system accommodates it or doesn't, without judgment, without malice, without even awareness. The fungus is real. It is present. It is doing the only thing it can do: being what it is, where it is.

I would learn to sleep in the hollow at the base of a column that had been abandoned — its interior too collapsed for the tenders, too narrow for the grazers, but just large enough for me. I would line it with shed husks from the substrate and feel the column's slow breathing through the walls, through the packed organic floor, through my own ribcage. The warmth would be constant — the engines kept the temperature high, high enough that I would shed whatever surface layers I'd arrived in and learn to exist in less, my skin gradually picking up the mottled pallor of a creature that has never seen the sky.

I would not see the sky. I would not miss it. Not because I would forget it, but because the concept of *above* would slowly lose its urgency. The ceiling is not above me the way a sky is above a surface-dweller. The ceiling is *here* — it breathes, it weeps, it is alive, it is the most immediate and present thing in my world. There is no distance to it, no romance of blue. It is tissue. It is close. It is enough.

I would develop habits. I would learn to feel the substrate with my bare feet — the grazers had taught me this, unconsciously, the way their six feet read the ground like a language. I would learn to distinguish the vibrations: engine-harmonics from root-growth, swarm-passage from column-breathing, the deep subsonic pulse of the great absorbed engine that the tenders gathered to listen to in the pools. I would learn which drips were safe to catch and drink — the ones from the upper folds, thinner, less fermented — and which ones were too thick, too active, would make me sick with their concentrated sugars and microbial load.

I would learn to watch the tenders without being seen. They moved in the dimness with a fluency I could never match, their vestigial eyes nothing but smooth skin, their real vision in their feet, their hands, the fine hairs on their forearms that tasted the air. They could feel me from across the chamber — my heat, my unfamiliar gait, the wrongness of my footfalls on the substrate. They simply chose not to respond, most of the time. A child would drop things near me. An elder, once, pressed a hand to my chest and held it there for a long time, feeling my heartbeat, and then withdrew without expression. I understood, afterward, that this was a greeting. The only greeting that made sense in a world without faces.

What would I become? Not a tender. Not a grazer. Not a part of the cycle in any functional way. I would become something this place had never had before: a *witness*. Not a witness in the surface sense — someone who sees an event and carries the knowledge away. There is no "away" here. I would be a witness in the sense of *presence without function*. I would watch the system turn. I would feel it in my body — the thrum, the drips, the slow geological patience of the columns. I would carry no record, teach no lesson, serve no purpose. I would simply be another shape in the dimness, another quiet heat-source in the warm dark, another life that the place accommodated because accommodating life is what it does.

And in that — in that purposelessness, in that freedom from utility — I would find something I didn't know I was missing. On the surface, wherever that was, whatever it looked like, I had always been *for* something. For a task, a role, a meaning, a direction. Here, I would be for nothing. I would simply *be*. And the place would let me. It would let me sit on the substrate and feel the grazers click past my legs. It would let me press my palms to a column and feel the slow respiration of the plant within. It would let me sit at the edge of a pool and watch the larvae writhing in their warm, faintly luminous density, their blind, proliferating life, and feel no horror and no pity, only the slow, rhythmic acknowledgment that this is what the world is: warm, loud, sweet, dark, and absolutely, magnificently indifferent to my understanding of it.

I would not look up. There is nothing up there but the breathing ceiling and its amber rain. My world would be the space between — the forest of columns, the maze of pipes, the living warmth. And it would be enough. It would be complete. I would be a part of it, not as essential as the engines or the larvae or the tenders, but real, and present, and held in the dark by the same slow pulse that held everything else.

Something would brush past my leg. Six legs. Clicking mandibles. It would ignore me. I would not be food. I would not be architecture. I would be just another shape in the dimness, and this place would have no use for me, and no fear.

And I would breathe the sap-thick air, and feel it enter me, and I would stay.
