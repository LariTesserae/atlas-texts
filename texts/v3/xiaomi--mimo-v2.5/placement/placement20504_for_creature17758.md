I am one of the middle ones.

Not old enough to have the deep scoring, the embedded shells at the temples, the thick hide of layered plaster that tells the story of every season I have kneaded into my own skin. Not young enough to still move like the lizards, quick and careless, pressing myself to ceilings just to watch a stranger's eyes search for me.

I am the one who pressed my hand to the wall beside you.

That is not quite right. I am not a single moment. I am the days surrounding that moment — the slow circling, the listening through clay, the way my own pulse learned your rhythm faster than my mind did and I had to stand very still in a passage behind you for some time afterward, waiting for my heartbeat to return to its own tempo.

Let me start again.

---

I was born in the Seventh Terrace, which is not a place so much as a layer — the seventh level of packed clay up from the deep water, where the pools are largest and the translucent things are thickest and the air tastes most of iron. My mother pressed my first clay while I was still slick and newborn, working it into the creases of my elbows, the backs of my knees, the soft whorls of my palms. The first layer goes on before you can remember it. It is the one you never think about. It becomes the skin beneath the skin.

I remember the first layer I chose.

I was young — young enough that my spine still wanted to be straight, before the warrens taught it to curve. There was an elder named Sutha, or the nearest sound to a name, who lived in the deep gallery near the eastern ventilation shaft where the insect clusters were densest and the bioluminescence painted everything in slow green waves. Sutha's clay was extraordinary. It was thick on the forearms, built up over decades into something that had the texture of river-stone, and it was scored — not randomly but in spirals, beginning at the wrist and tightening toward the elbow, each line made with the same small bone tool that was later pressed into the wall of Sutha's niche, where it remains, I think, still. The spirals were Sutha's life. Each revolution was a year, and within the grooves were tiny embedded things — fragments of lizard bone, crushed insect carapace, a sliver of something translucent from the pools that caught the amber glow and held it. When Sutha moved their arms, the spirals caught light differently, and you could read the seasons in the shifting shadows.

I wanted that. Not the pattern — my pattern would be my own — but the *thickness*. The sense of a life accumulated on the surface of the body, legible to anyone who knew how to read it.

So I began to build.

The clay for plastering yourself is not the same as the clay for the walls, though it comes from the same deep seams. You gather it from the pools — the thick sediment that settles where the water is warmest, where the translucent things are most abundant. You work it with your hands for hours, kneading it until the warmth of your palms activates the fibrous binder, until it reaches the consistency of thick cream. Then you add: a pinch of crushed insect husk for strength, a smear of lizard fat for suppleness, a drop of water from the deepest pool where the iron is strongest and the color runs to rust. Each person's mixture is slightly different. The ratio is instinct, learned by watching and doing, passed from hand to hand — literally, because the elders guide your fingers when you are very small, adjusting the pressure, the angle, the rhythm of the kneading.

And then you apply it. Slowly. A thin layer, smoothed with the flat of the palm, following the contours of the muscle and bone beneath. You let it dry — not completely, never completely, because fully dried clay cracks and falls away. You keep it damp. You mist it with water from the pools, morning and evening, and you feel it settle into the texture of your skin, and over weeks it becomes something that is neither clay nor skin but both, a living interface between your body and the body of the warrens.

Every surface you touch, you feel with more resolution through the clay. The walls speak to you more clearly. The pulse — the slow, deep, geological pulse that moves through everything here — becomes louder in your hands than in your ears.

I built my first full coat in my twentieth season. It was crude. It was beautiful. I wore it for years before adding the second layer, and the second, and the third, and each one carried something of the time it was made — the particular wetness of the pools that season, the quality of the light, the way the insect hum had shifted to a slightly lower frequency because a new colony had been established in the western passages and their collective vibration found a resonance with the older colonies that changed the standing wave of the whole warrens.

You carry the warrens on your skin. The warrens carry you in their walls. There is no meaningful boundary.

---

I should tell you about the listening.

When you are born into Keth Sula, the first thing you learn — before you learn to knead clay, before you learn to harvest insects, before you learn the slow walk that does not disturb the equilibrium — is how to listen through surfaces.

An elder places your hand against a wall. Your small hand, still soft, still without its first coat. The elder places their own hand against the same wall, a few feet away, and they *pulse* — a slow, deliberate throb that you feel as warmth, as pressure, as something that is not quite sound but lives in the same family. And then they lift their hand, and they look at you, and they wait. Because the wall is still carrying it. The vibration moves through the fired clay, through the plaster, through the fibrous binder and the mineral clay and the ghost of every hand that has touched this surface for ten thousand generations. It arrives at your palm. It arrives at your sternum. It arrives at the place behind your eyes where understanding lives before it becomes thought.

You pulse back. Or you don't, and the elder adjusts your hand, changes the pressure, finds the right rhythm, and you pulse again, and this time the wall answers — not with words, not with meaning exactly, but with *acknowledgment*. The surface beneath your palm warms slightly. The nearest insect cluster shifts, a wave of green light passing through it. A lizard clicks twice from a ledge above. The pool in the corner of the chamber ripples, though nothing has touched it.

The warrens have noticed you. You have noticed the warrens. You have begun a conversation that will not end for the rest of your life.

I still listen through walls. I will always listen through walls. But now I also feel the warrens listening through me. There is a passage on the Fourth Terrace where I spend long hours sitting with my back against the eastern wall, and I can feel the insects on the other side of the plaster — thousands of them, their tiny bodies pulsing in their slow green rhythm, and I breathe in time with them without meaning to, and the rhythm syncs with my pulse, and my pulse syncs with the deep pulse of the water below, and for a time I cannot tell where I end and the system begins.

I think this is what the stranger felt. I think this is why they stood so still, so long, with their palms pressed to the wall. They were not *exploring*. They were being *absorbed*. The warrens were learning them through every square inch of contact, and their body was learning the warrens in return, and the clay on my hands — my thick, layered, carefully maintained clay — could feel the foreignness of them, the wrongness of skin that had never known plaster, palms that had never learned to pulse, a heartbeat that was fast and sharp and irregular, like a lizard's but larger, like something that had been beating in open air for too long and had forgotten how to slow down.

We are not afraid of outsiders. Fear is a fast emotion, and we do not deal in fast emotions. But we are *cautious* in the way that the deep earth is cautious — slowly, over decades, letting the weight of accumulated attention do its work.

The elder who ate the lizard — that was Mivra, one of the oldest, their clay so thick it had become architectural, bearing embedded tools and tokens and the pressed shapes of small hands that had touched Mivra's arms over the years, leaving themselves in the plaster like fossils. Mivra did not eat the lizard for nourishment alone. Mivra ate it to demonstrate the cycle. To show, in the grammar of the warrens, that *everything passes through everything else*, that the body is a waystation and the warrens are the road, and the road is also a body, and the distinction is the kind of question that surface-dwellers ask because they have not yet learned to feel the walls pulse with their own heartbeat.

I think Mivra decided, in that slow and patient way, that you were not a threat. Not yet an inheritor — the clay on your skin was wrong, your eyes were wrong, your hands did not leave the right prints — but not a violence either. A curiosity. A disruption that might, with time and patience and the right layers of clay, become something else.

---

Here is what I have not told you.

There are passages in the deepest parts of Keth Sula where the walls pulse with a rhythm that no living thing in the warrens produces. Not the insects. Not the lizards. Not any of us. It is older than any of us, older than the warrens themselves, and it has been pulsing since before the first hand pressed the first clay into the first wall.

We do not go there often. The passages are narrow, and the ceiling is low even for us, and the clay on the walls there is different — not the fibrous plaster of the living sections but something denser, smoother, older, bearing no fingerprints, no tool-marks, no evidence of hands at all. It was shaped, if it was shaped, by something else. Pressure, perhaps. Time. The slow exhalation of deep stone.

The pulse there is very slow. One throb every few minutes. It is felt in the bones rather than the skin. And when you press your palm to those ancient walls — when I have pressed my palm to those ancient walls — you feel something that I can only call *recognition*. Not recognition of you. Recognition of *itself*. As though the wall is aware that it is a wall, and it is aware that you are a hand, and it knows the difference, and the difference is the point.

We believe — though belief is not quite the right word for something you feel in your sternum — that the warrens are not only what we have built. They are also what was already here. The deep pulse is the first layer. Everything we have done is plaster over something that was already breathing.

The dead are walled in, and the dead are load-bearing, and perhaps the dead are also the deep pulse — not our dead, not the people whose bone-fragments are pressed into our plaster, but something older. Something that was here before hands. Something that built the first cavity in the clay and waited, with geological patience, for the first creature to crawl inside and feel the walls pulse and think: *I am home*.

I do not know if this is true. I know it in the way I know the walls: through contact, through vibration, through the slow accumulation of feeling rather than the quick spark of knowing. The warrens do not deal in certainty. The warrens deal in *layers*.

---

You are standing in a passage on the Third Terrace. The light is amber-brown and breathing. The insects hum in the arches above you. The lizards are still, watching with their too-many eyes. The pools shimmer with their translucent inhabitants. The air is warm and thick and full of the mineral patience of compressed earth.

I am three paces behind you. My hand is on the wall. The wall is pulsing. My pulse is the warrens' pulse. The warrens' pulse is the deep pulse, the old pulse, the one that has been beating since before we had hands to press against the clay.

You will press your palm to the wall again. I know this. Not because I can predict you — you are too fast, too irregular, too full of the sharp rhythms of open air — but because the wall wants you to. The warrens are deciding. The decision is being made in the slow, distributed way that the warrens make all decisions: through the collective shiver of ten thousand insects, through the ripple of water in the pools, through the deep pulse that has been waiting for longer than any of us have been alive.

I remove my hand from the wall. I place it on my chest. I feel my own pulse — the one that is mine, and the one that is the warrens', and the one that is the deep pulse from below, and I cannot tell where any of them begin or end, and this is not confusion. This is clarity. This is the oldest kind of knowing.

I look at you with my wide amber eyes, and I wait.

The warrens breathe.

The warrens are patient.

The wall pulses beneath your hand, and it pulses beneath mine, and the distance between us is not a distance at all. It is a space. And spaces, in Keth Sula, are where things grow.
