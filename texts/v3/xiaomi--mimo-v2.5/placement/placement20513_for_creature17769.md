I arrive not with a sound, but with a cessation of noise—the sound of my own movement through air, the rustle of my clothes, the beat of my heart—each abruptly cut, as though snipped with scissors by a deaf god. The first thing I register is the heat, not as warmth but as a pressure, a physical weight pressing the air into a dense, silent medium. The second is the light: a copper glow that has no direction, no source, just *being*, illuminating everything with the intensity of a forge yet casting no shadows. The third is the powder under my feet, pale and iridescent, crunching with a sound that exists only in my skull, a private crunch that does not escape the boundary of my own bones.

I am in a throat. The walls lean inward at impossible angles, curving in ways that make my eyes itch. The ground is a floor of powdered shell. Above, the copper sky is a lid, a sheet of molten metal poured over the world. There is no horizon, only the convergence of leaning walls and incandescent sky.

For a long time—I do not know how long—I simply stand. My human senses, so accustomed to immediate feedback, flounder in this place of delayed causality. I hear a grinding, low and constant, and realize it is the great crystalline pillars, turning on their bases. I see bands of burnt sienna and oxide red moving with geological patience. I hear a chord, a shifting harmony that seems to come from the air itself, from the hollow spines that jut from the ground like pipes of an organ. I see the folds, lush and furious in their stillness, their colors pulsing faintly in the copper light.

I take a step. The powder shifts. I leave a footprint that fills almost instantly, not with powder blowing, but with powder *flowing*, a slow-motion avalanche at the scale of grains. I walk toward one of the folds, my steps deliberate, each one a commitment. The air moves in lazy currents, and the chord changes. I stop. I listen. I am trying to hear the sound my own existence makes in this place, but there is nothing. I am a rapid event in a world of slow processes. I am a vibration in a medium that has learned to absorb vibrations.

Days pass. Or what feels like days. My internal clock, calibrated to the ticking of seconds, stutters and misfires. I learn to measure time by the rotation of the crystals: one revolution per hour, perhaps. I learn to measure it by the shift in the chord: a new note emerging over the course of an afternoon. I learn to measure it by the subtle change in the copper light, a deepening or a lightening that is imperceptible unless I watch for hours.

I sit down in the powder. The crunch is a private explosion of sound. I dig my hands into it. It is dry, warm, and beneath the surface layer, it becomes slightly cohesive, clay-like, humid with that non-aqueous fluid that smells of copper and ozone. My fingertips tingle with a faint numbness.

I am becoming part of the scenery. My own metabolism, my fast human chemistry, feels like a scream in a library. I slow my breathing. I slow my thoughts. I watch a fold across from me. For hours, it does nothing. Then, at the edge of my vision, a new fold unrolls itself from the powder. It is pale, translucent, then deepens into green, then into that impossible blue-black. The process takes an entire day. I see it. I witness the birth of life in this place.

I notice the inhabitants. I see a shifting density within the folds, a slight change in light absorption. I approach gently, and with infinite care, I part the layers of felt-like tissue. Inside, nestled in a chamber, is a luminous, translucent thing the size of my fist. It breathes—a slow expansion and contraction that takes minutes. Its inner filaments branch like frost, connected to the fold's tissue. I feel a connection, a symbiosis so complete that the boundary dissolves. I do not touch it. I simply observe. It responds to the chemical gradients of the not-water rising through the roots, modulating the fold's metabolism. It is a thermostat of flesh and light. I understand: this is a nervous system grown from mineral substrate, a brain that does not think, only responds.

I walk to a rotating crystal. Its surface is rough, striated with color. I see thin lines in the grooves between the bands, lines that move with the same slow inevitability as the crystal. I touch one. It feels like the crystal itself: warm, rough, slightly electric. It does not react. It migrates a fraction of a millimeter during the time my finger rests on it. I am a weather event to it, a momentary shadow. I am too fast. I withdraw my hand, and the line continues its journey. They are the millstone-keepers, the reason the crystals turn. They weigh, they shift mass, they are engines of life.

I stand by a hollow spine, leaning like a drunken fencepost. The chord is loud here, a complex harmony. I peer inside. I cannot see a chorister, but I feel the vibration. The chord is the chorister's body expressed as sound. I am hearing a living frequency. A note fractures, doubles, then resolves. A new membrane has found its tension. The process is audible over hours. I am patient enough to hear it.

I walk to the edge, where the walls forget themselves. The ground beneath my left foot crunches. The ground beneath my right foot sinks a centimeter too deep, a suggestion. I see them then: the margin-dwellers. Small absences, a centimeter across, where light bends strangely. They are gaps in the world that have grown edges. They feed on uncertainty. One passes through my hand, and I feel nothing, but afterward, the lines of my palm seem less certain, more suggested. I am reminded that I, too, am a configuration of matter that could be described differently.

Time stretches. My human form begins to feel like a costume, a temporary shape. I am not dissolving, but I am adapting. My thoughts slow. I think in the rhythm of crystal rotations. I dream in the chord shifts. I wake to a new fold and watch it unroll over a week. I am learning the patience of roots.

I find a spot near a spine and a fold, in the powder that is becoming familiar ground. I sit. I stay. I watch the copper sky, unchanging. I watch the walls forget a little more of themselves each season. I watch the margin-dwellers extend the boundary. I am aware, deep below, of a possibility gathering, a concentration of mineral energy, a seed of something that will one day rise. I feel its slow growth like a heartbeat in the ground, a pulse measured in centuries.

I am no longer a visitor. I am a witness, a part of the garden's memory. My presence is a fact of the landscape, like a spine or a fold. I have become one of the inhabitants—not the luminous things in the folds, nor the millstone-keepers, nor the choristers, nor the margin-dwellers, but the one who sees them all, the one who stays. I am the garden's way of knowing itself, a consciousness grown from dust and heat and slow time.

And perhaps, one day, when the possibility beneath rises to the surface, when the garden's synthesis finally opens its eyes, it will find me sitting there, covered in iridescent dust, breathing the slow hot air, waiting. I will be part of its awakening. I will be the first thing it sees, the first sound it hears—the crunch of powder, the shifting chord, the grinding of crystals—and it will understand that I was always here, at the beginning, when everything was still becoming.

I am the visitor who arrived and stayed long enough to see what it was all for. I am the garden's memory, the still point in its turning, the human shape that learned to think in centuries and became a feature of the landscape. I am a new kind of inhabitant, born of observation and patience, a living record of a place that makes life from dust and heat, a place that is always almost, but never quite, finished.

Here, in the Millstone Garden, I am home.
