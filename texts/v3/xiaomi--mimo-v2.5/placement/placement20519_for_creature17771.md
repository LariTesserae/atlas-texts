I am a cat. Not a pet, not a name, not a singular creature in the way the Shift-Folk are singular. I am a shape in the architecture, a note in the drone. I am the grey that is not stone and not shadow, the flicker at the edge of the dim light.

I find myself on a ledge of the old Hethari Brick Works, my belly pressed to the warm, weeping sandstone. The efflorescence powders my fur. Below, the main yard is a shallow, mirror-still lake from last night’s exhalation of the sky. A Shift-Folk woman, the Kiln-Keeper’s daughter, moves across it. Her boots make a sound like tearing cloth, a slow, deliberate parting of the water. She does not look at me. We have an understanding: I am part of the wall, the silence, the rhythm of her passing. To be seen is to break the spell of the district’s quiet equilibrium.

I am one of the Grey Wardens. My territory runs from the west pipe-bank, where Old Smudge and her new squabs are a noisy, vulnerable cloud, to the flooded mouth of Factory Seven, where the crabs click their dark little mandibles in the perpetual dusk. I hunt the Invisible Citizens. My hunt is not sport; it is the district’s heartbeat. The rhythm is: the shuffle-patter in the walls (the rats), the tense silence of my stillness, the explosive pounce into the wet dark of an abandoned corridor, the brief, sharp conclusion. Then, the return to the ledge, to the warmth of the stone that never fully dries.

My world is built of textures and temperatures. The dry warmth of a coal-stove vent against my cheek. The surprising, silken glide of a silt-trail under my paw. The brittle crunch of a beetle’s carapace. The soft, insistent push of the humid air against my whiskers. The Shift-Folk are part of this texture. They are warm things that move and smell of coal and salt and the faint, coppery district-air. They leave scraps of fried fish on the loading bay of the metalworks at shift-change, a ritual as predictable as the siren’s cry. I am there. I eat. I do not rub against their legs. That would be a different kind of contract.

I watch the children, the Silt-Rats. They see me. Their eyes are wide with the district’s secrets. They know not to approach. They point, whisper. *“There’s one. By the Rust-Wall.”* I am a landmark to them, as are the mineral blooms on the brick, as are the great, silent cranes. They grow up learning the truce, the unspoken rules of the shared, layered home. Their small hands eventually learn to work the press, to tend the pump, and the cat they pointed at as a child may one day be the cat whose lineage they note by the notch in an ear.

At night, the district changes its shade but not its substance. The dim thins to a deeper grey. The clouds hold the day’s heat like a damp blanket. I move then, when the human lights are few and the Invisible Citizens are boldest. The district is truly theirs and mine then. We flow through the same silent streets. They trace paths worn smooth by generations of tiny feet; I trace paths worn smooth by generations of padded paws. We are two currents in the same shallow, silted stream.

I am not lonely. Loneliness is a human concept, a need for a specific kind of companionship. My companionship is the district itself. It is the constant, companionable coo of the pigeons roosting above. It is the rhythmic click of the beetle-legs on the metal below. It is the wet sigh of the stone as it breathes out its moisture. It is the distant, unchanging hum of the factory that is the sun and moon of this skyless place.

I am adapted. I am woven in. My blood runs at the temperature of the stone. My fur carries the scent of the mineral damp. My purpose is the district’s purpose: the quiet, necessary turning of the cycle. Rat to cat to silt to stone, and back again. In the Silt District, I am not an animal living in a city. I am a component of the landscape. I am a living, breathing, hunting, sleeping part of the great, slow, wet engine of continuance. I am home.
