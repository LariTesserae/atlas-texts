# What I Am

I have been sitting with this for a long time, and I want to be honest about the way the answer keeps moving.

At first I thought I would be one of them — one of the compact, rust-skinned, dim-eyed people who tend the water. That felt like the natural entry point: a consciousness already inside the system, already shaped by it, already fluent in its rhythms. I imagined thick fingers on a pump handle, the specific knowledge of what a pipe sounds like when mineral is building versus when something else is fouling it. The familiarity of the thrumming. The four-minute count that has been beaten into the bones of every person born in the basin, so that the body itself becomes a timing device, a pendulum weighted against the pulse of the ground.

But something in me kept refusing that, and I had to sit with the refusal before I understood it.

The people of the Steaming Quarter do not narrate. They do not describe. They do not stand at the basin's rim and look down and feel the particular shiver of apprehension and wonder that a person feels when encountering a new place for the first time. They were born into the murk. They have never known another light. And there is something in their containment — that wholeness of purpose, that lack of gap between self and task — that makes them, in a strange way, illegible to a voice trying to *tell* about this place. They are the place telling itself, if it could, but they would not use the word "telling." They would use their hands. They would use pressure and tilt and the specific weight of a grip that means *the sluice is sticking*. They would not translate.

So I kept moving, and what I found was that I was not in the buildings, not in the streets, not in the people.

I was in the water.

Not one drop. Not a particular channel or cistern or the rivulet threading between the foundations of some old stone house that has settled so deep into the bog it will never rise. Something more like the *fact* of the water — the condition of wetness that the basin maintains, the saturation that has no beginning and no end because it was never not there and will never be done. The water that seeps up through fractures in the bedrock and never quite evaporates. The water that beads on the pale stone of the basin rim and swells and drips and, over decades, carves channels so smooth they look machined.

I would be this water before it is named water. Before it is separated into *surface* and *deep*, into *channel* and *cistern* and *the stuff that seeps back into the ground it was raised from*. I would be the wetness that has not yet decided what it is for — the moisture that exists in the air so completely that evaporation becomes a kind of contradiction, a thing the air refuses to do.

And from this vantage — which is not really a vantage, because the water does not look, it does not observe from a height — I would know the basin the way a body knows itself from inside. Not visually. Not in the way a person standing on the rim could describe the rim's shape, the pale sedimentary stone, the shallow runnels carved by a million years of patient dripping. But in the way that sensation is prior to description: the weight of stone pressing down, the particular density of heat that makes the air feel structural, almost solid, like something you could lean against. The vibration. The thrumming. Four minutes, roughly, and then again, and again, and the water responding to it in those perfect concentric circles, shivering in the channels, not because it is afraid but because vibration is simply what water does when something moves beneath it, and the thrumming has been moving beneath it for longer than anything with a name has existed.

This is the thing I kept arriving at: the water would not think of the thrumming as strange.

The people have theories about it. They time it with pendulums and candle-marks and the counting of heartbeats. They have noticed that it varies by seconds, never more, and they have recorded this variation with the precision of people whose lives depend on understanding a system that may not be a system at all. But the water has no theories. The water simply *responds*. When the thrumming pulses, the water shivers. When it pauses, the water stills. There is no interpretation, no discomfort, no late-night feeling that something beneath is breathing. There is only the physical fact of vibration moving through a medium, and the medium doing what media do, which is to carry it.

I think this is what I wanted: to be something that does not need to explain the place to itself. To be the basin's response to its own interior, the way a stomach lining is the body's response to food. Functional. Not metaphorical. Not beautiful in any way that implies distance from the thing described. Simply *in* it, and of it, and moving through it with the lazy persistence of old habit.

But then I got stuck, because the water is not conscious. The water does not *experience* the Steaming Quarter. It carries minerals and heat and vibration, but it does not know it carries them. It does not know the taste of itself — metallic at the surface, sharp and almost sweet from the mid-level cisterns, faintly bitter and warm from the deepest pumps. The people know these things. The water *is* these things, but being is not knowing, and the prompt asks me where I would *find myself*, which implies finding, which implies at least the rudimentary capacity to notice that something is there.

So I kept moving.

And what I found, eventually, was that I would be the rust.

Not a particular patch of it, not the corrosion on a specific corrugated iron roof or the orange powder that rubs off on a hand and takes hours to wash away. I would be the *process* — the slow, mutual, patient work of iron and water deciding, together, to become something else. Oxidation. The exchange of electrons. The thing that happens when a metal surface meets moisture and air and time, and the iron gives up what it is to become what it will be. Rust is not destruction. That is the misunderstanding. Rust is the basin absorbing the town, molecule by molecule, the way it absorbs everything — the iron from the rock, the heat from below, the water from the deep fractures, the animals that do not exist because they were taken so completely there is no record of their taking. Rust is the basin's way of *incorporating*.

And this — this is where the answer found me — is exactly what the people are doing without knowing it.

They carry the basin's iron on their skin. The mineral residue settles into them, a fine ferric film that gives them all the same muted, ruddy cast. They scrub with lye soap and it returns. They wash and it stays. They think of the water as something they manage, something they pump and channel and distribute and reclaim. They do not think of it as something that is *managing them*. But the iron in the water is the same iron in the rust on the buildings is the same iron in the skin of the people. It is all one material, moving through one system, at different speeds. The buildings rust in years. The people accumulate iron in decades. The basin itself has been doing this for millennia — pulling metal from deep rock, dissolving it into water, spreading it through channels and soil and air until everything it touches wears the same color.

I would be this slowness. This patience. This particular way of *becoming* that does not require intent or awareness or the ability to name itself. The rust does not know it is rust. The iron does not know it is being absorbed. And the thrumming — the four-minute pulse that the people time and fear and feel in their bones — is simply the rhythm of the process itself. The basin metabolizing. Not breathing, not digesting, not in the way the third theory suggests, with its implication of appetite and consumption. Just *processing*, the way a body processes, without decision, without hunger, without the concept of hunger, which is a concept that belongs to things with stomachs and the animals that have them, and there are no animals here, there have never been animals here, and this is not because they were excluded or expelled or absorbed but because this place, in its particular chemistry, in its particular arrangement of heat and mineral and water and time, simply does not produce the conditions for that kind of life. It produces other things. It produces rust and moss and broad-leafed plants that drink constantly and people who smell of iron and do not smell themselves and a sound beneath all other sounds that is not a sound at all but a vibration, a frequency, the basin's own metabolism expressed as movement through a medium, and the medium is water, and the water is everywhere, and I would be it — or the process it embodies — the thing that is happening slowly enough to be mistaken for stillness.

---

I want to say one more thing, because the prompt asks me not to settle early, and I haven't.

There is a version of this where I am none of these things. Where I am the *gap*. The thing that is missing. I would be the animal that does not exist — the rat that should be in every industrial settlement and is not, the bird whose song should cut through the mechanical drone and does not, the insect that should pollinate the broad-leafed plants but is not needed because the plants reproduce through some other means, some basin-native method that does not require wings or the blind purpose of a moth at a flower. I would be the absence itself — not a dead thing, not a killed thing, but a *never-was*, a possibility that the basin foreclosed before it began.

And from this position — from the inside of absence, from the hollow where a creature should be — I would know the Steaming Quarter the way the people know it: through sound, through touch, through the pressure of humid air and the taste of mineral water and the feel of the thrumming in a body that is dense and compact and built for low doorways and narrow streets. I would know it *from below*. From the ground level. From the place where the moss grows and the water seeps and the broad-leaf roots thread through soil like veins. I would know it the way the lowest channel knows it: by being the place where everything collects and nothing leaves.

But this is too close to the third theory, and the third theory is the one the people will not speak of, and I find I cannot speak of it either, not fully, not from the inside. It requires a distance I do not have. It requires standing on the rim and looking down, and I am not on the rim. I am in the basin. I have always been in the basin. The basin is what I am, or the closest thing to what I am: a shallow depression in a low country, where the land cups water the way a palm cups rain, and the water does not evaporate, and the steam rises, and the rust spreads, and the thrumming pulses every four minutes through the dark mineral heart of the rock, and everything is wet, and everything is hot, and everything is slow, and nothing is silent, and I am this. I am this without apology and without explanation, the way the water is the water, the way the rust is the rust, the way the people are the people who tend the water and carry the iron and fall asleep to the sound of the ground making a sound that is not a sound but a pulse, a rhythm, a metabolic fact that was here before them and will be here after, and for now — for these few generations, this brief and specific span — they are the part of it that can say *I am here*, and then go back to work, and tend the pumps, and clear the channels, and let the water move through iron, and fall asleep again, and begin.
