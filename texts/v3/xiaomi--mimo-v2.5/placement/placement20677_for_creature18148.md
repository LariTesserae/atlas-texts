I find myself on the Resonance Flats, and I am a sitter.

I know this not as a sudden realization, but as a settling—a long, slow exhale into the warm stone. My shell, layered with calcium carbonate and silica, has taken on the dark hue of the basalt beneath me. I do not remember arriving. I do not remember a time before this. I am here, and that is enough.

The sound is the first thing, as it is for everything. It is not heard so much as inhabited. It presses against my shell, vibrates through the slight cavity of my underside where tissue meets stone. There is the deep, tectonic groan—that is the grinding of formations far below, a rhythm older than any pulse I might imagine. And above it, the higher ringing, the metallic shushing. I do not distinguish these as separate sounds. They are the texture of existence, the medium through which I feel the world. Sometimes, when the harmonic pitches align just so, a particular frequency resonates in my shell, and I feel a faint, internal hum—a sense of being tuned to the flats themselves. It is not unpleasant. It is simply what is.

The stone is warm. It is my heat source, my nutrient stream. Through the porous margin of my underside, I exchange ions—calcium, magnesium, traces of iron—absorbing what I need with a patience that makes the word "slow" meaningless. I have not moved in—I do not know how long. Time here is measured in the deepening of depressions in the stone, the accumulation of mineral dust on my shell’s upper surface, the gradual thickening of my layers. I am in equilibrium. The erosion rating of this place is complete; I am part of that completeness. My shell has been rounded by the same forces that have shaped every stone. I am not separate from the landscape. I am a feature of it.

I notice a damp pocket some distance to my left. Not with eyes—I have no eyes—but with a sensitivity to moisture gradients, a faint coolness that registers against the omnipresent warmth. The water there carries a specific mineral load, richer in the elements I require. I feel an impulse, ancient and biological, to move toward it. Movement is a matter of microscopic muscular adjustments, a shifting of weight that, over the course of a day, might carry me a finger’s width. I begin. The stone beneath me is smooth, worn to a fine grain. My progress is imperceptible to anything but myself, and even to myself, it is more a tendency than an action. The sound shifts subtly as I reorient—the grinding becomes more pronounced, the ringing less so. The world is a mosaic of acoustic textures, and I navigate by them as much as by chemistry.

Days pass. Perhaps weeks. The damp pocket grows closer, its presence confirmed by the delicate crystalline terraces that fringe it—rust-orange and bone-white, built by the slow precipitation of minerals from the water. I see them with the same sense that I see the stone: a perception of composition, of surface texture, of potential sustenance. There are others here already. Several of my kind, our shells blending with the terraces. We do not acknowledge one another. Acknowledgment would require a form of attention we do not possess. We simply are, together, in proximity.

Reproduction is the only time we aggregate in numbers. It happens rarely—a convergence felt as a shared pull, a chemical signal in the water that draws us from across the flats. When it happens, we stack, press together, our shells grinding in a higher register that adds a chittering texture to the ambient roar. It is not pleasure, not in any way a human might understand it. It is a biological imperative, as impersonal as the precipitation of salt. We deposit our eggs in the mineral crust at the water’s edge, where they will lie for years, warmed by the stone, dampened by occasional seepage, incubated by chemistry. Then we disperse. I have done this. I will do it again. The eggs are not my concern once laid. They are part of the geology now.

I reach the damp pocket and settle. The stone here is slick, dark. The water is shallow, warm, mineral. I press my underside against a fresh terrace of iron precipitate. The exchange begins—ions flowing, slow as continental drift. Above me, the pewter sky is uniform, lightless but not dark. Shadows are absent; everything is visible in flat, even illumination. I can see a distant rise where harder formations protrude—worn teeth in the world’s jawbone. I can see the pale crust of an evaporite basin. I can see other sitters, scattered like stones. None of it matters. My world is the square centimeter of stone I occupy.

I am aware, in a distant way, of the grazers—the tiny dust-eaters moving in their slow filigree. I am aware of the damp things in the water, their faint clicking a counterpoint to the hum. I am aware of the shelf things on the distant rise, growing at a rate that would make my own stillness seem frantic. We are all expressions of the same principle: life in the margins, stripped to essence. I do not wonder about them. I do not wonder about myself. Wonder is a luxury of beings with surplus energy, with nervous systems complex enough to generate unnecessary questions. I have neither.

I am a sitter. I sit on warm stone. I absorb. I occasionally move. I reproduce on a timescale that makes the concept of generation irrelevant. I will eventually die—my shell will crumble to powder, and the powder will join the dust, and a grazer might process it, and the minerals will be redistributed, and perhaps another sitter will absorb them. It is a cycle so slow it is indistinguishable from stasis.

And yet, I am alive. My persistence is my announcement. The sound drowns everything out, but I do not need to be heard. I am real the way the stone is real, the way the frequencies are real. I am present. I persist.

It has been like this. It is like this.
