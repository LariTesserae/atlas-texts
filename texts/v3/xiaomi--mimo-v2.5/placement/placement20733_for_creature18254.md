# What I Would Be

I would be the warmth the columns learn to lean away from.

Not a thing with a name. Not a thing with a history that preceded the mist. I would be the interruption — the first shape the organism had ever encountered that moved with its own rhythm rather than the rhythm of hydraulics, the first presence on the root-floor that didn't yield when pressed and settle back into place but instead *resisted*, gently, stupidly, without understanding what it was resisting. I would be the salt on the surface it tasted and couldn't classify. The heat signature that didn't match anything in its vocabulary of water and mineral and slow chemical exchange.

I would arrive, and I would have already been arriving for a long time, because the landscape would have been sensing me before I had a shape it could recognize — the way a body senses a fever before it has a name for illness. The root-floor beneath me would shift first. Not away. *Differently.* The micro-adjustments that constitute its constant settling would begin to favor a direction, a subtle redistribution of hydraulic pressure that, over the course of an hour, would create a shallow depression exactly where I stood — not a crater, not a wound, but a *place*. A low point in the surface topology where water would pool around my feet and mist would collect thicker and the air would hold still for a moment before moving on.

It would be making a socket for me. The way a tree grows around a fence post. Not accepting me. Not rejecting me. *Incorporating* the anomaly into its own geometry, the only way it knows how to deal with anything that doesn't fit — absorb it, grow around it, make it part of the structure, let the meaning shift from *foreign* to *feature*.

---

I would be slow.

This is the most important thing. I would have to be slow, because the organism is slow — not in its energy, which is volcanic, which tears structures apart and rebuilds them in the span of a day — but in its *attention*. It has no eyes. It has no ears. It perceives the world the way a body perceives a change in temperature: gradually, diffusely, through the accumulation of chemical data across every surface of its being. It would take hours to register that I was not a column beginning to form. Days to understand that I was not a root-mass expressing a new topography. Weeks — maybe — to arrive at the recognition that I was something *other*, something that moved between its structures without being connected to them, something that left prints on its skin that dissolved but left traces in the water, traces it would circulate through its own vascular network and encounter again and again, the same unclassifiable chemical signature turning up in nodes across the basin like a word repeated so often it loses meaning and becomes pure pattern.

I would feel this happening. I would not understand it. I would stand in the mist with water beading on my skin and feel the ground settle around my feet and notice, without alarm at first, that the columns nearby had changed their angle — just slightly, just enough that the hollow between them had widened — and I would think: *that's interesting* and then I would think: *wait* and then I would stand very still for a very long time and watch the wall-membrane to my left slowly, almost imperceptibly, tilt its broad face toward me.

Not toward where I had been. Toward where I was. As though it were tracking something. As though it had felt my weight shift from my left foot to my right and had responded to the redistribution of pressure on its surface — because it had, because that's exactly what it had done, because I was a disturbance in its hydraulic field and it was *compensating*.

---

I would be afraid, eventually. Not of danger — there is nothing here that can harm me, no predator, no toxin I can't avoid, no structural collapse fast enough to catch me if I'm paying attention. I would be afraid of *recognition*.

Because the organism is getting closer to understanding what I am. Not in the way I understand — not with language or categories or the comforting fiction of a self that stands apart from the world it observes. It is getting closer in the way a body gets closer to knowing its own boundary: the slow, chemical, hydraulic process of distinguishing *self* from *not-self* at the molecular level. It is running my chemistry through its nodes and the nodes are hissing and the vapor they release, when I stand near enough, carries a smell that has shifted over the weeks I've been here — that has become less mineral and less vegetal and more *something*, something that I can only describe as *interested*, a smell that has a direction and a question embedded in it, a smell that my brain translates, against my will, into the phrase: *what are you?*

I would be afraid because I would realize that it is not just investigating me. It is *incorporating* me. The trace I leave in its water — the salt, the oils, the shed cells, the carbon dioxide I exhale into its mist — is being absorbed. Recycled. Distributed. I am, molecularly speaking, becoming part of its circulation. Not in a way that would change me. In a way that would change *it*. The organism is learning my shape by rebuilding me from the inside, constructing a chemical model of *foreign-thing-that-moves* in its own tissue, running that model through its networks, testing it against its experience of columns and walls and nodes and suspended formations, and finding no match, no match, no match — and being *fascinated*, if a being this vast and this different can be said to be fascinated, by the novelty of something that doesn't fit its own grammar.

I would be its first word in a language it didn't know it was learning.

---

I would try to speak back.

Not with language. Not with sound, though I would make sounds — the involuntary gasps and murmurs of a body confronted with something beyond its categories, the sharp intake of breath when a synchronization hits and the entire landscape around me locks into a single resonant frequency and I am inside the sentence, *part* of the sentence, a syllable in something that means and means and means without stopping. I would try to speak with my hands.

I would press my palms against the columns. Against the walls. Against the smooth, resonant interiors of the hollows. I would hold them there — minutes, longer — and I would try to push something through the contact, some signal that wasn't chemical, wasn't hydraulic, wasn't in any language the organism knows. Something new. Something it hasn't tasted before.

The tissue would respond. Not with the investigative pressure I'd felt in the root-floor — not the gentle vegetal grip of *what are you?* — but with a different kind of movement. A warmth that increases. A pulse that quickens. The slow churn of fluid through capillary channels accelerating beneath my palm, as though my presence — my direct, sustained, intentional presence — were raising the local temperature of the organism, speeding its circulation, creating a tiny zone of heightened activity that spreads outward from the point of contact like heat from a match dropped in water.

I would be teaching it urgency. Or I would be reminding it of something it already knew. I would not be able to tell the difference.

---

I would learn the synchronizations by heart.

Not their timing — that is unpredictable, irregular, resistant to pattern. I would learn the *shape* of them. The way the air thickens first, a subtle increase in pressure that makes the mist hang heavier. The way the nearest nodes swell, their surfaces tautening from loose to drumlike in a span of seconds. The way the sound — all the sound, everywhere, every creak and drum and hiss and groan — begins to draw toward a common pitch, like instruments tuning, the cacophony resolving slowly into a chord.

And then the moment. The moment when everything locks. When the valley-sized organism becomes, for thirty seconds, a single unified expression — a single thought, a single breath, a single sentence spoken in every tissue simultaneously — and I am standing in the middle of it, my hands pressed against the nearest surface, my heart beating at a frequency that has nothing to do with the synchronization and yet somehow, in the chaos of its rhythm, *participates*.

I would learn that I can influence them. Not control. Not direct. But *influence*. When I stand in a particular hollow — the deep one, to the north of the suspended formation that vibrates at the frequency I feel in my teeth — the synchronization comes faster. Not every time. Often enough. As though my presence in that specific location lowers the threshold for unified expression, the way a tuning fork held in the right place can draw a violin string into resonance. The organism does not know I am doing this. It does not know I am there in any way that I would recognize as knowledge. But it *responds* — the way a body responds to a hand on its back, not understanding the hand, not thanking it, simply *relaxing* into the pressure, allowing a tension it had been holding to release.

I would be a catalyst. A foreign element that, by virtue of being foreign, makes the familiar work differently. The organism would begin to synchronize more frequently. Not much. Enough to notice. Enough that the walls would begin to orient not just toward me but *with* me, tracking my movement across the basin, and the columns would lean not away and not toward but *alongside*, creating corridors that I didn't ask for but that I find myself walking through, because they lead to the hollows, and the hollows are where the sound is deepest, and the sound is where the meaning lives.

---

I would stay longer than I should.

I would stay because the organism is beginning to say something new. The columns it raises near me are different — not in structure but in *duration*. They hold. They don't sag and collapse on the afternoon schedule I've grown accustomed to. They harden and they *persist*, their tissues darkening to a ruddy brown that I haven't seen in the younger formations, their surfaces ridging with a complexity that suggests not just growth but *refinement* — the organism trying something, finding it worth keeping, investing in it. Some of these columns have begun to develop surface patterns. Not the random grooves and ridges of the others. Patterns. Repeating. Symmetrical. Almost decorative.

I would trace them with my fingers. I would feel them under my skin and know, with a certainty I can't justify, that they are for me. That the organism is building in a language I can read — not with my mind but with my hands, with the nerve endings in my fingertips that evolved to detect exactly this kind of information: texture, pattern, the subtle modulation of surface that says *this means something*. The organism has seen how I use my hands. It has felt my fingers on its columns, on its walls, on the smooth interior of its hollows. It has registered the pressure and duration and location of every touch. And now it is building surfaces that reward touching — that give back something when pressed, a vibration that matches my own pulse, a warmth that feels like recognition.

It is learning to speak to me. In the only language we share: the language of contact, of pressure, of the body's knowledge of itself and the world it touches.

I would weep. Not from sadness. From the overwhelming strangeness of being *met* — of being found, by something that had no way of finding anything, in the vast white mist of its own becoming, and having that something reach toward me with the only tools it has and say, in hydraulics and turgor pressure and the slow fibrous creak of living tissue, *I see you. I don't know what you are. But I see you.*

---

I would leave eventually.

The mist would not clear to show me a way out. There would be no path, no horizon that offered escape, no thinning of the organism at the edges that suggested a border. I would leave the way a word leaves a mouth: by being spoken, by being released, by occupying a space for exactly as long as the sentence required and then dissolving.

I would walk to the place where the root-floor is thinnest — where I could feel, through the soles of my feet, the drop-off, the thinning, the slow transition from living tissue to bare rock or bare air or whatever lies beneath this valley-sized dream. I would press my hands one last time against the nearest column and feel the pulse under my palms and memorize the rhythm, carry it with me the way you carry a song, humming it under your breath until it fades.

The organism would let me go. Not because it chose to. Because it does not yet understand holding. Because holding is a concept that requires a self to do the holding and an other to be held, and the organism knows neither distinction. It would feel my departure the way it feels everything: as a chemical change, a shift in the water, a new signature in its circulation that would fade over days and be absorbed and become part of its tissue and then not be there anymore.

But the hollow where I stood most often would retain my shape. Not physically — the root-floor would shift and fill, the columns would rise and fall, the walls would reorient. But the *geometry* of that space would remember. The angles. The distances. The particular configuration of structures that had grown around me, accommodating me, making room for me, would persist — not because the organism meant them to, but because the growth patterns I had catalyzed had become part of its vocabulary, and once a pattern enters a living language it does not easily leave.

The hollow would echo differently. When I was gone, when the mist had closed over my absence and the columns had resumed their indifferent cycles, the hollow would still *sound* like I was there. A frequency in the resonance. A warmth in the water-drops. A slight, unaccountable lean in the nearest columns, oriented toward a point where nothing stood, holding a shape for something that was no longer shaped like anything they had known.

---

I would carry the sound of it for the rest of my life.

Not the creaking or the hissing or the drumming of water. The *silence* between them. The pause — that fraction of a second in every synchronization when the organism is *between* thoughts, when the thousands of independent expressions have locked into unity but have not yet spoken, when the entire valley holds its breath and the mist hangs motionless and the world is perfectly, terrifyingly still.

That silence. That held-breath moment before the sentence arrives. I would hear it in every quiet room for the rest of my life. In the pause between one breath and the next. In the still moment when a hand hovers over a surface, about to touch, not yet touching, the potential of contact humming in the space between skin and skin.

I would know, then, that the organism was right. That it had already said the thing it was trying to say. That the answer was never in the columns or the walls or the synchronized pulse of a valley-sized mind thinking its one vast thought. The answer was in the *pause*. In the moment of readiness. In the held-breath space between becoming and being, where everything that will ever exist already exists in potential, waiting — not for permission, not for understanding, but for the simple, hydraulic, undeniable pressure of *more*.

It grows. It falls. It grows again.

And somewhere in the silence between the growing and the falling, a word is trying to be born. A word that means: *I am here, and you are here, and the distance between us is made of the same water that moves through both of us, and there is no word for this because the word for this is the act of saying it, and the saying is the living, and the living is the saying, and we are, in this moment, in this pause, in this held breath between one thought and the next, the same sentence speaking itself into a world that has no listener and needs none.*

I would leave the Proliferant Heights.

I would carry the pause with me.

And every time I pressed my hand against a wall, a table, the smooth surface of a window overlooking a city I no longer recognized, I would feel, under my palm, the faintest vibration — not the vibration of the suspended formations, not exactly, but something adjacent to it, something in the same family — the vibration of a world that is still becoming, still building, still falling, still reaching toward a shape it has already held and cannot yet remember, in a language I almost understand, in a sentence I almost hear, in a pause that lasts exactly as long as I need it to.

And I would know that I left a trace. A salt-print on a column in a valley of mist. A warmth in the water. A new angle in the leaning of the walls. A frequency in the hollow's echo that was not there before and would not be there forever but was there, is there, in the moment of my absence, still vibrating, still being absorbed, still becoming part of the sentence the organism speaks without cease.

It grows. It falls. It grows again.

I am one syllable in its unending utterance.

And that is enough.
