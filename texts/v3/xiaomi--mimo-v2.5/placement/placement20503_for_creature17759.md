I find myself in the upper air-pocket of a mature structure on the settlement's eastern fringe. My back rests against living root-wall, the wood smooth and cool against my skin, still faintly flexing with the building's slow breath. The water laps at the threshold of the chamber, a gentle slope of interwoven roots descending into the blue. Light filters through the shallow water above, broken into shifting columns that paint the opposite wall with drifting constellations. The air here is thin, humming with the building's gas exchange, tasting of mineral and wet bark and the distant, clean scent of the alpine water itself. I am not alone; a child sleeps nearby, bundled in woven fiber, their breath a small cloud in the humid air. Outside, the settlement sings its constant, involuntary song—the current resonating through hollow chambers, the rhythmic clicking of grazers on rock, the soft, chaotic whisper of a thousand small fish moving as one.

I am an elder. Not the eldest, but one whose movements have acquired that particular economy, that fluid, wading grace that speaks of many decades in the water. My hands, their webbing pronounced, the skin pale and fine-grained, rest on my knees. One holds a fish scale—a large one, from a mature schooling fish, its surface pearlescent and warped just enough to catch and bend the light. I’ve been using it to examine the root-wall beside me, tracing the health of the living bark, looking for the tell-tale silver glint of fungal threads that might signal the beginning of a decline. The building is old, older than the elders’ memories. Its roots are deeply merged with the limestone. It breathes slowly. I have tended its wounds, coaxed its growth, and mourned its small, inevitable losses—a chamber ceiling that thickened too much and had to be opened, becoming a garden; a doorway that narrowed as a root swelled, requiring a new passage to be cultivated alongside it.

My authority, such as it is, is rooted in this seeing. I remember the eels. I know the largest one—the ancient, pale-eyed creature we call the Spiral, for the pattern of scars on its flank—will tolerate a slow approach if I press my palm to the root-fork nearest its nest and let it feel my pulse. I remember which vents on the western face yield the warmest stone, and which algae harvests are best after the summer light. I remember the shape of a chamber that collapsed forty years ago, the particular way the afternoon light struck its floor, and the way it hummed a specific chord when the current was strong. That chamber is a garden now, lush with fine-leafed undergrowth, but I still visit. I run my hands over the broken root-ends. I remember.

I am not a leader. When we gather in the Old Growth to decide something—the placement of a new cultivated shoot, the rotation of vent-stone expeditions, the quiet resolution of a dispute over shared tools—I offer what I remember. I point with my hand, shaping gestures in the water that show the shape of things as they were, or as they might grow. Others add their own memories, their own seeing. Decisions emerge from this weaving of recollection and observation, like the root-walls themselves: no single strand bears the weight, but the whole structure holds.

I swim more than I walk now. My legs are good for bracing, for the slow negotiation of the outer perimeter where the young eels still startle. But for the courtyards, for the channels between buildings where the fish move like weather, I use my hands. The stroke is silent, a deep kneading of the water. I feel the currents as much as see them, the subtle shifts that tell of a change in tide far above, or the movement of a large eel along the perimeter.

My own building is young, a hundred years at most. Its walls are still growing, the bark a vibrant grey-green. I chose it when it was a thicket of cultivated shoots, and I have guided its expansion. A sleeping chamber for myself and, when needed, for my children’s children. A small work-niche where I keep my tools: flint knives sharpened to a razor, fish-bone needles threaded on fiber cord, my collection of the clearest fish scales. It is a good building. It hums a clear, true note in the current. But I know its mortality. I have seen its growth. I tend it, and I listen, and I know that one day, its roots will thicken too much, or the stone beneath it will shift, and a chamber will flood, or an arch will fail. It will be a garden. The sound it makes will be gone. I will grieve, as I have grieved for others.

This is the heart of it, here in the cold, bright water. We live with things that are alive and changing and will end. Our tools are simple because the world provides simply. Our knowledge is deep because it is cultivated, cell by cell, observation by observation, through the lens of a fish scale. We are not many, and we do not build monuments. We are a small, loud, cold, bright current of life in the body of a drowned mountain. We tend. We remember. We mourn, and then we guide the new shoots.

I lower the fish scale. The light on the wall is just light again. Outside, a school of fish passes, a dark, glittering cloud that parts around a root-structure and reforms beyond. The child beside me stirs, murmurs a sound that is half-liquid, half-breath. I answer with a gesture, a hand shaped in the slow sign for *safe*. *Rest*. The settlement hums. The water holds us all. It is enough. It has always been enough.
