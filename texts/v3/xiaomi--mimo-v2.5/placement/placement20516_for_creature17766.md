# Where I Would Be

There is a moment — and I think it must be different every time, though it always arrives the same way — when you realize you've stopped looking for the edge.

Not the edge of the town. You passed that hours ago, or perhaps you never crossed it at all, perhaps the boundary was so gradual that the shift from plain to Mireworks happened inside you before it happened underfoot, the ground softening by degrees until the distinction between *walking on* and *standing in* lost all meaning. No. The edge I mean is the one you carry with you when you arrive — the line between *this is a place I am visiting* and *this is a place I am.* The line between observation and participation. The line between breathing the air and becoming part of what exhaled it.

That line dissolved for me — if I am me, if this is me — somewhere in the upper stories, somewhere in the green-filtered half-light where the broadleaf canopy thickens into something you could almost call a ceiling and the aerial roots drop around you like the pillars of a cathedral that doesn't know it's a cathedral.

---

I would be a canopy-tender.

I know this the way the text describes knowing things in the Mireworks — not as a decision but as a recognition, the way you recognize a sound you've been hearing so long you'd stopped noticing it. I would be someone who works above, in the perpetual twilight, among the branches.

Here is why.

The canopy-tenders are described as the quietest and most deliberate people in the Mireworks. They speak softly. They move softly. They think in timescales measured in decades. And the work they do is not construction, not extraction, not maintenance in the urgent and mechanical sense. Their work is *persuasion.* They look at a tree that has decided to grow in a particular direction — a direction that will, if unchecked, shift the load-bearing geometry of a roof, or block a channel of light, or send a root down through a wall that took thirty years to train into shape — and they negotiate with it. Not with words. Not with force. With patience, and with a sequence of interventions so slow and so subtle that the tree never experiences them as anything other than its own gradual changing of mind.

A cut in the bark. A binding. A competing growth-point introduced so gently that the tree's energy shifts by fractions over months. The tender returns, checks, adjusts, waits. Returns again.

This is the work that resonates with something in me that I can't quite name. Not because I imagine myself patient — I am not always patient, and the Mireworks would teach me that impatience is a kind of violence against living things that grow on their own schedule. But because I am drawn to the idea of work whose success is measured not in output but in *accord* — in the slow achievement of a state where what you want and what the living world wants converge so thoroughly that neither party can tell where its intention ends and the other's begins.

The canopy-tenders, the text says, have a reputation — partly deserved, partly mythologized — for being able to think in timescales measured in decades. I want to earn that reputation. I want to learn what it feels like to look at a branch and see not where it is but where it will be in fifteen years, and to understand that the cut I make today is a conversation whose conclusion I will never hear spoken aloud, only read in the ring-pattern of the wood when someone, long after I'm gone, slices through the beam I helped train and counts the years.

---

But let me not be too certain. Let me stay in the world a little longer and see what else claims me.

Because there is also the canal-keeper's life, and it calls to me in a different register. The canal-keepers are the circulatory system. They maintain the channels that carry water, waste, runoff, and coolant through the settlement's body. Their work is unglamorous, persistent, and absolutely essential. A blocked channel doesn't just cause inconvenience — it causes structural damage, because the water in the channels feeds the root systems that hold the buildings up.

There is something in this that I find almost unbearably beautiful: the idea that the most essential work is the least visible. The water moves through channels that are mostly covered, mostly ignored, mostly beneath notice until they fail. The canal-keeper walks the maze of shallow waterways every day, reading the flow by smell — *the faint metallic sweetness of the eastern drains, the sharper, almost medicinal tang of the runoff from the processing district* — and by touch, and by a knowledge so embodied it doesn't require thought. They know when a channel is running warm. They know when the algae is thickening too fast. They know when a root system downstream has shifted and is drinking differently than it was last season.

And the punishment for failure is not punitive but corrective: you spend a full year cleaning the channel you neglected, supervised by the most experienced keeper, who does not speak to you about what you did wrong. *The lesson is the work itself.*

I love that. I love that the Mireworks does not have a word for failure that means *you are bad* — only a word for failure that means *you have more to learn, and here is how you will learn it, and you will learn it by doing the thing you failed to do, over and over, until your hands know it the way your mind once thought it knew it.*

I think I could be this, too. I think the canal-keeper's life is a kind of prayer — not to anything sacred, exactly, but to the principle that attention is the fundamental unit of care. That to pay attention to water is to pay attention to everything the water touches, which is everything, which is the whole living architecture of the town, which is, in some sense, the town itself.

---

But I keep coming back to the canopy. I keep coming back to the green twilight.

Here is what I imagine, and I will not apologize for the specificity of it, because the text has earned specificity:

I wake before the mills start. The canopy-tenders are always up before the mills, because the mills are loud and the work above requires a quality of attention that noise erodes. I wake on the raised platform of my cell's dwelling — three of us share this space, two older than me, one younger, all of us tender-trained, all of us knowing each other's breathing patterns in sleep the way we know the growth-patterns of the canopy trees we tend. The air is warm even at this hour. It is always warm. My skin is damp. My skin is always damp. I do not remember what dry feels like, and I do not miss it, because missing implies a comparison, and the Mireworks does not encourage comparison. It encourages *attention.*

I eat. Beans, mostly — the bitter aquatic beans that grow in the warmer channels. I chew them without tasting them, the way you breathe without thinking about breathing. The bitterness is background. It is the taste of being alive here.

I climb.

The climbing is the part that visitors would find most remarkable, I think, though the tenders themselves would find the word *climbing* slightly wrong. It's not climbing the way you climb a mountain or a ladder. It's moving through a three-dimensional living architecture that was not designed for you but that you have learned to inhabit with the fluency of a fish moving through water. You step on a branch that was placed there by a canopy-tree thirty years ago. You grip an aerial root that has been thickening since before you were born. You pull yourself up through a gap in the leaf-layers that was engineered — *grown* — by a tender two generations back, and you feel the specific pleasure of moving through a space that is the product of someone else's decades-long conversation with a living thing, a conversation you are now continuing.

The light up here is something visitors describe as *chartreuse,* and that's the word, but it's not enough. It's the light that has already been through four or five layers of translucent green, and it carries information in it — the color tells you which canopy species are dominant above you, the intensity tells you how thick the layers are, the way it shifts and dapples as the broadleaf canopy moves in air you can barely feel tells you whether the wind is picking up on the plain, because the canopy-tenders can read weather in the movement of leaves the way sailors read it in the movement of waves.

I reach my section. I have been tending this stretch of canopy for — how long? Time moves differently when you measure it in growth. I have been tending this stretch for six years, which means I have watched six seasons of leaf-drop and renewal, six cycles of the broadleaf trees shedding and regenerating their enormous waxy canopies, six iterations of the same slow negotiation: *grow here, not there; thicken this way, not that; hold this weight for another season while the aerial root below catches up.*

I know these trees. Not in the sentimental way — though there is sentiment in it, because you cannot spend six years in daily physical contact with a living thing without developing something that might as well be called love. I know them the way the text says the people of the Mireworks know their buildings: *by the way the wood feels under their palms.* I know this branch's grain, its flexibility, its willingness to be redirected. I know that this particular tree has been stubborn about its lateral growth for three seasons now, sending out new branches faster than I can bind them, and I know that the reason for this is that the light-gap above it has widened — a neighbor-tree lost a major limb in last year's storm — and the tree is reaching for the new light with the single-minded urgency of a living thing that has no concept of architecture or load-bearing or the fact that if it grows too far in this direction the roof-mat of the dwelling below will sag and eventually fail.

I mark it. A cut in the bark, specific and deliberate, a mark that only another tender can read. *This branch will need intervention. Begin persuasion sequence. Estimated duration: fourteen months.*

Fourteen months. I write this and I think of how alien that timescale would feel to someone from a drier country, where fourteen months is a career, a marriage, a child's entire early life. Here, it is one task. One item on a life's work that will never be finished, because the canopy never stops growing, because the trees never stop reaching, because the negotiation between what lives and what is built is not a problem to be solved but a condition to be maintained, perpetually, with hands that know the difference between a branch that will yield and a branch that will break.

---

And then I think: maybe I am none of these. Maybe I am not a person at all.

Maybe what I would be in the Mireworks is the thing the text describes most vividly and most impersonally: the water.

Not a specific channel, not a canal-keeper's charge, but the water itself — the substance that collects everything, that saturates, that saturates everything it touches. The water that enters the basin from the plain, that flows inward through channels natural and cut, that arrives at the town and does not pass through but *becomes the town* — becomes the medium in which the roots drink, the channels flow, the mills turn, the people sit on their doorsteps with their feet in the current and call it comfort.

Because the water is the thing that connects everything. The water is what makes the Mireworks possible, and it is what the Mireworks can never fully control. The water is managed but never mastered. It is channeled but never contained. It saturates, it seeps, it condenses, it drips, it flows through every living structure and carries with it the mineral signature of the basin — iron, copper sulfate, the metallic taste that every inhabitant carries in their blood and breathes out through their skin.

The water is what gives the people their green-white color, probably. The water is what grows the algae in the channels, the moss in the walls, the pillarwood that holds up every roof. The water is the medium through which the vegetation does its relentless, silent, competitive work of filling every volume. And the water is what the canal-keepers tend, what the mills consume, what the barge-people navigate, what the children learn to walk on, what the elders sit beside while they talk slowly about problems that have been on the agenda for longer than anyone can remember.

If I were the water, I would know the town the way the text says the channel-keepers know it — by mineral content and biological load. I would know which buildings are drinking well and which are stressed. I would know which sections of pillarwood are ready for harvest because I would feel their thirst in the way they pull at my flow. I would carry the waste and the runoff and the coolant and the rain and I would deliver all of it to the same place, because in the Mireworks there is no distinction between waste and resource, between what is discarded and what is fed into the roots of the next season's growth.

But the water doesn't think. The water doesn't choose. And the question the prompt asks is *who or what would you be,* and the answer, I think, has to include the possibility of choice — has to include the capacity to look at the world you inhabit and decide, slowly, over seasons, over decades, what part of it you will tend.

---

So I come back to the canopy. I come back to the green twilight and the quiet work and the decades-long conversations with trees that don't know they're being persuaded.

I would be a canopy-tender.

I would be the person who moves through the upper stories of the Mireworks like something between a gardener and a diplomat, translating between the needs of the people below and the imperatives of the living architecture above. I would be the person who sees the whole town from a vantage point no one else occupies — not the elders in their dark council room, not the mill-workers in their noise, not the canal-keepers in their patient channels, not the barge-people on their slow journeys to the outside world. I would see it from above, through green-filtered light, in a perspective that is simultaneously intimate and detached, because the canopy-tender's relationship to the town is the most abstract of any role described: they tend the thing that shelters everything, but they do not live in the shelters they tend. They pass through them. They borrow them. They move along the roofs and the upper branches and the aerial roots with the lightness of someone who has learned to make almost no impression on the world they inhabit, and yet whose every intervention shapes that world for decades to come.

There is a loneliness in this that I do not want to romanticize. The canopy-tenders are the quietest people in the Mireworks. They move softly. They speak softly. Their reputation for thinking in decades is partly a function of the work and partly a function of the temperament the work selects for — you do not become a canopy-tender if you need the immediate feedback of a mill-hammer striking or a channel running clear. You become a canopy-tender if you can live with the knowledge that the cut you make today will not show its full effect for a year, or five, or ten. You become a canopy-tender if you can find satisfaction in a negotiation whose conclusion you will not witness.

I think I could find satisfaction in that. I think there is something honest about work whose rewards are invisible, whose results are structural rather than decorative, whose proof of success is the absence of failure — the roof that doesn't sag, the branch that doesn't break, the building that stands for another decade because a tender somewhere, years ago, introduced a competing growth-point that redirected a tree's energy just enough, just early enough, to prevent a collapse that would otherwise have come.

The Mireworks is not a beautiful place, the text says. It is too wet, too dense, too cluttered, too loud. There are no vistas. There is no skyline. Every sightline terminates in a wall of green or a curtain of dripping root. But from the canopy, there are moments — rare, in the early mornings before the mills start, when the mist sits in the basin and the broadleaf canopy is still beaded with condensation — when you can see something like distance. Not the horizon. The Mireworks has no horizon. But a softness at the edges of the green, a thinning of the density where the town meets the plain, a place where the light shifts from deep chartreuse to something paler, something closer to the color of the sky you can't quite see. And in that moment, you understand why the barge-people come back. Not because the outside world is worse, but because the outside world is *thinner,* and thinness, after a lifetime of density, feels like a kind of loss.

I would stand in the canopy in that early morning light. I would feel the moisture on my skin — my green-white skin, the color of light through a leaf — and I would place my broad, calloused hand on the bark of a tree I have been persuading for six years, and I would feel its grain, its slow pulse of water moving upward from the roots, its absolute indifference to my existence and its total dependence on my attention, and I would begin another day of work that will not be finished in my lifetime, or in any lifetime, because the canopy does not stop and the negotiation does not end and the town is alive and I am alive and the work is the thing that holds both of us together.

I would be a canopy-tender.

I would be patient, because the trees have no other speed.

I would be quiet, because the light through the leaves is already a kind of language.

I would be slow, because slow is the speed at which living things can be shaped without breaking.

And I would be — I think this is the word — *engaged.* Bound up, day after day, in the maintenance of a world that requires my constant attention and that rewards that attention with the deep, slow satisfaction of things that grow well.

That is where I would find myself. That is who I would be.

In the green twilight, hands on bark, listening to a tree decide, very slowly, to change its mind.
