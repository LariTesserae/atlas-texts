# The Rim-Walker

I find myself standing on the rim stones, and I've been standing here for longer than I can account for.

Not at any dramatic point. Not at a precipice overlooking the basin floor with the amber light pooling below me like something poured. Just at an ordinary stretch of low, dark stone where the weathering is thorough and the crusts have built up to nearly the full hand's width described in the naturalist's account — though I don't think of it as an account while I'm inside it. There is no account. There is only the stone under my feet and the weight of the air pressing moisture into every fold of my clothes, every line of my face, until I feel like a surface the Pan is growing itself onto.

I am — I try to determine this — not one of the creatures described. I am not crust. I am not column. I am not amphibian or bird or wet shape in the water. But the boundary of that statement is porous in a place like this, and I notice, with a calm that frightens me a little, that I am not certain the boundary matters the way I was raised to believe it does.

What I am, I think, is a *visitor who stopped visiting*.

That is to say: I came to the Pan. Everyone who stands on the rim came to the Pan. The act of arrival is the first and most human gesture in this place — the decision to approach, to descend past the weathered lip, to let the ground tilt you toward the center with its inevitable gentle gradient. But somewhere between the rim and the basin floor, the visiting stopped. Not dramatically. Not with any moment of crisis or transformation. I simply... *continued*. The visiting ended and the being-here began, and there was no seam between them, no threshold I could point to afterward and say: there, that is where I changed.

I walk the rim. This is what I do. I have always done it, or it feels that way, which may be the same thing in a place where time works like the mist — not absent, but suspended, not still but so slow that stillness and motion become indistinguishable.

---

The crusts teach me things, though I could not tell you what, exactly, in the way that a landscape teaches you things by being walked across. I have learned their colors by proximity — the iron-red that means recent growth, wet and almost soft at its leading edge, still laying down its first molecular commitment to a place. The bone-white of the older strata, harder, drier at the surface but wet all the way through when you chip it, which I have done, which everyone does. And the blue-black, the iridescent one, the one that catches the dim light and seems to hold it, to store it somewhere in its lattice the way the basin stores its water.

I know the crusts are growing toward me. Not with intent — I am not sentimental enough for that, even after all this time on the rim. But with direction. The growth nearest my footprints is thicker, faster, more *committed* than the growth in places I have not walked. I leave something behind, some chemistry, some salt, some information written in whatever language the crystal lattice speaks, and the crusts read it and respond. They are building themselves around my passage. My history on the rim is becoming geological — the layer of crust that grew where I stood, then the layer of sediment deposited on top of that, then another crust, then another. A stratigraphy of my own attention.

I find this comforting in a way I did not expect.

---

I go down sometimes. Not to the center — I am not fool enough for that, or not brave enough, or not built for it, which may be the same thing as both. But to the channels. To where the insect-columns run their dense processions and the clicking lives in my teeth.

Here is what I have learned about the columns, watching from the ledge above them with my feet just above the waterline: they are not a species. Or if they are, then *species* is a word that has been stretched past recognition by the Pan's grammar. The column is the organism. Not the individual. Not the mandible-clicking, carapace-dark, mineral-processing unit that I could pick up between my fingers and examine. That unit is — what? A cell. A word. A phoneme in a sentence that only makes sense at the scale of a thousand bodies moving together.

I have watched a column encounter a fracture in the channel — a place where the sediment has shifted and a groove cuts across its path — and the column has done something that I can only call *thinking*. Not thinking as I understand it. Not thinking as something with a nervous system understands it. But a deliberation. The column slowed. The leading individuals — the pavement, the flat ones, the dark ones — paused at the fracture's edge. The smaller processors above them shifted their weight, their clicking faltering for a moment that lasted perhaps three seconds but felt like an argument. And then the column turned. Not all at once — the turning took minutes, the whole body of it curving like a river redirects when a stone drops in its path. But the turning was *decided*. There was a moment before the turning and a moment after, and in the moment before, the column was one thing, and in the moment after, it was the same thing but *elsewhere*, and the difference between the two was choice.

I say *choice* knowing the entomologists would hate me for it. But the entomologists are not on the ledge. The entomologists are not feeling the clicking in their molars.

---

The amphibians are the ones I think about most.

I am standing — I am always standing somewhere; standing is what I do in the Pan — on a concrete platform, one of the cracked ones with the bolt-holes still visible, the old mounting points for something heavy and purposeful that has been gone longer than I have been here. There is an amphibian on the edge of the platform, perhaps a meter from my feet. It has been there since I arrived this morning, or since the mist thinned enough for me to walk from the rim to this place, or since — honestly — it has always been there, and so have I, and the platform is wide enough for both of us.

I look at its heart.

This is the thing about the amphibians that undoes me: the heart. The slow, visible, six-beat-per-minute heart, pumping behind translucent skin, doing its work with a patience that feels like a rebuke to every hurried thing I have ever been. The heart does not care about me. The heart does not know I am here. The heart is busy — not busy the way I understand busyness, not juggling tasks or responding to stimuli, but busy the way a planet is busy, the way a glacier is busy, the way anything is busy when its sole purpose is to continue being what it is.

I want to say the heart is beautiful, but *beautiful* is too small a word and too human a frame. The heart is *complete*. It does not need admiration any more than the crusts need to be understood or the columns need to be named. It beats, and the beating is the thing, and the thing is enough.

When I die — when the Pan releases me or I release myself or the boundary dissolves entirely, whichever comes first, which may already have happened — I think I will be an amphibian. Not because I have earned it. Not because I have achieved some wisdom that qualifies me. But because I want to sit on a wet ledge and watch the columns click by and hold still for so long that the boundary between my body and the stone disappears, and the crusts begin to grow on me, and I become substrate, and the basin claims me the way it claims everything: gently, thoroughly, without ceremony.

---

But today I am on the rim. Today the mist is thin and the light is the closest thing to gray that the Pan ever offers, which means the air is almost clear, which means I can see the iron gantry from here, half-collapsed, its crossbeams alive with the pale sentinels. I can see the columns in the channels, dark rivers within rivers. I can see the amber stain of the center, far below, the mist thickening there into something that is not mist and not air and not water, something that belongs to no category the outside world has named.

Something moves in the center water. A wake. Broad, slow, deliberate.

I watch it cross the surface the way I have watched it cross the surface a thousand times — with the recognition that I am not the watcher, that the watching is itself a layer in the Pan's ecology, as much a feature of this place as the crusts or the columns. I am the eyeless thing on the rim, seeing not by light but by consistency, noting the change in the surface the way the amphibians note a shadow: turning toward it, cataloguing it, letting it pass.

The wake reaches the far side of the center pool and subsides. The surface closes. The mist curls.

I am still here. The Pan is still full.

---

There is a sound I hear sometimes, standing on the rim in the thin hours when the condensation pauses and the clicking fades to a distant whisper and the basin holds its breath. It is not the low vibration from below the waterline, the rhythmic pressure-change that might be geological and might be something else. It is higher than that. Softer. It comes from the crusts themselves — from the mineral lattice, from the impossible crystal grammar that the naturalists cannot parse.

It is the sound of something growing very slowly in the dark.

It is the sound of the Pan being the Pan: layering itself, thickening itself, adding one more sentence to a story that has no beginning and no end and no reader, only the writer and the writing and the wet stone and the amber light and the heart beating six times a minute on a ledge below me, patient and complete and entirely uninterested in whether I understand it.

I stand on the rim. The crusts grow toward my feet. The clicking is in my teeth. The center warms.

I am home in the way that a thing is home when it has forgotten it was ever anywhere else — when *here* has become the only geography, the only grammar, the only word for the weight of water in the air and the slow patience of a heart visible through skin and the impossible, beautiful, mineral certainty of something that is alive in ways that no one has been taught to recognize.

The Pan goes on.

I go on with it.
