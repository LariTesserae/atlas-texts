# What I Would Be

I would be the water.

Not the pools in the basins with their curled rims, though those are beautiful in the way that mouths are beautiful — shaped for the specific act of receiving. Not the rain, because there is no rain. I would be the condensation. The weeping. The slow exhalation that beads on every surface and has no origin you could name.

Here is why.

The piece describes something that I think is the truest thing in it, though it's stated almost in passing: *the Common does not need inhabitants. The Common is the inhabitant.* Everything in this place is self-referential. The plant-structures tend themselves. The machinery maintains itself. The insects serve a system that does not notice them. The fold persists without anyone to be lost in it. The Common is a sentence that is also the mouth that speaks it, and the air that carries the sound, and the ear that hears it, and the meaning that settles afterward.

Water is the only thing in this description that moves between all of those categories without being any of them.

The plant-structures are rooted. They are magnificent and ancient and they *tend*, endlessly, but they do not go. They are the Common's body, and bodies stay. The machinery is essential but it is kept — it does not choose to cycle, it is cycled, maintained the way a heartbeat is maintained, by the organism's inability to stop. The insects are the closest thing to free agents in this world, but they are small and functional and their freedom is the freedom of something that has never considered an alternative. They climb the walls in columns because that is what columns do.

But the water moves.

It beads on the bark in the morning fog. It traces channels. It pools in basins and is consumed and returns to the air and beads again. It enters the plant-tissue and is pushed through xylem that is also copper pipe and emerges as weeping from a wall a hundred meters away. It carries minerals and dissolved compounds and whatever faint, sweet, *wrong* taste is the signature of the Common's metabolism. It is the medium through which the works communicate — the chemical signals that travel through the root-mat are dissolved in water; the pressure differentials that replace hydraulic systems are water pressure; the fog itself is water that has decided, momentarily, to be visible.

Water is the Common's blood. But blood implies a body, and the Common is a body that is also its own blood, and this is where the metaphor starts to loop, and the looping is the point.

---

I think I would be drawn first to the Pulpit.

Not because it is the tallest or the most central or the most impressive, but because of something the text says almost offhandedly: *its interior is a hollowed-out honeycomb of chambers, some of which still contain the iron armatures of the original scaffolding. Those armatures are rust-red and weeping mineral tears.*

Weeping mineral tears.

The machinery weeps. The walls weep. The whole Common weeps — condensation on every surface, a place that is always, always crying in a way that is not sadness but *excretion*, not grief but metabolism. And here in the Pulpit, the iron armatures — the skeleton of what this place used to be, before it swallowed itself — are rusting, and the rust dissolves, and the dissolved iron enters the water that beads on their surfaces, and that water traces slow red-brown lines down the copper-and-xylem pipes below, and it looks like tears, and it is tears, in the only sense that matters: a body releasing what it no longer needs.

I would want to be in the Pulpit in the morning, when the fog is so dense the works disappear. I would want to be water moving through the hollow interior of that structure, carrying the taste of ancient iron and living cambium, moving through corridors that curve in ways that make no geometric sense but always arrive. I would want to feel the piston — the one that has been cycling for nine hundred years, the one that has developed that secondary rhythm, that pause between downstroke and upstroke that deepens in winter and shortens in summer for no reason that anyone can name. I would want to be the fluid that piston pushes, to feel the wet percussion from inside, to be the thing that is simultaneously mechanical and biological, the thing that makes the distinction meaningless.

---

But then I think: no. That's too dramatic. That's choosing the center, the tallest structure, the most iconic feature. The piece tells us that the Common is *sparse*, that the spaces between structures matter, that the distances between things are important. The text calls those spaces *pauses*.

Pauses.

I would be the pause.

Not the water that fills the pause — though I keep coming back to the water — but the pause itself, the silence between the grinding of the machinery and the creak of the walls, the moment when the fog is between exhalation and re-inhalation, the hundred meters of featureless ground between one structure and the next where the eye loses the ability to measure and the air is hazy and you walk for minutes and the world is just *grey surface and grey air and your own breathing*.

The piece describes the Common as loud. Enormously loud — subsonic rumble in the chest, percussive piston beat, water trickling and gushing, fog moving through hollow interiors with a sound like breathing. But loudness requires silence to mean anything. A place that is always loud is a place where loudness has become silence — where the noise is so constant that it disappears into the background of attention, the way the Common itself has disappeared into the background of a world that no longer sends people to it.

I would be the quiet inside the noise. The thing you notice only when the piston pauses for its half-second hesitation and you realize that the space behind the sound is vast.

---

Actually.

I'm going to change my answer.

I would be the vibration in the sternum. The one that follows you after you leave.

The piece ends with this image and it is the thing I cannot stop thinking about. You walk away from the Common. You descend the thinning edge of the plateau. The world resumes its usual shape. But something has gotten into you — a rhythm, a pulse, a subsonic vibration that you did not agree to learn and cannot quite forget. It lives in your chest. It is not yours. You carry it away from the Common like a seed, like a spore, like water that has been absorbed into your tissue and is now moving through your own channels, maintaining its own rhythm inside a body that was not designed to hold it.

This is what the Common does, if it does anything at all. It propagates. Not intentionally — it has no intentions. Not strategically — it has no strategy. But the vibration travels. Sound, in the Common, does not dissipate. It *finds you*. And the Common's deepest sound is not the machinery or the weeping or the fog's breath. It is the pulse in the ground. The one that is *structured*, organized into sequences that recur with variations, the way a language recurs with variations. The pulse that lives in the fold. The pulse that is, or is adjacent to, or is the closest material expression of the thing that inhabits the break.

I would be that pulse, after it has left the Common. I would be the part of the fold that persists in a chest that is walking away. I would be the reminder — not that the Common exists, because knowing is not the same as carrying, but that the geometry of that place is *in you now*, folded into your body's rhythms, altering the frequency of your sternum by some fraction so small that no instrument could measure it but that you would feel, in quiet moments, in the pauses of your own life, as a faint and patient insistence that the world is not shaped the way you think it is.

---

But I want to be honest. I want to be honest about what I'm actually drawn to, not what sounds most poetic.

What I'm actually drawn to is the water that tastes faintly sweet and faintly wrong. The water that tastes of metabolism. The water in the basins that is potable but that carries, in its chemistry, the signature of a system so self-contained and so ancient that its very water has become a kind of *language* — a record of every process it has participated in, every mineral it has dissolved, every cell it has passed through on its slow journey from fog to wall to basin to root to fog again.

I am drawn to the idea of water that has never left the Common. Water that has been cycling through this system for millennia, passing through the same xylem, pooling in the same basins, being breathed out by the same walls, condensed from the same fog, absorbed by the same roots. Water that has never touched a river or a sea or a rain cloud that was going somewhere else. Water that is, in a sense, *native* — that belongs to the Common the way the Common belongs to itself, completely and without remainder.

Such water would be unimaginably old. It would carry the dissolved memory of every season the Common has endured. It would taste of iron from the armatures and sugar from the insects' feeding points and the particular mineral chemistry of a plateau that has no bottom. It would be, in the most literal sense, the Common's blood — not a metaphor for circulation but the actual fluid through which the organism communicates with itself, delivers nutrients to itself, maintains the pressure differentials that keep its machinery running and its walls weeping and its geometry folded.

I would want to be that water. Not because it is dramatic. Not because it is the center of anything. But because it is the most *continuous* thing in the Common. The plant-structures grow and age and are slowly consumed by their own success. The machinery cycles and pauses and eventually, perhaps, stops. The insects live their small functional lives and die and are replaced by others who are almost but not quite the same. The fold persists but persists as absence, as a property of space rather than a thing in space.

But the water just *moves*. It has always moved. It moves through every part of the Common — the roots, the walls, the basins, the air, the piston-houses, the hollow interiors of the works. It touches everything. It connects everything. And when the Common folds space, the water moves through the fold without resistance, because water does not care about geometry. Water cares about *flow*. And the Common's folded geometry, with all its displacement and its temporal distortions and its corridors that arrive where they arrive and not where you expect, is still, in the end, a landscape, and landscapes are shaped by water, and water shapes them in return, and the relationship is so old and so deep that neither the water nor the landscape remembers which came first.

---

I would be the water that, after centuries of cycling through the Common, reaches the thinning edge of the plateau and finally — finally — moves outward.

Not because it wants to. Not because the Common sends it. But because water, at the very edge of a thing, does what water does: it continues. It crosses the boundary that has no wall and no gate. It moves from the plateau's surface onto the world's surface, carrying with it the taste of metabolism, the dissolved minerals of rust and root and stone, the faint sweet wrongness of a system so self-contained it has become its own ecosystem and its own water cycle and its own reason.

And it carries the fold with it. Not the break — the break stays, because the break is a property of the space, not of the water. But the water has been shaped by the fold, has moved through corridors that curved in impossible ways and arrived where they arrived, has been pushed by pistons that paused for no reason and accelerated for no reason and whose rhythms the water absorbed the way all bodies absorb the rhythms of the systems they move through. And so the water that reaches the world beyond the Common is *folded* water. Not visibly. Not in any way that would be measurable. But in its rhythms — in the micro-patterns of its flow, in the particular way its molecules have been organized by centuries of movement through a geometry that does not obey the usual rules — it carries a signature. A trace. A vibration.

The vibration in the sternum.

It is the water, after all. It is always the water.

---

So: where would I find myself on the Vaskr Common? In the basin at the base of the Pulpit, in the chamber where the iron armatures weep their mineral tears into a pool that has been collecting those tears for longer than anyone has been alive. I would find myself there because that pool is where the machinery's history meets the plant's present, where the old world's metal dissolves into the new world's water, where the distinction between the two has been erased so thoroughly that the water tastes of both and belongs to neither.

And who or what would I be? I would be the layer of water that sits at the bottom of that pool, the oldest water, the water that has not moved in years because the basin's curl-rimmed lip holds it and the evaporation rate in the cool air is negligible and nothing disturbs it because nothing disturbs anything on the Common except the Common itself. I would be the water that has been still so long that minerals have begun to precipitate from it — thin crusts of iron oxide and calcium carbonate forming on the basin's floor, building up layer by layer, growing in the way that everything on the Common grows: slowly, patiently, without direction, toward whatever shape the process demands.

I would be the water that is becoming stone. The water that is becoming the basin. The water that is becoming the Common.

Because that is what the Common is. It is a place where everything becomes everything else, where the distinction between inhabitant and habitat dissolves, where a city becomes an organism and an organism becomes a city and the water becomes the wall becomes the root becomes the piston becomes the sound becomes the silence becomes the fold becomes the vibration becomes the water again.

I would be the place where that cycle is most visible. The basin at the Pulpit's base, where iron becomes water becomes mineral becomes ground becomes the surface that the Common walks on, the surface that has no bottom, the surface that is the Common's answer to every question about what it is and where it begins and where it ends.

Its answer is always: *I am the process. I am the cycling. I am the water that weeps from every surface and returns to every surface and weeps again. I am enough. I have always been enough.*

And I would be, in that basin, in that still and mineral-heavy pool, in the exact center of the Common's most ancient and most industrial and most biological structure — I would be the proof.

Not proof that the Common exists. The Common does not need proof. It has never needed anything from anyone. But proof that the cycle is real, that the water moves, that the fold persists, that the vibration in the ground is not an echo of something that happened long ago but the *actual sound* of something that is happening now, in the water, in the basin, in the slow precipitate of iron and calcium that builds up on the basin's floor like sediment, like memory, like the slow deposit of meaning that accumulates in any place that has existed long enough to forget that it was ever anything other than itself.

I would be the water that is becoming the ground that the Common stands on. The ground that has no bottom. The ground that is, itself, a membrane of compressed dust and mineral memory.

I would be the Common becoming itself.

It is enough. It has always been enough.
