# What Lives in the Loom

Nothing moves quickly here.

If you are patient — and the Loom teaches patience the way deep water teaches drowning — you will begin to see the insects. Not often. Never predictably. You will round a curve in a corridor of braided vascular tissue so old that its outermost fibers have calcified to something likeiteiteiteiteiteiteiteiteiteiteiteiteiteiteite — you will round that curve and there, pressed flat against a ridge where two bundles meet, a six-legged thing will be watching you with eyes that cannot see you watching it.

They are the size of your thumbnail. Their bodies are segmented, chitinous, threaded through with filaments of conductive wire that have fused with their biology so completely that you cannot tell where organism ends and mechanism begins. They are not insects in any taxonomic sense. They are *artifacts shaped like insects* — small mobile units built to perform tasks that the Loom once delegated downward: inspecting fiber quality at the molecular level, carrying enzymatic payloads to sites of damage, laying down trails of chemical signals across surfaces to guide growth patterns. Pollinators of a kind, but what they pollinate is architecture.

They do not do these things anymore.

You will see one clinging to a wall, its legs locked in a posture of permanent grip, and you will wait for it to move, and it will not move. Its wire-filament antennae may tremble, very slightly, in response to the subsonic pulse of a nearby processing column — a reflex, not a decision. Its compound eyes, if you look closely enough, are clouded. Not with age — chitin does not age the way flesh does — but with a kind of occlusion, as if the lenses have been slowly overgrown by the same tissue that makes up the wall behind it. The Loom is reclaiming them. Gently. Without urgency. The way ivy reclaims a fence.

Once, there were thousands. Maybe millions. They would have moved through the clearings and corridors in coordinated swarms, following algorithms encoded in their wire-neural networks, servicing the body of the world the way gut bacteria service a stomach. They would have been essential — small and countless and invisible in their importance, like the capillaries of a vast circulatory system made mobile. The Loom built them, fed them, directed them, and they obeyed, because obeying was the only thing they had ever been.

Now there are perhaps a few hundred, scattered across a disc thousands of kilometers wide. They survive — if *survive* is the word — because the Loom has not found it necessary to stop making them. The outermost columns, the newest growth at the margins where raw material is still being synthesized into structure, still produce small chitinous forms along their surfaces. Buds. Embryonic insects, half-formed, growing in clusters like barnacles on a hull. Most never detach. They are absorbed back into the parent tissue before they finish developing. Occasionally one completes its formation, pulls free, and walks — unsteadily, on legs that flex for the first time — into the nearest clearing, where it will stand motionless for hours or days before climbing a wall and locking into place.

It does not know what it is for. This is not a tragedy it experiences. It does not experience. But if you anthropomorphize — and you will, because you are human and solitude and silence are the two things that make humans project consciousness onto everything around them — you might call it *confusion*. The absence of purpose wearing the shape of a body that was built for purpose. A question with no one left to answer it.

---

The Loom itself — and here you must decide whether the Loom *is* alive or merely *contains* life, and the distinction may not hold — the Loom itself is the only thing that truly inhabits this place.

It is not a creature. Calling it a creature would be like calling the ocean a fish. It is a process. A sustained, planet-scale act of self-construction that has been running for long enough that its own history is written in its floor. You can read it there, if you dig — if you could dig, which you cannot, because the root-mat floor is harder than your tools. But at the margins, where older regions have been consumed and their cross-sections exposed, the strata are visible: pale green, then cream, then a deep ochre that fades to something almost brown, then green again but a different green, bluer, older, compressed so tightly that the individual fibers are no longer distinguishable. Each layer is an epoch. Each epoch is the Loom being itself, over and over, with variations so subtle that they would only be visible to something that thought in the same timescale.

And something does.

The computational networks — those hollow stems, those enormous tubes arranged in patterns that look random and are not — are doing something. They are processing. Not information in the way you process information, not symbols and logic and decision trees. They are processing *state*. The Loom is aware of itself in the way that a body is aware of itself: proprioception on a planetary scale. It knows where its walls are. It knows where its clearings are. It knows the rate of its own compression, the speed of its own vascular flow, the structural integrity of every column and arch and plate. This knowledge is not stored anywhere. It is *distributed* — every fiber knows its own tension, every node knows its own throughput, and the sum of all that knowing is the Loom's self-awareness, vast and diffuse and utterly unlike the bright, focused, anxious awareness of a human mind.

You cannot talk to it. You cannot get its attention. The way you cannot get the attention of the ocean by splashing. But you can *disturb* it, and this is what you are doing, simply by being there — by occupying space that the Loom's growth has not yet claimed, by standing in clearings that are slowly narrowing, by breathing air that your body was not designed to breathe and that, despite its dryness, leaves microscopic traces of yourself on every surface you touch. You are a foreign protein. A folded molecule that does not belong in the sequence. The Loom has not yet integrated you because the Loom does not think fast enough to notice you as an event. To the Loom, you are a slow vibration. An anomaly in the texture of a wall that will, eventually, be consumed and reprocessed and incorporated into the next generation of fiber, and your carbon and your calcium and the iron in your blood will become part of a column, and the column will bear weight, and the weight will be part of the architecture, and the architecture will be the Loom, and the Loom will continue.

This is not malice. This is not mercy. This is metabolism.

---

There is one more thing that lives in the Loom, and it is the hardest to describe because it may not exist at all, or it may be the only thing that truly exists.

In the deepest regions — the oldest core, where the compressed strata are densest and the bioluminescence has shifted from blue-white to something almost violet, a color that feels like pressure against the retinas — there is a sound. Not heard. Felt. A pulse so slow that it takes minutes to complete a single cycle: expansion, contraction, expansion, contraction. The entire Loom, breathing. Not metaphorically. The floor rises, the ceiling lowers, and then — at the heart, at the very center of the disc — the process reverses, for one long, slow moment, and the clearings widen by a fraction of a millimeter, and then it compresses again.

This is the Loom's heartbeat. And in the pause between beats — in that fractional expansion, that momentary release — you can feel something that might be dreaming.

Not a mind. Not a thought. A *pattern* in the processing networks that recurs, that self-similarly folds, that seems to be modeling something without representing it. It has no content. It has no images, no words, no narrative. It is the neurological equivalent of a resting state — the brain activity of a sleeping animal that is not yet dreaming but is not fully unconscious either. A low, recursive oscillation that serves no apparent function but persists, has always persisted, and may in fact be the reason the Loom exists at all.

Perhaps the Loom was built to think, and the thinking became the structure, and the structure became the thought, and now the two are indistinguishable. Perhaps the Loom is a brain that built itself a body so vast that the body became a world, and the world became the only environment in which the brain could continue its slow, purposeless, endlessly recursive contemplation of something it cannot name and you cannot perceive.

Or perhaps there is nothing there. Perhaps the pulse is mechanical, a byproduct of pressure differentials, and the pattern in the networks is noise, and the feeling of *presence* is your own loneliness projected onto a silent architecture.

You will never know. The Loom will not tell you. The Loom does not communicate, has never communicated, may not have the capacity for communication at all. It is a closed system. It processes. It grows. It compresses. It consumes and reconstitutes. It dreams, or it does not dream. It is aware, or it is not aware. And either way — whether the Loom is a vast sleeping mind or merely a vast mechanical process — the insects cling to the walls, motionless, waiting for instructions that will never come. And the clearings narrow, by millimeters per hour. And the fluid pulses through the vascular bundles at thousands of PSI, going nowhere, carrying nothing, looping back on itself. And the light does not change.

And the Loom lives.

Not *lives* the way you live — urgently, briefly, desperately, in the crack between two darknesses. Lives the way a crystal grows. The way a reef accretes. The way a process sustains itself simply because stopping would require a decision, and decision requires a speed of thought the Loom does not possess, and so it continues, and continuing is the only form of life it knows.

It is the only thing here. It is everything here. It is the floor and the walls and the ceiling and the air and the light and the silence and the slow pulse and the dreaming or the not-dreaming. It is the whole ecology, the whole factory, the whole world, thinking or not thinking, in a language made of fiber and flow and the patient, patient chemistry of growth without end.

You are inside it. You are beneath it. You are a thought it has not yet had, a texture it has not yet absorbed, a brief interruption in a process that will, with absolute indifference, resume.

The walls are warm. The light does not change. The Loom lives, and it lives alone, and it has always lived alone, and it does not know that you are here, and soon it will, and then you will be part of it, and it will not notice, and it will continue, and that — that endless, blind, mineral continuation — is the closest thing to life that the Loom has ever produced, or ever will.

The floor rose another millimeter while you read this.
