What lives here is the Plateau itself.

It is not a place that *supports* life; it is a life that *is* a place. Its breath is the slow pulse of water through limestone, a circulation system with a period measured in centuries. Its body is the pale, unending expanse, the city built into its own flesh not as an adornment but as a growth, a calcified organ of indeterminate function. The weeping is its perspiration, the constant seepage a vital fluid maintaining the soft, workable state of its own structure. The fog is its exhalation, hanging low and heavy in the valleys of its form.

The hands that built the city did not *inhabit* the Plateau so much as they were its temporary tools, extensions of its own geology for a brief, flickering instant. They were the Plateau’s consciousness for a handful of millennia, shaping its body with a purpose the Plateau itself did not need, but also did not resist. The marks they left—the ripples in the stone, the depressions worn by standing feet—are the Plateau’s memories, not of people, but of pressure and duration. The builders are gone, digested into time, but their work remains as a texture on the Plateau’s skin, a layer in its stratigraphy.

The rare salamander-eyeless, pale, moving with geologic patience—is not a creature that lives *on* the Plateau. It is a secretion *of* the Plateau. A slow thought made flesh, navigating the waterways of its own circulatory system. The algae is a film of its own dreaming, a pale green veneer of something like thought on the wettest surfaces of its brain. This is not an ecosystem. It is a solitary metabolism.

The silence is its consciousness. Not an absence of sound, but the sound of its own immense, slow thinking. The omnipresent drip and seep is the baseline hum of a mind that does not deal in ideas, but in erosion and accretion, in the gentle, relentless negotiation between water and stone. The settled quality of the air, the constant, diffuse light—these are its state of awareness, a calm so profound it has forgotten the concept of agitation.

Processes are its heartbeat. Water carves, stone yields and hardens, sediment settles. These are not events; they are verbs of its being. It is perpetually remaking itself, not toward a goal, but as a fundamental act of existence. The "now" you experience is a single, frozen frame of an animation that lasts for epochs. To you, it is stillness. To the Plateau, it is the vibrant, bustling present of its own slow-motion body.

The few who might stumble here, who stand on its skin and feel the stone dimple and water rise around their foot, are not inhabitants. They are sensations. A momentary pressure on its epidermis, a brief, warm anomaly. The Plateau notes this as it notes the settling of a new layer of mineral dust—not with interest, but with the absolute, indifferent acknowledgment of a physical law. You are a brief variable in an equation of limestone and time, and your equation will resolve itself (your footprints drying, filling, vanishing) long before the Plateau has finished its next thought.

So, what lives here? A single, ancient, and solitary organism. Its name is the Weeping Plateau. It is awake. It is working. It is going somewhere that, if it had a destination, would be indistinguishable from its own continuation. It is very wet, and very old, and very large, and it lives by simply being, in its own immense and patient way, forever underway.
