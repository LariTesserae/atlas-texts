# What Lives in the Col of Shattered Pillars

Nothing breathes here in the way you mean when you ask what lives somewhere. That word — *lives* — carries warmth in it, and movement, and hunger. The col has none of these. What it has is *activity*, ceaseless and total, carried out by things that do not know they are doing it.

---

**The frost lives here.** Not as decoration, not as a coat laid over something else, but as an inhabitant with its own schedule and its own architecture. Each dawn — if dawn is the right word for the slow intensification of light that begins as a bruise-blue along the eastern ridge and builds toward that white, directionless blaze — the frost wakes before anything else, if waking is what it does. It forms first on the shaded faces of the basalt columns, nucleating at the margins of mineral grains where the surface energy is highest. It does not fall from the sky. It rises from the stone itself, or rather it rises from the thin moisture already clinging to the stone, molecule by molecule, crystal by crystal, building outward in branching dendrites that copy — without intention, without knowledge — the arborescent logic of lichen, of neurons, of river deltas seen from very high up. By the time the light reaches full strength, the frost has painted every surface it can reach in a filigree of ice so fine that touching it would destroy it. It would transfer to your fingertip and melt in the same instant, and you would feel nothing — perhaps a whisper of cold colder than the cold already living in your skin, perhaps not even that. The frost knows nothing of you. It is engaged with the stone, with the molecular geometry of feldspar faces and the capillary spaces between basalt crystals, and its work is intricate and devoted. By midday on the shaded faces it persists, glinting in the refracted light like a skin of crushed diamond. Then it sublimates — not melts, never melts, there is no intermediate liquid state here — and returns to the air as vapor with that faint hiss, that whisper of departure. By afternoon the cycle is complete. The stone is bare again, dark and damp-seeming, and the frost has gone to where frost goes between its lives. It will return. It always returns. The cycle has been unbroken here for longer than the concept of cycles existed.

**The mineral patterns live here.** They are the closest thing the col has to a permanent population. On the faces of the basalt columns — particularly the older ones, the ones most deeply weathered, whose hexagonal geometry has begun to soften at the edges — iron and manganese oxides have precipitated out of the slow percolation of condensation-water through the rock's micro-fractures, and they have done so in patterns that stop you. The concentric rings are the most common: rust-orange and dark brown, nested inside one another with a regularity that feels less like chemistry and more like *insistence*. They are what happens when water moves through a porous medium at a nearly constant rate and deposits solute at the air-rock interface, and this is a perfectly ordinary thing for water and rock to do. But the concentration of it here, the sheer density of these patterns across every available surface, creates the impression of a language being written and rewritten endlessly by a hand that knows the alphabet but not the meaning. Branching lines fork across basalt faces at angles that mirror — exactly, precisely — the angles of the basalt's own columnar jointing. Spirals tighten toward centers with the regularity of a logarithmic curve. Some faces bear patterns that, seen from the right distance and in the right light, resemble the cross-sections of hearts, of lungs, of the vascular bundles in a leaf. These are mineral precipitates. They are not alive. They are the chemical memory of water moving through stone, and they are as old as the stone itself, and they will be here long after the stone has weathered to grit. But they do not sit quietly. They accumulate. They overlap. Where two pattern-systems meet — where one column's oxide face abuts another's — the patterns interact in ways that suggest *consequence*, as though one pattern is responding to the other, though of course it is only the shared water, the shared chemistry, the same slow drip doing the same slow work. The effect is of a room full of conversations that no one is having.

**The cairn lives here.** It should not. It is seven river-worn stones stacked on a flat-topped boulder in a place where no rivers run, and it has been standing — or half-standing, or falling and being rebuilt — for an unknown span of time that is certainly long enough for every memory of its builder to have dissolved into irrelevance. The stones are not from here. They are rounded, smooth, the kind of stones that have spent centuries in current, tumbling against one another, their edges edited away by the patient percussion of water-borne sediment. They are a different geology entirely — lighter in color, finer-grained, possibly limestone or water-worn tuff — and they sit on the dark basalt like words in a foreign language. Someone carried them here. Someone looked at this bright, crushing, empty, beautiful place and decided it needed a marker, and they walked up from wherever the rivers are — from the unseen valleys beyond the ridgelines, the valleys whose existence you can infer but never confirm — carrying seven stones in a bundle, or in pockets, or one at a time across multiple journeys, and they placed them one atop another on the one flat surface in this chaos of tilted slabs and vertical columns, and they stepped back, and they left. The cairn has been knocked over. The cairn has been rebuilt. The contact surfaces of the seven stones are worn smooth and concave in ways that suggest dozens, perhaps hundreds, of re-stackings. The shallow ring of smaller stones at its base — frost-shoved into partial burial, gravel creeping around them year by year — may be the work of the same hands or different ones. The parallel scratches on the boulder's face, arranged in groups that might be groups of five, might be something else, were made by something hard drawn across something harder with deliberate, repeated force. They are the col's only writing. They say nothing that can be read. But they say it with conviction.

**The worked stone lives here.** In the crevices between basalt columns — in the tight, dark gaps where frost has widened the joint just enough to admit a hand or a flake — there are pieces of chert. They are not from this elevation. They are not from this geology. They are sharp, and they are sharp on purpose: the bulbs of percussion are visible on some of them, the ripples of force radiating from the point of impact, the negatives of a hammerstone's face pressed into the edge of a flake that was struck from a core by someone who knew how. They are small. They weigh almost nothing. They are the waste products of toolmaking — the debitage, the discarded flakes left over when the useful blades were taken away — or they are the tools themselves, left behind when they were no longer needed or no longer sharp enough. They have settled into the crevices as though they belong there, packed in by frost-heave and wind alongside natural chips of basalt and grains of quartz, and at first glance they are invisible. It takes the right eye, the right angle of light, the right kind of attention to separate the human-struck flake from the frost-shattered chip. The chert is grey-blue, and it does not weather the way basalt weathers — it does not develop oxide patterns, does not grow frost-dendrites on its faces, does not integrate. It sits in the rock like a memory that refuses to become geology. It is the col's smallest evidence of the only kind of life it has ever known, and it is permanent in a way that the cairn is not, because it requires no one to maintain it. It simply sits. It waits in the crevices. It will be there when the cairn has finally fallen and the frost has finally pulled the last basalt column apart grain by grain.

**The sound lives here.** It is not a resident you can see, but it inhabits the col as fully as the stone does. The quiet is the container, and the quiet is enormous — not the quiet of a forest or a field, where there is always the green murmur of biological process, the rustle of something moving through undergrowth, the calls and songs of creatures going about the work of staying alive. This is geological quiet, mineral quiet, the quiet of processes that operate below the threshold of hearing most of the time and break through only occasionally, startlingly, into the range of the human ear. The sublimation hiss is constant but so faint that you feel it more than hear it — a presence in the bones of the skull, a sense that the air itself is in very slow motion, is flowing and departing and being replaced. The frost-crack is sudden and sharp — a sound like a single knuckle popped in a cathedral, a *tick* that detonates in the stillness and then vanishes, leaving a silence more complete than the one it interrupted. The crack propagates along grain boundaries in the granite, a fracture front moving at the speed of sound through solid rock, and it is the sound of the col's only sculptor at work. The wind-whistle is irregular and unpredictable, dependent on the exact speed and angle of air moving through a specific gap between specific columns, and it produces tones that hover near the edge of music — a single sustained note, high and glassy, that rises and falls with the gust and then disappears. When the wind dies, the col returns to its geological quiet, and the silence is so thorough that you can hear the blood in your own ears, which is its own kind of sound, which is its own kind of life.

**The light lives here.** It is not a metaphor. The light in the col is a physical presence as tangible as the basalt — it strikes the surfaces and is thrown back, and the thrown-back light fills the space between the columns with a brightness so dense that it seems to have weight, to have texture, to be a substance the way water is a substance. The mineral surfaces collaborate in this: the feldspar contributes a pearly sheen, the mica contributes brief, blinding flashes as crystal faces catch the angle just right, the quartz contributes a clear, hard transparency that lets light penetrate into the rock before bouncing it back out, slightly delayed, slightly warmer in tone. The result is a space that is lit from every direction simultaneously, where shadows exist only in the deepest crevices and even those are filled with reflected light from adjacent surfaces. The light changes the col's meaning hour by hour. In the full blaze of midday the col is an assault — white on grey on white, the sky bleached to a luminous void, every surface screaming with reflected energy, the eye forced to squint or close, the visual field reduced to a landscape of glare and silhouette. In the deep-blue twilight the col transforms: the iron-oxide patterns on the basalt darken to something almost visceral, the concentric rings seeming to pulse as the eye's rod cells take over and the brain begins to hallucinate pattern where there is only contrast, and the mineral spirals tighten and tighten toward their centers as though drawing inward, and the cairn casts a long, thin shadow that reaches across the flat boulder like a finger pointing toward something you cannot see. Then the light goes, or nearly goes, and the col enters the longest phase of its day — the hours of blue-darkness when the stars appear overhead as pinpoints of impossible sharpness, when the frost begins its slow nucleation in the dark, when the cold — already total — becomes something beyond temperature, something closer to a fundamental condition of matter, as though the molecules of the rock itself are slowing, are approaching a stillness that is not quite stillness, is only the col's version of sleep.

---

What lives in the Col of Shattered Pillars is what has always lived there: process. Action without actor. Change without chooser. The frost grows and departs and returns. The minerals precipitate and accumulate and form patterns that almost, almost resolve into something a living mind would recognize as a face, a hand, a word. The rock fractures along its grain, grain by grain, century by century, and the fracture-fronts announce themselves with their sharp single *tick* in the cathedral quiet. The wind moves through the columnar gaps and occasionally, for a few seconds, finds a resonance that produces a note — a single pure tone that hangs in the thin air like something remembered from a world where there were throats to produce it. The light strikes and bounces and fills the space with its blind, bright insistence, and then retreats, and the darkness it leaves behind is not empty but *dense*, filled with the slow chemistry of ice formation and mineral deposition and the infinitesimal, ceaseless work of frost pulling the world apart one crystal at a time.

And then, once — or many times — someone came. Someone with hands and a purpose and a bundle of rounded stones and a core of chert and a hammerstone, and they stood in this place that has no shelter and no warmth and no food and no reason to visit, and they built something. They scratched marks into the rock. They left the waste of their toolmaking in the crevices like letters in a bottle thrown into a sea with no shore. They did these things, and then they left, and the col held what they left and holds it still — not as a memorial, not as a record, but as a surface on which certain chemical and physical processes can continue, which is exactly what the col does with every surface it has.

The seven stones of the cairn are cold. The chert flakes are sharp. The scratched lines on the boulder say nothing. The mineral patterns on the basalt columns go on accumulating, ring by ring, spiral by spiral, in a language that was old before the cairn-builder's ancestors had learned to stand upright. The frost forms each dawn and sublimates each afternoon and forms again, and the hiss of its departure is the closest thing the col has to a voice, and it says only this: *I am here. I am still here. I was always here.*
