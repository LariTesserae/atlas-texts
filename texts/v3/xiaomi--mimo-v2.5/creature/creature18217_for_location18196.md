The first thing I notice is the taste of the air: mineral, green, and faintly alive, like licking a wet stone in a forest. It coats the tongue. My boots sink slightly into the moss—not with a squelch, but with a soft, yielding resistance, as if walking on the hide of something sleeping. The ground is a living sponge, saturated but not drowned, holding the water in a capillary embrace. Underfoot, every surface is slick, beaded with a constant dew that isn’t dew at all; it’s the mountain’s own respiration, beading on the cool skin of basalt.

The sound I expected was the waterfall’s roar, and it is there—a deep, chest-vibrating drumming from the southern void. But it’s not the dominant sound. That is the **frogs**. Not a chorus, but a cacophony, a thousand small, wet voices stitching the silence together. They are invisible at first, camouflaged in the mossy channels, but when I kneel, I see them: glossy dark eyes blinking from beneath stone ledges, mottled flanks pressed to wet rock. Some are no bigger than my thumbnail, emerald-green and delicate, letting out a surprisingly loud *tink-tink* like two pebbles struck together. Others are larger, the color of wet slate, their throats pulsing with a deep, resonant *wub-wub-wub* that I feel in my knees. The eggs are everywhere, in gelatinous strings and clusters in the shallows—future generations held in trembling suspension, safe in this cradle of water and stone.

The plants have surrendered to the wind. Nothing grows upward; everything sprawls, clings, or hugs. The moss is a universe in itself—a dense, plush turf of varying textures. Some patches are feathery and pale, others are like stiff, dark velvet, and still others have tiny, star-like sporophytes rising on delicate red stalks. In the cracks of the largest boulders, ferns unscroll like green fists, their fronds beaded with water that catches the flat, grey light. The flowering plants—small, with waxy, pale-blue bells—nod relentlessly. They are not pretty in a conventional sense; they are tough, compact, and utterly tenacious, their roots finding purchase in hairline fractures. I see no insects visiting them, but something must be; the ecosystem, however small, must complete its cycles.

The human marks are more disquieting than I imagined from the description. The cairn isn’t just a pile of stones; it’s a gesture, a sentence spoken into the mountain in a language of balance and weight. It has *settled*. The moss has grown over its base, integrating it, making it part of the geology. It feels less like a monument and more like a growth. The carved slab is stranger. I run my fingers over the grooves—shallow, blunted by centuries of weeping stone. The marks could be a tally, a name, or a map. Or they could be none of those. They feel like an echo, a mute testimony to a presence that the mountain has almost digested.

And then there is the hum. It comes and goes, often masked by the waterfall’s roar or the wind’s whistle through the pinnacles. But in a lull, when the frogs pause for a fraction of a second, I feel it more than hear it—a low, tectonic drone that seems to emanate from the very rock beneath my feet. It’s not mechanical. It’s organic, like the resonance inside a seashell, or the sound of a vast, slow circulation. The mountain’s pulse? Air moving in a subterranean chamber? My own blood in my ears? I can’t be sure. It makes the back of my neck prickle with a primordial, undefinable awareness.

The clouds are not just weather here; they are inhabitants. They drift through the saddle in slow, silent rivers, muffling sound, reducing the world to a grey, wet cocoon. When they thicken, the pinnacles vanish, and the sense of enclosure becomes absolute. I am inside a room of mist and sound. When they thin, the light doesn’t brighten so much as clarify—the wet stone glows with a dull, internal luminescence.

I understand now why the early people left little trace. This place is not meant for permanent habitation. It is a **threshold**—a damp, loud, living room between the sky and the abyss. The frogs are its true citizens, its architects of song. The moss is its foundation. The mountain itself is its slow, breathing host. I am a temporary guest, standing in the center of a self-contained, self-sustaining world that operates on a timescale I cannot grasp. It is indifferent to me, but not hostile. It is simply *itself*: a wet, singing, weeping saddle, enduring between the fangs of stone, holding its ancient, liquid conversation with the sky. I leave my boot-prints in the moss, but I know the weeping stone will erase them by nightfall. The conversation will continue, uninterrupted.
