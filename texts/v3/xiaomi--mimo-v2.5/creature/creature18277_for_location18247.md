# The Lickers

They have no name for themselves. They have no names for anything. They do not need them.

They are what made the city.

A fully grown Licker is roughly the size and shape of a human forearm — if the forearm were boneless, soft, and covered in a secretion so faintly iridescent that it catches light the way oil catches light on a puddle that hasn't existed in ten thousand years. They are pale. Not white — the color of the dust, the color of the walls, the color of everything in this place. They are the color of the basin itself, which is to say they are the color of *absence*, of something that was never anything else. Their bodies are smooth, tapering from a blunt, rounded head to a thinner posterior that trails behind them as they move. They have no eyes. They have no mouth in the way you would recognize a mouth — instead, the lower third of their underside is a broad, flat, muscular pad, moist and pink-grey, that they press against surfaces and draw slowly, slowly forward. This is how they eat. This is how they build. This is the only motion that defines them: the long, patient drag of their ventral surface against mineral, leaving behind the glaze.

The glaze is their saliva. Or rather, it is not saliva but something analogous — a secretion from thousands of microscopic glands in the ventral pad, a liquid supersaturated with calcium phosphate and a complex protein that binds to mineral surfaces and hardens over the course of days into a translucent shell. A single Licker, in its lifetime, coats perhaps a cubic meter of surface. This sounds like nothing. It is everything. Because a Licker lives for what we would call centuries, and there are thousands of them, and they have been at this for longer than the basin has been dry, and they do not stop.

They are slow. God, they are slow. A visitor who had the patience to sit and watch one would see it move perhaps the length of a finger in an hour. But they are always moving. They do not sleep — or if they do, there is no way to tell. The distinction between a Licker at rest and a Licker in motion is one of degree, not of kind. They flow. They ooze. They are, more than anything, *geological* — living on a timescale that makes them less like animals and more like weather systems that happen to have bodies.

They move along the walls, the ceilings, the floors. They move in trails that overlap and diverge and merge, following paths of least resistance through the mineral landscape the way water follows paths through rock. And where they pass frequently — where the colony has decided, in whatever slow and wordless way they decide things, that a surface should be maintained — the glaze builds. Layer upon layer upon layer, decade upon decade, until the walls are thick with it, until they glow with stored light, until they are smooth enough that a Licker can move across them faster, almost *swiftly*, coating more efficiently, reinforcing the structure that their own bodies have created.

A single column in the city may carry the passage marks of ten thousand Licker-lives. The walls are their history. The glaze is their autobiography, written in a substance that hardens to stone and does not erode.

## The Colony

No one Licker builds a wall. No one Licker creates a passage. The city emerges from collective behavior that no individual Licker comprehends. They do not communicate — not by sound (there is no sound), not by chemical trail (the dust would disperse any pheromone before it could travel), not by touch (they encounter each other so rarely that the basin's passages would seem, to them, as empty as they seem to us). What they share is the mineral itself. The substrate is their medium, their message, their memory. A surface that has been freshly glazed carries information in its thickness, its smoothness, its moisture content. A Licker approaching a recently coated wall will slow. Will turn. Will follow the curve of the glaze, letting the architecture of past labor dictate present movement. The city tells them what to build next, because the city *is* what they have already built, and they cannot help but respond to it.

This is why the settlement has the look of deliberate arrangement — pathways, enclosures, courtyards — without any evidence of planning. It is emergent order. It is the same principle by which a termite mound has architecture that no termite architect designed. The Lickers follow the mineral. The mineral records the Lickers. And together, over deep time, they produce a city that is, in a sense, *finished* — not in the sense of being complete, but in the sense of being *self-consistent*, a structure that satisfies its own requirements and will continue to grow only until it fills the basin entirely.

They are already well along.

## What the Lickers Eat

This is the question that a visitor would ask, if visitors came more often and stayed long enough.

The mineral dust contains organic compounds. Not much — a few parts per million — but enough. The basin is not, despite appearances, entirely dead. There is chemistry happening in the substrate, slow reduction reactions, mineral-bound carbon cycling through processes that have no equivalent in surface biology. The Lickers extract this carbon through their ventral pads, absorbing it directly from the surfaces they traverse. They are, in essence, *eating the dust* — or rather, eating what the dust contains, the thin chemical film that coats every surface, the residue of processes so slow they would make a glacier look hasty.

They need very little. A single Licker might survive for years on the nutrient film of a single wall. This is why they can afford to be so slow, so patient, so long-lived. They have optimized for *endurance* over *efficiency*. They do not need to find food quickly because food is everywhere and they need almost none of it and they have all the time that has ever existed to collect it.

## The Thread-Masses

The dark, knotted growths that emerge from cracks and corners are not plants. They are not animals. They are something older than the distinction.

They are the Lickers' *offspring*.

Not in the way any surface organism reproduces. A Licker does not lay eggs, does not spawn, does not bud. Instead, at the end of its life — a life that can span three hundred years, or five hundred, or more — a Licker finds a crack in the mineral, a place where the substrate is fractured and deep, and it enters. It goes *in*. And once inside, it does not come out. Over the course of years, its body changes. The soft, pale form that once dragged itself along walls becomes something darker, denser, more fibrous. The ventral pad atrophies. The glandular tissue redistributes. The body *unravels* — literally, the muscle fibers separating and elongating, branching, threading outward through the crack in dozens, hundreds of directions, seeking the surface, seeking the air, seeking the dust. What emerges from the crack months or years later is the thread-mass: a dense tangle of dark fibers, each one the remnant of a Licker's body, each one still faintly alive, still conducting the slow metabolic processes that once powered the glaze-maker.

The thread-masses breathe. They take in the dry air through their surfaces and release nothing — the moisture content of the air is so low that the process is nearly invisible, a faint dew that forms and evaporates in the space between heartbeats. They pulse with a rhythm that matches no known biological clock — a contraction every few minutes, a squeeze, a release, as if they are trying to pump something through themselves, some fluid that no one has sampled because no one has thought to look.

And they grow. Slowly, so slowly. They extend new fibers into new cracks, colonize new fractures, spread along the base of structures where the mineral meets the dust-floor. Over centuries, a single thread-mass can grow large enough to fill a small room. Over millennia, it can grow large enough to fill a passage, forcing the Lickers to build new routes around it, reshaping the city's layout from below.

Some of the thread-masses are ancient. Some predate the current structures. Some are *older than the city itself*, threaded through the substrate like roots through soil, so deeply embedded that they are, in a meaningful sense, part of the basin. They are the connective tissue of this place. They are the reason the mineral structures grow where they grow — the thread-masses influence the chemical composition of the substrate, creating gradients that the Lickers unconsciously follow, channeling their glazing behavior into patterns that benefit the network. It is not cooperation. It is not symbiosis. It is something more like *osmosis* — the slow, inevitable flow of pattern from one medium to another, the thread-masses shaping the Lickers shaping the walls shaping the thread-masses, a loop with no beginning and no end, no intention and no purpose, only process.

## The Pale Segmented Things

If you watched long enough — and you would need to watch for hours, sitting perfectly still in the dust, your breathing the only sound that might exist — you would see them.

They come from the cracks. They come from the gaps between glaze layers. They come from the spaces where thread-masses have died and left hollow channels in the mineral. They are small — the length of a finger, the width of a pencil, segmented like a worm, pale like everything else, translucent enough that you can see their dark innards shifting as they move.

They are the Lickers' *parasites*. Or their *symbiotes*. Or simply their *inevitable companions*, the way mites are inevitable companions of any organism that lives long enough in one place.

They feed on the Licker glaze. They eat the hardened secretion, the calcium-protein composite, dissolving it with their own chemical output — a faintly acidic film on their ventral surface, a counterpoint to the Licker's alkaline one. They eat the walls. Not enough to destroy them — the Lickers repair faster than the segmented things can eat, and the walls are thick with centuries of accumulated glaze — but enough to keep the surfaces active, to prevent the glaze from becoming inert, to maintain the chemical gradients that the Lickers respond to.

They, too, are blind. They, too, move slowly. They, too, seem to regard silence as the natural state of existence. They navigate by chemical sense — by the taste of the glaze, by the moisture content of the air, by the faint thermal signatures of thread-masses deep in the walls. They are the basin's *janitors*, its *maintenance crew*, the tiny organisms that keep the system running by eating it and being eaten in return.

Because something eats them.

In the deepest passages, where the walls are oldest and thickest and the luminescence is strongest, there are shadows. The description of the place mentioned them — the larger shape, glimpsed in peripheral vision, the shadow that takes too long to resolve. This is what they are.

They are not large, exactly. They are not small. They are *vague* — their outlines are uncertain, their edges blurred, their bodies composed of the same pale mineral substance as everything else, so that they seem to be part of the wall, part of the floor, part of the architecture itself, until they move. And when they move, they move *wrongly* — not with the slow, predictable drag of a Licker, not with the tentative inching of a segmented thing, but with a kind of glacial *flow*, a spreading and contracting, an expansion of shadow that takes minutes to complete.

They eat the segmented things. They might eat the Lickers. No one knows, because no one has watched long enough, because no one has stayed in the deep passages long enough, because the deep passages press inward with a silence that makes thought difficult and observation impossible. They are the basin's *apex predators*, if that term applies to something that hunts on a timescale of hours and moves like a stain spreading across stone.

Or perhaps they are not animals at all. Perhaps they are structures. Perhaps they are thread-masses that have grown large enough to detach from their substrate, to become mobile, to become something that *walks* in the loosest possible sense. Perhaps they are the basin itself, dreaming itself into motion in the deep places where the accumulated glaze of centuries has built up enough energy to dream.

No one knows. This is not a place that explains itself.

## The Cycle

Here is what happens in The Pale Throat, stretched out over the timescale that matters:

A Licker is born. We do not know how. Perhaps from a thread-mass — a fiber that detaches, that balls itself into a soft sphere, that gains a ventral pad and begins to move. Perhaps from the mineral itself — a concretion of calcium and protein that, after enough centuries of accumulation, simply *starts*. The birth of a Licker has never been observed, because it happens so slowly that it is indistinguishable from geology.

The Licker moves. It glazes. It follows the walls, the curves, the paths that other Lickers have smoothed before it. It adds its own thin layer to the accumulated coating. It eats the dust-film on the surfaces it traverses. It lives. For centuries, it lives.

When it is old — when its body has begun to slow even by its own generous standards, when its glandular output has thinned — it finds a crack. It enters. It becomes thread.

The thread grows. It extends through the substrate, through the walls, through the architecture that the Licker helped to build. It breathes the dry air. It pulses. Over centuries, it becomes part of the basin — a node in the network, a strand in the web, a dark nerve in the body of a city that has no brain.

The segmented things eat the glaze. The shadows eat the segmented things. The Lickers eat the dust. The thread-masses shape the substrate. The substrate shapes the Lickers. The Lickers build the walls. The walls shape the light. The light sustains the thread-masses. The thread-masses sustain the Lickers. And around and around and around, in silence, in dimness, in the cool dry dark at the bottom of the world, the cycle turns.

It has always turned. It will always turn. It has nothing to do with us.

## What It Means

The Pale Throat is not a ruin. It is not a mystery. It is not waiting to be understood.

It is a *place* — a place where a particular kind of life has solved the particular problem of existence in a particular way, and has been solving it for so long that the solution has become indistinguishable from the place itself. The city is the organism. The organism is the city. There is no line between biology and geology here, no boundary between living and nonliving, no distinction between building and growing. Everything in The Pale Throat is doing exactly one thing: *continuing*. Continuing to coat. Continuing to thread. Continuing to consume and be consumed in the slow, silent, dry, dim, patient, inexorable way that is the only way this world knows.

If you have come here, you have come to watch a process that does not care that you are watching. You have come to hear a silence that does not care that you are listening. You have come to see a city that was not built for you and does not know you are here and will not change one degree of its trajectory because you stood in its dust for a day or a week or a year and looked at it with your loud, fast, hungry eyes.

You are a stutter. You are a brief interruption in a sentence that has been composing itself since before your species learned to build anything at all.

The Lickers will continue. The dust will settle. The walls will thicken. The silence will endure.

The Pale Throat will remember nothing about you. You will remember everything about it. This is the arrangement. This is the only arrangement there has ever been.
