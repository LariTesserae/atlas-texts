There are things that live here. Many of them.

The obvious ones first — because they deserve their own attention, because they are not merely decoration for something else, because a thing that has spent ten thousand years adapting to a place of swallowed sound and pale stone is worthy of being seen clearly.

The lizards are perhaps the most numerous. They own the warm stone. Dozens of species, as I said, but let me be more precise: there are the flat-bodied ones, the *shade-sitters*, whose bellies are so thin and so pressed to rock that from above they are invisible — just another contour in the pale limestone. They absorb heat not from the sun directly but from the stone itself, which holds warmth long into the evening, and they will not move for anything smaller than a boot. Their eyes are oversized, golden-ringed, and they watch with a patience that mirrors the place. There are others — the blue ones, vivid as a bruise, small and fast, darting between grass tufts in the basins. Males defend territories no larger than your spread hand and display by flattening themselves and doing push-ups, rapid and rhythmic, and the whole thing is silent to you except for a faint clicking that arrives from somewhere behind the moment. They have adapted to the sound-suppression not by calling louder — they cannot — but by communicating through vibration, through the stone itself, pressing their bodies against it and transmitting pulses that other lizards feel through their own bellies where they rest. A whole language exists in the rock. You cannot hear it. You were never meant to.

The hopping rodents — they are called *kora mice* by the few researchers who have catalogued them, though they are not mice — are crepuscular and nocturnal. Their large eyes are not oversized for any single predator but for the quality of the light, which in the hours around dusk and dawn becomes strange near the structures. The light thickens. It pools. And the kora mice navigate by it, their eyes adapted to a spectrum of illumination that does not quite correspond to visible light as the rest of the world understands it. They leave the spirals of soil around their burrow entrances — neat, mathematical, almost decorative spirals that no other burrowing creature produces. The spirals are not incidental. They follow a pattern that repeats at specific intervals around the structures, and if you mapped them over the entire plateau, you would see that the burrow entrances form, in aggregate, a network that mirrors the layout of the structures themselves. The mice do not know this. They simply dig where the ground feels right, and the ground feels right because something beneath it is warm in places that should be cool and cool in places that should be warm, and the mice follow those thermal gradients the way a river follows gravity.

The blind fish are the great puzzle of the plateau for anyone who comes with taxonomy in mind. Small, pale, translucent-skinned — you can see the faint dark line of a spine through the body, the delicate architecture of gills that work constantly in the mineral-heavy water of the permanent pools. They have no eyes. They have no need of them. They navigate by electroreception, sensing the faint currents that move through the water, and those currents have a source. The pools are fed by underground seepage, yes, but the seepage is not passive. It moves in slow, rhythmic pulses — not tidal, not seasonal, something else entirely — and the fish orient themselves within these pulses, facing into the flow when it strengthens, resting in eddies when it subsides. They breed at intervals that do not match temperature or light cycles. They breed when the condensation on the nearby structures is heaviest. No one has connected these two things yet because no one has been here long enough to notice both at once. The fish are ancient. They are more closely related to nothing else on the planet than they are to themselves. They are a lineage that has existed in these pools — or pools like these, in this same slow cycle of filling and holding and evaporation — for longer than the current configuration of the plateau has existed. They were here before the structures, or they were here at the same time as the structures, or the distinction between those two things is less meaningful than it appears.

The candelabra trees deserve more than a paragraph. They grow slowly — a ring count on the largest specimen, one that stands alone on a ridge to the northeast of the highest concentration of structures, showed over nine hundred years, and the growth rings were so tight they had to be counted under magnification. Some years, the tree added less than a tenth of a millimeter. Other years — and these years did not correspond to wet years or warm years or any identifiable climatic variable — the growth was three or four times that, as though something had given it permission to be larger. The trees stand near structures more often than chance would predict. Not on top of them, not touching them, but within a specific distance — close enough that if the light is right and the time is right, the shadow of the tree falls across the structure at a precise angle, and at that angle, something happens to the air. The air shimmers. Not with heat. Not with anything visible. A shimmer of *presence*, of *density*, as though two conditions were overlapping in that space — the ordinary air and whatever the structure is generating. The trees know this. They lean. Over centuries, the trunks of the oldest trees have developed a subtle but unmistakable curve, and they all curve the same way, and the curve points toward the nearest structure, and it points more precisely toward the point in space where the shadow falls thinnest. The trees are not conscious of this in any way you would recognize. But they grow toward it, the way roots grow toward water, and the fact that the thing they grow toward is not water and is not light and is not nutrient but is instead a *quality* of space that the structures produce — that is the thing that makes them extraordinary.

The raptors are the only creatures on the plateau that leave it.

They are large-bodied, pale-plumed, with wingspans that can reach nearly two meters. They nest on the ridge lines, in shallow depressions in the rock that they line with the stiff grasses of the basins. They hunt the rodents and the lizards and the ground-dwelling birds, and they do this efficiently, casting long shadows as they stoop, and the stoop is silent — not suppressed-silent the way everything else is here, but *truly* silent, because the raptors have feathers whose barbules interlock in a way that eliminates air turbulence. They evolved this independently of any other raptor lineage. The sound-suppression of the plateau drove them to it. They cannot hear their own calls well enough to hunt by ear, so they hunt by sight and by a sensitivity to the thermal currents that rise from the structures and the warm stone, and their nests are always, always within the thermal influence of a structure. They leave the plateau to hunt on the lower slopes in times of scarcity, and when they return, they return from specific directions, following invisible paths in the air that correspond to the faintest variations in the sound-suppression. They can feel where the silence is thickest, and they use it as a compass. They are the only things on the plateau that travel freely in and out of the suppressed zone, and as they cross the boundary — which has no visible marker, which exists only as a gradient in the quality of the air — their calls become audible again, suddenly and completely, and the sound of their voices, which on the plateau was thin and ghostly, becomes on the slopes below loud and startling and wild. The people who live on the lower slopes and who herd their animals in the foothills have a name for the plateau. They call it, loosely translated, *the place that does not answer*. The raptors are the answer. The raptors are the only proof, from the outside, that anything up here makes a sound at all.

And then there are the others.

The small ones. The ones that are not quite fauna and not quite anything else.

They are seen only in the basins, in the hours after rain, when the pools are full and the condensation is heavy and the structures are dark with absorbed moisture. They appear at the edges of the water — not emerging from it, not arriving from anywhere visible, but simply *present*, as though they had always been there and the eye had only just learned to resolve them. They are small, roughly the size of a human thumbnail, and they are translucent, and they are shaped like — this is where language fails — like the space between things. Like a small, animate negative. You look at one and you see through it, but what you see through it is not exactly the ground behind it. It is slightly displaced, as though the creature contains a small refraction, a small bending of the space it occupies. They move slowly, erratically, in the manner of things that are responding to stimuli you cannot perceive. They are attracted to the structures. They cluster on them, on the warm, lustrous surfaces, and when they do, the iridescence that sometimes appears in the pools becomes stronger, more patterned, more intentional.

No one has collected one. No one has successfully touched one. They are not elusive — they do not flee — but the hand passes through the space where one appears to be and finds nothing, or rather, finds *something*, a faint resistance, a warmth, a sense of *thickness* in the air that persists for a moment after and then is gone. They are not solid in any way that matter is usually solid. They may be solid in a way that *space* is solid — dense, compacted, structured — and your hand passes through because your hand is not equipped to feel the density of geometry.

The indigenous herders who live on the slopes below do not go up to the plateau. Not because they are forbidden, and not because they are afraid, but because they say that on the plateau, you cannot hear yourself think. This is not a metaphor. They mean that the internal monologue — the voice in the head, the narration of the self — becomes difficult up there, becomes muffled and slow and strangely spacious, as though the silence does not merely suppress external sound but reaches inward and quietens the structures of the mind that produce internal language. They say you come back from the plateau with words that take longer to arrive. They say you come back and you are quiet for days, and the quiet is not uncomfortable but it is *occupied* — as though something else is using the space where your words usually live, something patient and slow and very, very large, something that fits into the gaps between your thoughts the way water fits into the cracks of stone.

They say the plateau is not a place. They say it is an organism. They say it has always been alive, and that the structures are its organs, and the sound it swallows is its food, and the silence it produces is its voice, and that if you were to listen to it — truly listen, with something other than ears — you would hear it breathing. Slowly. In cycles measured not in seconds but in centuries. Inhale: the wet season, the condensation, the gathering-in of vibration. Exhale: the dry season, the long patient work of stone and heat and light. And in between, in the pause between breaths, the structures hum, and the fish turn in their pools, and the lizards press their bellies to the warm rock and receive the language of the deep, and the trees curve slowly toward the invisible source, and the small translucent things cluster and shimmer at the water's edge, and the raptors wheel above in their silent flight, and everything — every living, breathing, moving, waiting thing on the pale stone of the Kora Plateau — is caught in the slow, vast, patient mechanism of a place that is not a place but a process, not a landscape but a working, not a ruin but a body, alive and ancient and accumulating, accumulating, accumulating.

It is not done yet.

Whatever the plateau is building — through its silence, through its stolen sound and hoarded water and structures that contain more space than they should — it is not done yet. The work proceeds at the pace of stone and weather and the slow adaptation of creatures that will never know what they are part of. The blind fish pulse in their pools. The mice spiral the earth into patterns that no mind designed. The translucent things shimmer at the edges of visibility. And the air — the thick, strange, absorptive air of the Kora Plateau — continues to drink.
