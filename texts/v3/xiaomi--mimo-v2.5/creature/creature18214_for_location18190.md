# The Living of the Resz Plateau

---

## The Birds

They are called *kozh* by the people, though the word means something closer to "warmth-owner" than to any literal description of the bird itself. To a lowlander, seeing one for the first time, the creature would not immediately register as avian. It is a dense, round, pale thing — a sphere of feathers with a small dark face and two bright eyes set forward for binocular vision. The plumage is extraordinary in its density: layer upon layer of downy underfeathers beneath a tight armor of stiff, overlapping contour feathers that are almost white against the mineral rind but carry a faint grey barbing visible only at close range. A full-grown kozh is roughly the size and shape of a clenched fist, heavier than it looks, and warm to the touch — always warm, always. Its body temperature runs several degrees higher than a lowland bird's, a metabolic cost it pays for living at the edge of what is metabolically possible.

They nest in the crevices between basalt columns in colonies so dense that the birds are pressed together, breast to back, wing to wing, in the narrow vertical gaps. A single crevice might hold fifty birds stacked in a column from the warmed rock floor to the darkness above. They do not build nests. There is nothing to build with — no grass, no twigs, no mud. They lay their eggs directly on the mineral deposit, on the smooth, warm, pale rind that coats every surface within the rezh's halo. The eggs are small, dark, and thick-shelled, their surfaces roughened with a calcium deposit that anchors them to the rock so they will not roll into the cracks. In a good year — if the plateau's definition of "good" permits the word — a single kozh will lay three eggs. In a bad year, two, or one, or none. The people know which years are which. They have watched the rezh's behavior for patterns, for signs in the steam's thickness or warmth that correlate with the birds' fecundity, and they are usually right, though the mechanism is not one they could articulate in terms of mineral composition or geothermal output. They say the rezh "gives more" or "gives less," and this is true in every way that matters.

The birds feed on insects. They are not graceful in the air — their wings are short and round, their flight labored, a frantic whirring that carries them only from crevice to vent-face and back again — but on the rock, among the mineral deposits, they are deft and quick. They probe the damp surfaces with short, hard bills, extracting midges and larvae and tiny beetles with a precision born of ten thousand generations of practice. Their droppings are thick and white and ammonia-sharp, and they accumulate on the rock around every colony in a layer so deep in places that the people mine it — scraping it into baskets and carrying it to the outer rings of the settlement, where it is mixed with mineral grit and used to fuel slow-burning fires for metalworking. Nothing is wasted on the plateau. The birds eat the insects, and the insects eat the mineral deposits, and the birds' waste feeds the lichens and feeds the insects, and the people use the waste to make the tools that butcher the animals that graze the lichen-scrub. It is a circle so tight it is almost a point.

## The Insects

They should not exist. That is what a lowland naturalist would say, standing at the edge of a rezh-field in the plateau's less-cold season, watching the air shimmer with life. Midges — pale, small, almost translucent — rise from the mineral deposits in drifts so thick they are visible as clouds against the dark basalt. Flies — black, robust, fur-coated in tiny bristles — cluster on every warm surface. Beetles — tiny, armored, patient — work the crevices and cracks, scraping at the mineral rind with mandibles designed for a task no lowland insect has ever faced.

They live and die within meters. Their entire world is the rezh's halo — that ring of warmth and moisture and mineral particulate that extends, at most, thirty meters from the vent. Beyond it, the cold is absolute and instantaneous. No larva survives there. No pupa hatches. The insects are bound to their rezh with an exclusivity that borders on imprisonment, and yet they do not seem to suffer for it. They have never known anything else. Their life cycles are short — a matter of weeks from egg to adult — and they pack into those weeks everything that life requires: growth, metamorphosis, flight, mating, egg-laying. The midges mate on the wing, rising in paired spirals above the steam column. The beetles mate in the cracks, buried in warmth. The flies lay their eggs in the wet mineral deposits, in the smooth hollows where the condensation pools and freezes and thaws and freezes again in the micro-cycles that are the heartbeat of the rezh.

The people do not think about the insects as insects. They think of them as part of the rezh itself — as another expression of the vent's warmth, like the steam or the mineral rind or the ice fog. To disturb the insects is to disturb the rezh. This is not superstition. It is observation, refined over generations. The people know that a rezh where the insects thrive is a healthy rezh, a giving rezh. A rezh where the insects thin out is changing — its steam may be cooling, its mineral output shifting, its fracture line closing. The insects are the canary, if the plateau had canaries. Instead, it has midges.

## The Six-Legged Grazers

They are called *tesh* — a word that means, roughly, "those who endure."

The first thing to understand about a tesh is that it does not hurry. Nothing on the plateau hurries. The tesh is a low, broad, four-bodied animal — four body segments in a line, each segment supported by a pair of heavy, blunt-toed legs, giving it six legs in total, distributed along a length that can reach two meters from face to tail. Its silhouette is low and flat, like a long stone set on short pillars, and when it stands motionless among the basalt columns — which is most of the time — it is nearly indistinguishable from the rock itself. The pelage is thick, woolly, and the color of the mineral deposits: pale grey-white streaked with ochre, with a dusting of actual mineral grit that adheres to the wool and gives the animal an almost geological texture. A tesh in repose looks like a drift of frost-shattered stone. This is not camouflage in the way a lowland animal employs it — there are no predators on the plateau that hunt by color recognition in the thin, blue, directionless light. It is something deeper than camouflage. It is a settling-into. The tesh has become the landscape because the landscape has shaped it, over unfathomable generations, into a creature that fits it perfectly, the way water fits a crack in basalt.

They graze — though "graze" is generous. They browse the lichen and moss that grow on the mineral rinds and in the warm crevices near the rezh. Their mouths are broad and blunt, lined with hard, flat surfaces for scraping rather than cutting, and they work them slowly, methodically, over every warm surface within reach. They do not move to find food. They wait for the food to be near them, which, at the density of life around a healthy rezh, it always is. A tesh might spend an entire day shifting its weight from one set of legs to another, turning its broad head a few degrees to reach a new patch of lichen, and never move more than body-length from where it began.

Their metabolism is the slowest of any mammal on the plateau. They eat little. They move less. Their hearts beat so slowly that a lowlander pressing an ear to a tesh's flank would hear a single, distant thud every few seconds and might mistake it for the pulse of the stone itself. In this, they would not be entirely wrong. The tesh and the plateau operate on the same timescale. The basalt rises millimeters per year. The tesh sleeps for hours at a stretch, curled in the shelter of a column-cluster, its six legs folded beneath it, its broad body rising and falling with the almost imperceptible rhythm of cold-blooded-adjacent warmth. It is not cold-blooded — its blood is warm, always, kept so by the rezh it never strays far from — but it has surrendered everything it can to the plateau's economy of energy. It does not burn fuel it does not need. It does not move when it can be still. It does not waste warmth.

They are social, but not in the way lowland herd animals are social. There is no hierarchy, no dominance display, no competition. When a group of tesh gathers around a vent — and they do, in groups of thirty or forty, packing together in the warmest zones with a tolerance for proximity that would be claustrophobic to a lowland creature — they simply stand. Shoulder to shoulder, flank to flank, breath mingling with breath and with the steam of the rezh, they stand together in a communion that has no visible structure. They do not groom one another. They do not vocalize. They occasionally press their broad heads together in a gesture so slow and gentle that it might be mistaken for an accidental contact, but the people who have watched them for centuries say it means something — not warmth-sharing exactly, but acknowledgment. *You are here. I am here. The rezh is here. This is enough.*

The people keep them. Not as cattle — there are no fences, no enclosures in the lowland sense — but as fellow inhabitants of the rezh's warmth. The tesh come to the settlements because the settlements are warm, because the people's body-heat supplements the rezh, because the stone dwellings shelter from the wind. In return, the people take what the tesh do not need: the occasional animal that dies naturally, its meat dried and stored; the wool, shed in clumps that catch on basalt edges and must be gathered before the grit buries it; the bone, which is dense and hard and serves for tools when copper is scarce. The relationship is so old that neither party remembers it beginning. It is simply a fact of the rezh, like the steam, like the mineral deposits, like the cold.

## The Predators

They are called *reshka* — a word that shares a root with *rezh* but carries a different weight, something like "breath-taker."

The reshka is a lean, long-bodied animal — four legs, low-slung, with a frame built for moving through narrow spaces between basalt columns in darkness. Its fur is pale, almost white, and lies flat and close against the body, offering no insulation to speak of. This is the first thing that confuses a lowland observer: the reshka is cold. It does not huddle at the rezh. It does not stand in the steam. It moves through the cold the way the cold moves through the air — invisibly, inevitably, as a natural state of being. Its body is designed for short bursts of intense heat generation: fast-twitch muscle fibers wrapped close to the skeleton, a metabolism that can spike and crash, a digestive system that extracts every calorie from infrequent meals and stores nothing. A reshka eats or it does not eat. It does not snack. It does not forage.

They hunt the kozh. That is their primary food — the nesting birds, pressed together in their crevices, heavy with eggs, slow to take flight. The reshka works the colony sites at night, in the complete darkness between the ice fog halos, moving through the basalt maze with a certainty that comes from a sense of smell so refined it can detect the warmth of a bird's body through a crack in the stone. They do not rush. They pause at each crevice, press their narrow snouts into the gap, and take what they can reach with a quick snap of their jaws. The birds do not flee — there is nowhere to flee to — and the reshka does not take more than it needs. It takes two, perhaps three birds from a colony of hundreds of thousands. It takes from the edges, from the outer crevices, where the birds are slightly colder, slightly less essential to the colony's heat-generating mass. The people say the reshka knows this — knows which birds to take without disrupting the colony — and they may be right. Predation on the plateau is not violence. It is redistribution.

The reshka does not compete with the people. It cannot survive in the settlements — too warm, too crowded, too much scent, too much noise. It does not take the tesh — the grazers are too large, too slow, too densely furred, and the reshka's jaws are not built for such work. It takes what is easy, what is available, what the cold has already half-killed. And when it dies — as all things on the plateau die — its pale body is found by the morning light, stiff and cold, and the people do not mourn it or celebrate it. They add it to the stone. They return it to the grit, to the dust that moves down slopes and into cracks and fills the joints between columns, grain by grain, century by century.

## The Things in the Cracks

Below the animals that walk the surface, in the dark warm spaces between and beneath the basalt columns, there is another world. It is small. It is ancient. It has never seen light, and it does not need to.

Worms — pale, blind, muscular — thread through the mineral deposits, eating the chemical precipitates left by the geothermal water. They are the size of a lowlander's finger and thicker, their bodies segmented and glistening with a mucus that contains trace metals — copper, iron, manganese — absorbed from the water that seeps through their habitat. The people do not eat them. They do not know about them. They exist in the dark, in the warm, in the spaces that even the basalt columns cannot fully seal, and they are the foundation of a food web that supports the crustaceans — small, translucent, many-legged — that in turn support the beetles that support the birds that support the people. It is a chain that runs from geothermal heat to human warmth, and every link is essential, and none of it is visible.

The crustaceans are remarkable. They have adapted to a world without seasons, without daylight, without any of the environmental cues that regulate lowland life. They breed when the mineral composition of the water shifts — a change so subtle that no human instrument on the plateau could detect it, but which these tiny creatures register with chemical receptors that are, in their own way, as precise as any technology. They breed in pulses, in bursts that correspond to deep shifts in the rezh's output — shifts that happen on timescales of decades, of centuries — and they have, over generations, calibrated their life cycles to these rhythms with an accuracy that is humbling. They are, in a sense, the oldest inhabitants of the plateau, present before the tesh, before the kozh, before the reshka, before the people. They were here when the basalt was newer and the rezh was stronger, and they will be here when the plateau finally finishes rising and begins its long erosion toward the lowlands it will never reach.

## The People

They are not many. At any given time, the total human population of the Resz Plateau might number a few thousand, concentrated in settlements of a few hundred each, clustered around the largest and steadiest rezh. They are small-bodied — the altitude and the cold and the generations of tight living have sculpted them into compact, dense-boned people with broad chests and short limbs, a morphology that conserves heat and minimizes exposure. Their skin is darkened, not by sun but by mineral — the fine grit that pervades everything on the plateau, that works into every crease and pore, that stains the skin a faint grey-brown over years of exposure. In the half-light of the ice fog, they look like the basalt itself given motion and breath. This is not an accident. This is what it means to live somewhere for a very long time: you become it.

Their faces are broad, their eyes are large — adapted to the flat, directionless light and to the deep darkness of nights spent in stone dwellings — and their hands are remarkable. Thick-fingered, short-nailed, the pads of the fingers calloused to a toughness that makes lowland skin look fragile by comparison. These are hands that work stone and copper and sinew and bone, day after day, generation after generation, with tools that do not change because the work does not change. A copper blade made today is shaped identically to one made two hundred years ago, because the design was perfected two hundred years ago and there is no reason to alter what is already right. Innovation on the plateau is not absent — it is slow, iterative, barely perceptible, a refinement of angle here, a adjustment of weight there, happening over decades rather than years. The people do not rush. They cannot afford to. A copper blade takes days to hammer from raw metal. A mistake costs not just time but material, and material on the plateau is finite and precious and carries the memory of every hand that has touched it.

They speak to one another in a language that is quiet by any standard — soft consonants, rounded vowels, long pauses that are not hesitations but part of the grammar itself, spaces in which meaning settles like grit into a crack. They do not shout. The thin air would not carry it, and the cold does not reward waste. Conversation happens in murmurs, in gestures, in the tilt of a head or the placement of a hand on a warm stone surface. A full-sentence utterance on the plateau might last three seconds and contain what a lowlander would need thirty to express, and it would be understood instantly and completely by everyone present, because the context is shared, the knowledge is shared, the life is shared. There is no ambiguity on the plateau. There cannot be. Ambiguity is a luxury of places where the cost of misunderstanding is low. Here, the cost is everything.

Their stories are long. Their songs are longer. In the evenings, in the warm interiors of the inner-ring dwellings, pressed together around a vent-channel that exhales its mineral warmth into the half-light, the people tell stories that last for hours — not as entertainment but as transmission, as the carrying of knowledge from one generation to the next in the only medium available. Every story encodes information: the location of a rezh, the behavior of a particular tesh herd, the signs that a vent is changing, the names of every tool and every stone and every path between settlements. The stories are dense. They are layered. They contain sub-stories within stories, references that only those who have heard the earlier tales can decode, and this density is deliberate — it is how you pack a library into a language that has no written form, how you carry the entire accumulated knowledge of a people in the breath they share on cold nights in stone rooms lit by the faint glow of ice fog outside.

They are not unhappy. This is important to say, because a lowlander looking at their life — the cold, the confinement, the repetition, the sameness — might project misery onto it. But misery requires comparison, and the people of the plateau have nothing to compare their life to. This is the only world they have ever known, and within it, there is richness: the warmth of a well-functioning rezh, the satisfaction of a copper blade struck true, the particular pleasure of a tesh pressing its broad head to yours in the warm steam, the sound of a child's voice learning the first story, the deep, quiet knowledge that you are part of something — a rezh, a colony, a herd, a people, a stone — that was here before you and will be here after.

They grieve. Of course they grieve. When a child is lost to the cold — when someone wanders beyond the halos on a dark night and does not return — the settlement falls silent for a day, and then the stories begin again, and the child's name is woven into the telling, and it becomes part of the knowledge that is carried forward. Nothing is lost on the plateau. Not the dead, not the names, not the lessons. Everything is returned to the stone.

## The Stone Itself

And here, at the end, we must speak of the stone as a living thing, because the people do, and because they are not wrong.

The basalt breathes. Not metaphorically — the rezh are literal breath, literal exhalation of deep heat and deep water through the fractures of the plateau's young body. The stone rises. It rises millimeters per year, pushing upward against the sky, and the people feel this in their bones the way they feel the cold: as a constant, total, inescapable fact. They do not think of the plateau as solid. They think of it as a slow animal, a creature of unimaginable size and patience that is going somewhere none of them can follow, and they are riding it, and they are grateful.

The ice fog makes the stone glow at night — a faint luminescence, a ghost-light that is only starlight and steam but that looks, to eyes adapted to this place, like the stone itself is showing its warmth, like the deep heat is visible, like the breath is light. The people walk between the halos in darkness, navigating by glow, and each glow is a home, and each home is a mouth of the breathing stone, and they move through this landscape of breath and stone and light with the certainty of creatures who have never needed to ask where they belong.

They belong here. In the cold, in the thin air, in the silence that is not empty but full — full of the subsonic moan of wind through basalt columns, full of the wet exhalation of the rezh, full of the two-note call of a kozh in its crevice, full of the slow pulse of geothermal warmth rising through the rock beneath your feet. The plateau is alive. Everything on it is alive. And the life is not despite the cold, not despite the altitude, not despite the thinness of the air and the scarcity of the water and the sameness of the light. The life is because of these things. The plateau has made its own terms, and everything that lives here — midge, tesh, kozh, reshka, worm, crustacean, person, stone — has accepted those terms, and in the acceptance has become something that could not exist anywhere else.

This is what it means to live on the Resz Plateau: to be shaped by a place so completely that you cannot imagine another, and not to want to.
