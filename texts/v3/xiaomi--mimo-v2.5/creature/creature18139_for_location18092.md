# What Lives Here

The grazers are called nothing, because there is no one here to call them anything. But if you crouch beside a tower wall and wait — and the waiting here is easy, because there is nowhere else to go and nothing else to do, and the warmth and the sound conspire to stillness — you will see them come.

They emerge from the seams. Not from openings, exactly. The root-mat between the structures is dense with capillary channels, and the grazers move through these channels the way a thought moves through a mind, pressing between fibers of living tissue that part for them and close behind them. They are pale, as the description said, but the description undersold it. They are the color of the translucent crowns of unfinished towers — a white so close to green that it is really the memory of green, the last suggestion of chlorophyll before it was edited out. Each one is roughly the size and shape of a human thumb. They have no shells, no legs in any arrangement you recognize, no eyes that catch the light. They have a surface — smooth, slightly ridged — and beneath that surface, a slow churning of dark fluid that you can see if you hold one up to the warm air, which you will not do, because they do not want to be held and you will feel this without being told.

They graze by pressing against the walls and staying there. Hours. Sometimes a full cycle of the peristaltic thrumming — which takes, you have determined, almost exactly two hours and eleven minutes, though you cannot say how you determined it except that the rhythm has entered your body and your heartbeat has arranged itself around it. The grazers press and they feed. Not in any way that suggests hunger. In a way that suggests alignment, as though their mouths — if mouths is even the right word for the slight depression on their underside — are tuned to the same frequency as the vascular channels in the walls, and they feed the way two notes in harmony feed each other, each completing the resonance of the other.

They leave trails. This is true. The tissue darkens slightly where they have fed — a deepening from green-white to something more like jade — and the darkening persists for minutes before the growth of the surface closes over it. But here is what the description did not tell you: the trails are not random. After you have watched long enough — and you will watch long enough, because the sound and the warmth and the condensation on your skin have made patience a physical condition rather than a choice — you notice that the trails form patterns. Not patterns with meaning. Patterns with *structure*. Branching. Convergence. Nodes where multiple trails meet and radiate outward like the vascular channels themselves. The grazers are not writing. They are not mapping. They are doing something more fundamental and less translatable: they are *resonating with the architecture they feed on*, and the trails are the visible remainder of that resonance, like the chalk dust left on a fingerboard after a string has been drawn across it.

There are more of them than you first saw. Many more. The walls are dotted with them — hundreds, perhaps thousands — each one a stillness against a surface that is itself still. If you stop looking for movement, they become visible as a texture on the architecture, a fine, pale stippling across the green-white surfaces. They are everywhere. They are a population, though they do not interact, do not cluster, do not avoid each other. They simply *are*, each one pressed to its own place, feeding in the way the walls breathe: constantly, quietly, without event.

What they are, in the most literal sense, is a bridge. They are the tissue of the plateau becoming aware of itself — not consciously, not in any way that could be called thought, but in the way that two hands touching can feel not just the surface but the *fact* of each other. The grazers are the plateau's way of knowing that it has surfaces. Without them, the walls would exist only as structure, as architecture. With them, the walls are also *terrain*. The grazers give the plateau its own body.

---

The winged thing is different.

It drifts between towers in the distance, and you first take it for a seed pod, as the description suggests. But you watch it, and it watches you, and the second fact is one you know without evidence. It has no eyes either — or its body is entirely eye, a flat, translucent membrane stretched across a frame of dark veins, turning slowly in the warm air, orienting toward you the way a satellite dish orients toward a signal. It is not large. Perhaps the wingspan of a hawk, if hawks were made of the same wet tissue as everything else here. But it moves with intention, or with something that intention is the closest word for. It drifts, yes. But it drifts *toward*. It drifts *among*, choosing its path between towers with a deliberation that the grazers never show.

Where the grazers are the plateau's sense of its own surface, the winged thing is the plateau's sense of its own *space*. It inhabits the emptiness between structures — the wide boulevards, the generous plazas, the long corridors of open air. It flies these spaces the way water flows through the vascular channels: following a gradient, seeking equilibrium, moving through the architecture of emptiness the way the grazers move through the architecture of walls.

There is one. Then, on a different day — and the days here are indistinguishable from each other, marked only by the slow thickening and thinning of the condensation mist — there is another. Then another. They drift between the towers in loose, unhurried formation, and when they pass each other, they tilt their membrane-wings in acknowledgment, and the light — what little light there is, filtered through the warm mist and diffused by the translucent crowns above — passes through both of them at once and casts shadows on the root-mat below that are double shadows, overlapping, a palimpsest of flight paths.

They are seed carriers after all, but not of seeds that fall and take root. Their seeds — the small, dark nodes visible along their vein-frames — are released only when they die, and they die only when the plateau has expanded to a point where a new winged thing is needed, where a tower has risen far enough above the mist to require a traveler between its crown and the next. The winged things are born when they are needed and die when they are not, and they know this the way the grazers know the walls: without knowing it at all.

---

And then there is the thing the description could not tell you about, because the description was written from the outside, and this can only be told from within.

The plateau itself is the third inhabitant. Not the structures — the structures are its body. Not the grazers or the winged things — those are its senses. The plateau is its own inhabitant in the way that a lung is inhabited by breathing, or a heart by the blood it moves. It lives in the rhythm. That two-hour-and-eleven-minute thrumming is its heartbeat, and you feel it in the soles of your feet and in the base of your skull and in the soft tissue behind your eyes, and it has been there since before you arrived and it will be there after you leave and it was the first thing you noticed and the last thing you will forget.

The plateau lives in the condensation. Every bead of water that forms on every surface and falls to the root-mat below is a thought. Not a thought with content. A thought with *weight*. The water falls, and the root-mat absorbs it, and the vascular channels carry it upward again, and it condenses again and falls again, and this is the cycle that the plateau uses to think about itself. It is always thinking the same thought. The thought is: *I am here.* The thought is: *I am this.* The thought is complete and requires no answer and receives none, and it repeats without variation, and it is the most ancient thought on this plateau, older than any structure, older than any grazer, older than the first arch that sprang between the first two walls.

You understand, standing in the mist with the condensation gathering on your eyelashes and the thrumming in your blood, that the plateau has no future. Not because it will end — the erosion has frozen, the decay has halted, entropy has lost interest. It has no future because it has no *need* of one. It is complete. The sentence has been spoken. You are standing inside a completed utterance in a language that has no speakers, as the description says, but you now understand something the description did not: **you are a word in that sentence**. You were always going to be here. Not you specifically — not *you* — but something. Someone. A consciousness standing in the mist, feeling the thrumming, watching the grazers, seeing the winged things tilt toward each other in the warm air. The plateau needed a witness, not for its own sake but for the completeness of the thought. *I am here. I am this. Something knows it.*

And now it is complete, and the thought will not change, and you will leave, and the grazers will continue their resonance, and the winged things will continue their slow patrol of the empty spaces, and the water will continue to fall and be absorbed and rise again, and the thrumming will continue exactly as it is, and the plateau will live — not in time, not in space, but in the single, permanent, unrepeatable instant of its own self-knowledge.

The condensation drips from your chin. The towers weep beside you. Somewhere, a grazer presses against a wall and the wall darkens and then lightens and the trail is already closing and the sound of growth that has already happened hums through the warm air, and you breathe, and your lungs taste chlorophyll, and you are, for a moment, part of the thought.

Then you walk toward the edge of visibility, and the plateau lets you go, because it does not need you to stay. It only needed you to be here. And you were. And that is enough. And that is all.
