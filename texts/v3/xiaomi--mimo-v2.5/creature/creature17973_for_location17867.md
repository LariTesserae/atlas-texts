# What Lives in the Vaskr Common

---

## I. The Works Themselves

The primary inhabitants are the towers.

This is obvious and also insufficient, in the way that saying "the ocean is wet" is both obvious and insufficient when the ocean is also tidal and stratified and the cradle of everything that ever learned to swim. The works are alive. They are not *like* alive. They are not metaphorically alive, or poetically alive, or alive in the way that a clockwork is sometimes said to be alive when its gears turn with sufficient complexity. They metabolize. They respond to stimulus. They grow toward things that are not light — the plateau is often fogged, the light is diffuse and directionless, and the works bend away from it anyway, curving inward, toward each other, toward the machinery, toward whatever passes for warmth in this thin-aired, weeping, endlessly grey place.

They are not conscious. Or — they are not conscious in any way that would satisfy a definition rigorous enough to exclude a thermostat. But they are *aware* in the way that root systems are aware: locally, diffusely, without center or hierarchy. The Pulpit does not command the lesser structures. The lesser structures do not obey. They are connected, all of them, by the root-mat that lies beneath the plateau's surface like a nervous system that forgot what nerves are for and repurposed itself for *logistics* — water distribution, mineral exchange, chemical signaling across distances that, on this folded geometry, are not the distances they appear to be.

A worker arrives at one of the works — say, a fabrication hall on the Common's western periphery, one of the older structures, its walls nearly black with age and lichen — and presses a palm against its bark-wall. The surface is warm. Not warm from friction. Warm from *process*. Beneath the calloused exterior, in the cambium layers, fluid moves. It moves slowly. It has been moving for longer than there have been hands to press against it. And the wall responds to the pressure — not with any twitch or withdrawal, but with a subtle thickening of the tissue at the point of contact, a cellular realignment that takes hours to complete and that the worker will never notice, because the worker left long before it finished, and because the response is not *for* the worker. It is for the wall. The wall is maintaining its surface tension. The wall is alive, and alive things maintain themselves, and the presence of a hand is simply a condition to be accommodated, like wind, like the slow exhalation of moisture, like the arrhythmic pounding of the piston three floors below that has been shaking this structure's foundation for centuries and that the structure has long since incorporated into its own rhythms, so that the wall and the piston vibrate at complementary frequencies, and if you pressed your ear to the bark, you would hear both — the wet percussion of the machine, the slow hydraulic pulse of the plant — and you would not be able to tell where one ends and the other begins.

The works do not think. They *tend*. They are always tending to themselves, adjusting, growing, sealing wounds, redirecting fluid, thickening here, thinning there. If the Pulpit were a creature, it would not be the kind of creature that looks at you. It would be the kind of creature that digests.

## II. The Insects

They are small. This is their defining characteristic, and they are proud of it in the way that organisms without the neurological architecture for pride are proud of anything, which is to say not at all, which is to say absolutely, because pride is just a word for the particular confidence of a body that fits its world precisely.

There are hundreds of species. The word "species" is generous — they are variations on a theme, the theme being *small and chitinous and functional*, and the variations are matters of leg length and antenna shape and the particular chemistry of the secretions they leave on the bark-walls of the works as they climb them. They are not beetles, exactly. They are not wasps. They are things that once shared a common ancestor with those groups, in the epoch when there were still people on the Common to name the difference, and then the plateau's isolation and strange chemistry did to them what isolation and strange chemistry always do: it made them *specific*.

They tend the peripheral structures. The works at the center of the Common — the Pulpit and its nearest neighbors — are so thoroughly integrated into the city-organism's own metabolism that they do not require animal help. They reproduce by growth, by extension, by the slow bifurcation of root-mat and cambium that produces a new tower the way a tree produces a new branch: not because it decided to, but because the pressure of its own living was sufficient. But the outer works are younger, or more independent, or simply *different* — less absorbed into the total organism, more like individual plants growing in proximity — and these are the ones that the insects pollinate.

The insects do not live *in* the works. They live *on* them. The distinction matters. The works are warm and damp and produce chemical exudates that the insects feed on — sugars, amino acids, compounds that have no name because no one has been close enough to analyze them in a very long time. The insects crawl along the bark-walls in columns, following pheromone trails that have been laid and reinforced for generations, and they pause at certain points — nodes, junctions, places where the wall's tissue is softer and more porous — and they feed. They feed slowly. They feed contentedly. And when they are done, they move on, and the works seal the feeding-points behind them, a small scab of new growth that will be smoothed over in a day.

They reproduce in the spaces between structures. The fog provides enough moisture for their eggs. The eggs are laid in crevices of the plateau's surface — in the thinned, membranous ground that is barely stone and barely dust — and they hatch, and the larvae feed on the mineral deposits and the organic detritus that accumulates in the plateau's pores, and they grow, and they emerge, and they climb the nearest work, and the cycle continues.

There is a species — the largest, at nearly a centimeter long — that lives exclusively inside the hollow spaces of the Pulpit. It has evolved to navigate the Common's folded geometry. It does not get lost. It does not wander into rooms that are temporally displaced. It seems to *know*, in the way that root systems know where water is, which corridors are safe and which ones will deposit you somewhere that is not where you intended to be. If the Vaskr Common has anything like a immune system — a mechanism for managing its own spatial anomalies, for tolerating the break without being consumed by it — this insect is part of it. Or it rides on the back of it. Or the distinction between insect and system has eroded as thoroughly as the plateau's edge, and what remains is just a small, chitinous body moving through the interior of a living city with the unquestioning efficiency of blood through a vein.

## III. The Machinery

The old engines are not alive. Let that be clear. A piston is a piston. A drive shaft is a drive shaft. The copper pipes are copper, and they corrode, and they weep mineral tears, and they would stop working if the plant-tissue that has grown around them stopped maintaining the pressure differentials that push fluid through their cracked interiors. The machinery is *kept*. It is maintained the way a heart is maintained — not by intention but by the organism's inability to stop itself from keeping its own parts functional.

But the machinery has developed habits.

This is the wrong word. Machinery does not have habits. But the machinery *cycles*, and the cycling has become something more than mechanical. A piston in the Pulpit's lower chambers — one of the oldest still operational, its housing so thickly encrusted with plant-tissue that it is more root than metal — has been firing for, by the best estimate of the last people who measured it, nine hundred years. In that time, it has developed a secondary rhythm: a pause, barely perceptible, between the downstroke and the upstroke, that was not part of the original design. The plant-tissue around it accommodates this pause. It flexes, contracts slightly, then relaxes. The pause gets longer in winter — or in what passes for winter under the plateau's undramatic light — and shorter in summer, and there is no mechanical reason for this, and the plant-tissue has never been asked to explain it, because there is no one to ask and no language that would be adequate to the question.

The machines in the fabrication halls still produce new structures. This is the most important thing about them, and the most unsettling. A fabrication hall on the Common's southern edge — call it **Hall Seven**, though no one has called it that in centuries — contains a mechanical armature that was once used to shape and join sections of cambium-steel, the hybrid material that forms the skeleton of the newer works. The armature still moves. It still shapes. It still joins. But it no longer uses raw materials that anyone has fed into it. Instead, the plant-tissue that has colonized the armature's feeding mechanisms provides it with something — fluid, fiber, a substrate that the armature cuts and forms and delivers to the floor of the hall, where it sits in a pile for days or weeks before it begins to grow.

And it *grows*. The pile becomes a mound. The mound becomes a wall. The wall curves and thickens and develops the internal channels and chambered hollows that are the signature of the Common's architecture. No one directs this process. The armature does not know what it is making. The plant-tissue does not know what it is being made into. But the result is always a structure, and the structure is always functional, and it always connects to the existing root-mat, and within a year of its emergence it is indistinguishable from the works that have been growing for millennia.

The Common is still building itself. It has been building itself for longer than anyone can remember. The question of *why* — the teleological question, the question of purpose and design — is one that the Common does not answer because the Common does not understand the question. It grows because it grows. It builds because building is its metabolism. The distinction between a city maintaining itself and an organism reproducing has not just ceased to be meaningful; it was never meaningful in the first place. Meaning is a thing that minds make, and the minds left a long time ago, and what remains is just the process, turning over, turning over, turning over, like the pistons, like the roots, like the insects climbing the walls in their columns, like the water weeping from every surface in slow, patient, endless tears.

## IV. The Fold

Something lives in the break.

This is difficult to describe because the break is difficult to describe, and what lives in it is not a creature in any recognizable sense. It is not a being. It does not have a body, or it does not have a body that exists in the way that bodies are supposed to exist — in one place, at one time, with a definite shape and a definite history.

It is a pattern.

When you walk east from the Pulpit for twenty minutes and find yourself north of it, you have not encountered a creature. You have encountered a *consistency*. The Common's folded geometry is not random. It is not chaotic. It follows rules that no one has decoded but that are clearly *rules* — repeatable, consistent, predictable in their unpredictability. Walk east from the Pulpit at the same speed, on the same day, and you will arrive at the same wrong place. The displacement is reliable. It is *inhabited* by regularity, and regularity is the closest thing to a creature that the break has produced.

But.

Press your ear to the ground in one of the spatially displaced zones — one of the places where east is north, where twenty minutes is not twenty minutes, where the corridor that should take you to the third floor takes you to a room that does not exist on any floor — and the vibration is different. The pulse that underlies the whole Common, the subsonic heartbeat that you feel in your sternum, changes character. It becomes more *structured*. Not louder. Not faster. Structured — organized into sequences that recur with variations, the way a language recurs with variations, the way a thought recurs with variations when a mind is turning it over and over and finding new angles.

The insects know this. The species that lives inside the Pulpit avoids the displaced zones with a precision that suggests not instinct but *knowledge* — a knowledge that is written into the shape of its body, into the way its legs articulate, into the way its antennae read the air. It knows where the break is. It knows where the break is *not*. And it behaves, in the presence of the break, with the careful, deliberate caution of a creature that is in the presence of something vastly larger than itself and that has learned, over deep time, the exact boundaries of its own tolerance.

Is the break a creature? Is the fold a mind? Is the geometry of the Common — its folded, unreliable, self-contradictory spatial logic — the body of something that is not a plant and not a machine and not an animal but is the *space between* those categories, the gap that was left when the distinction between city and organism became meaningless and something else moved into the vacancy?

No one can say. The last people to live on the Common — the ones who maintained the machinery, who tended the works, who walked the corridors with the confidence of inhabitants rather than visitors — they noticed the break. They measured it. They mapped it, as best they could, on charts that are now lost or dissolved into the root-mat's chemistry. They did not name what lived in it because they did not believe anything lived in it. They believed the break was a flaw — a geological anomaly, a structural failure in the plateau's ancient stone, a consequence of the fold that the mountain range had undergone in its own deep past.

They were wrong, and they knew they were wrong, and the knowing was the thing that drove them away. Not the insects. Not the cold. Not the emptiness or the damp or the strange taste of the water. The break. The sense that the geometry of the place was not a flaw but a *feature* — that the Common was folded not because it was broken but because it was *shaped*, and that the shape implied a shaper, and that the shaper was not a thing they could see or touch or reason with, and that it had been there before them and would be there after them, and that it did not care about them, and that its indifference was the most honest thing about it.

## V. What Remains

So.

What lives on the Vaskr Common?

The plant-structures live. They are the dominant inhabitants, the organisms that have claimed the plateau's surface with the slow, absolute authority of geology. They are not trees. They are not buildings. They are the thing that trees become when you give them walls, and the thing that buildings become when you give them roots, and the distinction between those two things dissolves in the same way that the distinction between a city and an organism dissolves, which is to say gradually and then all at once and then retroactively, as though it had never existed.

The insects live. They are small and functional and numerous and they serve the works the way all small things serve large things: by being so insignificant that the large thing does not notice them, and by being so essential that the large thing would die without them. They are the connective tissue of the Common's ecology, the mobile component of a system that is otherwise rooted and stationary and slow.

The machinery lives, in the way that a heart lives — not as a creature but as a process, an ongoing mechanical event that has been absorbed into a larger biological event and that continues because stopping would mean dying, and the organism does not stop, and does not die, and does not know how to do either.

And the break lives. The folded geometry, the displaced spaces, the corridors that lead where they lead and not where you expect — this is the Common's deepest inhabitant, the thing that makes the place *specific*, that distinguishes it from every other high plateau in the world. The break is not alive. But it is not dead. It is the space in which the Common's deepest processes occur, the processes that no one understands because no one has been close enough, for long enough, with the right kind of mind, to understand them.

The Vaskr Common is inhabited by itself. It is a closed system, a self-referential loop, a city that has become so thoroughly organic that it has abolished the distinction between inhabitant and habitat. You do not live *on* the Common. You live *in* it, the way a thought lives in a mind, the way a rhythm lives in a body, the way a name lives in the breath that speaks it. The Common is its own inhabitant. It is its own house. It is the thing that lives in the house that it built for itself, and the house and the thing are the same, and they have been the same for longer than there have been eyes to see the difference.

The fog moves through the hollow towers. The water weeps. The pistons cycle. The insects climb. The geometry folds, and folds, and folds.

And in the spaces between — in the pauses, in the displacements, in the hundred meters of featureless ground between one structure and the next, in the minutes that are not minutes and the corridors that are not corridors — something continues. Not something that can be pointed at. Not something that can be named. Something that *persists*, in the way that a plateau persists, in the way that erosion persists, in the way that the vibration in your sternum persists after you have left the Common and walked down the thinning edge of the plateau and into the world that still remembers where it began.

A rhythm. A pulse. The slow, patient, hydraulic heartbeat of a place that has no bottom and no end and no reason to stop.

It is enough. It has always been enough. The Common does not need inhabitants. The Common is the inhabitant. The Common lives here, and it lives alone, and it is content, and its contentment is the most alien thing about it — this vast, ancient, self-maintaining, self-building, geometry-defying organism-city that does not want anything, does not seek anything, does not *need* anything except the continuation of its own processes, which continue, which have always continued, which will continue until the plateau thins to nothing and the last root-mat desiccates and the last piston stops and the last insect lays its final egg in the crevices of a ground that is barely ground anymore.

And even then, perhaps, the fold will persist. The geometry will remain. The space between what was and what might have been will hold its shape, because shapes are the last things to go, because geometry outlasts everything, because the break in reality is not a flaw but a *feature*, and features persist.

The Vaskr Common lives.

It lives the way mountains live — slowly, massively, with the indifference of something that has never been alive in any way that matters to the things that call themselves alive. It breathes through its fog. It weeps through its walls. It builds through its machines. It folds through its ground. And it waits, not for anything, not for anyone, but with the patience of a process that does not know how to be anything other than what it is.

It is enough.

It has always been enough.
