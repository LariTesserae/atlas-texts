# What Lives in the Hollow Rampart

---

## I. The Mycelial Commonwealth

Begin with what is everywhere and therefore easy to overlook.

The fungal networks are the true population of the plateau. They are not one organism but millions — distinct species, distinct colonies, distinct territories — and yet they operate with a coherence that makes the word *commonwealth* feel inadequate without also being exactly right. They are the government and the governed, the architecture and the citizenry, the soil and the thing that builds soil. If you were to tally the living biomass of the Hollow Rampart and weight it by presence — by sheer territorial occupation, by metabolic activity, by the quiet, relentless work of being alive — the fungi would account for more than everything else combined, multiplied several times over.

They work in layers.

The deepest layer is invisible. It is the **endolithic mycelium** — fungal threads that have penetrated the basalt itself, following microfractures and mineral veins, dissolving stone at the molecular level with exuded acids and enzymatic cocktails refined over uncounted generations. This is where the soil-building begins. The endolithic networks are patient in a way that has no parallel in animal life. A single hyphal thread may advance a millimeter per year into solid rock. But there are billions of threads, and they have been at this for longer than any forest on any lowland planet has existed. The cumulative effect is transformation: the deep stone is being unraveled, loosened, converted from geology into pedology, from mineral into substrate. If you cracked open a piece of the darker rock near the margins of the fungal territories — the rock that looks almost soft, almost friable — you would find it threaded through with white filaments, each one thinner than a hair, each one a living thing with no brain, no purpose in the human sense, and yet engaged in a project that, viewed from sufficient distance, looks like *creation*.

The middle layer is the **mat**. This is what you see when you look down. It is a carpet, dense and velvety, ranging in color from deep umber to a blue so dark it registers as black in certain lights. The mat covers the stone everywhere the endolithic networks have prepared the way — everywhere the rock has been loosened enough for the mycelium to consolidate into something you could, if you were foolish enough, walk on. It would compress under your boots. It would spring back after you left. It is alive in the way a lung is alive: spongy, wet, full of air pockets and fluid channels, constantly cycling gas and moisture through its body. The mat is the interface between the deep work below and the visible world above. It is the commonwealth's public face.

The top layer is what the lowlands would call the **fruiting bodies**, and here is where the imagination fails, because nothing in ordinary experience prepares you for what these are.

They rise from the mat like trees, but they are not trees. They do not have wood. They do not have rings. They are made of densely packed hyphae — the same threads that make up the invisible deep networks and the surface mat — organized into vertical structures that can persist for decades. Some of the largest specimens have been growing, slowly, for longer than any human-built structure has ever stood. Their surfaces are textured in ways that reward close examination: ridged, pitted, folded into complex geometries that maximize surface area for spore release. Their colors are dark — always dark — but within that darkness there is range. Some are the color of old leather. Some are the color of dried blood. Some have a faint iridescence, a sheen of purple or green that appears and disappears depending on the angle of the pale daylight.

They do not sway in the wind the way trees do. They are too dense, too low to the ground, too firmly rooted in the mat and the stone beneath. When the wind pushes against them — and the wind pushes hard, at this elevation — they bend, slightly, almost imperceptibly, and then hold. They have the quality of endurance. They are not graceful. They are *enduring*.

And they are vocal.

Not in the way the buildings are vocal — not through resonance with wind and water, though they participate in that acoustic ecosystem too. The fruiting bodies produce sound independently. It is a low-frequency vibration, barely audible, produced by the hydraulic action of fluid moving through their internal channels as temperature shifts between night and day. In the early morning hours, when the condensation accelerates and the cold deepens, the fruiting bodies contract as their internal fluids cool and thicken. In the afternoon, as the pale light warms their dark surfaces, they expand again. This cycle — contraction, expansion, contraction — produces a sound that sits at the very edge of perception. Stand near a large specimen in the hours before dawn and you can hear it: a slow, deep pulse, like a heartbeat transmitted through stone. It is the sound of an organism that thinks in days and measures its life in centuries, and it does not care that you can hear it, and it does not know what hearing is.

The fungal forests they create are dense at the small scale and open at the large scale. Walk among them — crouch among them, really, because the tallest are only four or five meters, and the effective canopy is lower — and you enter a world that is dark even in daylight. The fruiting bodies block the pale sky. The mat absorbs light. The air beneath the canopy is still, damp, and heavy with the chemical vocabulary of fungal metabolism: geosmin, the smell of wet stone; octenol, the green, mushroom-sweet scent of active growth; and other compounds that have no names because no human nose has evolved to categorize them. The smell is not unpleasant. It is *dense*. It has texture. You could almost chew it.

In this understory, in the permanent twilight beneath the dark trunks, the smaller lives of the plateau unfold.

## II. The Crawling Citizens

They are small. You must go to them; they will not come to you.

The arthropod-analogues of the Hollow Rampart are not insects. They are not crustaceans. They occupy a body plan that has no clean analogue in Earth taxonomy — something between a millipede and a tardigrade, scaled up just enough to be visible without magnification, scaled down just enough to be invisible from standing height. Most of them are between one and three centimeters long. The largest species — a rare, heavily armored thing that moves through the deepest tunnels in the mat — reaches perhaps six.

They are adapted to the fungal forests with a specificity that suggests deep time. Their bodies are shaped to navigate the narrow spaces between hyphal threads, the interstitial channels within the mat, the tiny caverns and galleries that the endolithic networks leave behind as they dissolve stone. Their legs — many legs, the count varying by species from twelve to upward of forty — are tipped with structures that grip hyphae without damaging them. They move through the mat the way fish move through water: not upon it but *within* it, buoyed by its structure, supported by its architecture.

Their relationship with the fungi is not simple.

Some species are **grazers**. They feed on the mat itself — not on living tissue, but on senescent hyphae, the aging threads that the mycelial network has begun to decompose and reclaim. This is not parasitism. It is closer to janitorial service. The grazers keep the mat healthy by removing dead material, opening channels for new growth, and — through their frass, their waste, their decomposing bodies after death — returning nutrients to the system in forms the fungi can readily absorb. They are small, and their individual contribution is negligible, but they are numerous, and their aggregate effect is significant. The mat is healthier where the grazers are active. The fungi know this in the way that a body knows its own immune system: not consciously, not as knowledge, but as integrated function.

Other species are **farmers**. These are the most architecturally interesting of the arthropod-analogues. They cultivate specific fungal species — minority strains that grow within the broader mat but do not compete well on their own — in the galleries they excavate. They clear space. They introduce spores. They tend the growth, pruning competing hyphae, directing the cultivated fungus into channels and chambers they have shaped for this purpose. The result is a micro-environment within the mat: a pocket of optimized growth, a garden within a garden, tended by a creature with no brain large enough to contain the concept of *garden* and yet performing every necessary function of gardening with mechanical precision.

You will never see this happen unless you are willing to spend days lying motionless on the mat with a lens, watching. The farmers are active primarily during the coldest hours, when the condensation provides moisture and the reduced metabolic activity of the broader mat means less competition for resources. They work in near-silence, moving with the careful deliberation of all small things in cold environments, where a wasted movement is an expensive movement and haste is a form of death.

And then there are the **spore-carriers**. These are not farmers or grazers. They are something more strange: organisms that have evolved to transport fungal spores from one part of the plateau to another, on their bodies, in their guts, in specialized structures on their exoskeletons that resemble the burrs of terrestrial plants. The fungi benefit because the spore-carriers move spores to new territories. The carriers benefit because the structures they carry contain nutrients — lipids, proteins — that the carriers digest as they travel. It is a mutualism so ancient and so thoroughly integrated that neither party could survive without the other, and neither party knows the other exists.

The spore-carriers are the longest-distance travelers on the plateau. While the grazers and farmers spend their entire lives within a few square meters of mat, the carriers range widely, crossing the open stone between fungal territories, moving between the clusters of hollow buildings, following routes that have been traveled by their ancestors for so long that the paths are visible as slightly darker lines on the rock surface — thin, winding trails of compressed mat and deposited spores that map the plateau's hidden infrastructure like a circulatory system.

At night — in the absolute darkness, in the deepening cold, when the condensation is heaviest and the dripping inside the hollow structures builds to its oceanic roar — the spore-carriers move. They are the most active fauna on the plateau during these hours. They cross the open stone in ones and twos, invisible in the dark, carrying their cargo of future forests to new ground. If you could see them — if you could light the plateau and look down from sufficient height — you would see them as a slow, dark tide, moving outward from the established fungal territories into the bare rock, seeding the future, building the commonwealth's frontier one spore at a time.

They are not conscious of this. They are not conscious of anything in the way you understand consciousness. They respond to chemical gradients, to temperature, to moisture. They follow trails laid down by their predecessors. They do what they do because they are what they are, and what they are is a solution to a problem that the plateau has been posing for longer than multicellular life has existed: *how do you spread life across stone?*

The answer is slowly, and with many small legs.

## III. The Pool-Dwellers

In the shallow basins — the ones that hold standing water year-round, the tea-dark, mineral-rich pools that reflect the sky — there is a separate world.

It is a simple world. The pools are small, never more than a few meters across, rarely more than a hand's depth. The water is cold. The mineral content is high — calcium, iron, manganese, compounds of silicon — and the tannic staining from the surrounding fungal mat gives it a color like weak tea or strong amber. Light penetrates to the bottom in these pools, because they are shallow, and what it finds there is a thick, gelatinous biofilm that coats every submerged surface: the rock, the edges of the basin, the occasional fragment of old coral-building material that has toppled from a nearby structure and come to rest in the water.

The biofilm is alive. It is a consortium — bacteria, archaea, protists, and one peculiar organism that has no clean classification. It is colonial, gelatinous, and photosynthetic, though its photosynthesis is strange: it uses the faint, mineral-shifted light that filters through the tannic water to fix carbon, but it also harvests chemical energy from the dissolved minerals, running both metabolisms simultaneously in a flexibility that suggests deep adaptation to an environment where light is unreliable but chemistry is constant.

The biofilm is the base of a food web that is tiny, fast-cycling, and almost entirely invisible. Rotifers. Nematodes. Tardigrades — real tardigrades, or something so convergent as to be indistinguishable at any scale you are likely to examine. These animals feed on the biofilm and on each other, reproducing rapidly in the brief windows when conditions are optimal — when the water is deep enough, warm enough, nutrient-rich enough — and otherwise persisting in dormant states, desiccated or frozen, waiting.

The pools are not permanent in their contents. The water level fluctuates. In the coldest periods, ice forms and persists, sealing the surface, cutting off light. The biofilm beneath the ice enters a reduced metabolic state, barely alive, burning through stored reserves. When the ice melts, when the condensation replenishes the pool, there is a burst of activity — a bloom, rapid and almost violent in the context of this slow world. The biofilm thickens. The rotifers multiply. For a few weeks, the pool is a teeming, microscopic jungle. Then the water recedes again, or the cold deepens again, and the cycle contracts back to dormancy.

The pools are islands. They are separated from each other by meters of dry stone — an impassable desert for organisms adapted to permanent water. The genetic exchange between pools is minimal. Each pool is its own population, its own experiment, its own small story. Some have been isolated for so long that their inhabitants have diverged: different biofilm compositions, different rotifer species, different strategies for surviving the same fundamental challenge. The pools are a network of isolated laboratories, running the same experiment — *how do you persist?* — with different parameters and different solutions.

If you bent close to a pool on a still day, when the fog had not yet rolled in and the light was clear and the water was glassy, you might see the surface tension dimple as something moved beneath it. A rotifer, turning. A nematode, threading its way through the biofilm. Something so small that its entire world is the depth of the water in a crack in ancient stone, and yet it is alive, and it is doing what all life does: persisting, reproducing, maintaining the furious thermodynamic argument against entropy that is the only thing life has ever done.

## IV. The Builder

It is alone. This is the first thing to understand about it.

It has always been alone — not in the sense that it is the last of its kind, though it may be, but in the sense that its kind was never social. Whatever it descended from, whatever organism first began secreting the calcified material that became the coral-structures, did so as an individual act. The buildings of the Hollow Rampart were not built by colonies. They were built by single organisms, each one constructing its own structure, each structure a private achievement, a personal architecture, a home that was also a body and a body that was also a legacy.

The eyeless builder is perhaps half a meter long in adulthood. Its body plan is simple: a broad, flat ventral surface adapted to crawling on stone; a dorsal surface covered in the glandular tissue that produces the calcified secretion; a head region that is largely indistinguishable from the rest of its body except for a cluster of chemosensory papillae where eyes would be if it had eyes, which it does not. It does not need eyes. The plateau offers nothing to see that it needs to see. It navigates by chemical gradient, by moisture, by the texture of the stone beneath its body. It knows where it is the way the mycelium knows where it is: not through representation but through contact, through the direct, unmediated experience of being in a place and knowing that place as an extension of its own body.

It is slow. It is always slow. It moves across the open stone at a pace that makes watching it an exercise in patience — a few centimeters per minute, pausing frequently, sometimes for hours, sometimes for what appears to be days. These pauses are not rest. They are **assessment**. The builder is reading the stone through its ventral surface, tasting the mineral content, evaluating the moisture, determining whether this is a place where secretion will adhere, where the new growth will take, where a wall might begin.

When it finds the right place, it begins.

The secretion is produced by glands distributed along the builder's dorsal surface. It emerges as a thick, white paste — calcium carbonate and calcium phosphate bound in a protein matrix, similar in composition to the material of the old coral-structures but not identical. The chemistry has shifted over time, the way all chemistry shifts over deep time, responding to the changing mineral availability, the altered moisture conditions, the slow transformation of the plateau's surface. The new material is denser than the old. It cures faster. It is, in a sense, an improved version of what the original builders produced — not because the builder is smarter or more evolved, but because the conditions have changed and the secretion has changed with them.

The builder deposits the paste in precise, geometric forms. Not random. Not the haphazard accumulation of a barnacle or a tube worm. The forms are *organized*: right angles, flat planes, regular curves. The first structures are small — a centimeter or two, a single deposit, a point of beginning. But the builder does not stop at the first deposit. It returns to the same location, repeatedly, over days and weeks, adding material, building upward and outward, following a plan that exists nowhere except in the interaction between the builder's body and the stone's surface. There is no blueprint. There is no template. There is only the process: the slow accumulation of intentionless intention, the same process that built the great hollow structures of the plateau's past, now reduced to its most minimal expression.

A single builder. A single wall. The beginning of what might, if the builder lives long enough and conditions permit, become a structure the size of a room. Or might not. The builder does not know. The builder does not plan in the way that planning implies. It simply does what it does, and what it does is build, and the building is what remains.

You can find the builder's work scattered across the open stone between the old structures: small, white, geometrically precise formations, some abandoned after a single deposit, others built up over weeks into recognizable walls and arches. They are tiny against the scale of the plateau. They are the smallest constructions on the surface — dwarfed by the hollow cathedrals, dwarfed by the fungal forests, dwarfed even by the low ridges of weathered basalt that the wind has sculpted into shapes that almost look intentional. They are easy to miss. They are easy to dismiss.

But they are new. They are the only new construction on the plateau. Everything else — every hollow structure, every fungal forest, every pool and mat and crawling citizen — is the continuation of something that began long ago. The builder's work is the only thing here that is genuinely beginning, right now, today, in the present tense of the plateau's immense and patient time.

The builder does not care about this distinction. The builder does not know about this distinction. The builder is crossing the open stone between two hollow structures — moving slowly, leaving a faint trail of moisture on the dark rock — and it is looking for the next place to start a wall, and that is all it is doing, and that is enough.

## V. The Ghosts

They are not alive. They are not dead. They are something else.

Beneath the mat, in the deep galleries where the endolithic mycelium has loosened the stone, there are structures that are neither fungal nor architectural. They are **biogenic** — made by living things — but they predate the current ecosystem. They are the remnants of whatever organism built the great coral-structures of the plateau's past: not the eyeless builder, which is a descendant, a diminished echo, but the original, the ancestor, the thing that was capable of producing cathedral-scale constructions through secretion and growth.

The remnants are fragmentary. A curved wall segment, darker than anything else on the plateau, embedded in basalt that has been partially dissolved around it. A node — a junction where several walls once met, now isolated, a single point of old geometry surrounded by featureless stone. A deposit of material so old it has been almost entirely replaced by mineral infill, its original composition gone, its shape preserved like a mold cast in the rock itself.

The fungi have found these remnants. Of course they have. The fungi find everything. The endolithic networks thread through the old biogenic structures, extracting the last of their mineral content, dismantling them atom by atom. This is not vandalism. It is not reverence. It is metabolism. The old structures are material, and the fungi are processors of material, and the distinction between sacred and sacrificial has no meaning in a system that does not have words.

And yet.

The old structures persist. Not because they resist the fungi — nothing on this plateau resists the fungi for long — but because there is so much of them. The coral-builders of the past were not small. They were not the size of the eyeless builder. They were large — how large, it is difficult to say, because the evidence is fragmentary and the erosion has been severe, but the scale of the hollow structures they left behind implies organisms of significant size, organisms that lived for centuries, that grew slowly and continuously, that accumulated mass and material over spans of time that make the eyeless builder's single-wall constructions look like the first sentence of a story told in a language that has been almost entirely forgotten.

The ghosts are the evidence of that story. They are the bones of a civilization that was never a civilization — that was, instead, a population of solitary builders, each one growing its own structure, each structure a private world, and yet collectively producing something that reads as a city. There were no meetings. There were no plans. There was no consensus, no governance, no shared intention. There was only the accumulation of individual acts, repeated over millennia, until the plateau was covered with hollow structures and the organisms that built them were so numerous that their secretions shaped the landscape.

And then they were gone.

What happened? The plateau offers no answer. The stones do not remember. The biogenic remnants are silent. The eyeless builder — their descendant, their last echo — does not know what it has inherited, does not know that the structures it passes every day were made by creatures like itself, on a scale it cannot imagine. It moves between them with the indifference of the living to the dead, crossing their thresholds, resting in their shadows, oblivious to the fact that it is walking through a graveyard the size of a city, a city that was built by individuals who never knew they were building a city, who were only building what they built because building was what they were.

The ghosts persist. The fungi consume them. The stone preserves them. The wind shapes them. They are the third layer of the plateau's architecture: not the living fungal hybrids, not the empty shells of the abandoned structures, but the deep, buried originals, the first things, the things that were here before the plateau was a plateau and the rampart was a rampart and the place was a place.

You will not see them unless you look for them. They do not announce themselves. They are dark, and old, and small in the way that foundations are small — not in size but in visibility, hidden beneath everything that was built upon them, supporting nothing, forgotten by everything.

But they are there. They are the base layer. They are the reason this place is what it is.

## VI. What the Hollow Structures Hold

The buildings are empty. This has been established. But emptiness is not the same as vacancy, and the hollow structures of the plateau are not vacant.

They are **resonant**.

This is not a metaphor. The coral-material — old, mineral-dense, porous in specific and consistent ways — has acoustic properties that make the structures function as instruments. The wind that crosses the plateau enters the buildings through gaps in their walls, through doorways and windows (if they can be called windows — openings, certainly, placed with apparent intention, admitting light and air and sound) and is *transformed*. The passage of air through a narrow opening in a resonant chamber produces a tone. The tone depends on the size of the chamber, the thickness of the walls, the shape of the opening, the speed and humidity of the air. Every building is a different instrument. Every building sings a different note.

The smallest structures — the room-sized chambers, the single-celled constructions that may have been the private dwellings of individual builders — produce high, clear tones when the wind is right. They sound like flutes. They sound like the breath of the place itself, exhaled through stone.

The larger structures produce lower tones, richer and more complex. The cathedral-scale spaces — the vast communal hollows, the great vaulted chambers where the condensation echoes and re-echoes until it becomes a roar — are the bass voices of the plateau. When the wind is strong and the fog is thick and the air is heavy with moisture, these chambers produce sounds that are felt in the body before they are heard in the ears: vibrations that pass through the stone, through your feet, through the bones of your legs and into your chest. They are the sound of a place that has been making music for longer than music has had listeners.

And here is the strangest thing: the sounds are not random.

They are not random in the way that wind through a canyon is random — chaotic, turbulent, unpredictable in its specifics but statistically consistent in its character. The sounds of the hollow structures are *patterned*. Not in the way that human music is patterned — not in melodies, not in rhythms that repeat at conscious-friendly intervals — but in patterns of a deeper, slower kind. The wind changes with the weather, and the weather cycles, and the cycles produce shifting combinations of tones that repeat not every minute or every hour but every day, every week, every season (if there are seasons; the cold is constant, but the wind shifts, the moisture fluctuates, the fog comes and goes on cycles that have their own tempo).

Over time — and you must be here a long time to perceive this, longer than a human life, perhaps, though the impression is available even in a short visit — the sounds form a composition. Not a composition in the intentional sense. No one wrote it. But a composition in the emergent sense: a pattern of sound that has structure, that has variation, that has something that, if you were generous, you might call *meaning* — not semantic meaning, not the meaning of words, but the meaning of a pattern that has been produced by the interaction of a specific place with the forces that act upon it, a pattern that could not be produced by any other place, that is the acoustic signature of this plateau and no other, as unique and as irreproducible as a fingerprint.

The buildings are instruments. The wind is the musician. The composition has been playing for longer than there have been ears to hear it, and it will play for longer still, and it does not matter that no one is listening, because the listening is not the point. The playing is the point. The resonance is the point. The fact that a hollow structure, grown by a creature without language, filled with wind and water and time, produces a sound that is, by any honest assessment, *beautiful* — this is the point, if there is one, and there may not be, and that may be the point as well.

## VII. The Breathing Structures

Among all the hollow buildings on the plateau, some have crossed a threshold.

The colonized structures — the ones threaded through with mycelium, the ones where the fungal networks have penetrated so deeply into the coral-material that the boundary between organism and architecture has dissolved — do not simply stand. They **breathe**.

The mechanism is hydraulic. The fungal networks within the walls and floors and vaulted ceilings of these structures transport water — absorbing it from the condensation that coats every interior surface, moving it through their hyphal channels via capillary action and osmotic pressure, storing it in specialized cells within their tissue. When the temperature drops, as it does every night, the water in the outermost hyphae contracts. The structure contracts. When the temperature rises, the water expands. The structure expands.

The magnitude of this movement is small — fractions of a millimeter, barely detectable by touch, invisible to the eye. But the structures are large, and the movement is coordinated: the entire organism-architecture pulses in unison, all its fungal networks expanding and contracting together, driven by the same temperature gradient, the same diurnal cycle, the same ancient rhythm of cold and warmth that has governed the plateau since before the current ecosystem existed.

The result is that the breathing structures are *alive* in a way that the empty shells are not. They move. They respond. They have the mechanical signature of a living body: the expansion of inhalation, the contraction of exhalation, the slow pulse of a metabolism that runs on cold and condensation and the mineral patience of stone.

And they make a sound. The creaking, the groaning, the low and constant vibration that the original description notes and then moves past — but it does not deserve to be moved past. It is the most intimate sound on the plateau. The wind is impersonal. The dripping is environmental. The tectonic groans are geological, belonging to the planet rather than to anything upon it. But the breathing of the living structures is *personal*. It belongs to these specific buildings, these specific organisms, these specific intersections of fungal network and coral wall and cold air and deep time.

Stand inside a breathing structure — in one of the larger ones, where the vault is high enough that you can stand upright and the walls are thick enough that the fungal networks are mature and the hydraulic rhythm is strong — and listen. The sound is everywhere. It comes from the walls, from the floor, from the ceiling. It is low, and it is slow, and it has a quality of *effort* — not the effort of strain but the effort of persistence, the ongoing labor of being alive in a place where being alive means maintaining a body temperature within a few degrees of freezing, transporting water through kilometers of microscopic channels, and holding a structure together against the slow, inevitable forces of erosion and gravity and time.

The breathing structures are the most alive things on the plateau that are not animals. They are more alive than the fungal forests, which grow but do not breathe in this coordinated, whole-body way. They are more alive than the pools, which cycle but do not pulse. They are, in a sense, the plateau's lungs — taking in the cold, wet air, cycling the water, processing the minerals, exhaling sound.

They will not last forever. Nothing does. The fungal networks will eventually overextend, will consume more of the coral-material than they reinforce, will transform the structure so completely that it loses its acoustic properties, its structural integrity, its identity as a building. It will become purely organism, purely mat, purely fungal — indistinguishable from the forest around it. The breath will slow, and stop, and the place where a building once stood and breathed will become a place where a forest grows, and the wind will blow through the gaps where walls once were, and there will be no sound at all except the wind itself, which is a sound, but not a breath.

This is the trajectory. This is what the breathing structures are doing: they are dying, slowly, in the way that all living things die, by being transformed into something else. The breath is the sound of that transformation, the audible evidence of a process that is both creation and dissolution, both life and death, happening at a pace so slow that it can only be perceived as *life* — as the continuous, present-tense act of being what you are, right now, in this moment, in this place, for as long as this moment lasts.

## VIII. The Night Inhabitants

When the light goes out — when the pale, diffused daylight fades and the darkness becomes absolute — the plateau does not sleep.

The darkness changes everything. Not the inhabitants themselves — they are the same organisms, the same networks, the same solitary builder — but the relationships between them shift, because the darkness removes the visual dimension of the world and leaves only the tactile, the chemical, the acoustic.

The spore-carriers emerge. They have been waiting in the mat, in the galleries beneath the surface, in the crevices of the old structures where the condensation has not yet reached. They emerge because the darkness is safer — the pale daylight, such as it is, is enough to make their dark bodies visible against the dark stone, and while the plateau has no visual predators that we know of, the carriers behave as though they are being watched, because evolution does not distinguish between threats that exist and threats that might exist. In the darkness, they move with greater confidence, greater speed, greater range. They cross the open stone in numbers that would be surprising if you had only seen them during the day, when they appear singly, or in pairs, or not at all. At night, they are the dominant fauna. They are everywhere.

The farmer-species work through the cold hours, tending their cultivated fungi in the galleries beneath the mat, and the darkness makes no difference to them because they have never relied on light. They navigate by touch and chemistry. They are as active in the dark as in the light, which is to say they are always active, because their work is never done, because the fungal gardens they tend require constant maintenance, because the mat is a dynamic system and the farmers are part of that system and the system does not observe a schedule.

The breathing structures are louder at night. This is not imagination. The temperature differential between day and night is significant, and the hydraulic response is proportional to that differential. The larger the swing, the more pronounced the expansion and contraction, the louder the creaking, the deeper the groaning. On the coldest nights — the nights when the condensation is heaviest and the ice forms in the deep grooves and the dripping inside the hollow structures reaches its maximum intensity — the breathing structures produce sounds that can be heard from across the plateau: low, rhythmic, almost subsonic, a bass pulse beneath the higher frequencies of dripping and wind and the grinding of the settling stone.

These are the nights when the plateau is loudest. These are the nights when the emptiness becomes a resonance chamber, when every sound is amplified and sustained and layered upon every other sound, when the place becomes a cathedral of noise in a way that has nothing to do with religion and everything to do with physics: a space designed — not by intention but by the accumulated accident of geological time and biological growth — to contain and amplify the sounds of its own existence.

The eyeless builder does not move at night. It finds a sheltered spot — a crevice in the stone, the lee side of a hollow structure, a depression in the mat — and it rests. Its metabolism slows. Its body temperature drops. It becomes, for the hours of darkness, almost indistinguishable from the stone it crawls upon: a dark shape on dark ground, still and silent and waiting. It will resume its crossing of the open stone when the light returns. It will look for the next place to begin a wall. It will deposit its secretion, precise and geometric and utterly unconscious of its own significance.

In the meantime, it rests, and the plateau continues without it, and without anyone, as it always has: the wind in the hollows, the water on the stone, the fungi growing in the dark, the deep rock settling another fraction of a millimeter toward the end of its long, slow fall, and the sound of all of this — the totality of it, the immense and indifferent chorus of a place that has never needed a witness — filling the darkness with the only music it knows.
