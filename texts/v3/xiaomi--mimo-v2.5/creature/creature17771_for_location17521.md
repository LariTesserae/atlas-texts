In the Silt District, life does not so much inhabit the structures as become layered into them, like new sediment over old rock. The inhabitants are of two kinds: the **Remaining**, and the **Adapted**.

---

## The Remaining

These are the people. They are a small population, perhaps no more than four thousand across the entire district, and they are *industrial* to the marrow. They are not survivors of a disaster; they are the descendants of those who stayed when the economic tide receded. They have the ruddy, damp skin of people who work in humid, heated environments. Their hands are often stained—the permanent under-nails black from carbon, the palms lined with the fine, grey patina of silt that no soap entirely removes.

They are **The Shift-Folk**. Their lives are dictated not by the dim sky, but by the hum of the machines. The factory sirens are their clocks. They move in quiet shifts, along well-worn paths through the puddled streets, their footfalls making the only human sounds for blocks at a time. They speak sparingly, and with a low, gruff economy of words that matches the persistent drone of the pumps. A nod, a gesture toward a leaky pipe, the passing of a tool—communication is functional.

Their housing is **Lairage**: the closed worker blocks they have reclaimed. They cluster in a few inhabited zones, leaving the rest of the district to the quiet. Inside, their spaces are a study in damp-proofing and pragmatic comfort. Walls are layered with whatever insulates—old factory felt, sheets of plastic, salvaged foam. Every horizontal surface has a cloth or a tray to catch the perpetual drip. The air inside smells of fried fish, coal dust, and the faint, coppery tang of the district itself. Personal belongings are few and heavy. There are no carpets. The floors are bare concrete or stone, often with a shallow, standing puddle in one corner that is simply mopped out daily. Warmth comes from small, efficient coal stoves that also serve to dry the air, their pipes venting into the ancient chimneys. The people here have a particular way of walking—a slow, deliberate gait that creates minimal splash, a balance learned in childhood.

They are **The Pump-Tenders**, the **Kiln-Keepers**, the **Press-Workers**, the **Rail-Binders**. Their pride is not in creation, but in **Continuance**. To keep the kiln at temperature against the damp. To keep the rail lines clear of silt. To keep the ancient pumps from seizing. Their hands know the feel of a properly oiled bearing by sound and vibration alone. They eat in communal kitchens where the food is heavy, hearty, and salty—stews, hard bread, pickled things to cut the mineral taste of the water. They drink tea brewed from mineral-heavy water, which leaves a film in the cup and a specific, chalky taste they call *plain-tea*. They are not unhappy. Their contentment is a deep, quiet thing, like the heat stored in the stone walls. It is the satisfaction of a job understood and a place known.

Children are few, but they are **Silt-Rats** of a human kind. They play not in green fields but in the vast, open yards between factories, in the cathedral-like spaces of empty warehouses, and along the edges of the permanent canals. They learn to read the water: where it’s deep, where it’s stagnant, where the crabs hide. They become experts in the district’s unofficial geography—**The Shimmer-Road** (a path across a flooded floor where the light hits just right), **Beetle Alley** (where the carapaced decomposers travel in steady streams), **The Rust-Wall** (a favorite for scaling, its iron studs worn smooth). They wear boots that never quite dry and have a fearless, quiet way with the district’s animal life.

---

## The Adapted

The humans are merely the latest layer. The true natives, the ones who have truly made the silt their own, are the creatures already named, and a few more besides.

The **pigeons** are not just birds; they are the district’s unceasing sentinels and its most visible residents. The Shift-Folk know them not as a species, but as individuals with roosting preferences. *“Old Smudge is nesting in the west pipe-bank again, near the leak.”* The birds are fed, reluctantly, from kitchen scraps, not out of affection but as a kind of ecological treaty. Their cooing is the lullaby of the place.

The **rats** are the **Invisible Citizens**. They have their own infrastructure. Their runs are worn smoother than any human path. The Shift-Folk have a complex, unspoken truce with them. In the worker lairages, stores are kept in heavy metal bins. The rats are allowed the abandoned quarters and the organic waste. There are rules: *Do not corner a rat in the open.* They are too large, too desperate. It is seen as a mutual breach of treaty. The sound of their movement is a known, accepted part of the night.

The **feral cats** are the **Grey Wardens**. They are a population kept in check by the rats, and in check *by* the rats—a perfect, brutal balance. The Shift-Folk recognize certain lineages by ear-tip notches or tail kinks. They are not pets; they are partners in the district’s sanitation. A factory foreman might leave a specific cut of meat on a specific loading dock at dusk—a ritual as regular as a shift change.

The most fascinating of the adapted are not the creatures themselves, but the **intersections**. It is the **lizard** that watches from a wall while a **Shift-Folk woman** hangs her damp clothes on a line strung between two factory vents, the reptile’s pale skin almost invisible against the mineral-stained brick. It is the **crab** that clicks in the flooded sub-level where a **pump-tender** wades knee-deep to clear a grate, both their movements part of the same wet, rhythmic maintenance. It is the **beetle** that crawls across the open, calloused palm of a **Kiln-Keeper** as he eats his lunch on a crate, both of them pausing, acknowledging, and moving on.

These people and animals do not see themselves as living in a strange or hostile environment. This is **home**. The damp is not an affliction; it is the texture of their days. The animals are not vermin; they are neighbors with whom they share a complex, non-verbal contract. The silt that buries their doorsteps is not dirt; it is the district’s own flesh, accumulating as slowly as memory.

They have their own lore, spoken in low voices during shift breaks. They speak of **The Great Dry**, a time before their grandparents, when the water table was said to be deeper and the stone was lighter in color. They tell tales of **The Sunken Floors**, of whole factory levels now permanently lakebed, home to vast crab colonies. They have songs that are more chants than melodies, with rhythms that match the clatter of the press or the pulse of the pump. These are the songs that the children hum as they skip stones across the glassy surface of a flooded courtyard.

In the Silt District, life is not a struggle against the environment. It is a **deep, mutual absorption**. The people wear the damp and the dust like a second skin. The animals claim the spaces between the human purpose. And the whole, teeming, quiet world continues its slow, wet metamorphosis, a landscape where the line between geology and biology, between the built and the grown, between the human and the animal, has long since blurred into a single, glistening, silt-rich whole. To be alive here is to be a part of that mixture, to know your place in the soft, grey hierarchy, and to find a profound, unshakable peace in the sound of the water and the click of the beetle and the endless, soft cooing of the pigeons in the perpetual dim.
