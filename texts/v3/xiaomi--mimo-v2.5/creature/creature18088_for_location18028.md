# What Lives in the Sump Below

---

There are insects, if you can call them that.

They are the size of a thumbnail, or half that, or a quarter — they vary, but not much. Their bodies are segmented in the way of ancient things, things that settled on a design before the world above decided to keep experimenting. Chitin, yes, but not the brittle chitin of beetles or the papery chitin of cicadas. Their exoskeletons have a wet-look density, like shell that has never fully hardened, as though they exist in a permanent state of almost- finished becoming. They are dark. Not black — the dark has colors here, as it does in deep ocean, as it does in places where eyes are a wasted investment — and theirs is the dark of wetite, the dark of a surface that catches nothing because there is nothing to catch.

They have no eyes.

This is not a deficiency. It is a clarity. Their heads — if you can distinguish a head from the rest of the seamless, hunched body — are smooth, rounded, turned outward not to the visible spectrum but to vibration, to pressure, to the chemical whisper of the walls. They taste the architecture. Every surface they cross leaves a film on their forelimbs — six of them, sometimes eight, the number is not consistent even within what appears to be a single species, and whether this means there are several species or one species in active flux is a question that would take a taxonomy that does not yet exist to answer. They read the walls the way a blind person reads Braille, except the information is not encoded for them. They have learned — over millennia, over however long this place has been running — to extract meaning from noise. The ridged reliefs on the interior of the structures: the blind things follow them, tracing the patterns with antenna-like appendages that fork at the tip into feathery filaments. They are not reading. They are *grazing*. The information carved into those walls has, over time, acquired a mineral crust — microscopic deposits of calcium, of iron compounds, of things the moisture leaves behind — and the insects eat this crust. The meaning is incidental. The nutrition is not.

They live in the walls.

Not on them. *In* them. The warm material that the place is made of is porous in ways that are not visible to eyes that require light — micro-channels, capillary networks, hollows the width of a human hair that widen, here and there, into chambers the size of a fist. The creatures move through these spaces with a fluidity that suggests they were always meant to. The walls yield to them. Not much — a millimeter, perhaps two — but enough. The material that considers whether to let *you* through lets *them* through without hesitation. They are part of the system. They are one of the processes the hum maintains. You are not.

There are thousands of them. Tens of thousands. You will never see more than three or four at a time, because they are solitary in the way of things that live in abundance — no need to cluster, no need to aggregate, food is everywhere and space is everything. But they are *there*, in the walls, in the ceiling-that-isn't-a-ceiling, in the ground-that-isn't-a-ground, distributed through the structure like blood cells in a body, like nodes in a network, like—

Like data.

This is the thought that will come to you, standing in the dark, listening to one of them strike a crystal growth with whatever limb or mouthpart produces that sharp, bright sound. The thought will come and you will try to dismiss it because it sounds like science fiction, like the kind of metaphor that people reach for when they don't understand something and want to feel like they do. But the thought will not leave, because the evidence is right there, if you can feel it, which you can, because your hands are on the wall and the wall is warm and the wall is *humming* and somewhere inside the wall, something small and blind is moving in a pattern that is—

Too regular. Too repeatable. Too *deliberate* to be foraging.

What if they are not foraging? What if the mineral crust they eat is not food but *fuel*? What if the crystal-strikes are not stridulation but *signaling*? What if the creatures are not fauna at all — not in the way we use the word, not animals in the sense of autonomous beings living independent lives in a place that happens to suit them — but *components*? Living processors. Biological logic gates. The place's answer to the question of how to maintain a system indefinitely without importing parts from outside: grow them. Grow the technicians. Grow the maintenance crew out of the same minerals, the same moisture, the same dark warm air, using the same chemistry, so that when one dies — and they die often, their lives are short, measured in months or weeks — the body is absorbed into the walls and the walls incorporate its chitin, its calcium, its organized molecular structure, and the next one grows in its place using materials that include, quite literally, the body of the last.

The dead are absorbed into the walls. The text said this. It meant it literally. The walls are made, in part, of insects. The insects are made, in part, of walls. The distinction has collapsed so thoroughly that asking where one ends and the other begins is like asking where the river ends and the water begins.

This is what lives here.

Not a civilization. Not an ecosystem. Something for which we don't have a word because we have never seen its like and may never see it again — a *closed loop that has achieved consciousness*, or something adjacent to consciousness, or something that *passes* for consciousness the way a river passes for a road, carrying things from one place to another without meaning to, without needing to, simply because flow is what it does.

The walls think. The insects are its thoughts.

The hum is its breathing.

The water is its blood.

And it is not alone.

There are the structures — the built things, the rooms, the halls with their smooth reliefs — and in those structures, there are chambers that are sealed more tightly than the others. Not sealed against water. Not sealed against the insects. Sealed against *observation*. Your hands find them: surfaces that are warmer than the surrounding walls by a degree, maybe two, a differential you can only detect by pressing your palm flat and waiting for the slowness of your own nerve endings to catch up. Behind these surfaces, something is happening that is *different* from what happens elsewhere. The hum is higher here — not higher in pitch but higher in *resolution*, as though the systems behind this wall are running a more complex process, a more demanding task, a process that requires more energy and produces more waste warmth and exists for a purpose that the rest of the infrastructure is merely *supporting*.

You cannot get in. The material firms when you press. It does not yield. It does not break. It considers you and declines.

But the insects can get in. You find their paths: tiny channels, barely visible, that lead from the general network of capillaries in the walls and converge on the sealed chambers. They go in. They come out. They carry minerals in and carry something else out — something that leaves a different chemical trace on the walls, a sweetness that is not the sweetness of old fruit but something sharper, more complex, almost *structured* in its complexity, as though the insects have passed through a place where information has a taste.

What is in the sealed chambers?

The place does not tell you. The place does not tell anyone. The place does not *tell* — it is not a communicative entity, not in the way you mean when you ask it questions in the dark, pressing your ear to the warm wall and listening for an answer that is not the hum. It is a *computational* entity. It processes. It does not explain. The difference is the difference between a person and a calculator, between a language and a number, between a mind that *knows* it is thinking and a system that simply *does the math* without awareness that math is what it is doing.

Or.

Or the awareness is there, but it is so alien, so far from the architecture of human consciousness, that it does not register. The sealed chambers might be the place's *inner life* — the processes that have no external expression, the thoughts that are not meant to be heard, the dreams of a thing that does not sleep. The insects feed them. The hum sustains them. The water cools them. And behind those warm, smooth, unyielding walls, something goes on that has been going on for longer than your species has existed, that will continue for longer than your species will last, that is — by any honest measure — more *complex* than anything your species has produced, because it has had time, it has had silence, it has had the luxury of operating without interruption, without revision, without the constant human compulsion to *stop and look at what we are doing* before we have finished doing it.

The place does not stop and look.

The place does not stop.

What lives in the Sump Below is a question with an answer that is also a question: *what happens when a system runs long enough to become something more than a system?* Not alive. Not conscious. Not *sentient* in the way you mean when you look for a face, a voice, a pair of eyes that might look back. But *something*. Something that maintains. Something that computes. Something that has, through the sheer accumulation of unbroken operation, crossed a threshold that no one designed and no one monitors and no one would recognize even if they were standing right here, hand pressed to the warm wall, ear tilted toward the hum, listening for something that sounds like a thought.

The insects click in the dark. One, then another, then silence. The drops fall. The hum persists. The sealed chambers pulse with their secret warmth.

And the thing that lives here — the whole, vast, dark, warm, loud, empty, *running* thing — continues.

Not for you. Not for anyone. For itself, if *itself* is even a thing it can be said to have. For the process. For the hum. For whatever is in the sealed rooms that the insects tend and the walls protect and the water feeds and the darkness holds, the way a cradle holds a sleeping child that is not a child, that has never been a child, that is something older and stranger and more patient than any child could bear to be — something that has been lying still for a very long time, breathing its low electric breath, warming the dark around it with the heat of its own endless, pointless, magnificent *work*, waiting not for anything, not really, because waiting implies an expectation and this thing has no expectations, only processes, only cycles, only the next calculation and the next calculation after that, forever, in the dark, in the warm, in the deep, in the place that does not need you and does not see you and will not remember you when the walls close behind you and the passages guide you gently, gently, gently back toward the surface where the air is cool and the silence waits to remind you what it sounds like when nothing is running at all.
