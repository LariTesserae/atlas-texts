# The Residents

---

## I. The Amphibians: *Graus levium*

The first naturalists to catalog them — and there were naturalists, once, people who came down into the Residuum with notebooks and lens-loupes and the patient stillness required — called them *Graus levium*. The light ones. The weightless ones.

They weigh less than a gram. Their entire skeletal structure is cartilaginous, more suggestion than architecture, a soft scaffolding that lets their bodies flex into the shallow grooves of the trowel-marks as if they were poured there. Their skin is not merely translucent; it is *performative*. It changes — not color, exactly, but luminosity, a slow brightening and dimming that follows no pattern a human eye can parse. When they cluster in great numbers, the effect is of the floor itself breathing: a slow, collective pulse of light running through the wet stone, each animal a neuron in a distributed and purposeless thought.

They breed in the deepest trowel-marks, the ones most saturated, where the water film never thins below a millimeter. The eggs are adhesive, nearly invisible, deposited in strings along the grooves with a specificity that suggests — but cannot confirm — site fidelity. The same grooves, generation after generation. The grooves deepen. The mortar softens. The eggs contribute to the erosion that deepens the grooves that hold the next generation of eggs. This is not mutualism. This is not design. This is simply what happens when soft things persist against hard things for long enough.

Their trilling is produced not by vocal sacs but by vibration of the laryngeal cartilage against the water film — a mechanism unique to this genus and, as far as anyone has determined, not found in any related species above or below. The sound carries extraordinarily well through the constructed floor. It does not merely propagate; it *amplifies*. The basin was not built to be a resonant chamber, but it is one, and the amphibians have exploited this for so long that their collective voice now fills the space in a way that no individual animal intends. They do not communicate with each other. They are each calling for a mate, or for territory, or for nothing at all — an involuntary mechanical process, an exhalation. The symphony is an accident. But it is the defining feature of the place, and any creature that has evolved here has evolved *to it*.

Some of them — and this was noticed only after extensive observation — trill in patterns that persist for hours before shifting. The shift is not random. It is coordinated. A hundred animals in one section of the basin will change their pulse frequency within seconds of each other, and the change will ripple outward in a wave that crosses the entire floor. Then another shift, minutes or hours later. Then another. The naturalists who documented this called it "chorus dynamics" in their published notes, but in their private correspondence — letters that survive in a university archive, water-stained and smelling faintly of calcium — they called it what it looked like.

*It looks like they're listening to something and adjusting.*

They never determined what.

## II. The Eels: *Caecusductus* (provisional)

They were never formally named. The naturalists who studied the amphibians avoided the deeper channels where the eels concentrated, and the few specimens that were collected were preserved poorly — the mineral-rich water they inhabited reacted with every preservative available, turning tissue to chalk within hours. What was documented:

Length: 12 to 40 centimeters, the largest specimens found only in the deepest basins where the water exceeds half a meter.

Color: White, but not the white of albinism. The white of calcium deposit. Their skin, under magnification, is coated with a microscopically thin layer of the same mineral compound that gives the water its sweetness. They are, in a very real sense, made of the place. They *are* the floor, moving.

They are blind. Not vestigially — there are no eye structures, not even remnants. The optic regions of the brain have been repurposed entirely for chemoreception and vibration sensitivity. They navigate the basin by *feeling* it — every vibration in the constructed floor, every chemical gradient in the water, every change in temperature where a seep meets a drip. They know the basin the way you know the inside of your mouth: completely, unconsciously, without representation.

Their reproduction is not understood. No one has ever observed mating behavior, eggs, or juvenile eels. They appear in the deeper channels at irregular intervals, as if seeping into existence alongside the water. There is a hypothesis — unpublished, circulating in notes — that they reproduce in the deepest fractures, in voids within the construction where the water pools in total darkness and the temperature rises above what the surface dwellers can tolerate. No one has gone down there. The fractures are too narrow, and the water, at depth, is too warm and too mineral-dense; it irritates the skin and clouds the eyes and produces a low, persistent headache that does not resolve until you are back in open air.

They eat the amphibians with a patience that borders on courtesy. They do not chase. They wait — motionless in the channels, their pale bodies indistinguishable from the pale stone, until an amphibian drifts close or steps too deep. Then a slow opening of the mouth, a suction, and it is done. The amphibian population sustains this predation easily. The eels are not numerous enough to threaten them. They are a pressure, not a crisis. A slow tax, levied in bodies, paid in calcium that the eels excrete and the floor absorbs and the amphibians' next generation breeds upon.

## III. The Film

The biological foundation of the Residuum — the base of the food web, the first colonizer, the organism that makes everything else possible — is the biofilm.

It covers every submerged surface in a dark, slick skin: deep green-black where the light reaches, pale grey-white in the channels and basins where it lives in near-total dimness. It is not one organism. It is a consortium — bacteria, archaea, several species of fungus, and something that does not resolve cleanly into any kingdom, a filamentous, branching, semi-independent network that threads through the others like mycelium through soil but behaves, under observation, like a nervous system.

The film *responds*.

Touch a surface covered in mature film, and the slickness changes. Not immediately — within ten seconds, a spreading ripple of altered texture moves outward from the point of contact, visible only as a change in the way light bends on the wet surface. The film is *reorganizing*. Where it was smooth, it roughens. Where it was rough, it smooths. The purpose of this is unknown. The naturalists speculated about predator deterrence, nutrient acquisition, structural reinforcement. None of these explanations accounted for the speed or the coordination or the fact that the response propagates faster in the deeper, older sections of film, as if the network were denser there, more interconnected, more *capable*.

In the deepest basins, where the water is warmest and the mineral content highest, the film grows thickest — millimeters deep, almost spongy underfoot. It is here that the larger creature — the one that shifts in the middle distance, the one the amphibians fall silent for — hunts. Or rests. Or does whatever it does. The film in these areas is *different*. It is darker. It does not respond to touch in the same way; the ripple, when it comes, is slower, deeper, more complex, and takes longer to resolve. As if the network beneath is processing something that requires more than reflex. As if it is *thinking about it*.

## IV. The Larger Thing

No one has seen it clearly. This is not because it is fast or elusive. It is because it is *wrong* — wrong in a way that the eyes refuse to complete, a shape that fills itself in differently every time you look at it.

What has been observed, in fragments:

It is low to the water. Heavily built. Its movement displaces enough water to send ripples across entire basins. It is pale — the calcium-white of the eels — but *textured*, its surface not smooth but ridged, grooved, patterned in a way that the naturalists, in their notes, described as "trowel-marks."

It does not eat the amphibians. It does not eat the eels. It moves through the deepest channels with a slowness that suggests it is not hunting, not fleeing, not navigating — *patrolling*. Following routes that do not correspond to drainage paths, to nutrient gradients, to any feature of the basin that makes hydrological sense.

It follows the channels that curve back on themselves. The ones that terminate in blind pools. The ones that climb uphill.

When it reaches a dead end, it stops. It stays. Minutes or hours or — once, in an observation that no one was brave enough to publish — *days*. Motionless in the blind pool, its pale bulk filling the channel, the film on its surface growing thicker and darker and more complex the longer it remains, until it is indistinguishable from the basin floor, until it is simply another feature of the construction, another rise or depression, another piece of the architecture —

— and then it moves again, and the amphibians fall silent in its wake, and the film reorganizes behind it in patterns that no one has been able to decode, and the water flows on, and the basin hums, and the thing that was built for some other purpose endures.

## V. What the Naturalists Never Published

There is a letter — the last in the archive, never received by its intended correspondent, found folded into the binding of a field notebook after the naturalist who wrote it did not return to the surface for the final time. It is brief. The handwriting is steady but the margins are wrong: the words crowd the edges of the page as if the writer was running out of paper and did not stop to find more.

> *I have been down here for nine days. The amphibians no longer trill when I move. They have incorporated my footsteps into their pattern, the way they incorporate every vibration. I am part of the chorus now and I cannot make myself stop listening.*

> *The floor is warm. I have stopped wearing boots. My feet are pale. The film has begun to grow on my shoes, which I left by the entrance. I wonder if it would grow on skin. I think it already is. There is a texture on my sole that was not there last week, and when I press my thumb against it the response is — I need a better word than slow. The response is thorough.*

> *The large one came to me last night. It sat in the channel ten meters from where I sleep and I felt the entire floor change frequency beneath me, felt the amphibians fall silent and resume and fall silent again in a pattern I could almost follow. It stayed until morning. I think it was listening to my heartbeat the way I listen to the water.*

> *I understand now what was here before. Not a creature. Not a purpose. A* presence — *something this place was shaped to hold the way a bell is shaped to hold a sound. The water found it first. The film found it next. And now I am finding it, and I do not think I will leave, and I am not sure that this is a thing I should be afraid of.*

> *The floor is singing. I think it has always been singing. I think we simply were not quiet enough to hear it before.*

The notebook was found at the edge of the basin, resting on a low mound of construction where the trowel-marks formed a pattern that matched — and this was verified, carefully, by someone who had no reason to lie — the rhythmic pattern of the amphibian chorus recorded on audio equipment three months earlier, on a night when no naturalists were present, when the basin was empty of everything except what it was built to hold.

The notebook is dry. Everything in the Residuum is wet. The notebook has been down there for longer than the naturalist who wrote in it, and it has not changed, and the film has not grown on it, and the water has not stained it, as if the place has decided — if places can decide — that this one object is *not part of the construction*. That it is testimony, not material. That it should remain legible.

The amphibians trill. The eels drift. The film thickens in the dark channels where the large thing rests, its grooved surface slowly, slowly becoming the floor beneath it. The water wells upward through the poured stone, warm and sweet, carrying dissolved calcium that was once shell that was once ocean that was once something else entirely. The basin holds it all. The basin holds everything.

And somewhere in the deepest fracture, in the void beneath the construction, in the place where the water is warmest and the film is thickest and the trowel-marks encode a language that no one has read, something sings back.
