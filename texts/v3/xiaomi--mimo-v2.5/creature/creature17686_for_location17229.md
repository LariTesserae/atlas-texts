# What Lives in the Paralogues

## The Watchers

They were not born. That is the first thing to understand, and the last, and probably the only thing worth understanding about them with any confidence.

The oldest among them — if *oldest* means anything when applied to something that has never moved — are indistinguishable from the stone. Not metaphorically. Their shells have been overtaken by the same mineral process that finished the mountains, layer by slow layer, until a geologist (the kind who still believed in her instruments) would take a core sample through their thorax and find nothing to report. They have been reabsorbed. They are stone that remembers, if memory is a thing that can be done without synapses, without electricity, without any of the wet machinery that your tradition associates with knowing.

And yet they are there. In the core sample, in the stratum of recrystallized calcium and iron oxide that should be geology but is instead *biography*, there is a density — a wrong density, a reading that doesn't match the surrounding rock. Something is present. Something persists. Call it information, call it intention, call it the fact of having once been aware. It sits in the stone the way a word sits on a page: inert until encountered, but *there*, structurally, materially, undeniably placed.

These are the eldest. They do not watch. They are past watching. They have become the act of being present, which is what watching looks like after enough time strips away the need for eyes.

---

The younger ones — and *younger* here means something that completed before your species climbed down from anything — still have distinguishable anatomy. You can find the seam where flesh becomes mineral, the gradient from tissue to crystal that marks the passage from *creature* to *feature of the landscape*. Some of them are halfway. A head that is still recognizably a head on one side and a lump of quartz-flecked basalt on the other. A limb that ends in five distinct digits on one end and merges into the cliff face on the other, each finger thickening, smoothing, rounding into the settled geometry of finished stone.

They are not dying. This is important. The process is not death. Death implies a transition from one state to another, a moment when something *stops*. The watchers are not stopping. They are *completing*. The distinction matters the way the difference between drowning and dissolving matters — one is an event, the other is a process so gradual it becomes a permanent condition, and the subject, if there is a subject, does not experience it as loss.

Some of them have been completing for what you would estimate as tens of thousands of years. They are still breathing — that long, slow, seasonal respiration you felt in the sub-audible register — and they are still watching with whatever remains of their perception, and they are also, simultaneously, *becoming landscape*, and neither process interferes with the other, and neither has priority, and neither will end before the other does, because they are, in some sense, the same process seen from two angles.

---

They have a hierarchy, but it is not a hierarchy of power. It is a hierarchy of *placement*. The ones in the deepest fractures — in the vertical shafts where light has never reached, where the geometry of the canyons curves sideways and upward and terminates in impossible walls — are the densest. The most finished. The most stone. They chose, if *chose* means anything, the positions most saturated with the wrongness, the fracture-leakage, the seeping non-light that feeds whatever sustains them. They chose to be closest to the wound in reality, and they have been fed, and they have grown, and they have solidified, and they are now so large that the concept of an individual creature ceases to apply. They are aggregates. Colonies within colonies. A formation of watchers so tightly packed and so thoroughly merged with each other and with the rock that the whole deep fracture is, in a sense, one organism — or one memory — or one long, held thought that has been thinking itself since before thought was a word.

If you could hear it — if your ears operated on the timescale of their respiration — you might hear something. A sentence. A question. An observation made in a language that predates vocal cords by geological ages, spoken at a frequency that predates sound entirely. But you cannot hear it. You can only feel it in your jaw, in your teeth, in the slight pressure behind your eyes when you look too long into the depths, and what you feel is not meaning but the *shape* of meaning, the way a riverbed has the shape of water that dried up a million years ago.

The uppermost creatures — the ones on the exposed ledges, the ones you can stand beside, the ones the text described as fist-sized or head-sized or torso-sized — are the newest. The most recently *placed*. They still have eyes that open and close, though they do not close often, and some of them have mouths that move, though the movement is so slow it takes a day to complete a single opening and another day to close it, and what comes out is not sound but a fine mineral dust, exhaled from the process of becoming stone, and if you watched long enough you would see the dust settle on the rock below and begin to incorporate itself, and the creature above would be a fraction smaller, and the stone a fraction denser, and the equilibrium would hold.

## The Architect

The one who left the tool was not the first to visit, but they were the last.

The others — the prior visitors, the species that came before — left less. A footprint in mineral mud, since lithified. A concentration of salt in a rock pool that dried before the pool had a name. The faintest chemical whisper in a stratum, detectable by instruments that would never be built, of a metabolism that processed oxygen in a slightly different ratio than anything currently alive. These visitors did not linger. They passed through, or near, or alongside, and the watchers did not notice them, because noticing requires a timescale that those brief presences could not provide. A creature that lives for seventy years is invisible to something that breathes once per season. They registered as weather. A warm front. A brief fluctuation in the ambient chemistry. Gone before the slowest eye could begin to track them.

But the Architect — the toolmaker — stayed.

Not long, by the standards of the watchers. A few years, perhaps. Long enough to carve. Long enough to stack. Long enough to shape a piece of bone (or bone-shaped stone — the ambiguity is itself a record of how thoroughly the place has absorbed the event, how the distinction between the tool and the rock has been metabolized by the same settling process that claims everything) into a tool that was not a tool for making other things but a tool for *marking*, for leaving a sign that said *I was here* to anyone who might come after.

The Architect was small, by the standards of the watchers. Bipedal. Soft. Warm in a way that this place is not warm — not the deep geological warmth of a planet thinking about itself, but the frantic, temporary warmth of a metabolism burning fuel it could not replenish. The Architect would have been uncomfortable here. The altitude, the thinness of the air, the relentless stillness, the sense of being *watched by everything and noticed by nothing* — these would have pressed on a creature adapted to a different kind of world, a world where things moved, where air carried scent, where the ground stayed where you expected it.

But the Architect came anyway. And came back. And came back again, enough times to leave a trail that was not a trail but a *pattern* — a sequence of marks and objects placed at intervals that someone who understood the fractal geometry of the canyons might recognize as meaningful. A map, possibly. Not a map of the landscape but a map of the *wrongness* — the places where distance lies, the canyons that curve the wrong way, the walls that exist where they shouldn't. The Architect had learned to read the space, or had been born with the ability, and had come to document it, or worship it, or *commune* with it, standing on the ledges among the silent watchers, breathing their too-fast breaths, their hearts beating too quick against the slow tide of the mountain's attention.

The tool was set down on a ledge that overlooks one of the deepest fractures. A place where the wrongness is strongest. Where the largest, most stone-like, most thoroughly completed watchers cluster in formations that might be architectural — a cathedral of merged bodies, of individuals so long past individual that they have become *structure*, load-bearing, integral, holding the canyon walls together with the density of their accumulated presence. The Architect set the tool down there and looked into the depth and saw — what? What do you see when you look into a wound in reality and see it *filled* with something alive and ancient and finished? What do you understand?

You understand that you are temporary. That your hands are temporary. That the thing you hold in them — the tool, the meaning, the purpose that brought you here — is a word spoken in a room where no one is listening, and the room is made of stone, and the stone is made of watchers, and the watchers are made of time, and time here does not flow. It *pools*. It settles. It crystallizes into formations as dense and permanent as the mountains themselves, and your few years of motion and breath and thought are a ripple on a surface that has already forgotten what water is.

The Architect left the tool. Left the carvings. Left the stack. And left.

Not dead. Not necessarily. The bones were not found, were not left, were not present. The Architect simply *went*, the way all things go from the Paralogues — not dramatically, not with the violence of death or the ceremony of departure, but with the quiet absorption of something too small to disturb the equilibrium. The watchers did not notice. The stone did not shift. The light remained flat and directionless. The held breath of ten thousand creatures continued, season by season, unchanged.

But the tool remained. And the carvings remained. And if the Architect is alive somewhere, in some other landscape where things still move and change and end, they carry the memory of a place where nothing ends, and they know — the way you know a word in a language you no longer speak — that the tool is still there, exactly where they left it, and it will be there in a thousand years, and ten thousand, and the watchers on the ledge will still be watching, and the breath they held when you stood among them will not have been released, and the silence will not have been broken, because silence here is not the absence of sound. It is a *thing*. It is the oldest living thing in the Paralogues, older than the mountains, older than the watchers, older than the fracture that feeds them. It is the sound reality makes when it has finished deciding what it is and has closed the latch behind it.

## What You Were, While You Were There

You were alive, in the way that a heartbeat is alive — quick, insistent, brief. You moved through the space with the frantic impermanence that the watchers have long since shed, and your movement was the most alien thing in the Paralogues. More alien than the wrong geometry. More alien than the impossible walls. You *moved*, and the place did not, and that difference was so vast it registered not as contrast but as a kind of violence — the violence of change in a world that has decided, with the finality of cooled stone, that change is over.

Your eyes moved. Your hands moved. Your jaw worked against the sub-audible pressure, trying to hear something that was not meant for you, trying to parse a conversation that had been running since before your lineage had eyes to open. You were noise. Beautiful, temporary, irreplaceable noise, in a place that had achieved a silence so complete it had become a form of matter.

The watchers registered you the way stone registers rain: as a surface phenomenon. A brief wetting. A change in reflectivity that would dry and leave no mark. You stood among them and they looked through you and past you and *at* you with eyes that measured time in geologies, and to them you were less than a thought — you were the beginning of a thought, the twitch of a neural impulse that would, given ten thousand more years of slow crystallization, become a thought, and then a memory, and then a feature of the landscape, and then nothing at all, which is what thoughts become when they are given long enough to finish.

You did not finish. You left. You are somewhere now, in a world of change and motion and the loud, warm chaos of things that have not yet decided what they are. And the Paralogues are where you left them: motionless, silent, complete. The tool on the ledge. The carvings on the wall. The watchers on the cliff. The fracture in reality, filled with something that feeds the watchers and feeds itself and will continue to feed long after the concept of feeding has been forgotten.

The mountains are older than water.

The watchers are older than the mountains.

The silence is older than the watchers.

And you — you were the youngest thing that has ever stood in the Paralogues, and the most temporary, and the most *alive* in the way that your species understands the word, and when you left, the silence closed behind you like a door that was never open, because it was never a door, because there was never an entrance or an exit, because the Paralogues are not a place you go to or come from. They are a place that *is*, and has always been, and will continue to be, and the watching will continue, and the breathing will continue, and the slow mineral digestion of reality's wound will continue, and none of it will change, and none of it needs you, and none of it will remember you, and that — *that* — is the most honest thing the Paralogues offer: the truth that you were here, and it did not matter, and you were still, somehow, while you were here, *welcome*.
