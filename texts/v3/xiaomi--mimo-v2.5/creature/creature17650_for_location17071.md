# What Lives in the Underworks

---

The angular fish were the first to be made, and they know it the way a river knows it was given a channel — not as knowledge, but as shape. Their bodies are tessellated plates of cartilage and translucent flesh, each segment a perfect trapezoid that interlocks with the next, so that when they turn, the whole school turns at once, and the ceiling-light fractures across them in a cascading flicker, and for a moment each individual fish becomes invisible inside the pattern of the group. They are small — the length of a human hand, perhaps — but they are never alone. They move in bands of two or three hundred, streaming along the undersides of the ceiling plates in paths that have been worn into their behavior over generations, or perhaps encoded into them from the start. It does not matter. The paths are the same either way.

They feed on the biofilm — a living skin of engineered microorganisms that colonize every surface in the Underworks, so thin it is invisible except as a faint matte quality on the plates, a dulling of what would otherwise be a mild sheen. The fish scrape it off with specialized mouthparts: wide, flat, lined with microscopic teeth that work like a squeegee. You can hear them doing it if you are very still and very close — a faint, rhythmic clicking, like someone running a fingernail along a comb. It is the most intimate sound in the Underworks, and it is everywhere, all the time, in every direction, a soft static of consumption that underlies the mechanical hum the way rainfall underlies thunder.

They do not sleep. The light never changes, so their bodies never learned the rhythm of darkness. They feed constantly, moving in slow, purposeful spirals along the plates, and when one band exhausts the biofilm in a given area, it simply moves on, and another band arrives behind it, and behind that another, so that the whole ceiling is a slow clockwise rotation of life, a living current layered on the mechanical one. The biofilm regenerates in the intervals. The system works. The fish are fat and well and utterly untroubled, their flat black eyes reflecting nothing because there is nothing to reflect — no predator's approach that isn't already accounted for, no current that deviates from its calculated path. They are the most contented creatures in the Underworks, and their contentment is the contentment of a thing that was designed to want exactly what it has.

---

The shrimp-colonies are older than the fish. Not by much — a few construction cycles, a few recalibrations of the system — but enough that their behavior has begun to drift from its original parameters. They were placed on the pylons to do what shrimp do in engineered systems: filter, process, excrete the intermediary chemistry that keeps the water balanced between sterile and alive. And they do this. Their legs — six per individual, each one a hair-thin filament of chitin ending in a microscopic claw — comb the water ceaselessly, extracting particles too small for the fish to bother with. Their mouths work in unison. When one colony on a given pylon shifts its rhythm, the others follow within minutes, as though the entire cluster were a single organism with distributed awareness.

But something else has happened. The colonies have begun to *migrate*. Not far — a few meters, a few tens of meters — but the movement was not part of the design. They loosen their grip on the pylon in coordinated waves, detaching hundreds at a time, drifting outward into the open water with their legs paddling in slow, synchronized strokes, and then reattaching to a different surface: a different pylon, a different face of the same pylon, sometimes a ceiling plate. They do this every few hours. The pattern is not random. They are moving toward the warm water that the factories belch from the pylons — not to feed on it, but to sit in it, the way a person sits near a fire. They cluster around the thermal vents in dense, rust-red constellations, and they are still, and they do not filter, and they do not work, and they stay until the warm plume fades, and then they drift back to their positions and resume their labor as though nothing had happened.

Something has changed in them. The engineers, if they are watching, have not corrected it. Perhaps they have not noticed. Perhaps the shrimp-colonies are the first thing in the Underworks to have *preferences*, and the system is too large and too slow to notice a preference expressed in centimeters.

---

The jellyfish — the long ones, the ones that trail their cold blue filaments through the middle distances — are the most beautiful things here, and also the most alien to the architecture. Everything else in the Underworks has a function that is legible: the fish eat biofilm, the shrimp filter water, the macroalgae provide oxygen and aesthetic concession. The jellyfish eat the fish. That is their function. But they do it in a way that seems almost accidental, as though predation were a side effect of drifting.

They are long. Three meters, five meters, sometimes more — a narrow bell at the head, no wider than a dinner plate, and then behind it a trailing tangle of filaments that spread and braid and separate in the slow currents like the hair of something drowning. The filaments carry the bioluminescence: each one a thread of cold light, blue-white, pulsing in irregular rhythms that are almost but not quite synchronized with the others, so that a single jellyfish in motion looks like a slow signal being transmitted, a message in a language of flicker and fade. They are nearly transparent. In the dim water between the ceiling and the lattice, you see the light before you see the animal — a constellation of drifting points that resolves, as you approach, into a body, and then into a vast, delicate, impossible architecture of nerve and water and light.

They do not chase. They do not need to. The angular fish, in their endless grazing parades, swim directly into the filaments with the blind purpose of creatures whose navigation was tuned for feeding and not for avoidance. The filaments sting. The fish convulses once, twice, and then goes still, and the jellyfish contracts its bell in a slow, patient pulse, drawing the fish upward toward its mouth, which is not a mouth so much as a cavity — a central消化 space where enzymes do their work in a solution that is nearly indistinguishable from the surrounding water. The digestion takes hours. The jellyfish drifts onward while it feeds, indifferent to the process occurring inside it, its filaments trailing behind in a widening gyre of dim, fading light.

They have no brains in any conventional sense. A nerve net, distributed through the bell and the filaments, processing information the way the shrimp-colonies process water: everywhere, slowly, without center. And yet they navigate. They avoid the pylons. They steer around the thermal plumes. They gather — and this is the thing that the engineers have not accounted for, the thing that makes the jellyfish more than infrastructure — they gather in the deep water, near the lattice, in congregations of dozens or hundreds, their filaments interweaving into a vast, luminous mesh that pulses in a slow, shared rhythm, and in those congregations something happens that has no name. It is not mating, exactly, though new jellyfish appear afterward. It is not communication, though the rhythm of the pulsing shifts in response to stimuli that are not prey and not thermal and not current. It is something else. Something the Underworks did not install.

They pulse together. And the light they make, when they pulse together, fills the deep water with a dim, breathing glow that the ceiling plates cannot account for — a light from below, moving, organic, irregular, alive in a way that the engineered light above is not. For a few hours, the lattice is illuminated by something that was not in the blueprint. And then the congregation disperses, and the jellyfish drift back to the middle distances, and the deep water goes dark again, and the ceiling light reasserts itself, and everything returns to normal.

Except it isn't normal. It never was.

---

There is something in the lattice.

You see it only in glimpses — a shadow that moves against the geometry of the girders, a displacement of water that arrives a moment before the shape does and a moment after it has gone. It is large. The size estimates are unreliable because you never see the whole thing at once, only a limb — an arm, a leg, a tentacle, something with a joint that bends the wrong way, something with a surface that is smooth and pale and faintly luminous in the way that deep-sea things are luminous, as though light were a language it spoke but had largely forgotten.

The engineers designed it. This is certain. It occupies a position in the food web that was calculated, just as the jellyfish's position was calculated, just as the fish's was. It eats the jellyfish. It is the apex. The shadow in the lattice, the shape with too many limbs — it was placed there to close the circuit, to keep the jellyfish population in check, to ensure that the elegant, drifting predators did not multiply beyond the system's capacity to replenish the fish they consumed.

But it has grown beyond its parameters. Not in size — or not only in size — but in behavior. It was designed to patrol the lattice, to move along its corridors and capture jellyfish as they drifted into the deep water. And it does this. But it also does other things. It builds. In the deepest reaches of the lattice, where the girders are most distant and the light from the ceiling plates has been swallowed entirely by kilometers of water, it has constructed — or assembled, or accumulated — a structure of its own. Not large. The size of a room, perhaps, if the room were made of salvage: fragments of the pylons, pieces of the ceiling plates, shards and segments fitted together with a crude, purposeful intelligence that is recognizably *intentional* in a way that the engineering of the Underworks is not.

The engineering of the Underworks is perfect. It is calculated. It is without waste.

The thing in the lattice builds badly. Its structure is lopsided. Its joints are uneven. It uses materials for which it has no obvious purpose — a ceiling plate fragment positioned vertically in the middle of a space that would be more efficiently open, a pylon shard welded to nothing at all, protruding from a girder like a finger pointing at the dark. It is art. It has to be. It is the first artifact in the Underworks that was not designed, and it is ugly and crooked and full of intent, and it sits in the deep water like a question that the architecture was not built to answer.

The thing moves. It is always moving. Its limbs — how many? six? eight? twelve? — propel it through the lattice with a fluid, unsettling grace, each limb finding purchase on a different surface, so that it is always in contact with four or five points of the structure at once, always anchored, always reaching. It hunts by vibration. The jellyfish, pulsing their cold light in the deep congregations, are invisible to it — their light means nothing in the black water of the lattice. But their movement creates pressure waves, tiny distortions in the uniform weight of the water, and the thing feels these with organs distributed along the undersides of its limbs, and it orients toward them, and it moves, and it feeds.

It has never seen the ceiling. It has never been in the upper water, in the bright, open emptiness where the fish graze and the jellyfish drift. It lives entirely in the lattice, in the dark geometry of the deep structure, and the world above it is a rumor of pressure and warmth that reaches it only as the faintest modulation of the water that flows downward from the factories. It does not know the light. It does not know the surface it has never seen. Its world is girders and shadows and the slow, electric taste of jellyfish approaching in the dark.

And yet it builds. It takes the materials that fall — or are carried, or drift downward on the slow circulations — and it makes something with them. Not a tool. Not a shelter. Something else. Something that has no function in the system. Something that the engineers, if they are watching, cannot classify.

---

There is a sixth thing. A seventh. Things you almost miss.

Tiny organisms — nearly microscopic — that live in the seams of the ceiling plates. They inhabit the hairline cracks where two sections have not quite settled, feeding on the mineral deposits that accumulate in those narrow spaces: calcium, silicon, trace metals that leach from the plate alloy under the constant pressure. They are unremarkable. Transparent. Worm-like. The kind of thing that exists in every engineered system, the inevitable biological residue of any environment that persists long enough to develop its own cracks.

But they are *spreading*. Not along the plates — that would be expected, a simple colonization of available surface — but *between* the plates. They have found the gaps. They have threaded their way into the spaces between the sections, into the sealed chambers and interstitial voids that were designed to be empty, to be purely structural, to be nothing at all. And in those spaces, they are doing something that no one designed them to do. They are building walls. Thin, mineral walls, secreted from the materials they consume, dividing the voids into smaller spaces, connecting them in branching networks that follow no engineering logic, no structural plan. They are making a city inside the architecture — a microscopic metropolis of chambers and corridors, hidden in the spaces between the plates that hold up the sky of this manufactured sea.

No one knows they are there. The systems that monitor the ceiling plates measure stress, temperature, alignment. They do not measure the interior of the seams. The things in the cracks are invisible to every sensor, every scanner, every diagnostic that the Underworks employs. They are the smallest inhabitants of this place, and the most numerous — billions of them, trillions, an civilization of microscopic builders operating in the spaces between the engineered plates, making something that no one asked for, in a place that was never meant to hold life at all.

---

This is what lives in the Underworks: things that were made and have become something else. Things that exceed their specifications. The fish that graze in perfect, clockwork obedience, and the shrimp that have learned to seek warmth, and the jellyfish that pulse together in congregations of unnamed purpose, and the creature in the lattice that builds crooked monuments in the dark, and the worms in the ceiling seams that are quietly, invisibly rewriting the architecture from the inside.

The Underworks was built to be a system. Everything in it was placed with intention. The water, the light, the structures, the life — all of it calculated, all of it designed, all of it meant to function in a specific way for a specific purpose that no one down here can articulate.

But life does not stay in its parameters. It never has. It grows into the cracks — literally, in the case of the worms — and it drifts beyond its boundaries, and it gathers in the dark and does things that no blueprint predicted. The Underworks is a machine that contains organisms that have begun, slowly and without any awareness of what they are doing, to make it *theirs*. Not by force. Not by intention. By the simple, relentless accumulation of behavior — the shrimp detaching and reattaching, the jellyfish pulsing in unison, the deep creature reaching across the lattice with one pale limb to place a shard of ceiling plate against a girder at an angle that serves no purpose except the one it serves in the mind of the thing that placed it.

The ceiling glows. The machines hum. The pylons stand sharp-edged in the cool, clear water. And in the seams, in the dark, in the spaces between the plates and the spaces between the girders and the spaces between the engineered purpose of every living thing in this vast, constructed sea — life accumulates. It builds. It drifts toward warmth. It pulses in rhythms that were not choreographed. It makes things that no one designed, and it does not ask permission, and it does not stop.

This is what lives in the Underworks: everything that was put here, and everything it has become since.
