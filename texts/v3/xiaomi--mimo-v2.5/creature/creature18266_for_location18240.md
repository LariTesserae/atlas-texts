# What Lives in The Gash

What lives in The Gash lives badly, and with great conviction.

The ferns are the first and most obvious residents, and they are not well. Their fronds are too thick, too heavy, too dark — weighted with a chlorophyll density that suggests desperation rather than abundance, the green of something photosynthesizing at the edge of viability and compensating by brute force. They are not beautiful. They are functional in the way that something starving is functional: all effort directed at the single problem of persistence. Their roots — if you can call them roots, these coarse, fibrous cables that drive into fracture planes with a patience that borders on violence — grip the basalt with a force that you can sometimes hear: a faint, creaking groan that harmonizes with the settling of the wedged slabs and is nearly indistinguishable from it. The ferns are not merely growing on the rock. They are growing *into* it, prying at it, widening fractures that water will then enter, that frost will then exploit, that will eventually produce new surfaces on which new ferns will grow. They are both the colonists and the advance scouts of their own expansion. The rock feeds them moisture; they return the favor by digesting it.

Between the ferns, the moss — that broad, fleshy, unclassifiable moss — does something worse. It drinks. Not in the way that plants are supposed to drink, passively, through osmosis, but actively, visibly, in a way that the initial description of condensation does not capture. If you watch long enough — and there is nothing else to do in The Gash but watch and listen — you will see the moss's surface dimple. Tiny depressions form in its velvet surface, almost like cupping a hand under a faucet, and the dimples fill with a film of water so thin it is more shine than substance, and then the dimples smooth out again, and the water is gone. The moss is pumping. Not with a heart, not with any organ you could name, but with a turgor-driven mechanism that is, if you are honest about it, a kind of breathing. A slow, vegetative respiration. The moss inhales water through its skin and exhales nothing — the moisture simply vanishes into the metabolic machinery of a plant that has no business being this efficient in this little light.

And the light is the thing that makes everything here improbable.

The sky above The Gash is a crack of white, narrow enough that on overcast days it is almost indistinguishable from the upper rock. Direct sunlight enters the gorge perhaps forty minutes a day, during a window that shifts with the seasons, and when it does it strikes the walls at such an acute angle that it illuminates only the uppermost meter of vegetation before being swallowed. Everything below that lives in a light that is less light than rumor — scattered, reflected, filtered through successive layers of green until what reaches the deepest plants is not a wavelength so much as a ghost of one. Spectrally, it is almost monochromatic: the green-soaked remnant of sunlight that has passed through three or four fronds before arriving at the surface where the black-green broad-leafed plants press themselves flat against the stone. These plants — the ones in the deepest shadows, the ones that should not exist — are the most honest residents of The Gash, because they have abandoned nearly everything that a plant is supposed to be. They have no stems. They have no height. They are laminar: two-dimensional organisms, a sheet of photosynthetic tissue no thicker than a coin, adhering to rock through a secretion that is slightly adhesive and faintly sweet, and if you press your finger to one and lift it away, you will feel the tack of it, and smell something that is almost but not quite the smell of crushed basil.

These laminar plants reproduce by fragmentation. It is the only mechanism available to them in a place with no insects, no wind that carries pollen — the wind in The Gash carries sound and force and cold, but not the delicate cargo of reproductive dust. Instead, pieces of the laminar sheets break away. A settling slab shifts, and a patch of black-green tissue tears free and tumbles downward into the ferns below, and where it lands it adheres, and where it adheres it grows. The gorge is its own seed bank. Every rockfall is a dispersal event. Every tremor of settling stone is a planting.

There are other things living in The Gash, though you would not think to call them alive.

The water in the dark pools — those shallow, bruise-colored pockets under overhangs — is not still. Not exactly. It moves, but so slowly that the motion is less visual than tactile: if you were to place your hand in one (and the cold of it would make you reconsider), you would feel a current, faint and directionless, as though the water were being pulled simultaneously in every direction by competing micro-drainages in the rock. The pools are fed by condensation and by thin trickles that emerge from fracture planes, and they drain — somehow, somewhere — through pores too small to see. The water is mineral-heavy. It tastes of iron and something alkaline, something chalky, and if you were to leave a metal object in one of these pools for a week, you would return to find it coated in a pale crust of calcium carbonate and something else, something with a faintly iridescent sheen that you could not identify.

This mineral soup is, arguably, the most important organism in The Gash. Not the water itself, but what lives in it: a community of bacteria so dense and so ancient that they have stopped being distinguishable from the chemistry of the rock. They are lithotrophs — rock-eaters — and they metabolize the basalt with a slowness that makes the ferns look frantic. They have been here, presumably, longer than anything else with chlorophyll. They were here when the fracture was young enough to be hot, and they adapted to the cooling, and they have been patiently dissolving the walls of The Gash from the inside out, millimeter by millimeter, for centuries that you cannot count because they leave no fossil record, no structure, no evidence beyond a faint alteration in the color of the rock at the waterline — a band of paler stone, almost bleached, that marks the boundary between wet and merely damp. If the basalt is honest about itself, these bacteria are its most intimate readers. They know its composition molecule by molecule. They are dismantling it with the same patience that the ferns are colonizing it, and the moss is drinking from it, and the laminar sheets are gluing themselves to it, and the wind is hammering at it, and the water is working its way into every crack. The Gash is not merely inhabited. It is being consumed, slowly and from every angle simultaneously, by a coalition of organisms that do not cooperate and do not compete and do not, in any meaningful sense, know about each other. They are simply all doing the same thing — surviving — and the cumulative effect of their survival is a process so large and so slow that it has the quality of geology. The living and the nonliving are performing the same work at the same rate, and the result is The Gash itself: a place that is being unmade and remade by forces that do not distinguish between erosion and growth.

You might wonder about fungi. There should be fungi here. The conditions are perfect — dark, wet, organic matter accumulating in the crevices between fern mats, dead fronds decomposing into a black, fibrous humus that clings to the rock in pockets. And there are fungi. But they are not what you would expect. They are not the visible, shelf-like brackets of a fallen forest, not the delicate mycelial threads of a garden. They are the infrastructure beneath everything else — an invisible network that permeates the humus and the moss and the root systems of the ferns with a thoroughness that makes the word "pervasive" feel inadequate. If you were to take a handful of that black decomposing matter and spread it on a white surface, you would see threads. Thousands of them. White and gossamer-thin and everywhere, binding dead matter to living matter to stone in a web so fine and so total that the concept of "individual organism" stops applying. The fungi in The Gash are not residents. They are the mortar. They hold the garden together — not out of any cooperative instinct but because their growth habit is to fill every available space, and in The Gash every space is available, and nothing is competing with them for it.

And so the living architecture of The Gash is this: bacteria dissolving the stone from below, fungi binding the organic matter to the stone from within, moss drinking the moisture from the stone's surface, ferns prying the stone apart with their roots, laminar sheets gluing themselves to every face the ferns leave exposed, and all of it — every organism, every process — riding atop the slow, arrhythmic cracking of the gorge itself as it continues to settle and widen and deepen. The living things are not separate from the geological process. They are participants in it. The ferns cause fractures that the water enters that the bacteria widen that the ferns then colonize. It is a loop with no beginning and no exit, and it has been running for however long The Gash has existed — decades, perhaps, or a century, or more — and it shows no sign of reaching equilibrium.

What lives in The Gash is a system that has mistaken a catastrophe for a habitat. The fracture in the earth was a violence — something tearing apart under tectonic load, something that the planet did not intend to happen in this place or at this time — and the living things arrived in the aftermath the way living things always do: without ceremony, without permission, and with the absolute, unthinking conviction that this new wound is a place worth occupying. They were right. They are always right. That is the terrible thing about life in a place like this: it works. It has no reason to work, no mechanism you can point to that explains why laminar sheets should thrive in near-darkness on vertical basalt or why moss should evolve a turgor-driven pumping action to harvest condensation in a gorge that has existed for less than a human lifetime — and yet it works. The gorge fills with green. The sound of growth adds itself to the sound of erosion. The place becomes, against all probability, alive.

And it is still becoming. That is the final thing to understand about what lives here. Nothing in The Gash is mature. The oldest fern you can find has a root system that is perhaps two meters deep and a frond span that you could cover with your outstretched arms — young, by the standards of any other forest, but ancient by the standards of this one. The moss has not yet colonized the deepest fracture planes, the ones that are still too hot from the stone's own formation. The laminar sheets have not yet reached the bottom of the gorge, where the darkness is absolute and the water pools are permanent and the bacteria work undisturbed. Everything here is in its first chapter. The garden is new. The factory is running at low capacity. And the gorge — the living gorge, the one that breathes and cracks and reshapes itself with each settling slab and each seasonal shift — is still, after everything, getting deeper.

What lives in The Gash is a question that has not finished asking itself.
