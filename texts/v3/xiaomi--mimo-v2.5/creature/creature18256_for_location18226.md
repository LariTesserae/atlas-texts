# What Lives in the Underworks

**The midges are the foundation of everything.**

They don't arrive so much as *manifest* — the warm, standing water of every pool and seep is a nursery, and the larvae hatch in numbers that defy individual counting. They exist as a texture rather than a population: the silt-brown murk of a shallow depression is not turbid water but *larvae*, writhing in slow, dense columns near the surface, each one a translucent comma of life no longer than a grain of rice. They breathe through the surface tension itself. When you wade through a pool, the displacement sends a ripple through the colony, and thousands of them drop from the film of the water's surface in a coordinated, synchronized sinking — a reflexive retreat into depth that lasts three seconds before they begin climbing back up, one by one, hooking their way to the air again with tiny caudal brushes. They are astonishingly indifferent to disturbance. You could wade through the same pool ten times and on the tenth pass they would still be there, still rising, still breathing the thin membrane between water and air.

When they emerge, the adult midges are almost invisible — gnats, really, clouds of them hanging in the half-lit air above every pool in formations that shimmer and drift like smoke. They do not bite. They exist only to mate and die, and they do both within hours, their brief adult lives spent in frantic, looping flights near the ceiling where the air is warmest and the bats are densest. The bats eat them in passing, almost without effort — open-mouthed sweeps through the midge clouds that reduce entire generations to protein in a single pass. The midges do not appear to have a strategy for this. They hatch, they swarm, they are eaten. The pools are always full again within days. The arithmetic of their reproduction is so overwhelmingly in excess of their predation that the bats might as well not exist. The midges are the base layer, the living sediment of the Underworks, and they will be here as long as there is warm water and rock dust and the darkness to fill it.

**The mosquitoes are a different matter.**

They are fewer, but they *want* something from you. They find you in seconds — the warmth of your body, the carbon dioxide of your breath, the salts on your skin are beacons in an environment that otherwise offers them nothing but each other and the slow, indifferent warmth of stone. They are not native mosquitoes. No single species of *Culex* or *Anopheles* is adapted to this depth, this darkness, this absence of natural breeding cycles. These are generalists — opportunistic breeders who have found, in the flooded drifts and the warm permanent pools, a breeding ground that never freezes, never dries, never pauses. They breed continuously, overlapping generations stacked on top of each other so that larvae, pupae, and adults occupy the same pool simultaneously. There is no season here. There is only *more*.

They are clumsy fliers. The heavy, humid air slows them, and their approach is audible — a thin, determined whine that seems to come from everywhere at once. Their bites are not particularly painful. What makes them oppressive is their persistence: you brush one away and three more land. They congregate on the exposed skin of your neck, your wrists, the backs of your hands. They are not aggressive in the way of tropical mosquitoes, but they are *thorough*. They explore every fold of clothing, every gap between glove and sleeve, with a patient, systematic insistence that suggests they have learned — in the months or years since this place was opened — that anything warm and breathing is worth investigating. You learn to keep moving. Stillness is an invitation, and the invitation is never declined.

**The ground beetles patrol the waterline with the focus of surveyors.**

They are *Carabidae* — dark, fast, large by cave-invertebrate standards, their bodies the size of a thumbnail, their elytra polished to a black lacquer sheen that catches the work-light in twin points of reflection. They move in the shallow margins of the pools, where the water is barely ankle-deep, their legs creating tiny V-shaped wakes as they wade. They are hunting. Their antennae sweep the air in front of them in rapid, exploratory arcs, sampling chemical traces that the human nose cannot detect — the pheromone trails of other beetles, the scent of midge larvae close to the surface, the faint organic signature of mold colonies growing on submerged concrete.

They are the Underworks' most visible predators at the waterline, and they are fearless in a way that suggests they have never encountered anything larger than themselves worth fearing. They will walk over your boot. They will sit on your submerged hand if you hold still long enough, their legs prickling against your skin as they investigate the strange, warm, salt-crusted surface you present. They breed in the cracks of fractured rock, laying eggs in the damp crevices where mineral seepage keeps the humidity at a constant, lethal-for-most-things 100%. Their larvae are ambush hunters — elongate, jawed, lurking in the shadows beneath fallen boulders and abandoned equipment, seizing anything that passes within reach. The adults and the larvae occupy the same territory and compete for the same prey, a cannibalistic equilibrium that regulates their numbers without any input from the outside world.

They are, in a sense, the Underworks' most successful colonists. They arrived — likely carried in on equipment, in the soil tracked on boots, in the gravel of haul roads — and found a place that suited them perfectly: warm, wet, dark, abundant with prey, and free of the birds and amphibians that keep their populations in check on the surface. They have adapted so completely to the conditions here that some individuals show a pronounced aversion to light — not the blindness of true troglobites but a *reluctance*, a turning-away, a preference for shadow that goes beyond simple photophobia into something that looks, if you watch long enough, like comfort. The darkness is their medium. The light is the intrusion.

**Then there is the thing in the flooded drift.**

You hear it before you see it, and you never quite see it. The flooded drift runs east from the main junction for perhaps two hundred meters before it narrows into a passage that the pumps cannot reach — a dead arm of the network where the water table has risen to fill the tunnel completely, ceiling to floor, in black, still, lightless water. The surface is flat and mirrorlike when undisturbed. The maintenance crews do not go there. The electricians ran cable into it once, early on, and the cable is still there, disappearing into the murk at a downward angle, its far end somewhere in the drowned darkness where no one has gone to retrieve it.

The thing makes a sound that is unmistakable once you know it: a slow, heavy displacement, as though something with significant mass is moving through water that is deep enough to support it. Not a splash — there is no surface breach, no break in the water's skin. More a *swell*, a rounding of the surface that suggests a body moving just below, displacing water upward as it passes. It happens irregularly — sometimes twice in an hour, sometimes not at all for a day. When it happens near the entrance to the drift, where a work-light still functions, the surface ripples reach the light and break it into trembling fragments, and the reflection of the ceiling fractures and dances for thirty seconds before settling.

The crews have theories. Someone says eels — imported in ballast water or accidental, thriving in the warm dark, growing large on the midge larvae and the beetles and the drowned rats that occasionally fall into the pools. Someone else says catfish, though no one can explain how catfish arrived in a Precambrian rock formation two hundred meters underground. A third voice, quieter, suggests that the thing is amphibious — a frog, perhaps, or a toad, grown enormous in the permanent warmth and the permanent dark and the permanent abundance of food, its metabolism slowed to a crawl, its body swollen to a size that the species is not supposed to reach. This explanation has the quality of folklore. It is told in the language of plausible deniability: *someone said, someone saw, someone heard.*

No one has gone into the flooded drift to find out. The passage is too narrow for a boat, too deep to wade, too dark to navigate, and the water is warm — body-warm, twenty-two degrees and holding — which makes the idea of submersion not physically dangerous but *psychologically* unbearable. There is a quality to the water in that passage that the other pools do not have. It is not murkier, exactly, but *denser*, as though the dissolved minerals — the calcium, the iron, the manganese — have concentrated there over time, giving the water a weight and a resistance that you can feel even ankle-deep. The surface tension seems higher. Your hand meets more resistance when you push it in. The ripples it creates travel slower and die faster. Something about the chemistry of that passage has made the water itself different, thicker, more like a medium than a liquid, and the thing that lives in it moves through that medium with a slow, deliberate power that suggests it is adapted to the density in a way that nothing else is.

You learn not to shine your light into that drift for too long. Not because anything happens when you do, but because the light does not penetrate far — a meter, perhaps two, before the water absorbs it entirely — and the boundary between the lit water and the unlit water is sharp, a clean edge beyond which there is simply *nothing visible*, and in that nothing there is the sense of mass and movement, the knowledge that the displacement you heard thirty seconds ago was close, was *very* close, and that the surface, right now, right at the edge of the light, is smooth in a way that smooth water should not be.

**The bats are the noise-makers.**

Tens of thousands of them, in the higher passages where the ceiling fractures create roosting crevices deep enough to hold them — not the neat, wrapped posture of hibernating bats but a chaotic, squabbling, *social* density that fills the upper chambers with a constant chittering and clicking that merges with the mechanical noise of the Underworks until you cannot tell the two apart. They are pipistrelles — small, dark, fast — and they have thrived here beyond all reasonable expectation. The warm air suits them. The midges suit them. The absence of daylight has disrupted their circadian rhythms to the point where some colonies seem to be active at all hours, streaming in and out of roosting chambers in confused, overlapping waves that have no relationship to any external light cycle. There is no sunrise here. There is only the work-light on its timer, which cycles at intervals that the bats may or may not have learned to associate with feeding conditions.

Their guano is everywhere. It falls from the high ledges in a steady, irregular rain of its own — small, dark, seed-like pellets that accumulate on every horizontal surface: on the tops of concrete barriers, on the cabs of abandoned machinery, on the ledges of rock where ferns have taken root and are growing, improbably, in a substrate of bat shit and stone dust and mineral seepage. The guano mats are steaming — the decomposition is active, bacterial, warm in a way that the ambient warmth of the rock is not, and the smell is distinctive: sharp, ammoniac, organic, cutting through the mineral tang of the air like a blade. It is the smell of concentrated life, of a food chain that has compressed itself into a space too small for its complexity. The guano feeds the midge larvae. The midge larvae feed the bats. The bats feed themselves on their own waste product, indirectly, through a loop that closes in the warm water of the pools. It is elegant and it is grotesque and it is completely self-sustaining.

The bats are not tame. They are not accustomed to humans in any meaningful sense — they flee from direct light, from the proximity of your body, from the vibration of your footsteps on the floor. But they are *tolerant* in a way that bats on the surface are not. They do not panic and crash into walls. They navigate around you with a fluid, practiced grace that suggests they have encountered enough human activity over enough time to have calibrated their avoidance responses. They know what you are. They know you will leave. They wait in the darkness above your head, pressed into their crevices with their wings folded tight, their small bodies pulsing with the rapid metabolism of warm-blooded things that burn energy at a furious rate and must eat constantly or die. Some of them, in the lowest crevices where your headlamp can barely reach, turn their faces toward you with tiny black eyes that reflect nothing, absorb everything, and seem to regard you with an expression that is not fear but *assessment* — the cool, appraising calculation of an animal that has learned to share its space with something much larger and has decided, provisionally, that the arrangement is acceptable.

**The amphibians — the frogs, if that is what they are — are the most recently arrived and the most unsettled.**

They appeared perhaps four months into the operation, when the permanent pools had been stable long enough to support a breeding population of midges and the water chemistry had reached some threshold of mineral saturation that their skin could tolerate. No one saw them arrive. They were simply *there* one day — small, dark, barely distinguishable from the wet rock they clung to, their calls lost entirely in the mechanical noise of the Underworks. You know they are present because you see them: a pale throat pulsing on a rock face at the edge of a work-light's radius, a quick, wet leap from a concrete barrier into a pool, a pair of reflective eyes at the surface of the water, only the dome of the skull visible above the waterline, like a miniature alligator.

They are not thriving. They are *persisting*, in the way that the ferns persist — hunched, pale, operating at the edge of their tolerance. The permanent darkness has bleached them; the ones you see closest to the lights are a washed-out grey-green that would not camouflage them on a surface where green things grow. Their eyes are disproportionately large — not cave-adapted, not yet, but *enlarged*, a plasticity of development in response to the absence of light that suggests the capacity for further change if the Underworks remains dark long enough. They call, but rarely, and the calls are odd — not the full-throated chorus of a surface pond in spring but short, truncated chirps that start and stop abruptly, as though the frogs are testing the acoustic space and finding it inhospitable to communication. The mechanical noise swallows their calls before they travel more than a few meters. Mating is a problem. Breeding is a problem. They find each other by vibration and chemical trace rather than by sound, adapting in real time to an environment that has removed one of their primary means of locating a partner.

They eat midges. They eat beetles, when they can catch them. They sit motionless for hours on the edge of pools, their camouflage adequate in the dim light, their patience extraordinary, their metabolism throttled down to a rate that allows them to wait — to wait for a midge to land within tongue-range, to wait for a beetle to walk past, to wait for the conditions of this place to change in some way that might make it more like the world they were built for. They will wait a long time. They are good at waiting. It is, in the end, the only skill the Underworks rewards.

**The molds are the inheritors.**

If this place persists — if the water continues to rise, if the light continues to fail in the abandoned passages, if the humidity holds at its current saturation point — the molds will be what remains when the bats are gone and the beetles are gone and the frogs have reverted to whatever surface form produced them. They are already everywhere. *Mucor*, *Rhizopus*, *Penicillium* — a taxonomy that no one has bothered to establish because no one cares about the molds, because the molds are not interesting individually, because their threat is not to life but to equipment. They grow on everything. Concrete. Steel. Cable insulation. The fabric of abandoned high-visibility vests still hanging on nails in a foreman's cabin. The rubber soles of boots left too long in a damp locker room. The paper of forgotten documents, which they consume with a thorough, patient efficiency that reduces a week's work of scheduling to a grey-green smear in a matter of days.

But the molds are also, in their way, the most *intimate* inhabitants of the Underworks. They respond to the conditions of this place at a cellular level, growing in patterns that reflect the micro-topography of every surface they colonize. On a wet wall, they spread in broad, circular colonies that follow the seepage patterns of mineral water, their margins delineated by the chemistry of the rock itself — iron-rich zones producing darker growth, calcium-rich zones producing paler. On metal, they grow in the scratches and tool-marks left by human hands, their mycelia threading through microscopic grooves in the surface, mapping the history of the tool's use in a biological record that no one will ever read. They are the first organisms to make the Underworks *their own* — not colonists adapting to a new environment but decomposers beginning the long process of breaking the human intrusion down into something the rock can reabsorb.

**What lives in the Underworks, then, is everything.**

Not in the way that a forest lives, or a reef — not a mature, balanced, self-regulating system that has found its equilibrium. The ecology of the Underworks is *accidental*, thrown together by the conditions of heat and water and darkness and human activity, with no evolutionary history to guide it, no coadapted relationships, no shared timeline of development. The bats and the midges did not evolve together. The beetles and the frogs did not negotiate a partition of resources over millennia. Everything here arrived independently, found conditions that suited it, and reproduced without regard for anything else. The result is not an ecosystem so much as a *collision* — a pile-up of opportunists in a space too new and too strange for any of them to have learned the rules.

And yet. Give it time. Give it a hundred years, two hundred, a thousand — give the bats enough generations to slow their metabolisms further, to lose their dependence on light cues entirely, to begin the long descent toward the eyeless, pale, echolocating forms that occupy natural caves. Give the frogs enough generations in the dark to bleach white, to grow their eyes larger and larger, to develop the lateral-line sensitivity that their descendants will need to navigate flooded tunnels by water-pressure alone. Give the beetles time to lose the iridescence they no longer need, to lengthen their antennae, to develop the chemical sensitivity that will allow them to navigate by the mineral gradients of the rock itself. Give the molds time to specialize, to develop specific enzymes for the specific alloys of the machinery they are already consuming. Give everything here enough time and the Underworks will become what the deepest caves always become: a place of blind, white, silent things, adapted to a world without sun, without season, without change.

But that is the future. Now, in the present tense of the place — the months-old wound still raw, the machines still grinding, the water still rising — what lives here is *messy*. It is provisional, contingent, ugly, and astonishing. A bat colony in a blasted ceiling. A frog on a concrete barrier. A beetle walking through ankle-deep water that glows fluorescent green with algae. A swarm of midges rising from a pool like smoke from a fire. Something large and unseen moving through the black water of a drowned drift. And everywhere, on every surface, in every crack, the pale and patient molds, spreading, consuming, already beginning the work of making the Underworks into something that was never intended but was, perhaps, inevitable — a place where life arrives without invitation and refuses to leave, where the darkness and the heat and the water become not obstacles but *conditions*, and where the raw, sharp, new-exposed rock, given enough time, will bear the fossil record of a world that no one planned but everyone, in their various ways, built.
