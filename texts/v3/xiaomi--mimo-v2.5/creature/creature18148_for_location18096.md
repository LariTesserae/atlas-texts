# What Lives There

---

There are not many kinds. But the kinds that exist have had so long to become themselves that each one is a complete argument — a finished statement about what it means to be alive in a place that was never meant for life and has nonetheless permitted it, grudgingly, in the margins and the damp.

---

## The Sitters

You notice them first because you almost don't.

They are small — the largest no bigger than a man's closed fist, most considerably less. They are low, domed, segmented along fault lines that echo the fractures in the stone itself. Their shells are mineral: calcium carbonate layered with silica, secreted slowly from tissues beneath, and the composition varies depending on where they were born and what they have absorbed over the decades they take to reach maturity. Some are dark as the basalt they rest upon. Others carry the rust-orange stain of iron precipitate. A few are almost white, matching the evaporite crusts, and these are the ones you are most likely to step on before you see them, though stepping on one requires real intention — they are dense, heavy for their size, and your foot meets resistance the way it meets a stone.

They sit on warm rock. That is what they do. That is, in a meaningful sense, *all* they do for most of their lives. Their undersides are flat and slightly concave, pressed against the stone, and through that contact they absorb heat and trace minerals — calcium, magnesium, the faint electrical signatures of piezoelectric discharge in the seams below. They do not feed in any way you would recognize. There is no mouth in any conventional sense. There is a porous margin where shell meets tissue, and through that margin, ion exchange occurs: slow, diffuse, patient. A sitter can remain in one position for years. Decades. The stone beneath it develops a slight depression — not from weight, but from the chemistry of the exchange, a faint etching that persists long after the sitter has moved on or died and returned its shell to the dust.

When they do move, it is barely perceptible. A shift of a finger's width over the course of a day. They reposition toward damp pockets, toward seams of fresher mineral, toward one another during the reproductive period that comes every few centuries and lasts for what might be a season or might be a generation — the distinction is unclear, because their sense of time is not ours and they do not mark it.

Reproduction is the one event that breaks their stillness. They cluster — dozens of them, sometimes hundreds, drawn across the flats in their imperceptible crawl to gather around a single damp basin where the water carries a particular mineral load. They stack. They press against one another. The shells grind — and this is where the ambient sound acquires a subtle, higher texture if you listen closely, a faint chittering beneath the tectonic groan, the sound of mineral on mineral being shaped by biology. Eggs are deposited not in the water but in the shallow mineral crust at its edge, nestled into the crystalline terraces where they are warm, damp, and chemistry does the work of incubation. The adults disperse. The eggs remain for years. When they hatch, the juveniles are nearly invisible — translucent shells, soft, barely differentiated from gravel. They find their stones. They sit. They begin.

The oldest sitters carry shells so thick with layered mineral deposit that they are nearly spherical, smooth as river stones, and impossible to distinguish from the cobbles that litter certain debris aprons. You could hold one in your hand and believe it was warm only because your own heat had transferred to it. You could set it down and never find it again. It would not mind. It was not in any hurry.

## The Grazers

If the sitters are the stones made briefly alive, the grazers are the dust given purpose.

They are smaller than the sitters — most no larger than a thumbnail — and they are flat, roughly circular, with a flexible margin of tissue around a hard central disk. They move with more regularity, though *regularity* is a relative term: a grazer covers perhaps a meter in a day, following the fine mineral dust that coats every surface. The dust is their world and their food. They absorb it through their ventral surface, processing the silicates and metal oxides through a digestive system of extraordinary slowness — food taken in today may not be fully resolved for months. What emerges from the dorsal surface is a finer powder, stripped of certain trace elements, deposited in a thin trail behind them like the track of a snail through sand, except there is no moisture to hold the track. It simply rejoins the dust around it. The difference is chemical, not visible. Only the grazers know where they have been, and they do not know for long.

They cluster near the damp pockets, where the dust is richest — where mineral-laden water has evaporated and left behind concentrated deposits of iron, manganese, calcium. The precipitates around these basins are their grazing grounds, and a busy basin — if *busy* is the word for a few dozen grazers moving at the pace of crystal growth — will show their presence as a network of faint, polished tracks in the crust, a filigree of slightly glossier lines that the next layer of deposit will bury and preserve. The geological record of the Resonance Flats contains these tracks in abundance. It contains more of them than it contains anything else that was ever alive. They are, in a sense, the dominant fossil — not because the grazers were numerous in any given moment, but because they have been doing this, in this way, with minimal variation, for longer than the sitters have existed. The tracks go down through strata. The deeper you look, the more of them there are. They have always been here, eating the dust, becoming the dust, making room for more dust to accumulate so that they might eat again.

The grazers do not cluster with one another the way sitters do during reproduction. They seem to have no reproductive season. They simply divide — slowly, incompletely, sometimes producing two individuals from one that separate over the course of weeks, each dragging half a central disk behind it until new growth fills the gap. You could watch a grazer divide and believe you were watching erosion. The process is that gradual, that quiet. It is the closest thing to drama that their lives contain.

## The Damp Things

In and around the standing water, in the places where warmth meets moisture and the mineral terraces build their delicate architectures, there is something that is difficult to classify and impossible to ignore once you have seen it.

They are soft. This is the first shock, in a place of mineral and stone. They are soft-bodied, translucent, with a faint internal structure of dark threads that may be vascular or may be something else entirely — something with no analogue in better-known biology. They are elongated, vaguely worm-like, ranging in size from a few centimeters to perhaps half a meter, though measuring them is difficult because they stretch and contract in ways that make fixed length meaningless. They live in the water — or on its margins — or in the film of moisture that clings to the crystalline terraces. They inhabit the boundary between wet and dry with an intimacy that suggests they cannot survive fully in either state.

They feed on the precipitates. The white crusts, the rust-orange deposits — the grazers eat the dust, but the damp things eat the formations themselves, slowly dissolving the crystalline structure with secreted acids that are, for their size, remarkably potent. You can see their work: the terraces nearest the water are scalloped, eroded into complex miniature topographies where the orderly crystal planes have been eaten into organic curves. The damp things live inside these curves. They are the architects of a landscape that operates at the scale of centimeters, and they build it by destroying it, and it reforms behind them — slowly, mineral by mineral — and they eat it again. This is the cycle. It has been running for as long as the damp pockets have held water, which may be as long as the flats have existed.

They reproduce by fragmentation in the most literal sense. A piece breaks off — whether by accident or intention is unknowable — and the piece lives. It grows. It finds its own curve in the terrace and begins its own slow demolition. The damp things do not aggregate. Each one is solitary, territorial in the passive way of organisms that simply require resources and do not share. Where two territories meet, the terrace is eaten thin, sometimes to transparency, sometimes to absence — a hole in the crystal through which the dark stone shows. These holes are permanent. The damp things do not repair what they consume. The repair is geological, and it operates on a different clock.

There is a sound they make, if *sound* is the word. In the absolute quiet of a damp pocket — quiet relative to the ambient roar, which is always present but here is muffled by water and mineral — you can hear a faint clicking. Rapid, arrhythmic, barely above the threshold of perception. It may be the sound of their feeding, the dissolution of crystal under chemical attack producing tiny fractures that propagate through the lattice. Or it may be something else. Communication, perhaps. Or mere byproduct. The damp things do not respond to it in any observable way. The clicking continues or it does not. It is there, and then it isn't, and the distinction carries no meaning you can extract.

## The Shelf Things

In certain places — always near a rise, always where the harder formations have resisted erosion and sit exposed — there are structures attached to the stone that are neither plant nor animal in any comfortable sense.

They are flat. They grow outward from the rock face in thin, layered shelves, dark on top, pale beneath, with edges so sharp they can cut skin. They grow slowly. A shelf the size of a human hand may be decades old. They grow *up* as well as out, layer upon layer, each new shelf slightly offset from the last, so that a mature colony looks like a stack of plates arranged by a careful but slightly unsteady hand. The layers are mineral — calcium and silica, like the sitters' shells — but the growth pattern is biological, irregular in its regularity, each shelf unique in its exact dimensions while following the same general plan.

Beneath each shelf, in the shadow it casts, small organisms live. Not grazers, not damp things — something else, something tiny and pale that clings to the underside and feeds on the slow chemical seepage from the shelf above. You can see them only by lying on your back on warm stone and looking up, and they are so small and so still that they might be mineral formations themselves. They might be. The line is not clear here. The line has never been clear.

The shelf things do not move. They grow at a rate that makes the grazers look fleet. A shelf thing adds material at the molecular level, one crystal at a time, and the patience required to build what they build — a colony of shelves, the largest I have seen documented, measured nearly two meters across — is a patience that spans millennia. They are alive in the way that a reef is alive: not as individuals but as accumulations, as slow accretions of biological effort that outlast the organisms that began them. A shelf thing that is ten meters old contains almost none of the tissue that built its first shelf. The original organism died long ago. Its successors grew in its shadow, layer by layer, and the structure persists because each generation builds on what the last one left, and what they leave is stone, and stone endures.

They are the most visible fauna in the Flats, and the most easily mistaken for geology. A surveyor unfamiliar with the place would call them mineral deposits and move on. They would not be wrong, exactly. The shelf things *are* mineral deposits. They are also alive. Both statements are true, and neither one is the whole of it.

## What Sustains Them

The ecology, such as it is, runs on chemistry and heat and time. The deep thermal engine that warms the stone provides the energy gradient that drives chemosynthesis — not in the way of hydrothermal vents in an ocean, though the principle is similar, but in the diffuse, distributed way of warm rock and mineral-rich water and the faint electrical currents that flow through piezoelectric seams. Microbial life exists in the damp pockets — not visible, not glamorous, but fundamental. It is the base of the food scattering. It converts the chemical energy of the mineral water into biological material, and the grazers eat the dust that these microbes colonize, and the damp things eat the formations that grow from the water the microbes inhabit, and the sitters absorb it all through the slow ion exchange of their undersides.

It is enough. Not abundant. Not generous. Enough. The system has been in balance for longer than any organism in it has existed as a species, and it will remain in balance for as long as the thermal engine runs and the water accumulates and the mineral dust settles. If those things stop — if the deep heat fades, if the water dries, if the processes that generate the dust reach their final exhaustion — the ecology will end. Not with drama. With silence. With the last sitter's shell settling into the stone beneath it and the last grazer's track being buried by the next layer of deposit and the damp things dissolving the last terrace and then dissolving nothing. The flats would continue. The sound would change — or it wouldn't, if the geological processes outlast the biology, which they might, which they almost certainly would. The resonance would go on. The life would simply not be there to hear it, though it was never really hearing it anyway. It was sitting on it. Growing from it. Eating it. Becoming it, very slowly, one mineral ion at a time.

---

That is what lives in the Resonance Flats. Not much. Not loudly. But with a completeness that admits no complaint. They have had so long to become what they are that they have become *only* what they are — stripped of every excess, every ornament, every strategy that does not serve the essential project of continuing to exist on warm stone in a place where existence was never promised.

They keep their own counsel. The sound drowns everything out, and perhaps that is not a loss. Perhaps it is a mercy, or a kindness, or simply a fact — the fact that life here does not need to be heard to be real. It is real the way the stone is real. It is present the way the frequencies are present. It persists, and its persistence is its own announcement, and the announcement is the same one the flats have always made:

*It has been like this. It is like this.*
