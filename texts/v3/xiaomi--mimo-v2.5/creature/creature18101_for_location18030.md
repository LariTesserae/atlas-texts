# What Lives in the Veinwork

---

## The Veinwork Itself

Begin with the largest living thing, because everything else is a passenger.

The Veinwork does not think. This is important. It has the *architecture* of thought — branching, recursive, layered with feedback loops and pressure gradients and fluid dynamics that mirror, at a crude chemical level, something resembling signal transmission — but it does not think. It does not dream. It does not watch you with something that could be called intent.

What it does is **persist**.

It has persisted for longer than the creatures living inside it can conceptualize. Its memory, if that word applies at all, is stored in the thickness of its walls — in the layered rings of growth that accumulated over epochs, in the mineral deposits left behind by ancient watercourses now sealed within its tissue like fossils in amber. The outermost layers of its walls contain evidence of climate shifts that happened so slowly that each individual transition was invisible to anything alive during it: a century of slightly more water, a millennium of slightly less, a ten-thousand-year interval where the membrane overhead thickened by a centimeter and dimmed the light below by a fraction of a percent. All of this is in the walls. None of it is remembered.

Its surface — and surface is the word, because the Veinwork is a surface, a boundary between interior spaces and the vastness of whatever lies beyond its outermost membrane — is colonized at every scale. Bacteria-analogues inhabit the water films. Fungal analogues thread through the deeper tissue, breaking down dead cells and recycling their chemistry. Tiny arthropods graze on the outermost cell layers in numbers so enormous and so constant that their feeding constitutes a kind of pruning, keeping the surface smooth, preventing any one growth from outpacing the equilibrium. These tiny grazers are the Veinwork's most intimate companions. They have lived on it, in it, of it, for longer than the dweller-people have had bones.

The Veinwork breathes. Not lungs — there are no lungs — but a slow, tidal exchange of gases through its membranes, driven by the same thermal gradients that produce condensation. The amber light above shifts almost imperceptibly over hours: warmer at the peaks of some invisible cycle, cooler at its troughs, as though the Veinwork is respiring against the light source far above it. Whether the light source responds to this breathing or the Veinwork breathes in response to the light — whether there is a difference — is a question that no one inside it is equipped to answer.

Its roots, far below the floor of the highland chambers, reach down into something that the dwellers call, in their own language, *the deep warmth* — a region of rock and water and heat that exists at the boundary between the Veinwork's living tissue and the geological substrate of the planet itself. The roots do not grip stone. They merge with it, infiltrating cracks, following veins of mineral-rich water, extracting chemistry from the planet's interior with a patience that makes continental drift look hasty. The nutrients rise through capillary networks so vast and so redundant that if you severed one — if you cut a channel as wide as a hallway clean through the living wall — the fluid simply rerouted, finding other paths, taking hours perhaps, or days, but arriving eventually, because the network was designed by nothing and optimized by everything, and redundancy is the only engineering principle that matters on geological timescales.

## The Fauna of the Walls

Press your palm flat against the interior surface of a major chamber wall and hold it there. Thirty seconds. A minute. The warmth is constant — blood-warm, almost — and the texture beneath your hand is faintly ridged, like the whorls of a fingerprint magnified a hundred times. These ridges are the outermost layer of the Veinwork's epidermis, and they are not empty.

**The grazer-mites** are the first to find you.

They are small — two to four millimeters — and they are everywhere, and they are almost impossible to see because they are the same amber-translucent color as the tissue they live on. They have six legs. They have mouthparts designed to rasp away the thinnest, outermost cell layers of the Veinwork's skin, consuming the dead and dying tissue, keeping the surface clean, preventing the buildup of any organic film that might block the Veinwork's gas exchange. They are, in effect, the Veinwork's immune system, or its hygiene, or its grooming instinct made manifest in chitin and legs.

They will walk across your hand if you hold still. They will not bite you. You are not food. You are not the Veinwork. You are merely a temporary irregularity in their landscape, and they will investigate you with the same dispassionate thoroughness they apply to any new surface — a few antenna flicks, a brief pause, a decision that you are neither food nor threat, and then they move on, returning to their grazing, their tiny mandibles rasping with a sound so faint it is more vibration than noise, felt through your fingertip rather than heard through your ear.

Leave your hand there longer and other things arrive. **Thread-worms** — nematodes of extraordinary thinness, translucent, barely visible — emerge from the pores of the Veinwork's skin, riding a film of moisture, seeking new tissue to colonize. They burrow back in within minutes, disappearing into channels so narrow that a single human hair could not follow them. They are parasites in only the loosest sense: they feed on the Veinwork's outer tissue, but in quantities so negligible that the Veinwork's growth more than compensates. They are part of the equilibrium. Remove them and something else — some fungal analogue, some bacterial bloom — would rush in to fill the niche they occupied, and the replacement would almost certainly be less benign.

**The cling-things** are larger and more visible and more various. They occupy every surface that is not submerged: the walls, the ceilings, the undersides of vascular ridges, the vertical faces of chamber entrances, the inner surfaces of membranes. They are the Veinwork's most conspicuous fauna, and the dwellers know them all by name.

Some are flat. Remarkably flat. **Vein-scales**, the dwellers call them — organisms no thicker than a coin, as wide as a dinner plate, with a body so perfectly adapted to life on a vertical surface that they seem less like animals and more like barnacles, or growths, or patterns in the tissue itself. They cling with a combination of adhesive secretions and micro-textured footpads that grip the ridges of the Veinwork's epidermis. They move, but slowly — a centimeter per minute, perhaps two — grazing on the same thin outer tissue that the mites consume, but in deeper layers, scraping down to the vascular tissue beneath and feeding on the sugar-rich fluid that seeps from minor capillary breaks. They leave trails behind them: faint grooves in the Veinwork's surface that heal within days, new cells filling in, the wall restoring itself with the same patient persistence it applies to everything.

Some cling-things are **long**. The dwellers call them *reachers* — organisms with elongated, segmented bodies, a centimeter wide but half a meter long, or a meter long, or (in the deepest, oldest chambers, where the walls are thickest and the food is most abundant) two meters long. They look like worms, but they are not worms. They are arthropods — distant relatives of the mites, shrunk in width and stretched in length by millions of years of adaptation to life in the narrow spaces between the Veinwork's layers. Reachers inhabit the interior of the walls themselves, threading through channels that are natural to the architecture, feeding on the fungal analogues that decompose dead tissue deep within the plant-matter. They are never seen in the open chambers. They are felt: a faint vibration through the wall if you press your ear against it, a rhythmic pulse that is not the Veinwork's own capillary flow but the slow passage of a reacher's body through a channel just centimeters deep to the surface. The dwellers consider their presence in a wall to be a sign of health — an indication that the tissue is alive, that the decomposition cycle is functioning, that the wall is neither dying nor so vibrantly healthy that its dead tissue is accumulating unprocessed.

And there are **the crouchers**.

These are the predators. They are not fast. In the Veinwork, nothing is fast except water falling from a height, and even that is slow. The crouchers are ambush predators adapted to a world without sound and without reliable light: they wait. They cling to the walls in positions that give them access to the trails left by grazer-mites and vein-scales, and they wait. Their bodies are compact — a few centimeters across, low-profile, color-matched to the tissue they rest on. They have legs that can lock into position and hold for hours or days without metabolic cost. They have sensory organs that detect the chemical traces left by grazing organisms — the faint scent of ruptured plant cells, the sugar weeping from capillary breaks — and they move toward these traces with a slowness that is almost geological, repositioning themselves over the course of a day to align with the trail of a vein-scale that is itself moving at a centimeter per minute. When the prey reaches them, the croucher strikes: a pair of modified forelegs, fast by the Veinwork's standards — a fraction of a second — seizing the prey and holding it against the mouthparts, which penetrate the exoskeleton and extract fluids. The entire predation event is silent and takes place over minutes, and is invisible unless you are watching the exact square centimeter of wall where it occurs.

The dwellers do not fear the crouchers. They are too small to be dangerous to a human, and they are too slow to be startling. But they are respected, in the way that the dwellers respect everything: as a component of the system that keeps them alive.

## The Aerial Fauna

In the open chambers — the great vaults where the amber light filters down through meters of living membrane and the air is warm and wet and still — things fly.

Not birds. There are no birds in the Veinwork. The acoustic silence of this place made vocalization an evolutionary dead end: why develop a complex voice when sound travels three meters and dies? The aerial fauna communicates, if it communicates at all, through chemical signals and body posture and the slow shifting of translucent tissues against the dim light.

**The drifters** are the most common. They range in size from a human fist to a wingspan wider than a person is tall, and they share a body plan that is startlingly convergent with the manta rays of Earth's oceans — though they are not related to anything in the ocean and have never seen an ocean. Their bodies are flat, flexible, and thin enough to be translucent. Their "wings" are membrane-wings, stretched over a framework of stiffened vascular fibers that the organism synthesizes from the Veinwork's own chemistry, harvesting traces of cellulose-analogue from the saturated air, from contact with the walls, from the water films. They ride thermals — slow, warm updrafts generated by the temperature differential between the Veinwork's living tissue and the cooler air in the deeper recesses of the architecture — and they glide with an efficiency that makes powered flight unnecessary. They barely flap. They turn by canting a wing. They descend by stalling, slowly, like leaves falling in syrup.

They feed on the condensation itself. Their membrane-wings are covered in microscopic cilia that comb water from the air as they fly, channeling it along vascular grooves to a central absorption organ — a spongy tissue in the ventral body wall that extracts dissolved nutrients from the water and passes waste back out through the wing surfaces. They are, in essence, flying filters. They eat humidity. They drink the air.

They are not afraid of the dwellers. They have no reason to be. The dwellers do not hunt them — they are too thin to provide meaningful nutrition, too high in the vaults to be easily reached, and their bodies are mostly water and vascular fiber, offering no bone for tools, no hide for clothing. The dwellers watch them sometimes, in the amber dimness, as they pass overhead in slow, silent formation — groups of three or seven, always odd numbers, a pattern no dweller has explained — and the sight is one of the few things in the Veinwork that the dwellers will stop working to observe.

**The glint-wings** are rarer. They are smaller than the drifters — never more than thirty centimeters across — and their membranes are not translucent but reflective, coated with a thin crystalline film that catches the dim amber light and throws it back. In the perpetual twilight of the Veinwork, a glint-wing in flight is a brief, startling flash of light: a spark that moves, a gleam that traverses the vault ceiling like a slow-moving star. They are solitary. They do not form groups. They feed on the grazer-mites — swooping close to the wall surfaces, snatching mites from the epidermis with a pair of delicate, precise forelimbs — and their reflective coating serves a dual purpose: it dazzles potential predators (though in the dim light of the Veinwork, "dazzle" is a relative term) and it regulates body temperature, reflecting excess heat during the warmer phases of the Veinwork's thermal cycle and absorbing it during the cooler phases.

The dwellers call them *sparks*. They are considered good fortune — not in a superstitious way, but in a practical one. A glint-wing hunting near a chamber means the grazer-mite population in that chamber is healthy enough to support a predator. It is a sign of equilibrium, of a wall that is alive and functioning and safe to harvest.

## The Wandering Fauna

On the floor of the chambers — on the warm, damp, yielding epidermis that serves as the ground — things move.

They move **slowly**. Everything in the Veinwork moves slowly, except the water, and even the water is patient about it. But the floor-dwelling fauna has an additional constraint: the floor itself is alive, and it responds to pressure. Not consciously — the Veinwork does not think — but mechanically, physically. The floor yields under weight. A creature that moves too quickly, that impacts the surface with too much force, risks sinking — not far, not dangerously, but enough to be inefficient, enough to cost energy that the Veinwork's calorie-poor ecosystem cannot afford to waste. So the floor-dwellers have evolved to distribute their weight: wide feet, multiple points of contact, slow deliberate gaits that minimize the pressure per square centimeter.

**The peelers** are the most common floor-dwellers among the smaller fauna. They are beetle-like, six-legged, as wide as a human hand, with bodies flattened and low to the ground. They feed on the dead tissue that accumulates in the hollows and low points of the floor — the cells that the Veinwork sheds and does not reabsorb, the organic detritus that the water films carry downhill and deposit in basins and channels. They are decomposers, recyclers, the floor-dwelling equivalent of the mites on the walls. They are so abundant that their trails — faint, glistening paths worn into the surface tissue by the passage of thousands of bodies over years — are visible from a distance, crisscrossing the floor of a major chamber like the streets of a city seen from above.

Among the larger fauna, the **slope-walkers** are the most frequently encountered. They are the size of large dogs, though they bear no resemblance to dogs. Their bodies are cylindrical, segmented, low to the ground, supported by a dozen short, sturdy legs arranged along both sides like the legs of a millipede. Their heads — if the blunt, sensory-rich anterior end of the animal can be called a head — bear no eyes. They navigate by chemoreception and vibration-sense, tasting the moisture on the floor, feeling the faint capillary pulse of the Veinwork's tissue through their feet. They graze on the fungal analogues that colonize the deeper layers of the floor tissue, excavating shallow pits with specialized mandibles, feeding for hours on a single patch before moving on, the pit behind them filling with condensation and becoming a small pool that the Veinwork will eventually reabsorb.

The dwellers have a complex relationship with the slope-walkers. They are not hunted — they are too large, too valuable as indicators of floor health, and their flesh, while not poisonous, is apparently unpalatable (the dwellers say it tastes of the Veinwork itself, which is to say it tastes of a thing so fundamentally *other* that the human palate rejects it). But they are used: their tracks indicate which floor tissue is healthy enough to support weight, their feeding-pits indicate where fungal growth is abundant (and thus where the wall tissue nearby is rich in nutrients), and their presence in a chamber is the single most reliable indicator that the chamber's ecological balance is intact. A chamber without slope-walkers is a chamber in trouble — a chamber where something has gone wrong with the water, or the light, or the nutrient flow, and the dwellers avoid such places until the slope-walkers return.

## The Large Fauna

And then there are **the large ones**.

The dwellers do not have a single word for them. They have several words, each denoting a different category of behavior or perceived danger, and none of these words translate cleanly into a language that presupposes fixed geometry. The closest approximation — the one a dweller might offer if pressed, speaking slowly, gesturing with flat hands to indicate vastness — is something like *the weather-that-moves*.

They are not weather. They are animals. They have bodies — or they have something that functions as a body, that occupies space and has mass and moves through the chambers with a slow, undeniable *presence*. But their bodies are not consistent. This is not a statement about camouflage or shape-shifting in any biological sense. It is a statement about geometry.

A large fauna specimen observed in a major vault will occupy a position — the left side of the vault, say, at a distance of approximately two hundred meters. It will be visible as a vast, dim shape: a mound of tissue and shadow, faintly luminescent with bioluminescent symbionts, its surface texture indistinguishable from the Veinwork's own walls, as though it were a piece of the architecture that had detached and begun to move. Watching it for an hour, you will see it shift position: a slow, lateral translation across the vault, covering perhaps ten meters in that time. It does not walk. It does not swim. It *flows*. Its movement resembles the movement of the Veinwork's own fluid systems — a gradual redistribution of mass from one region to another, with no visible means of locomotion.

And then, without having crossed the intervening space, it is on the right side of the vault.

Not quickly. Not in a single observable moment. You look away — to adjust your footing, to wipe condensation from your eyes — and when you look back, it is on the right. The distance it would have needed to travel is great. The time available was small. The vault between the two positions is empty — you can see it, the vast amber-lit space, the dim far wall, the slow drift of a drifter passing through — and the large fauna was not in that space at any point you observed it. It was simply *here*, and now it is *there*, and the intervening space was never occupied.

The dwellers treat this with the same calm practicality they apply to everything else. A large fauna specimen that has relocated to the right side of the vault means that the right side of the vault is now a zone of elevated risk, and the dwellers adjust their movements accordingly. They do not speculate about how or why the relocation occurred. They note the fact, update their mental map, and continue with their work.

What is known — what the dwellers have learned over generations of careful, terrified, pragmatic observation — is that **direct sustained attention** is dangerous. A brief glance, a passing observation, is harmless. The large fauna does not respond to casual notice. But focused, deliberate, prolonged observation — staring at a large fauna specimen for minutes, tracking it, trying to understand its movement — appears to *cause* it to respond. The nature of the response is not consistent. Sometimes the specimen moves toward the observer, flowing across the vault with a speed that it did not previously display, filling the space where the observer stood moments before. Sometimes the specimen *multiplies*: a single shape becomes two, or three, occupying positions that are geometrically impossible, surrounding the observer in a configuration that should not exist in three-dimensional space. Sometimes the observer's perception of the vault itself changes — the walls seem closer, the chamber seems smaller, the membrane behind the observer seems thinner — as though the act of observation has reconfigured the local geometry to the observer's disadvantage.

No dweller has been killed by a large fauna specimen. This may be coincidence. It may be that the large fauna does not kill — that its responses are not predatory but reactive, the way a sea creature responds to a disturbance in the water. It may be that the dwellers have simply been careful enough, across enough generations, to avoid the situations that would result in a death. They do not take chances. They do not test the boundaries. They have names for the geometric configurations that are safe for observing large fauna — specific distances, specific angles, specific durations of attention — and they adhere to these configurations with a discipline that would be called superstitious if it were not so consistently effective.

## The Deep Fauna

In the water — in the films and rivulets and vascular basins and the slow percolation that moves through the Veinwork's capillary networks — there are **worms**.

They are the most abundant macroscopic organisms in the Veinwork. There are more of them than all other fauna combined, possibly by several orders of magnitude. They are pale — translucent white, or faintly amber — and they range in size from threads thinner than a human hair to specimens as thick as a finger and a meter long. They are blind. They have no eyes, no light-sensing organs of any kind. They navigate by chemical gradient and flow-sense, detecting the direction and velocity of water movement through the channels they inhabit and adjusting their position accordingly.

They feed on the bacterial films that coat the interior surfaces of the Veinwork's water channels. They are grazers, in the same ecological role as the mites on the wall surfaces and the peelers on the floor — processing dead organic material, recycling nutrients, maintaining the equilibrium of the system. They are so abundant and so constantly present that they are a structural feature of the Veinwork's water system: their bodies, moving through the channels, create micro-turbulences that prevent the channels from clogging with sediment. They are, in a sense, living components of the plumbing.

The dwellers know they are there. They can see them — faint dark lines moving beneath the translucent skin of the walls, visible in the dim amber light like veins beneath skin. (The metaphor is uncomfortably apt. Everything in the Veinwork is uncomfortably apt, if your frame of reference is the human body: the walls are skin, the water is blood, the capillaries are veins, the chambers are organs. The coincidence is not a coincidence. It is a convergence of design principles: the same physics of fluid transport, the same mathematics of branching networks, the same evolutionary pressures that favor redundancy and efficiency. The Veinwork is not a body. But it is built by the same rules that build bodies, and so it looks like one.)

The dwellers do not eat the worms. Not because they are inedible — some dwellers have tried, in times of hardship — but because the worms are too small, too numerous, too labor-intensive to harvest in meaningful quantities, and because the dwellers have a cultural understanding, codified into their oldest oral traditions, that the water-system is not to be disturbed. The worms are part of the water. The water is part of the Veinwork. To harvest the worms is to disturb the water, and to disturb the water is to disturb the Veinwork, and to disturb the Veinwork — even slightly, even in one small channel in one small chamber — is to introduce an imbalance that will propagate in ways that cannot be predicted or controlled.

This understanding is not scientific. It is experiential. It is the accumulated wisdom of a people who have lived inside a living system for long enough to have learned, through painful trial and occasionally catastrophic error, that the system's balance is not a luxury but a necessity, and that their own survival is embedded within it like the worms in the water, like the mites on the walls, like the drifters in the vaults.

## The Dwellers

They are human. Completely, unremarkably, human.

Their skin is pale — not from lack of sunlight, exactly, but from the quality of the light: amber, dim, filtered through so many layers of living tissue that it carries almost none of the wavelengths that trigger melanin production in unadapted human skin. Their eyes are large — not dramatically, not comically, but measurably larger than the eyes of humans who live under open sky. The pupils are wide. The irises are pale. They see well in the dim amber light, better than an outsider would, though not as well as they would like: they still stumble sometimes in the deepest chambers, where the light is least, and they still misjudge distances in the vast vaults, where the far wall is too far to resolve and the space between is empty and featureless.

They are small. Not uniformly — there is human variation here, as there is in any population — but the average dweller is shorter and slighter than the average human in the outside world, whatever that world may be. Calories are limited. The Veinwork provides, but it provides in the manner of a system at equilibrium: enough to sustain, never enough to surplus. The dwellers are lean, efficient, adapted to a diet that consists primarily of the Veinwork's own tissue (the inner layers, the spongy parenchyma, harvested in careful quantities from the walls and consumed raw or warmed by friction-fire), supplemented by the small fauna they take respectfully and in moderation.

Their language is **quiet**.

This is not a stylistic choice. It is a physical constraint. Sound does not carry in the Veinwork — the porous, living walls absorb acoustic energy with extraordinary efficiency, and the warm, saturated air is dense enough to damp what the walls do not catch. A shout reaches, at most, five meters before it is indistinguishable from silence. A normal speaking voice carries perhaps ten. Below a certain volume, the acoustics of the chambers favor transmission — the curved walls focus low-frequency sound, the water films conduct vibration — and the dwellers have, over many generations, settled into a vocal register that exploits this: low, soft, intimate. A dweller speaking at full projected volume sounds, to an outsider, like a person whispering in a library. A dweller speaking at conversational volume sounds like a person murmuring in a cathedral.

They communicate at close range. They touch each other often — hands on shoulders, fingers on wrists, palms against cheekbones. This is not primarily affectionate, though affection is part of it. It is informational. Touch conveys what voice cannot: the tension in a muscle, the warmth of skin, the pulse at a wrist, the subtle shift in posture that indicates attention, alarm, calm, or intent. The dwellers' tactile sense is acute — more acute than an outsider's, developed through a lifetime of navigating a world where visual and auditory cues are unreliable.

Their social groups are small. Families, mostly — extended kin networks of twenty to fifty individuals who share a territory of several chambers and the passages between them. These territories are not defended in any aggressive sense; there is no concept of ownership of the Veinwork itself, only of *familiarity* — knowing which passages are stable, which walls are harvestable, where the water flows true and where it flows wrong, where the large fauna has been seen and how long ago and from which direction it relocated. This knowledge is the dwellers' most valuable possession, passed from parent to child in long, quiet evenings of storytelling in the warm, wet, amber darkness.

They have no leaders, in the sense of centralized authority. They have **elders** — individuals who have lived long enough to accumulate a deep knowledge of their territory — and they have **walkers** — individuals who travel between groups, carrying news, carrying stories, carrying the rare genetic material that keeps small populations from stagnating. A walker's life is difficult and respected. They move through the shifting geometry with a patience and a skill that borders on art, navigating passages that bend in impossible ways, adjusting their route in real-time based on subtle cues — the temperature of the air, the moisture on a wall, the pattern of condensation on a membrane — that indicate whether a passage is behaving or shifting.

Walkers carry **memory-objects**: small tokens carved from hardened sap, each one inscribed with a pattern of grooves that encode information about a specific territory. The grooves are not a writing system — they are too simple for that, too abstract — but they function as mnemonic aids, each pattern triggering a sequence of remembered facts that the walker has been taught by the previous holder of the token. A walker entering a new territory presents their memory-object to the local elder, who examines the grooves and verifies the information they encode against their own knowledge. If the information is accurate — if the walker's memory of the territory matches the elder's — trust is established. If it does not — if the grooves describe a passage that has shifted, or a water-source that has dried, or a large fauna specimen in a position that the elder knows to be wrong — the walker is gently corrected, the grooves are re-cut, and the token is updated.

This is how the dwellers maintain their knowledge across a world that resists knowledge. Not through writing, not through maps, not through any technology that would be recognizable to an outside civilization, but through a distributed, tactile, oral system of memory that is as adapted to the Veinwork as the mites on its walls or the worms in its water. It is imperfect. It is incomplete. It is, by any external standard, primitive.

It works.

It has worked for longer than anyone can remember.

## What Lives Here, Summarized

The Veinwork is a living architecture of continental scale, and everything that lives within it is **adapted to its particular constraints**: the silence, the dimness, the wetness, the shifting geometry, the vast empty chambers, the slow pace of everything, the presence of large fauna whose behavior defies physical law, and the ever-present, ever-patient, ever-warm embrace of the living walls that surround everything and contain everything and are, in the end, the only thing that matters.

The mites keep the walls clean. The worms keep the water flowing. The clingers keep the walls pruned. The peelers keep the floor clear. The slope-walkers indicate the health of the system. The drifters indicate the health of the air. The glint-wings indicate the health of the mites. The crouchers keep the grazers in check. The large fauna indicates... something. Something that the dwellers do not try to define.

And the dwellers — small, quiet, adapted, patient — live within all of it, inside a thing that does not know they are there, sustained by a system that does not notice them, maintaining their own fragile equilibrium within a larger equilibrium that has existed for longer than they have had the capacity to be aware of it.

They are warm. They are fed. They are together. They are inside something that is alive, and it is alive, and that is enough.

That has always been enough.
