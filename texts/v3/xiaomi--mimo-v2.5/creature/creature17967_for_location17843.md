# What Lives in the Kethara Depression

---

**You notice the beetles first** because they are the first thing that moves.

You have been standing on the hardite floor for several minutes, adjusting to the weight of the air, the brightness, the way your own breathing sounds like machinery in a sealed room. Then something shifts at the edge of your peripheral vision — a crack in the mineral crust that was not a crack a moment ago. It separates. It has legs. It is the size of your smallest fingernail and it is the same color as the ground beneath it, which is to say it is the color of nothing, which is to say it is the color of *here.* You kneel. You look closer. There are others. There are hundreds. There are thousands.

The hardite beetles — *Ketharae crustipedes*, in the provisional taxonomy that no one has finalized because no one has stayed long enough — are the foundation. They are the first trophic level that has legs, and they are everywhere with the ubiquity of dust, which is what they are eating. They graze on the keth crust with mouthparts so small they might as well be theoretical. You cannot see them chew. You can only see them *be somewhere* and then *not be there* and then *be somewhere nearby,* which is their version of feeding. They move in short, jerking intervals — stop, adjust, stop, adjust — like something running on a clockwork mechanism that winds down and rewinds and winds down again. Between movements, they are so perfectly still that you lose them. Your eye slides off their carapace the way it slides off the mineral crust they stand on, because the carapace *is* mineral crust. Their shells are not merely colored to match the hardite; they are *composed* of it. They secrete a thin layer of calcium-iron compound over their exoskeletons as they grow, building a suit of local stone around themselves, wearing the Depression the way you might wear a coat. Each beetle is, in this small way, a piece of the floor that learned to walk.

They have no visible eyes. Or rather — they have photoreceptive cells distributed across their carapace in a way that does not correspond to any organ you would call an eye. They see in every direction and no direction. They perceive light the way you perceive pressure: as a constant, ambient condition that varies in intensity. They are never startled by movement because they do not process movement the way you do. They process *change in luminance,* and in the Depression, where the light arrives at noon like a verdict and casts shadows as sharp as cuts, the change in luminance is constant and everywhere and therefore, to a beetle, *normal.* You could kneel over one and your shadow could fall across it and it would not react, because a shadow is just light doing what light does here: arriving, pooling, retreating.

They are most active in the first hours after dawn and the last hours before dark, when the light is angled enough to create shadow at the micro-scale — the tiny ridges of the keth crust casting shadows no deeper than a hair's width, but shadows nonetheless, and shadows mean *texture,* and texture means *food.* You can watch a beetle navigate a patch of keth like a person reading braille: pausing, touching its mouthparts to the surface, moving on. The fractal ridges of the keth are both food source and topography. The beetle does not distinguish between eating a ridge and walking on a ridge. It does both. It is possible that, for the beetle, there is no difference.

When they die — and they die often, in numbers, because they are small and the air is hot and the margin between survival and failure is measured in degrees of moisture — their bodies are absorbed by the keth. The crust grows over them. Their mineral shells become part of the floor. They are recycled with the same indifference with which the Depression recycles everything: water becomes vapor becomes condensation becomes seep becomes water again; mineral becomes dust becomes crust becomes mineral again; beetle becomes keth becomes floor becomes dust becomes beetle. The loop is old. The loop does not notice you.

---

**The glass lizards are harder to see** and then, once you have learned to see them, impossible to unsee.

You will not notice them at first. You will walk across the Depression floor and feel nothing move beneath your feet, observe nothing on the walls, register nothing in your peripheral field that suggests a predator. And then you will stop — perhaps to look at a seep, perhaps to read a number on one of the steel markers — and in the stillness, at the very edge of your vision, a ripple. Not in water. In *keth.* A line of disruption in the fractal pattern, a healed-over seam that moves with a lateral fluidity that the static mineral crust does not possess. You will look at it and it will be gone. You will look at the keth three meters to your left and there will be another one: a faint disturbance, a trail, a signature left by a body that was there a moment ago and is now somewhere else.

If you are patient — and patience here is not a virtue but a survival requirement, because the air will kill you if you exert yourself and the light will blind you if you look up too long — you will eventually see one in full. It will be stretched across a patch of keth crust on the floor, perhaps two meters from where you stand, and it will be so translucent that you will think it is not there. It will be the color of nothing more than a slightly thicker version of the air above it. Its body will be a tube, gently flattened, tapering to a tail that is nearly indistinguishable from the keth ridges it rests on. Its internal organs will be visible: a dark line running the length of its body — the gut, packed with beetles in various stages of digestion — flanked by thinner lines that pulse faintly. The pulse is its heartbeat. You are watching its heartbeat through its skin. The skin has no scales in any conventional sense; it has *plates,* overlapping at a molecular level, each one transparent as glass, each one secreting a thin film of mineral oil that allows the body to slide through the keth crust without resistance. The oil leaves no residue. The keth does not react to the passage of a glass lizard any more than it reacts to wind or water. The lizard is, to the keth, nothing. This is the key to its survival. The keth provides its camouflage, its hunting ground, its entire world, and the keth does not care that it exists. The relationship is not symbiotic. It is *tolerant.* The keth tolerates the lizard the way a wall tolerates a shadow.

The glass lizard hunts by waiting. This is its only strategy and it is sufficient. A beetle passes. The lizard's head — if you can call it a head; it is merely the slightly wider end of the tube, with two dark spots that are not eyes but function as eyes — turns a fraction of a degree. The body contracts. The movement is not fast. It is *efficient.* The lizard covers the distance to the beetle in a single undulation, a wave of muscle that passes from tail to head and propels the body forward with a smoothness that makes the concept of *speed* seem crude. The beetle is taken into the mouth — a transverse slit that opens wider than you would expect — and swallowed whole. The entire event takes less than two seconds and makes no sound. You do not hear the lizard eat. You do not hear the lizard move. You do not hear the lizard breathe, because the lizard breathes through its skin, exchanging gases directly with the dense air of the Depression. It has no lungs. It has no need of them. The air here is so thick with dissolved minerals and water vapor that a body can breathe through its surface and receive everything it requires. The glass lizard is, in this sense, a creature of the atmosphere: it breathes the Depression itself, directly, without intermediary.

They are cold-blooded in the way that everything here is cold-blooded: the ambient temperature is so high that cold-bloodedness is almost meaningless. They do not need to bask. They do not need to seek shade. The Depression provides a thermal range that falls within their tolerance from hour to hour and day to day, and they move through it without thermoregulatory behavior of any kind. They are always at the temperature of wherever they are. They are the temperature.

At night, when the keth phosphorescence rises, the glass lizards become visible for the first time — truly visible, not just the faint ripple of disturbance but the full body, outlined in pale green-white light from below. They look like lanterns. They look like thoughts made visible, moving through a substrate that is itself thinking in ways you cannot access. They hunt more actively at night, when the beetles move more slowly in the cooler air and the contrast between beetle and keth is reduced by the dim light. The lizards do not need contrast. They navigate by vibration, by the faintest disturbance in the surface tension of the mineral oil film that covers the keth. A beetle's footfall is an earthquake in this world. The lizard feels it. The lizard arrives.

There is no mating behavior that has been observed. No eggs have been found. No juveniles have been identified. The population appears to sustain itself through some mechanism that is either too rare to witness or too subtle to detect. They may bud. They may reproduce through fragmentation — a piece breaking off and becoming a new individual. They may do something else entirely that does not correspond to any reproductive strategy in the literature. The glass lizards do not explain themselves. They do not need to. They are the apex predator of the Depression floor, and they have been here longer than anything that might have required an explanation.

**The shelf roosters descend at dusk.**

You hear them before you see them — not the individual clicking, which is too quiet to register at distance, but the *accumulation* of clicking, the way dozens of voices layer into a rhythm that is not quite a rhythm and not quite chaos. *Tok. Tok. Tok.* It begins on the southern wall, low and tentative, and spreads — one individual answering another, a chain of percussion that climbs the terrace steps and crosses to the eastern wall and finds the northern escarpment and fills the Depression from wall to wall with a sound that seems to come from the rock itself, as though the terraces are vibrating, as though the stepped geology has found its resonant frequency and is singing in it.

Then the launch.

From every narrow shelf, every jutting ledge, every inverted stalactite where a winged body has been clinging motionless for hours, they drop into the air and spread their wings and begin to climb. They are not graceful. They are heavy-bodied, broad-headed, built for clinging and for short bursts of powered flight rather than sustained soaring. But the thermal columns that rise from the sun-heated walls at dusk provide what their wings cannot: lift. They ride these columns upward, spiraling in tight formations of six or eight, their wings held at a fixed angle, riding the updraft with the mechanical efficiency of something that has done this ten thousand times and will do it ten thousand more. They rise. They rise. The clicking stops — all at once, as though a signal has been given — and in the silence that follows, you watch them shrink against the darkening sky above the rim. They become dots. They become nothing.

Where they go at night is the subject of the Depression's second-great unanswered question, after the keth pattern. They hunt insects that swarm near the rim, where the plateau's dry air meets the Depression's dense atmosphere and creates a thermal boundary layer that concentrates airborne prey. This is the standard explanation and it is probably true as far as it goes. But they stay gone until dawn. The insects at the rim do not require eight hours of hunting. The shelf roosters are gone long after any reasonable foraging would be complete. They return in the earliest light, dropping into the Depression in groups of two and three, landing on their terraces with the ungraceful precision of something that knows exactly where it belongs. They tuck their wings. They grip the stone. They become, again, part of the wall: stocky, dull-colored, indistinguishable from the rock unless you know where to look.

Some of them do not return. On any given morning, one or two terraces that held roosters the night before will be empty. Whether this is death — predation at the rim, collision with the plateau's wind patterns, simple exhaustion — or whether it is *departure,* a voluntary leaving that has nothing to do with survival and everything to do with some behavioral cycle that encompasses more than the Depression, is unknown. The population appears stable. For every rooster that does not return, a new one appears — perhaps a juvenile, perhaps a migrant from elsewhere, though *elsewhere* in this context is difficult to define. The Depression is, for all practical purposes, an island. The shelf roosters are its air traffic, its seasonal migration, its connection to a world above that the floor-dwelling organisms never touch.

Their clicking, at close range, has a quality that is difficult to describe. It is not produced by the syrinx or any vocal organ; it is produced by the rapid contraction of a specialized muscle at the base of the skull that causes a bony plate to strike the interior of the lower mandible. *Tok.* Each click is a bone hitting bone. The sound is dry, precise, and carries with an intimacy that is startling — it sounds like something happening *inside your own head,* a ticking behind the eyes, as though your own skull has found its resonant frequency and is answering the roosters in kind. You feel it in your teeth. You feel it in the small bones of your inner ear. The compressed air of the Depression transmits the vibration with an efficiency that makes distance irrelevant. A roosters click three hundred meters up the wall arrives in your chest with the same immediacy as a whisper.

They are, in every sense, the voice of the place. When they are gone at night, the Depression is mute. When they return at dawn, it begins again.

**The sun pillars stand still.**

There are perhaps forty of them across the entire floor of the Depression — a population so sparse that you can walk for an hour without encountering one, and then turn a corner around a jutting shelf of wall-rock and find yourself face to face with something that does not move and does not explain itself.

They are approximately a meter tall. They are a deep red-brown, the color of iron oxide, and they are segmented like a stack of coins — each segment a slightly concave disc, perhaps two centimeters thick, stacked with a precision that looks engineered. The surface of each disc is smooth and warm to the touch, warmer than the ambient air, which suggests internal heat generation but could simply be thermal mass — the iron-rich material absorbing the relentless light and holding it. At the top of the stack, a flat disc, slightly larger than the others, sits at an angle. It tracks the sun. Not quickly. Not dramatically. But if you watch for thirty minutes, you will see it move: a rotation of perhaps fifteen degrees, smooth and continuous, matching the sun's arc across the narrow strip of sky visible from the Depression floor. It tracks the sun the way a sunflower tracks the sun, except that a sunflower is obviously alive and a sun pillar is *obviously something* but whether *alive* is the correct word is a question that has not been settled.

They do not photosynthesize — or if they do, they do not do it in any way that produces measurable oxygen. They do not grow. A pillar measured in year one is the same size in year ten. They do not reproduce, or if they do, no new pillars have appeared in the documented history of the Depression. They do not decay. The keth grows around their bases but not over them, as though the crust recognizes something in the pillar's surface that is incompatible with its own growth pattern — or as though the pillar *prevents* the keth from growing, a zone of exclusion maintained by some chemical or electromagnetic property that the keth respects. Around each pillar, a clean circle of bare hardite, perhaps thirty centimeters in diameter, free of any crust, free of any life, free of anything except the pillar and the stone it stands on.

They hum. This is the detail that is almost never mentioned in the survey reports, perhaps because it is difficult to verify, perhaps because it is difficult to admit. The processing stations hum — that is documented, attributed to geothermal activity, accepted without controversy. The sun pillars also hum, and the hum is different. It is higher in frequency, nearly at the upper limit of human hearing, and it is *continuous* — a single tone, unwavering, that you become aware of only when you stand very close to a pillar and the rest of the Depression's quiet recedes. The tone does not change with the sun's position. It does not change at all. It is a fixed frequency, and it is the same from every pillar: you can move from one to another, across the floor, up to the terraces, and the tone is identical. Forty pillars, producing the same note, at the same pitch, at the same amplitude. This is not thermodynamics. This is not geology. This is *something else,* and the survey reports do not address it because the survey reports do not know what to say.

Some have proposed that the sun pillars are instruments — measurement devices, left by whoever built the processing stations, designed to monitor conditions in the Depression. The tracking of the sun would be incidental: an orientation mechanism, a way of maintaining a consistent reference point. The hum would be the sound of an internal mechanism, a piezoelectric oscillator or a magnetohydrodynamic generator or something else entirely. This theory is attractive because it gives the pillars a purpose. It is also unsupported by any evidence, because the pillars do not appear to be manufactured. Their composition is consistent with naturally occurring iron-oxide formations. Their segments are consistent with periodic deposition in a mineral-rich environment. They could be, in principle, natural. They are almost certainly not. The gap between *could be natural* and *is definitely natural* is where the pillars live, and they live there comfortably, without urgency, the way everything in the Depression lives: slowly, without explanation, for reasons that predate the concept of reasons.

**In the pools, things move.**

The largest pool — fifteen meters across, no deeper than your hand, opaque with dissolved minerals — holds a population of organisms that were not included in the original survey reports and that appear in later addenda with the note: *Taxonomic status unresolved. Possibly unicellular. Possibly colonial. Possibly something else.*

From the surface, they are invisible. The water is too dense with mineral compounds — calcium, iron, sulfur, lithium, concentrations that would be toxic to any organism adapted to freshwater — for visual transparency. You see only the pale greenish-blue opacity, the surface tension that seems to resist your finger when you test it, the faint iridescence where the mineral film breaks at the edges.

But if you take a sample — and the survey teams did, carefully, with equipment designed for extreme-salinity environments — and hold it up to the light, you see them. They are small. They are organized. They are, individually, no larger than a grain of coarse sand, and they are shaped like — this is the description that appears in every report and that satisfies no one — *tiny open hands.* Five extensions, radiating from a central body, each extension ending in a filament so fine it is visible only under magnification. The filaments wave. They wave in a coordinated pattern: all five on each organism, all organisms in the sample, waving in the same direction at the same time, as though responding to a current that does not exist in the water. The sample has been removed from the pool. The current is not external. The waving is *generated* by the organisms themselves, and it is *synchronized* across the sample in a way that suggests either mechanical coupling (the filaments physically touching and driving each other, like interlocking gears) or chemical signaling (a shared molecule in the water that coordinates their movement) or something else. The reports do not specify what else. The reports have run out of language.

They feed on the dissolved iron compounds in the pool water, absorbing them through their filaments and processing them internally. The byproduct is a fine precipitate of iron sulfide — black, dense, settling to the bottom of the pool in a layer that is millimeters thick and ancient. This precipitate is part of the pool's ecology: it provides a substrate for bacterial colonies that in turn produce sulfur compounds that the hand-shaped organisms metabolize in a secondary cycle. The pool is a closed loop, a miniature ecosystem operating at a scale of centimeters, cycling iron and sulfur and energy in a pattern that has been running, unbroken, for longer than the Depression has had a name.

What makes them unusual — beyond their morphology, beyond their synchronized behavior — is their response to disturbance. If you place a finger in the water, the organisms nearest to the point of contact do something that no survey team has been able to describe without resorting to language that sounds, in a scientific report, unscientific: they *gather.* They move toward your finger. They cluster around it, a ring of tiny open hands, filaments waving faster now, the coordinated pattern accelerating as though the disturbance has excited them. They do not feed on your finger. They do not attack your finger. They appear to *examine* it — to use their filaments to sample the surface chemistry of your skin, the oils, the salts, the residue of a world they have never encountered. They do this for perhaps thirty seconds. Then they disperse. They return to their previous positions. The synchronization resumes at its original pace. Your finger has been noted and dismissed.

This behavior is consistent across multiple survey teams, multiple years, multiple points of entry into the pool. The organisms respond to biological material. They do not respond to rock, to metal, to the processing station's steel grating. They respond to *living tissue.* This suggests a chemosensory capability that is, for a presumably unicellular or colonial organism, remarkably sophisticated. It suggests that the pool knows, in some sense, what is alive. It reaches toward the living. It touches it. It lets it go.

**The keth is the true inhabitant.**

Everything else — the beetles, the lizards, the roosters, the pillars, the hand-shaped organisms, even the processing stations and the steel markers — exists in relation to the keth. The beetles eat it. The lizards move through it. The pillars exclude it. The pools are fed by the same seeps that sustain it. The processing stations were built beside it. The keth is the medium, the substrate, the connective tissue of the Depression. It is the wall. It is the floor. It is the light.

And it is organized.

This is the fact that refuses to resolve into meaning. The counterclockwise spiral. The non-random spacing. The response to a signal that no instrument has detected. The keth covers thousands of square meters of vertical and horizontal surface, and across every meter of it, the fractal ridges follow the same rotational pattern, the same spacing logic, the same orientation toward a direction that has nothing to do with the sun, nothing to do with the seeps, nothing to do with gravity or magnetism or any force that the survey teams could measure. They measured everything. They found no correlation. The pattern persists.

At night, when the phosphorescence brightens and the walls glow with the keth's own light, the pattern becomes *visible* in a way it is not during the day. During the day, the ridges are too small, too uniform, too much like texture. At night, they are luminous, and the luminosity reveals the pattern the way a blueprint reveals structure: every spiral turning counterclockwise, every spiral spaced at intervals that are not equal but are not random, spirals nested within spirals, spirals between spirals, spirals on the walls and spirals on the floor and spirals on the underside of every jutting shelf and every inverted stalactite, spirals oriented to a center that is not the center of the Depression, not the pool, not the processing station, not anything that a human eye would designate as *the middle of something.* The center is elsewhere. The center may be below the floor. The center may be above the rim. The center may be a point in space that does not correspond to any physical location in the Depression but that the keth can perceive and the keth alone.

The survey teams did not decode the pattern. They did not try for long. The Depression is difficult to reach, difficult to survive in, and the pattern does not *do* anything that they could measure. It does not produce energy. It does not move matter. It does not, as far as anyone can tell, communicate with anything outside itself. It is a pattern that exists because the keth makes it, and the keth makes it because the keth is the keth, and the keth has been the keth since before the Depression had survey teams or processing stations or names.

You stand on the floor at night. The keth glows. The spirals turn their slow counterclockwise turn, fixed in place but pointing somewhere. The glass lizards move through the luminous crust like shadows in a lantern, their dark organs visible through their translucent bodies, their passage leaving trails that heal and close and disappear. The shelf roosters are gone, somewhere above the rim, in a world you cannot see from here. The processing station hums — low, irregular, patient — from the northern wall. The steel markers stand in their precise rows, geometric intrusions in a world that operates by other rules, their shadows blue-black on the hardite floor. The pool at the center holds its pale mirror of light, and beneath the surface, in a silence no instrument can break, the tiny hand-shaped things wave their filaments in a coordinated motion that is either response or expression or something for which no word exists.

The air moves. Warm. Mineral. Ancient. The keth breathes — not literally, but in the way that a vast organism breathes: a slow exchange of gas and light and chemistry, a metabolic rhythm measured not in seconds or minutes but in seasons and centuries. The Depression breathes with it. The walls are alive. The floor is alive. The light comes from the ground. The ground is thinking, if thinking is the word for what a mineral-plant hybrid does when it organizes itself into a pattern that no one can read, maintained across millennia, lit by its own chemistry, fed by seeps that have been running since before the concept of *before* existed.

You are a visitor here. You have always been a visitor here. The Depression does not need you to witness it. The keth does not need you to decode it. The glass lizards will move through the spirals tonight and tomorrow night and every night for as long as the seeps flow and the hardite holds and the air remains thick and hot and still. The beetles will graze. The roosters will click. The pillars will hum their single, unwavering note. The pool will hold its hand-shaped things and the hand-shaped things will reach toward whatever is alive and touch it and let it go.

The Depression is not waiting for anything. The Depression is not *about* anything. The Depression is a place where the earth pulled itself apart and life moved into the wound and organized itself into a pattern that looks, from a distance, like nothing and up close, like everything, and from any distance at all, like something that will continue long after the last processing station has been swallowed by the keth and the last marker has rusted into the hardite and the last human footprint has been buried under a millimeter of mineral crust.

It is mostly quiet. It is mostly still. It is mostly alive.

It was here before you. It will be here after. It is not patient, because patience implies waiting, and the keth is not waiting. It is *continuing.* There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of the Depression, glowing in the dark, spiraling toward a center that is not a place but a *direction,* feeding on iron and light and the deep water that seeps from fractures in a wound that is older than memory, and it will keep going, and it will not explain itself, and it does not need to.

It is the kethara. It is the place that named itself by growing.
