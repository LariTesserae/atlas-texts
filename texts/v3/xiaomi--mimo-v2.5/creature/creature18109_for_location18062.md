They are shaped by the place as the place is shaped by them. You would not call them human, though they walk upright and have hands for shaping stone. Their skin carries the same grey-pink hue as the wet rock in morning, and it is cool to the touch, perpetually damp without being slick. Their hair, if it is hair, grows in thick, fibrous mats close to the skull, the color of lichen—ash-grey, with occasional filaments of paler white. It serves no aesthetic purpose; it insulates. Their eyes are the most telling: large, dark, and filmed with a secondary translucent lid that protects against the constant grit of windborne dust and microscopic droplets. The irises are the deep, wet grey of stone after rain.

They move with a slowness that is not lethargy but a deep, conserved efficiency. There is no wasted motion. When they lift a stone, they do not strain; they settle into the weight, let their bones align with its center, and move it in one smooth, committed arc. Their footfalls are near-silent, their feet broad and calloused, prehensile in a way, the toes splayed to grip the wet, rounded cobbles. They do not run. The concept seems foreign, like an unnecessary expenditure of heat and breath.

Their dwellings are extensions of their bodies, and vice versa. They are seen occasionally in doorways or along the paths, but they are never *seen through*. They do not contrast with their environment; they complete it. One will be sitting on a low stone bench outside a doorway, hands resting on knees, watching the diffuse light with an expression of deep, vacant attention that might be meditation or might be the simple act of being. Another will be passing a shaped stone from hand to hand, feeling for flaws or perhaps just feeling its texture, its coolness, its solid reality.

They communicate in tones just at the edge of hearing—low, resonant hums that travel well through the damp air and solid stone, more felt in the chest cavity than heard with the ear. Occasionally, a word emerges, a sharp, wet consonant sound like a pebble dropped in a shallow pool. It is always sufficient. The quiet is not empty; it is a medium, and their voices are part of its texture.

Their tasks are rituals of maintenance. They do not build new structures; the town is complete. Instead, they tend. One is seen daily at the base of a wall, not repairing it, but pressing fresh mortar paste—a grey,iteiteite paste of crushediteiteiteiteiteiteite grit anditeite organic binder—into hairline cracks that have appeared over decades. Another will spend a day clearing moss from a single stretch of cobbles, not eradicated, but pruned back to its defined crevices, keeping the path bare. They do not hurry. The crack will still be there tomorrow. The moss grows slowly.

They eat little. The sparse, tough grass from the shelf's edge is gathered in small, leathery bundles and chewed for hours, releasing a slow, bitter sustenance. They drink the air, or seem to; their lips are often parted slightly, the moisture condensing on their tongue and inner cheek. They do not seem to cook. Fire is a concept of change, and change is not native here.

Their young are rare and arrive slowly. A child, if it can be called that, is seen clinging to an adult's leg with a grip of startling strength, its movements uncoordinated but deliberate. It does not cry. Its skin is smoother, its eyes wider, but it already possesses the stillness. It learns by observing, by absorbing the rhythm of the place through its skin. It will grow into the stone as the stone grows into it.

They know you are there. This is certain. You feel it as a subtle shift in the quality of the silence, a momentary increase in its depth. They do not look at you with curiosity or fear. They look with the same steady, wet gaze they turn upon the stone or the moss. You are a variation in the pattern, a temporary disturbance in the damp air. You are as real and as transient as the single bird's call. They have seen your like before, and they will see your like again, and both moments are the same moment, spread thin over an eternity of grey light.

Their lifespan is unmeasured. There are no birthdays, no records. An elder is distinguished only by the increased smoothness of their skin, the deeper set of their eyes in their sockets, the way they move as if they are part of the stone they sit upon. They do not die in any visible way. They simply become less distinct. Their movements slow to match the moss's growth. Their skin takes on the exact hue and texture of the wall they rest against. Their breathing shallows to the frequency of condensation forming on granite. At some point, they are not a person sitting by a wall, but a warmth in the stone, a slightly smoother curve in the rock's surface. The other inhabitants do not mourn this. It is not an end, but a completion—a stone finally settling into its bed, a sentence gravity has finished speaking.

They are the town. The town is them. To ask who lives here is to ask what the stone dreams of, what the moisture remembers. They are the answer, and the answer is: *This. Only this. And this is enough.*
