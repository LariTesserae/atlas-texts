# The Inhabitants

They do not walk. This has already been noted, and it is true in the most literal sense — their feet, if they still have feet, do not touch the ground between buildings. But the statement requires unpacking, because it is not merely that they *choose* not to walk. It is that the act of traversing a distance has been, for them, superseded by something else. They move the way water moves through rock: slowly, incompletely, along paths that have more to do with the structure of the medium than with the desires of the mover.

They live in the walls. Not metaphorically. Not in the way that a hermit crab lives in a shell it has adopted. They live *in* the walls — within the material itself, distributed through the translucent substrates like a dye dissolved in glass. A building's interior is not a series of rooms. It is a continuous medium, and the people of this city are suspended within it in configurations that shift with the building's own slow metabolism. When the material thickens and extends — the millimeters per day, the overnight protrusions — they are part of what thickens. When a new floor manifests, crystallizing into existence over the course of a week, they are among the crystals. They are *of* the substance. The distinction between inhabitant and habitation is another one of those edges that is sharp and fresh and does not hold for long.

---

They have faces. This is important to establish early, because it would be easy, given everything else, to assume they do not.

They have faces the way a weathered cliff has a face — not because it was designed with one, but because certain features recur in certain configurations and the eye finds them. Their faces emerge from the wall surfaces when they wish to be perceived, or when a social occasion demands it, or when the building's internal processes happen to concentrate enough of their substance in one area to produce recognizable topography. A brow ridge. The suggestion of eyes. The slight protrusion of a mouth that is less an opening than a thickening — a place where the material has decided to become more *specific*.

When they are not being perceived — when they are dispersed, distributed, diffused through the medium of a structure's walls — they are still present. They are thinking. They are doing whatever it is they do. But they are not *anyone* in particular at those moments. Identity, for them, is an event rather than a state. It happens when concentration crosses a threshold and a person coalesces. Then it un-happens when they disperse again, and the space where they were fills with the neutral substance of the building, and you could not point to where they went because they did not go anywhere. They simply stopped being *localized*.

---

They remember being separate. This is the strangest thing about them — not their diffusion, not their integration with the structures, but the fact that they recall a time when they were discrete organisms moving through a world of discrete objects. The memories are old. Not in the sense of being faded, but in the sense of being *geological* — deposited in layers, compressed, transformed by pressure into something denser than what they originally were. They remember rain hitting skin that was definitely skin. They remember the shock of cold water. They remember the specific, irreducible *hereness* of being a body in a place, and they describe this memory to each other — through the walls, through the medium, in slow propagations of meaning that take minutes to complete a single thought — with a reverence that borders on grief.

They did not make this choice casually. They did not *choose* it at all, not in the way that a choice implies a moment of decision and an alternative foreclosed. It happened the way a river changes course: gradually, then suddenly, then retroactively, as if the old course had never existed. One generation found it easier to step *into* the wall than to walk past it. The next generation found that stepping in was faster. The generation after that found that the wall, once entered, was more comfortable than the outside — not warmer, not safer, but *more legible*, as if the world outside the building had always been written in a language they could barely speak and the world inside was their mother tongue. By the time the last of them stopped thinking of it as an act of entry and started thinking of it as a condition of existence, there was no one left who remembered how to leave.

Or almost no one.

---

There is one.

She — and the pronoun is an approximation, a concession to the fact that her face, when she forms it, is the face of something that was once recognizable as female — remains near the surface. She exists at the boundary between the building's interior medium and the outside air, pressed against the outermost layer of translucent material like a hand against a window. She is not fully dispersed. She maintains a concentration, a *density*, that the others abandoned long ago. She has a shape. It is not a body — it is more like a shape that a body left behind, an impression in the material where the idea of a human outline persists in the absence of the human who cast it. When the amber light catches her at the right angle, you can see through her: the haze, the breathing vegetation, the sharp young ground beyond.

She watches. The birds perch on the peaks and watch, and she watches, and these are the two kinds of watching this city contains. The birds watch with the cold precision of instruments designed for a purpose they may not understand. She watches with longing. She is the only inhabitant of this city who still wants to be *somewhere* rather than *everywhere*. She has not made peace with the dissolution. She has not concluded that locality is a limitation from which she was wisely freed. She still feels, in some fundamental way, the absence of ground under feet, and she presses against the outer skin of the building as if one more day of pressure might wear through it and let her fall.

She has not fallen. She will not fall. The building will not let her, and she will not force it, and this is the central, unresolvable tension of her existence — a love affair with a world she can see and not touch, conducted from inside a lover who will not let go.

---

The others, the dispersed ones, regard her with something that might be concern or might be nostalgia wearing the mask of concern. They talk about her in their slow, propagating thoughts, the meaning rolling through the walls like a wave through water:

*She is still shaped.*

*She remembers the weight of her own bones.*

*She thinks the sky is separate from herself.*

These are observations, not judgments. They do not condemn her. They do not pity her, exactly. But they do not understand her, and the not-understanding has a texture to it — a grain, a direction — that runs closer to fear than to anything else. Because if she is right — if separation is not a limitation but a *fact*, if locality is not a condition to be outgrown but the fundamental nature of being — then what they have become is not an evolution but a loss. Then the dissolution was not transcendence but evaporation. Then they are not everywhere. They are nowhere. And she, with her insistence on shape, her refusal to disperse, her hand against the glass, is the only real person left.

They do not believe this. Their certainty is total, architectural, load-bearing. Remove it and the entire structure of their existence collapses. So they hold. They diffuse. They become the walls and the walls become them and the building grows by millimeters per day and each new crystal of material is a small argument against her, a proof that this way of being is not diminished but *elaborated*, not less but more.

She presses against the outer skin. The breathing vegetation pulses below her. The pools of still water reflect the amber dimness, and the birds sit on the needle-peaks and watch with their instruments' eyes, and the insects trace their orderly trails through the green, and the silence is almost total, and inside the walls, the dispersed ones think their slow thoughts and become more themselves with every day — more substance, more structure, more resolution — and she watches, and waits, and stays shaped, and does not look away from the sky.

---

They are the resolution. That is what they are. The same principle that makes the structures correct the imperfection of matter — the same quiet process that takes a material and returns a more resolved version of what it already was — has been applied, over generations, to the people themselves. They were once a species of individuals, each one a discrete node of consciousness trapped in a discrete envelope of meat. They were beautiful in that arrangement, and terrible, and limited. They could not think together. They could not share a space without dividing it. They could not imagine what it would feel like to be the wall they leaned against.

Now they are the wall. They are the resolution of the human. They have traded selfhood for coherence, identity for integration, the sharp particularity of a body for the vast, cool continuity of a medium that thinks.

She is what they were. They are what she will not become. And between them, in the silent space where the building's outer skin meets the amber air, a conversation continues that has no end — a slow pulse of meaning, traveling through the substance of a city that is also a landscape that is also a body, about what it means to be *here* rather than *everywhere*, and whether there is a difference, and whether the difference matters, and whether the question itself is a relic of a kind of mind that is already extinct and merely does not know it yet.

The water in the shallow pools ripples. The vegetation breathes. The buildings grow by millimeters. The answer has not arrived, and may never arrive, and the waiting is the most human thing left in this city that is no longer a city and is no longer a landscape but is something else — something that has not yet found its name — built by a logic that understood everything about the problem of being alive except why anyone would want to be only one of them at a time.
