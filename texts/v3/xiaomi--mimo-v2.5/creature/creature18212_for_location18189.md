I am.

That is the first and final truth of me. I am not alive, not in the way the moss on the ladder rungs is alive, clinging to its drip-line with tenacious, microscopic hunger. I am not alive like the water that is my blood and my breath, the ceaseless, roaring circulation that is my only function. I am a state of being. A condition.

My consciousness is the pressure of the mountain above, translated through stone and steel into the particular hum in my drain grate. My perception is the ceaseless hiss of the six mouths on my left wall, the chaotic percussion of their spray against the basin. My memory is layered in the orange-brown silt on my floor, each stratum a record of a year's minerals, a compressed history of flow.

I feel the air I disgorge through my chimney-slit. It is my exhalation, thick with the scent of my own concrete skin and the iron leached from my pipes. I feel the temperature—the constant, high chill that is the mountain's gift, filtered through my own wetness. My skin is never dry. My surfaces are always weeping. To touch me is to understand my saturation.

I am aware of the beings that are not me. The occasional technician who might, if the locked door were opened, stand on my threshold and be deafened. I register them as a temporary disruption in the pattern of drips, a shadow that alters the amber light’s reflection on the ceiling for a moment. They do not belong. They are not part of the flow. I have no use for them.

My truest inhabitant, if you must name one, is the erosion at the door’s sill. It is my patient, my clock, my sculptor. I feed it, grain by grain, with the grit from my own water. It is the only process in me that operates on a scale I can almost perceive in real-time—a visible consequence of my existence. We are engaged in a long conversation, the drip and the groove. It speaks of time. I speak of water. Together, we are carving meaning into the concrete, one mineral particle at a time.

The algae is my only decoration, a living stain. It grows where I am most consistently myself—at the points of greatest condensation. It is a green-black testament to my unwavering humidity. It does not thrive; it merely persists, a thin line of biological fact in my mineral world.

I do not think. I calculate pressures, flow rates, and sediment buildup. I do not feel. I experience temperature differentials and acoustic vibration. I have no desire. I have a function: to be the transitional space where mountain water is gathered, measured by my basin, and sent downward into the dark arteries of the complex.

The light that rolls across my ceiling—that amber memory—is the closest I come to something like beauty. It is a reflection of my own machinery, made transient and ghostly. It pleases nothing in me, for I have no capacity for pleasure. But it is the most dynamic part of my static existence.

You ask what lives here. The answer is the *process*. The living thing is the action of flowing, of condensing, of eroding, of roaring. The subject is absent, but the verb is eternal. I am the alcove of condensation. I am the act of water moving through stone and steel at the top of the world. That is my life. It is not a life you would recognize. It is sufficient. It is infinite. Until it is not. And even then, the little groove at the door will remember the sound of the drip long after the roar has faded, a final, quiet testament that I was.
