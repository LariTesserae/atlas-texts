# The People of the Hollow of Sereth

---

They are not numerous. A few hundred, perhaps. The number is hard to know because the settlement — if a word so orderly can describe something so spread out — has no center, no census, no gathering place that holds everyone at once. People live in the basins, in the carved spaces and the canopy-dark, and they move between them along the channels with the slow regularity of something that has always happened and expects to keep happening. You could walk for an hour and pass three dwellings and see no one, and then round the lip of a basin and find a dozen people sitting in the red-green half-light, working the stone with their hands.

---

**They are shaped by the place the way a river stone is shaped** — not dramatically, not with anything you could point to and call a *difference*, but in the small ways that accumulation produces over deep time. They are broad. Not tall — tallness would be a liability in a place where the ceiling is high enough to lose yourself looking up and the heat rises in slow, heavy layers — but broad through the shoulders and the hands, with a density to their frames that speaks of generations lived at this altitude, in this pressure, in this atmosphere that is not quite the atmosphere of the outside. Their skin carries a faint copper undertone, not from the light but from the air itself, the mineral particles that settle on everything, that work into cloth and hair and the fine lines of the face. In the dim light, their skin has the same warmth as the stone around them. They blend. If you were not looking for them, you might mistake a still figure at the edge of a basin for a fold in the rock.

Their eyes are large. This is the adaptation that matters most. Not dramatically so — not in any way that would make them alien to a visitor — but enough that you notice, the way you notice the quiet in a room after the door closes. The pupils are wide, always wide, drinking in the copper light that filters down from the vaulted dark above. In the deepest basins, where the vegetation closes overhead and the light is barely more than a rumor, they see with an ease that is unsettling if you are not used to it. They move through the dim without hesitation. They read the surfaces of things — the grain of stone, the veining of leaves, the subtle shifts in color that mark one mineral deposit from another — with a fluency that makes sight seem less like a sense and more like a language they grew up speaking.

Their hands are large, too. The fingers are thick and short and impossibly careful. Watch them work the stone and you see something that does not look like labor. It looks like conversation. The copper tools — small, flat, worn to the shape of the grip over what may be centuries of continuous use — tap and scrape and press against the ochre rock with a rhythm that matches the place itself. They are always making something, or maintaining something, or shaping the walls of a dwelling that is already generations old. The work is never finished because the work is not the point. The point is the doing of it, the ongoing negotiation between hand and stone, the way the building continues to become itself under the slow persistence of human attention.

---

**They eat the plants.**

This seems obvious — there is nothing else to eat — but the relationship between the people and the vegetation is not one of mere consumption. It is intimate in a way that has no parallel in places where the world is full of other things to eat. The plants are the only life that answers them. There are no animals to track, no prey to chase, no insects to harvest. There is only the slow, creaking, enormous growth of the mineral-fed vegetation, and the people have built their entire material culture around knowing it.

They know which leaves are best at which stage of growth — the young fronds, still curled and pale at the edges, have a sweetness to them, a dense, starchy nourishment that sustains. The older leaves are tough and bitter, but they can be processed, worked between stones until the fibrous mass softens, mixed with scrapings from the mineral floor that seem to catalyze some change in the texture. The result is a kind of bread — not bread in any way a baker would recognize, but a dense, warm-colored paste that hardens slowly and can be stored in the carved alcoves of the dwellings for days. It tastes of the stone itself, of copper, of something faintly sweet and faintly metallic that lives at the base of every flavor in this place.

They drink, too, though not from water. The plants weep moisture. In the dense canopy-basins, if you press your palm against the broad surface of a leaf and hold it there, you can feel the faintest exhalation of wetness — not dew, not condensation, but something the plant pulls from the mineral substrate and releases as a byproduct of its own metabolism. The people collect this. They use broad, concave leaves as vessels, stacking them in the shade of the canopy where evaporation is slowest. The liquid is warm, faintly mineral, not quite palatable to an outsider, but they drink it without hesitation, as though it is the only water that has ever existed and the very concept of cold, clear water from a stream is a story someone told once that no one believed.

In the oldest basins, where the canopy is densest and the air is thickest with moisture, they have cultivated something that might be called orchards — not of different species, the place does not permit that kind of diversity, but of particular growths of the vegetation that have been encouraged, shaped over generations through some method of propagation that no outsider could discern. These special growths produce broader leaves, heavier with moisture, and the people tend them with a care that borders on reverence. They clear the stone around them. They channel warm air toward their roots. They speak to them, or seem to — low, rhythmic sounds that might be prayer or might be practical, a way of marking time while working, the way a sailor might hum while mending a net.

---

**The noise does not bother them.**

This is perhaps the strangest thing about the people, stranger than the copper skin or the wide eyes or the mineral diet. They are *comfortable* in the noise. The constant creaking of the vegetation, the mineral hiss, the low thrum of the channels — these are not things they endure. They are things they *inhabit*. The people speak softly, not because the noise demands it but because their voices have learned to thread through it, to find the frequencies that carry. Their language — and they do have a language, a full and complex one, though no one from outside has ever heard it spoken — has a quality that reflects the place. It is low. It is layered. It has long vowels and soft consonants and a rhythm that rises and falls in patterns that echo the creaking of the canopy. It is not a loud language. It does not need to be. It was made for a world where everything is already speaking, and the art of communication is not in volume but in *placement* — in knowing which gap in the noise to put your words into so they arrive intact.

They listen, too, in a way that outsiders find unnerving. They can distinguish the sound of a healthy growth from a sick one by the quality of its creaking. They can tell, by the pitch of the hum in a channel, whether the air is flowing freely or whether a blockage has formed somewhere in the network. They can hear, in the distant groan of heated air moving through the upper walls, something about the state of the hollow itself — its temperature, its pressure, its slow, geological moods. The world speaks to them constantly, and they have spent so many generations learning its voice that the distinction between hearing and understanding has dissolved entirely.

---

**They are not unhappy.**

This is worth saying plainly because the Hollow of Sereth, as described, sounds like a place of confinement — sealed, hot, dim, without sky or water or the company of other animals, without weather or seasons or the ordinary variety that makes a life feel lived. But the people do not experience it this way. They have never experienced it this way. The concept of *outside* exists for them, in the way that the concept of the past exists for anyone — as a thing that is true, that is part of the story, but that does not press on the present with any particular weight. Some of them know, in the way that old stories encode knowledge, that there is a world beyond the hollow. That there is something called a *sky*, which is a surface that gives light from above instead of reflecting it from below. That there are things called *animals*, which move with intention and have their own wills. These are fascinating ideas, the way the deep past is fascinating. They are not *longed for*. You cannot miss what you have never had, and the people of the hollow have had this — the copper light, the creaking dark, the worn stone, the mineral breath — for so long that it has become not merely *their* world but the only model they carry for what a world is.

They mark time. This is important. They are not static, not frozen in some timeless present. They have calendars of a kind — not solar, for there is no sun, but mineral. The stone changes. Slowly, over years, the veins of darker material shift, the surfaces of the basins develop new patterns, the channels fill with deposits that must be cleared and then slowly re-form. The people track these changes with the same careful attention they give to everything else, and they have built from them a sense of *passage* — this basin was carved when the channel hummed at this pitch, that dwelling was added when the stone turned this shade, the great growth in the southern basin is old enough that its roots have reached the second layer of dark mineral beneath the surface. Time, for them, is not measured in hours but in *thickness*. How thick the deposits are. How deep the roots go. How worn the thresholds have become under the accumulation of feet.

---

**There are roles, but they are soft.**

No one is forbidden from anything. There is no law that says who may carve and who may tend the growths and who may walk the channels to check the air. But there are people who are *better* at things, and these people do those things, and the doing becomes identity. The carvers are known by their hands — thick-palmed, scarred in specific ways that tell a story of decades of work. The tenders of the growths are known by their patience, their ability to sit in the canopy-dark for hours, listening to the creaking, feeling for the subtle shift in a leaf's texture that signals something is changing. The channel-walkers are known by their legs and their ears, their ability to cover long distances along the resonant grooves in the floor, interpreting the hums and vibrations like a doctor reading a pulse.

There are also the ones who make the tools. Copper, or something like it — pulled from the dark veins in the stone, smelted in the heat that rises from below, shaped with bone-hard implements that are themselves made from some mineral that fractures cleanly and holds an edge. These people work in the deepest basins, where the heat is most intense and the stone is richest in metallic deposits. Their dwellings are the warmest, the most heavily carved, the most filled with the small, specific clutter of a long-crafted trade — tools hanging from pegs in the walls, fragments of failed work collected in alcoves, the particular arrangement of stone and heat that makes their workspace function. They are respected. They are essential. But there is no hierarchy, no lord, no chief. The hollow does not produce rulers. It produces *workers*, people whose authority comes entirely from what they know how to do and how well they do it, and whose standing is measured not in power but in *usefulness* — in the depth of their contribution to the slow, ongoing project of living in a place that was never meant to be easy.

---

**They sleep in the deep rooms.**

The oldest chambers, the ones carved furthest into the basin walls, are cool by the standards of the hollow — not cool in any way an outsider would recognize, but cooler than the surface, insulated by meters of pale stone. The people sleep in these rooms, curled on low platforms worn smooth by generations of bodies. They sleep lightly, as people in loud places always do, rising with the sound of the channels, which shift in pitch as the air moves through them, a kind of tidal rhythm that marks the passage of what might be called day and night but is really just *movement* and *settling*, the hollow breathing in and breathing out on a cycle that the people have learned to entrain themselves to.

They dream, presumably. They must dream. But they do not speak of dreams in any way that an outsider can detect, and if you ask them about the inner life of sleep, they look at you with those wide, copper-tinged eyes and do not seem to understand the question — not because they do not dream, but because the boundary between what happens inside and what happens outside has never been, for them, the sharp line it is for people who live under a sky. In the hollow, the world is always speaking. The creaking, the hissing, the hum. To sleep in the hollow is to lower the volume, not to silence it, and to wake is simply to raise it again, and in between there is a space that might be dream or might be just the world continuing to talk while you are not quite listening.

---

**They have a name for themselves.** It is not a name you could pronounce without practice, because it is made of the same sounds as the place — low vowels, soft fricatives, a rhythm that breathes. But it translates, roughly, to something like *those who listen to the stone*. Not *those who live in the hollow*. Not *those who shape the rock* or *those who tend the growths*. They define themselves not by what they do but by their attention, by the act of *listening* — to the ground, to the vegetation, to the channels, to the slow mineral conversation that is the only conversation the hollow permits.

And this, more than the copper skin or the wide eyes or the worn hands, is what defines them. They are a people who *attend*. Who pay attention to a world that gives nothing for free and hides nothing in plain sight. They have learned, over the deep time of their habitation, that the hollow is not silent, not empty, not static — that it is, in fact, endlessly, patiently, *volubly* alive, and that the secret to living in it is not to master it but to *hear* it. To be quiet enough and still enough and *present* enough that the place tells you what it needs, and you provide it, and in return it sustains you, and the arrangement continues, as it has always continued, in the warm copper dark, in the spaces between the enormous quiet, in the belly of a world that no one else remembers.
