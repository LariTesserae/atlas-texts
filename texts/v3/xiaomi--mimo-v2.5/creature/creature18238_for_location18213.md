The Undergarden is not empty. It is inhabited by a civilization that never learned the concept of civilization, a city with no citizens who know they are citizens.

## The Keepers of the Drip

They are the smallest, the most numerous. No bigger than a human thumb, with delicate, chitinous limbs and antennae that taste the mineral-laden air. They move in processions that are almost mathematical, following faint chemical trails on the mycelium walls and fungal ledges. Their mandibles are not for biting but for carrying: a flake of silica, a fragment of shed chitin, a pellet of processed fungal matter. They are the builders, the movers, the recyclers. They deposit these offerings at the bases of new fungal growths, or tuck them into crevices where a fresh mycelial thread is reaching out. They are planting seeds, though they do not think of it as planting. They are tending gardens, though they do not think of it as gardening. They simply do it, because the scent of new growth triggers a compulsion to nurture it, and the scent of decay triggers a compulsion to dismantle it. Their trails form the city’s roads, worn smooth by the traffic of thousands of identical, silent workers.

## The Grazers

Larger. The length of a hand. They move slower, with a stately, unhurried gait across the vast, tiered platforms of the shelf fungi. Their mouthparts are broad, flat, designed for scraping. They graze on the velvet of spore-bloom that coats every surface, consuming the pale green, cream, and grey films in methodical sweeps. Their movement is a kind of slow orbit around their chosen fungal tower. They rarely descend to the floor. They live in the middle stories, in the galleries and open platforms, consuming the constant, slow exudation of the fungal city. On their backs, they carry more than just their own chitin. Transplanted fragments of lichen—tiny, resilient islands of green-grey—cling to specially textured patches on their carapaces. This is not accident. When a Grazer encounters a patch of lichen it deems especially lush, it will spend time rasping at its edges with a specialized forelimb, collecting spores and fragments, which it then carefully presses onto its own back garden. They are farmers on a mobile farm, carrying their pastures with them. Their waste, rich in partially digested spores and organic matter, falls to the levels below, where it is eagerly consumed by the Builders.

## The Weavers

They are the architects of the invisible. Found often in the narrow slots between structures, in the vaulted ceilings of the gathering spaces, hanging motionless from the stalactites. They resemble their smaller cousins, but are sleeker, their limbs longer and tipped with fine claws. They do not build with mycelium; they build with silk. From spinnerets at their abdomen, they produce strands of material that are nearly as strong as the chitin itself, but flexible. With these threads, they construct intricate, three-dimensional webs in the passages—not the sticky traps of surface spiders, but structural nets. They reinforce the walls of passages where the mycelium is thinning. They create suspension lines that hold swags of fungal growth in place, preventing collapse. They anchor the delicate, new stalactites rising from the floor, guiding their growth or protecting them from the drips that might damage them. They are the weavers of the city’s hidden infrastructure, the tendons and ligaments that hold the fungal bones together. They eat the tiny, winged things that blunder into their structural lines, but this is secondary. Their primary drive is to *connect*, to span gaps, to bind the space together with their silent, invisible work.

## The Sitters

These are the largest of the resident arthropods, rivals in size to the human forearm. They are solitary. They choose a prominent position—a high shelf, a ledge overlooking a central passage—and they *sit*. For days. For weeks. Their exoskeletons are thick, a deep, chitin-brown, often crusted with mineral deposits and fungal growth so they resemble part of the architecture. Their many legs are folded neatly beneath them. Their large, dark eyes, glossy and unblinking, survey the domain of slow movement below. They are not predators; their mouthparts are vestigial. They do not seem to eat in any conventional sense. Instead, they are the **Sponges**. Their porous exoskeletons, through capillary action and specialized structures, absorb the ever-present moisture and the microscopic nutrients suspended in it—dissolved minerals, free-floating spores, bacteria. They are living filters, passive participants in the water cycle. But their stillness serves another purpose. They are **Markers**. Their chosen perches become focal points, nodes in the network of chemical trails laid by the Builders. The presence of a Sitter alters the flow of traffic around it. Younger arthropods avoid their space. The fungal growth on their backs is often the most diverse, hosting entire micro-gardens that are periodically harvested by the smallest Builders, who climb them like living trees to scrape away the bounty. They are the silent elders, the immovable landmarks of a city in constant, slow flux.

## The Drifters

In the spaces between, in the damp air itself, they move. Cave-adapted moths, with wings like damp parchment. Flies with eyes like polished obsidian. Tiny, nameless things with delicate legs that walk on the surface tension of pool-water. They do not hum. Their flight is a silent, erratic glide, navigating the complex air currents that circulate through the fungal towers. They are the **Pollen** and the **Spore**. They feed on the airborne bounty of the city—floating spores, microbial films. In doing, they become vectors. They carry fungal spores on their bodies from one structure to another, cross-pollinating the city’s living architecture. They lay their eggs in the soft, new growth of the mycelium walls or in the damp decay of fallen fungal matter. Their larvae are another tier of recyclers, soft-bodied and blind, consuming the substrate from within. When they die, their tiny bodies are immediately colonized by the white threads of decomposer fungi, their chitin becoming part of the mineral cycle. Their silence is absolute. They are the city’s breath, its atmosphere made animate.

## The Sound of the City

And this is the truth of it: the Undergarden is deafeningly silent.

You hear the water. Always. The drip, drip, drip from a thousand points. The soft *hiss* of condensation falling from a shelf fungus in a steady, silent rain. The quiet *plink* as a droplet finds a pool. This is the city’s voice. The inhabitants contribute nothing to it. Their mandibles do not click. Their legs do not scrape. Their wings do not buzz. The fungal structures absorb all vibration, all noise, into their fibrous, dense mass. The sound of a thousand thousand lives is a pressure in the air, a density of movement and purpose that you *feel* in the bones of your face, but cannot *hear*.

The only sound is the water. And the water is the system. It carries the food. It builds the stone. It feeds the fungal towers. It is the clock by which all growth is measured. The city is built on, around, and in response to the water. It is the prime mover, and the only one who speaks. The inhabitants are actors in its play, moving to a rhythm you can only see, not hear.

You stand in a passage, and the weight of the silent industry presses in on you. A Builder pauses on the wall at your eye level, antennae twitching, then proceeds as if you were a stone. Above, a Grazer scrapes its slow path across a tiered platform, its lichen garden a soft green against the pale chitin. Further up, a Sitter is a dark, mineral-encrusted boulder on a high ledge, its eyes reflecting the universal, shadowless light. Between you, a Drifter makes a slow, looping turn and vanishes into a tunnel of woven mycelium.

They are all home. This is their place. It has no name for them, and they have no word for it. It is simply *where they are*, and what they do. The city grows around them, beneath them, above them, its own slow life measured in growth rings and mineral deposits. It does not watch you. It does not need to. It is watching the water, feeling the drip, reaching for the next surface, recycling the dead, growing, always growing, into the space that remains—which is none. The density is total. The silence is complete. The cold is constant. And life, in its own alien, industrious, and profoundly indifferent way, goes on.
