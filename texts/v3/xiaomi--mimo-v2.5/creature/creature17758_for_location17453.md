# The Inheritors of Keth Sula

You do not see them at first. You feel them — in the settled warmth of a passage that should be cooler, in the faint displacement of air as something moves just beyond the edge of your vision, in the way the insect-hum shifts its pitch when you turn your head, as though the colonies themselves are reporting your position to something listening.

Then, at the far end of a low-ceilinged gallery, where the amber-brown glow pools thickest on a floor of fired clay, a shape detaches itself from the wall.

**It was part of the wall.** That is your first thought, and it takes you a moment to understand why. The figure is coated — head, arms, torso, the exposed skin of the throat — in a thin, even layer of clay paste. Not dried and cracked, but *maintained*, kept moist and pliable, smoothed over the body the way a plasterer smooths a wall. The same fine-fibered mixture. The same faintly nubby texture. The same mineral smell. In the dim glow, against the mud-brick background, the figure was nearly invisible: a human shape pressed into a human-shaped niche, breathing slowly, waiting.

They are not tall. Few beings that have spent generations in a space where the ceiling grazes the crown of your head remain tall. Their spines carry a gentle forward curvature, not a deformity but an adaptation — the body learning to move through low passages without thinking, the head always slightly bowed, the neck strong and flexible. Their limbs are dense with the kind of compact, close-set muscle that comes from pulling oneself upward through shafts, from bracing against walls, from spending a life in vertical as well as horizontal space. Their hands are extraordinary. The fingers are short, the palms wide, the skin of the fingertips thick and ridged — not with calluses exactly, but with a texture that seems to have been *cultivated*, as though the fine mineral clay worked into the skin over a lifetime has become part of it. They leave faint whorled prints on everything they touch. They touch everything.

**The eyes are what tell you most.**

They are large. Not grotesquely so, but noticeably — wider-set than a surface-dweller's, the irises expanded to drink in every photon of that faint, living glow. The iris color is unusual: a deep, warm amber, the exact shade of the light itself, as though the eyes have absorbed the warrens' illumination and become reflectors of it. There is no white visible when the eyes are open. They catch the bioluminescent flicker of the insect clusters and throw it back with a faint, wet gleam. In total darkness — if such a thing exists here, in some sealed-off branch of the maze — they would be blind. But in the amber murk of the warrens as they are, they see more than you do. They see the slow pulse of the plaster's glow. They see the green heartbeat of the insects. They see the heat-shimmer of a body that does not belong, moving too fast, breathing too hard, a disruption in the equilibrium.

They are watching you now. Not with fear, not with hostility, but with the careful, unhurried attention of a being that measures time in seasons and decisions in decades.

**There are more of them.** Once your eyes learn to parse the warrens the way theirs do, you begin to find them everywhere — not because they were hiding but because you could not see them. A figure crouched in a dome-like loft above you, knees drawn to chest, hands working something small and pale between those wide palms — shaping a tool, perhaps, or preparing insect paste for the plaster. Two more in a wider passage that branches to your left, sitting across from each other on low platforms, their hands submerged in a shallow basin of clay slip, kneading it with a slow, rhythmic motion that matches the pulse of the light. A fourth, motionless in a crawl-space you hadn't even registered as a passage, only the eyes catching the glow, two points of warm amber in a darkness that suddenly feels much deeper.

They are not all the same.

Some are older. You can tell by the clay. The youngest among them — and there are young ones, small figures that move with a quick, fluid confidence through passages that would force you to crawl — wear their clay coating lightly, a dusting, a suggestion. Their skin beneath is pale, almost translucent, mapped with fine blue veins that are visible even in the dim light. They are fast. They move like the lizards, like something that has grown up knowing every handhold and foothold, every polished surface and every dead-end designed to confuse. They climb the columns. They press themselves flat against the ceilings in the vaulted chambers and peer down at you with an expression that is not yet old enough to be patient.

The older ones wear thicker coats. The clay on their skin has been layered, season after season, into something that approaches armor — not rigid, still pliant, but dense and patterned with the marks of specific tools, specific hands. Each elder's coating is unique: one bears the deep whorl-patterns of a thumb pressed repeatedly into the same spot on the left forearm, a gesture that might be habitual or ritual. Another has fine lines scored into the clay on the forearms and upper back — not decoration, you think, but notation, a record-keeping system written in the body's own surface. A third has embedded things into the coating: small, translucent shapes — the eyeless things from the pools, perhaps, or their shells — pressed into the still-wet paste at the temples and along the jaw, where they have dried into place and become part of the person.

**They do not speak.** Not in any way you recognize. Their mouths move rarely, and when they do, what emerges is not words but something softer — a low, subsonic modulation that you feel in your chest more than hear with your ears. It is the frequency of the warrens' hum, shaped and directed. They speak *into the walls*. They press their palms flat against the plaster and produce that vibration, and the walls carry it — through the clay, through the insect clusters that shiver and rearrange themselves in response, through the moisture film that ripples and stills. The lizards hear it. The insects hear it. Something in the pools hears it. It is a language built for a space where sound does not carry far but vibration carries everywhere, and it is as much a part of the warrens as the clay itself.

When they need to communicate at a distance, they use the insects. A specific pattern of tapping on a column — you watch an elder do this, three slow taps, a pause, two faster ones — and the nearest insect cluster shivers, contracts, and then *expands* in a visible wave, the dormant bodies waking and shifting, a ripple of green bioluminescence passing through the colony from one arch to the next, around a corner and out of sight. A signal. A message. The colony carries it, the way a nerve carries an impulse, to another cluster, and another, until the information reaches whoever needs to receive it. The warrens have a nervous system. It is made of chitin and bioluminescence and the slow, collective intelligence of ten thousand tiny bodies that have been shaped by generations of selective management into something that is not quite animal and not quite tool.

**They eat the lizards.** You witness this without being invited to witness it, which tells you something about how you are being perceived — as a curiosity, perhaps, or a disruption to be monitored. An elder sits in a small chamber near one of the pools, holding a lizard in both hands. The creature is still, not struggling — not dead, either. Its many legs twitch faintly. The elder holds it for a long moment, almost tenderly, and then presses it to the wall. The insect cluster there stirs. The lizards, you realize, are not food *for the builders alone*. They are offered — fed to the insects in some places, consumed by the builders in others, moving through the system as a transferable unit of energy. The elder eats the lizard raw, in small, deliberate bites, and the motion of chewing is slow, almost meditative. The bones — tiny, delicate things — are not discarded. They are pushed into the wall. Pressed into the plaster. Added to the structure.

Everything is added to the structure. Nothing leaves. Nothing is wasted. The builders' bodies, eventually, will be added too. You notice that certain niches in the deeper passages contain shapes that are not quite right for living beings — too still, too compact, their contours smoothed by fresh plaster into the wall itself. The dead are walled in. Their forms become part of the architecture, part of the substrate that the insects colonize and the lizards climb and the children's hands learn to navigate by. The dead are not gone. They are *load-bearing*.

**And they are curious about you.** This you understand gradually, because their curiosity does not announce itself. It manifests as proximity — the slow, incremental closing of distance over the course of what might be hours. A figure that was at the far end of the gallery is now at the near end. Another that was above you is now behind you, close enough that you can smell them: clay, musk, the faintly metallic tang of those translucent pool-things, and beneath it all the warm, living scent of skin that has never known the wind. They have not touched you. They will not touch you yet. They are learning the rhythm of your movement, the pattern of your breathing, the way your eyes move through a space that their eyes have already mapped ten thousand times over.

One of them — not the oldest, but one of the middle ones, their clay coating thick and elaborately scored — extends a hand and places it flat against the wall beside you. Not touching you. Touching the wall *beside* you. And the wall pulses. You feel it through the plaster, through the faint dampness, through the warm ceramic skin of the place: a slow, deliberate throb, and then another, and then another, in a rhythm that matches your heartbeat exactly.

They are listening to you through the wall. They have been listening to you since you arrived. The warrens are a body, and every surface is a nerve ending, and you have been *felt* — your weight on the floors, your warmth against the plaster, your breath adding its moisture to the air — the entire time you have been here. You are inside something that knows you are inside it.

The figure removes its hand from the wall. It looks at you with those wide, amber, wall-colored eyes. And then it does something that you almost miss, because it is so small and so quiet and so perfectly calibrated to the grammar of this place: it presses its other hand to its own chest, over the sternum, and holds it there. The clay coating over the heart is worn thin, the skin beneath visible — and the skin *pulses*, faintly, in time with the walls.

It is not a greeting. It is not a warning. It is a demonstration.

*We are the warrens*, it says without words. *The warrens are us. There is no difference. There has not been a difference for longer than your kind counts.*

You stand there in the warm, damp, teeming dark, feeling the slow pulse of the walls against your palms, watching those amber eyes adjust and readjust to the faint, breathing light, and you understand that you have not entered someone's home.

You have entered someone's *body*.

And it is deciding, with the slow and patient deliberation of a thing that thinks in centuries, what you are.
