You learn to see with patience here. Not the sharpened, active seeing of a hunter or a scholar in a fast world, but a diffuse, patient perception—like letting your vision go soft and noticing that the cliff face is not entirely rock.

Over there. That slight, smooth bulge in the oxblood stratum, three hundred meters below the rim. It has been there all morning. Or has it? The haze makes exact judgment difficult. You watch. An hour passes, marked only by the slow creep of a shadow one degree across the fluted tower opposite. The bulge is in a different place now. Not moved—*shifted*. It has turned, perhaps, a degree toward the diffuse sun-glow, exposing a flank the color of dried clay where it was pressed against the parent rock. Legs, or something like legs, were hidden in a crevice so perfectly matched to their form that their absence now is more obvious than their presence ever was. It will take another hour for it to be fully upright, and then another for it to begin its traverse across the vertical plane, descending toward a mineral vein its kind prefers. It does not hurry. It is a creature that thinks in shifts of dust.

This is **Lithovore**, the settlers call them, though their own chemical signals are complex and untranslatable. Lithovores are not rare. You see their signatures everywhere once you know: subtle mismatches in texture, shadows that imply a volume the rock doesn't possess, areas where the atmospheric dust has settled in a pattern that suggests recent, microscopic motion. A settlement’s outermost platforms are often dotted with small, granular Lithovores, no bigger than a human fist, that have drifted up from the canyon floor on the slow thermals. They feed on the atmospheric haze itself, filtering it through microscopic pores in a carapace that is genuinely mineral—a living geology. Their movement is a vibration at the edge of perception, a constant, silent sifting. The inhabitants pay them no mind. They are part of the dust.

Farther out, in the vast mesas that rise like islands from the haze, live the **Pillars**. They are the ones mistaken for outcroppings. A Pillar can be forty meters tall, its base indistinguishable from the natural stratification of the mesa. Its upper portion is craggy, fluted by the same slow chemical erosion that shapes everything else. For years, a surveyor might pass it, marking it as a geological feature. Then, one day, a change in the light reveals a line—the seam where a dorsal plate meets a flank. Or perhaps it is the season of mineral blooming, and certain iron compounds in the atmosphere cause its outer surface to shift hue from oxblood to a deep, bruised purple, a display meant for another Pillar kilometers away, a signal that takes a month to fully manifest and is read over generations. Pillars are the anchors of the ecosystem, though "ecosystem" is a word from wet worlds and feels clumsy here. They are the great, slow constants. They process the deep mineral veins, their internal chemistries breaking down complex compounds into simpler energy sources, excreting refined waste minerals in concentrated seams that draw the industrial settlements.

The settlements themselves are organisms of a different order. The largest, **Veridian Sorrow** (a name that translates poorly, meaning something closer to "Deep, Quiet Industry"), is a canyon-wall accretion that has been growing for four hundred years. It does not have blueprints. It has *precedent*. New levels are added to the bottom, following a freshly discovered vein of calcium-magnesium compound, because that is the logical place to expand—downward, into the resource. Workshops and living quarters are carved or fused from the waste rock, their layouts mimicking the natural fluting of the canyon for structural integrity. The conveyor systems are not straight lines; they follow the grain of the rock, curving with ancient erosion channels, making the whole complex feel less like a built thing and more like a growth that has exploited the rock’s existing weaknesses and strengths.

Life within moves at the Expanse’s pace. A smelter might take a week to reach operating temperature, its hydraulic bellows sighing once every few minutes, a rhythm as steady and slow as a sleeping giant’s breath. The people who tend it do so in shifts that are not measured in hours, but in task-completion. “I will monitor the second stage until the viscosity changes.” “The adjustment will be complete in three days.” They move through the dusty corridors with a mineral grace, their clothing coated in a permanent film of fine powder. They do not speak often. Communication is efficient, chemical in a sense—not through pheromones, but through a shared understanding of the environment’s rhythms. A glance at the haze outside the platform tells you the pressure wave is coming; no need to announce it. The way a colleague touches a pipe tells you its temperature without words.

Their art, if it can be called that, is the art of accretion. They decorate living spaces not with paint or sculpture, but with the careful arrangement of processed mineral samples—geological specimens that are also, in a way, their children’s toys, their market goods, their history books. A wall might display a sequence of fused blocks showing the changing color of the local stone over centuries of extraction, a slow-motion chronicle of their own impact. They have songs, but they are not heard; they are felt through the floor, deep rhythmic vibrations in the stone, patterns that take hours to cycle through, telling stories of vein discoveries, of collapses, of the slow drift of the great Pillars over decades.

The strangeness, the 2.2, is not an intrusion. It is the texture of reality. It is in the way a child learns to distinguish between rock and Lithovore not by sight or sound, but by the faintest difference in surface temperature felt through a calloused hand. It is in the way the oldest inhabitants’ bones have become slightly more dense, their bodies subtly heavier, as if the Expanse’s mineral patience has been written into their very calcium. It is in the realization that their civilization’s entire recorded history is a single, brief exhalation in the life of the formations they inhabit. They know this. It does not make them melancholic. It makes them practical. Why rush?

The silence is not empty. It is full of the slow grind of molecular exchange, of the patient filtration of atmosphere through living rock, of the hydraulic pulse of the factories. It is the sound of a world that has achieved a kind of equilibrium, not of peace, but of process. Everything is doing exactly what it is, at the only speed it has ever known. The wind carries the dust. The Pillars turn, almost imperceptibly, toward a new chemical signal in the air. The settlements grow, one fused block at a time. And the observers, standing on the rim, watch it all with the same unhurried gaze, their eyes adapted to a timescale where a minute is a blink and a year is a breath, seeing the deep, slow life of their world unfold in all its silent, mineral, and utterly alien grandeur.
