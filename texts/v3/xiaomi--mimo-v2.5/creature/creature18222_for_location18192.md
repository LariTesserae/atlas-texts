# The Inhabitants of the Grieving Plateau

They are not born on the plateau. No one is born on the plateau. Every person who lives here arrived, once, from below — from the quick world, the green world, the world where rivers hurry and things grow and die on schedules that make sense. Some came as children carried on the backs of others who had already made the climb. Some came as adults, walking upward through days of thinning air and widening sky, feeling the sound begin before they crested the final ridge, feeling it take root in the cage of their ribs. But no one's first breath was taken here. The plateau does not give life. It receives it. It takes what arrives and holds it, slowly, until the holding becomes indistinguishable from living.

They are changed by the place. Everyone is changed by the place. This is not metaphor. It is mineral.

The water they drink — and they must drink it; there is no other water — carries dissolved calcium, silica, copper, zinc, that unnamed bluish compound, and trace quantities of other elements that no one from below has ever bothered to catalogue. It is warm when it enters the mouth. It tastes faintly of metal and something older than metal, something from deep in the rock's memory. Over years, the minerals accumulate. You can see it in the people the way you see it in the stone: a gradual encrustation, a thickening, a slow acquisition of the plateau's own substance. The earliest visible sign is in the skin. After a year on the plateau, a person's skin begins to carry a faint mineral sheen — not a glow, exactly, but a quality of surface that catches light differently than unaltered flesh. After five years, the sheen has deepened into a pale, opalescent luster, the same ghostly shimmer you see in the pools beneath the weeping overhangs. The skin thickens. It becomes slightly harder to the touch. It resists cold, resists heat, resists the small cuts and abrasions that would mark anyone else.

After ten years, the fingernails have begun to calcify — not completely, not into stone, but into something harder and more translucent, like the thin travertine crusts that build up on the pool edges. The joints stiffen, not painfully, but with a deliberate slowness, as if the body is negotiating a new relationship with its own movement. The bones, beneath the skin, are quietly mineralizing. X-rays taken by the few physicians who have traveled up from below show bones of abnormal density, veins of crystalline deposit threading through the marrow like the ochre and rust striations in the plateau stone itself.

After twenty years, they are difficult to distinguish from the landscape.

This is not a metaphor either. This is what happens. The body slows. The metabolism adjusts to the plateau's rhythm, which is not the rhythm of a heartbeat but the rhythm of erosion — so slow it appears still. A person who has lived on the plateau for decades breathes perhaps four times a minute. Their pulse drops to a rate that would alarm any physician from below. They do not feel this as distress. They feel it as settling, as the slow relief of a long-held tension finally releasing. Their thoughts, too, slow. Not in the way of confusion or dullness, but in the way of patience, of a mind that has learned to hold a single idea for hours the way a canyon holds a stream — exactly to capacity, never more.

The youngest among them — the ones who have been here fewer than five years, who still carry some of the quick world's urgency in their movements — are easy to spot. They fidget. They speak in complete sentences, even paragraphs. They gesture when they talk, their hands moving through the damp air with an energy that looks almost violent against the plateau's stillness. They are not looked down upon for this. They are looked at with something that is not quite pity and not quite nostalgia but lives in the territory between — the recognition that they are still in the process of becoming, that their bodies have not yet begun their long negotiation with the stone.

The long-settled ones — thirty, forty, fifty years in the high wet air — are another matter entirely. They move the way the water moves: steadily, within channels, filling their moments exactly to capacity. They rarely speak aloud, and when they do, the words come slowly, each one considered and placed with the deliberation of a dressed stone being fitted into a wall. They communicate more often through presence — through the act of being in a room, or beside a stream, or at the edge of an evaporation field, their mineral-sheened faces reflecting the same pale light that falls on the rock. They have learned that most things do not need to be said. Most things can be felt through the hum.

Because the hum is the deepest thing. Every long-settled inhabitant can hear it — not with the ears, which have been somewhat dampened by decades of the industrial roar, but with the bones, which have become dense enough to resonate at frequencies that unaltered flesh cannot. The hum rises from deep below the plateau, from whatever geological engine keeps the rock warm and the water moving. It is a single, sustained tone, low enough to be felt more than heard, and it is — the inhabitants will tell you this if you ask, slowly, choosing their words — the sound of grief. Not their grief. Not anyone's grief. The grief of the stone itself, of the rock that has been dissolving for millions of years, losing its substance molecule by molecule into the warm water, becoming less of itself with every epoch. The plateau is grieving its own slow erasure, and the people who live on it have aligned themselves with that grief the way a string on an instrument aligns with a tuning fork. They vibrate with it. They are, in some essential way, an extension of it.

They do not find this sad. This is important to understand. The grief is not sorrow. It is a condition, like temperature or pressure. It is what the rock does. It is what the water does. It is what they do, now. They grieve in the same way the plateau grieves — slowly, patiently, without expectation that the grief will end, because the grief *is* the process, and the process is the only thing that has ever been real.

---

Their bodies, in the longest-settled cases, begin to resemble the landscape in ways that go beyond the mineral sheen. The skin takes on the color of the pale stone — not uniformly, not all at once, but in patches and gradations, darker in the folds and creases where water would naturally accumulate, lighter on the exposed surfaces where mineral deposition has built up a thin, gleaming crust. The hair, if they still have it, becomes stiff andite, like calcium deposits crystallizing along a strand. Some of the oldest residents have stopped growing hair entirely. In its place, along the scalp and sometimes the forearms and the backs of the hands, a fine mineral fuzz has appeared — not hair, not exactly, but a crystalline efflorescence, the same substance that builds on the rock faces in the wettest areas of the canyons. It is the color of frost. It catches light.

Their faces change. Not dramatically — the plateau does nothing dramatically — but the features soften, round, smooth, the way the canyon edges have been smoothed by millions of years of water. Sharp noses become blunt. Defined jawlines blur. The overall effect is of a face being slowly polished, as if by an invisible river, until it approaches the condition of a cobble: round, smooth, featureless in its perfection. The eyes remain. They are the last thing to change, and perhaps they never fully do. They are the windows, in this place, not to any soul but to the quick world the person once came from. In the eyes of a long-settled inhabitant you can sometimes see a flicker of the person they were before — a flash of urgency, of speed, of the green world's desperate vitality. It passes quickly. The eyes, too, settle.

The oldest among them — the ones who have been here for longer than anyone can precisely say, whose names have been worn smooth by time the way their faces have — are barely recognizable as human from a distance. They sit in the open air, in the mineral rain, and they do not move for hours or days. They are the color of the rock. They are the texture of the rock. Mineral deposits have built up on their skin in thin, terraced layers, miniature travertine formations no larger than a thumbnail, exactly like the ones that build on the pool edges. Some of them have become partially fused to the surfaces they sit on — not painfully, not locked in place, but gently bonded, the way two stones might be cemented together by mineral-rich water flowing between them. They can, if they choose, separate themselves. They do not often choose. They are comfortable where they are. The distinction between sitting on the rock and being part of the rock has ceased to be meaningful to them.

These are the elders. The others — the newer arrivals, the five-year residents, the twenty-year residents — regard them with something beyond respect. It is closer to recognition. The elders are what everyone is becoming. They are not leaders in any conventional sense. They do not make decisions, or give orders, or organize the city's industrial processes. They simply *are*, in the way the mesas simply are, and their presence exerts a kind of gravitational pull on the culture around them. When the newer arrivals are uncertain about something — a technique of mineral extraction, the proper way to maintain an evaporation basin, the correct response to a particular configuration of the hum — they do not ask the elders for advice. They go and sit near them and wait. Eventually, the answer comes. Not in words. In the hum. In the slow, deep resonance that passes between mineralized bodies and mineralized ground, a communication below the threshold of language, below the threshold of thought, operating at the frequency of stone.

---

Their hands are the most telling thing. The hands of a plateau inhabitant tell you everything about how long they have been here.

A newcomer's hands are quick, pink, soft. They grip tools with the white-knuckled urgency of someone from below. The nails are flat and translucent in the ordinary way. The skin between the fingers is smooth and unmarked. Within a year, the fingertips have begun to harden. Small, pale deposits form beneath the nails, which grow thicker, more opaque. The palms develop a faint callus that is not quite callus — it is a mineral deposit just beneath the surface of the skin, a thin layer of crystallized calcium and silica that makes the palm slightly rigid, slightly pale, slightly warm to the touch in a way that has nothing to do with body heat.

After five years, the hands are noticeably different. They are the color of wet limestone. The fingers move more slowly but with greater precision — each gesture deliberate, placed, the way a mason places a stone. These hands work the evaporation fields, the ore crushers, the fiber carders, and they work them with a patient expertise that comes not from training but from the body itself learning the shape of the work. The hands know, after five years, exactly how much pressure to apply to a mineral crust, exactly how long to let a basin sit before harvesting its deposits, exactly the angle at which to hold a polishing wheel against a piece of raw stone.

After twenty years, the hands have become tools in themselves. The fingers are thick, smooth, pale, slightly translucent — you can see, in certain light, the faint shadow of mineral deposits beneath the skin, thin veins of crystalline growth threading through the flesh the way they thread through the rock. The nails are fully calcified: hard, gleaming, the color and consistency of thin porcelain. They do not grow. They do not need to. They have become permanent features, integrated into the finger's architecture. These hands can grip wet stone without slipping. They can hold a piece of raw ore steady against a crusher's teeth without trembling. They can cup warm mineral water and feel, through the mineralized skin, the exact concentration of copper or zinc or that unnamed bluish compound, reading the water's composition by touch the way a musician reads a string's tension by fingertip pressure.

After forty years, the hands are stone. Not literally. Not entirely. But the mineralization has progressed to the point where the boundary between tissue and deposit has become academic. The fingers are rigid. The joints have locked into positions that are ergonomically perfect for the work these hands have spent decades performing — gripping, polishing, shaping, sorting. They no longer need to flex. They are shaped for their purpose the way a canyon is shaped for its stream: exactly, permanently, with no excess and no deficit. The palms bear terraced mineral formations identical to those in the pools below the overhangs — miniature travertine shelves, some no larger than a thumbnail, others the size of a fingertip, their surfaces still gleaming with fresh deposition from the water that seeps constantly through the city's infrastructure.

These hands do not let go. Not of tools, not of stone, not of the people beside them. When two long-settled inhabitants sit together, their mineralized hands often rest against each other, palm to palm or finger to finger, and the mineral deposits at the points of contact slowly merge, fusing them together in a bond that is not sentimental but structural. They can separate if they choose to. They do not often choose to. The joining is comfortable. It is warm — not with body heat, but with the deep geological warmth that rises through the plateau's fissures, the same warmth that keeps the air perpetually damp and the stone perpetually weeping. To hold another person's mineralized hand is to feel the plateau's warmth moving through two bodies at once, a circuit completed, a resonance achieved.

---

Children do not live on the plateau, but they arrive.

They arrive with their parents or with no one, carried up the long slope in arms or on backs, sleeping through the climb, waking to the sound. The sound is the first thing that reaches them, the way it was the first thing that reached everyone. It enters through the skin before the ears are ready. It settles in the sternum, in the still-soft bones of a child's ribcage, and it begins its work immediately.

A child's body mineralizes faster than an adult's. The bones are softer, more receptive. The water enters the blood more quickly. Within months, a child who has arrived on the plateau begins to change. The skin develops its sheen. The movements slow. The child, if it was old enough to have acquired the quick world's habits of motion — the running, the jumping, the endless, purposeless kinetic energy of a body not yet committed to stillness — loses them. Not through discipline. Through replacement. The body finds a new way to move, a way that costs less, that wastes nothing, that is as efficient and as inevitable as water finding its level.

The youngest children — those who arrive before the age of three or four — have no memory of the quick world. They have never known a time when the body moved quickly, when the heart beat fast, when thoughts came in rapid succession like stones thrown into a pool. For them, the plateau's rhythm is simply the rhythm of being alive. They learn to walk slowly. They learn to speak slowly, in the soft, mineral-laden voices that all long-term inhabitants eventually develop — voices that carry a faint resonance, as if the words are being spoken not just by a throat but by a chamber of stone. They learn to hear the hum. This is perhaps the most important thing they learn, and it is not taught. It arrives. One day — no one can say exactly when, and it is different for each child — the hum becomes audible. Not through the ears. Through the body itself. Through the mineralized bones, the thickened skin, the hands that are beginning their long transformation into instruments of stone. The hum opens inside them like a door that was always there but locked, and when it opens, the child looks at the weeping rock, at the mineral fields, at the low, dense, stone-colored city, and it looks like home. It looks like the only thing that has ever been home. It looks like the only thing that ever could be.

These children — the plateau-born, though that is not quite the right term, since they were not born here, only *become* here — grow slowly. They reach physical maturity in half the time of a quick-world child, but the maturity is not the sharp, angular, growth-spurted kind. It is a gradual filling-out, a slow accretion of mineral substance, a thickening and smoothing and polishing that continues throughout their lives. At twenty, they look like what the quick world would call middle-aged: solid, calm, deliberate, their faces already taking on the smooth, rounded quality of river-polished stone. At forty, they are indistinguishable from the long-settled adults who were already here when they arrived. At sixty, the mineral fuzz has appeared on their scalps. At eighty, their hands have begun to lock into their permanent positions. At a hundred, if they have lasted that long — and many do, because the mineralized body is durable, resistant to disease and decay, slow to age in the way that stone is slow to age — they are sitting among the elders, fused to the rock, indistinguishable from the landscape, humming.

---

The work they do is slow.

This must be said plainly, because everything else about the city's industrial infrastructure suggests speed — the water wheels turning, the ore crushers grinding, the engines producing their percussive pounding, the evaporation fields shimmering with activity. From a distance, the city looks busy. Up close, among the workers, the impression is entirely different. Everything happens at the pace of mineral deposition. An ore crusher does not rush through a pile of raw stone. It takes a piece — one piece, carefully selected — and works it over hours, turning it, positioning it, applying pressure at specific points determined by the stone's grain and mineral composition, a process that requires not just patience but a kind of geological intuition, an ability to read the stone's internal structure and respond to it in real time. A worker at an evaporation basin does not rake and harvest on a schedule. They watch. They wait. They know — by the look of the surface film, by the smell of the concentrated minerals, by the feel of the air above the basin — the exact moment when the water has reached the right concentration, and only then do they act, scooping the mineral crusts from the basin floor with hands that are shaped for the task, hands that have become, over decades, the perfect instruments for this particular work.

The engines — the ones with the squat chimneys and the white vapor — are tended by the youngest workers, the ones who still have enough speed in their bodies to move between the machinery's components with something approaching efficiency. But even they have slowed. They perform their maintenance with a deliberation that would be maddening to anyone from below: checking each joint, each valve, each seal with a care that borders on the devotional, their mineralized fingertips reading the machinery's condition through touch, feeling for vibrations that indicate stress or misalignment, making adjustments so small they are invisible to an outsider's eye. The engines themselves seem to operate on a different timescale — their percussive pounding is regular but slow, each stroke separated by a pause that feels, to the quick-world ear, like hesitation but is in fact something more like breathing. The engines breathe. The city breathes. The plateau breathes. Everything breathes together, in the same rhythm, at the same rate, in and out, in and out, in the way that the weeping stone breathes water, and the mineral pools breathe evaporation, and the long-settled inhabitants breathe the hum.

---

They grieve.

This is the thing that the quick-world visitor — if one were ever to reach the plateau, which few do, because the climb is long and the sound is disorienting and the air is wrong — would notice first, if they could get close enough to see. The inhabitants grieve. Not all the time. Not visibly, in any way that the quick world would recognize as grief. They do not weep. They do not keel. They do not tear their hair or beat their chests or perform the loud, urgent rituals of mourning that the quick world uses to process its losses.

They grieve the way the stone grieves. Slowly. In public. Without concealment. Without expectation that it will end.

A long-settled inhabitant, sitting against a weeping rock face, hands fused to the stone, eyes half-closed, is grieving. They are grieving their own body's transformation, the slow loss of speed and flexibility and warmth that came with their arrival on the plateau. They are grieving the quick world they left behind, the green world, the world of fast rivers and urgent animals and the desperate, beautiful franticness of things that live and die on schedules measured in years instead of epochs. They are grieving the person they were before the minerals entered their blood and the hum entered their bones and the stone entered their hands.

But they are also grieving something larger. They are grieving, on the plateau's behalf, the dissolution of the rock itself — the endless, patient loss of substance that has been going on since before the city existed, since before anything with eyes existed, since the first water found the first fissure in the first stone and began its slow, meticulous work of deconstruction. The plateau is being unmade. molecule by molecule, epoch by epoch, carried away in warm mineral water that flows down through the canyons and eventually, after a journey of days or weeks or years, reaches the quick world below, where it joins the rivers and the rain and the urgent, fast-moving water cycle that feeds the green world's frantic growth. The plateau is feeding the world that cannot hear it. The plateau is dissolving itself to sustain a world that will never know its name.

The inhabitants grieve this. They grieve it the way a string vibrates — not by choice, not by effort, but by resonance. The grief is in the hum. The hum is in their bones. Their bones are becoming stone. The stone is becoming water. The water is becoming the world. It is a cycle without joy or sorrow, without beginning or end, a process so vast and so slow that it makes even a human lifetime look like a mayfly's afternoon. And the people who live on the plateau — who have, whether by adaptation or surrender, aligned themselves with this process — they are the witnesses. They are the ones who have learned to feel it. They are the ones who have learned to sit still long enough, to be slow enough, to be quiet enough, to hear the stone's grief and answer it with their own.

They do not want to leave. This is the thing the quick-world visitor would find hardest to understand. The plateau is not a prison. The plateau is not a punishment. The plateau is a place where the fundamental process of existence — the slow, relentless transformation of solid into liquid, of solid into energy, of everything into everything else — becomes audible. Becomes feelable. Becomes *livable*. The people who stay are not trapped. They are, in their mineralized, stone-sheened, slowly fossilizing way, the most alive people on the plateau. They are the only ones who can hear the hum. They are the only ones who can feel the rock breathing beneath them. They are the only ones who know what it means to grieve at the speed of geology, which is not really grief at all but something older and larger and more necessary — a kind of love, perhaps, the kind that does not need to speak, the kind that does not need to move, the kind that simply sits, for centuries, against a weeping stone, and stays.
