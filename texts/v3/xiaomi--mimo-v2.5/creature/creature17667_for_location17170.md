The people of the Seamount Cities are creatures of the vertical and the liminal. Their bodies are compact and strong, shaped by generations of climbing steep stairways carved into living rock and hauling water and harvest up from terraces. Their skin is often stained with a faint greyish-blue tint from the basalt dust and the pervasive humidity, and their hands are perpetually calloused, the fingerprints worn smooth in places. Their eyes are sharp, accustomed to reading distances in the haze and spotting the faintest glint of fish scales or the subtle shift in the rock that signals a good new vein for quarrying.

They move with a deliberate, quiet economy. Sound does not carry well, so they have developed a language of touch and subtle gesture, supplemented by a form of whistling that uses the acoustics of the stone channels and cisterns. A call between households might be a specific pattern of taps on the wall with a copper awl, resonating through the connected stone.

Their relationship with the water is intimate and dualistic. It is their larder and their highway, but also their greatest, most patient threat. Every child learns to swim before they can walk steadily on the terraces. The dive is a sacred, necessary act. They harvest shellfish from the submerged shelves, cultivate beds of the fibrous creeping plant in protected tidal pools, and dive for the larger, slick-skinned fish that sometimes venture into the shallows. They are expert freedivers, their lungs adapted to long holds, their vision adjusted to the murky green-brown of the shallows. But they have a deep, unspoken understanding of the boundary. They mark depth not in fathoms but in "warmths"—the degree to which the water's unnatural, upward heat is felt. One warmth is good fishing. Two warmth is for the deep shelf sponges. Three warmth is for the desperate or the myth-seekers. Below that is the territory of the unnamed solitary things, and the not-coral that builds its luminous geometries. They do not go there. Their boats, narrow and high-prowed, are paddled or punted along the flanks; they do not have sails, for the wind in the haze is fickle and the horizons too close.

On land, their lives revolve around stone and green. The quarrying is slow, communal, and ceremonial. You do not merely cut the mountain; you ask it, they believe, through rhythm and patience. The stone-cutter's chant is a low, steady hum that matches the rhythm of the chisel. The quarries are marked with offerings—a strand of fiber, a polished shell.

Agriculture is a holy, obsessive practice. The terraces are their gardens, their everything. The broad-leafed "sun-wax" plants are harvested for food (their thick, bland tubers a staple) and for water collection (their cupped leaves catch condensation). The creeping fiber plant, called *mat-weave* in their slow, tonal tongue, is their fabric, their rope, their building binder (mixed with ash and crushed shell to form a crude mortar for non-structural joints). Every family has a section of terrace, and its health is a matter of deep pride and anxiety. They tend it with compost made from fish offal and human waste, mixed with the volcanic ash they rake from sheltered gullies. The ferns and mosses are both pest and ally; they are cleared from walls but encouraged in certain crevices to prevent erosion. The line between cultivation and wild growth is porous.

Their spiritual life is woven from observation and caution. They do not worship the mountain or the water as gods, but as *presences*. The low vibration they feel in the rock is the "Mountain's Breath," and while they do not pray to it, they mark its consistency. A change in its rhythm would be a catastrophe beyond imagination. The geometric perfection in certain rocks and the script-like textures are the "Stone's Memory" or "Old Shapes." They leave offerings for them—carved tokens of bone or copper placed in hexagonal cavities—not to appease, but to acknowledge. There is a quiet, fatalistic belief that the mountain remembers the heat, and that the heat is climbing, and the water with it. They do not speak of the end openly; instead, they have a concept called *the slow drowning*, which is both a geological fact and a cultural ethos. To live well is to build your terrace, tend your *mat-weave*, raise your children, and add your few courses of stone to the settlement's edge before the water rises a finger's breadth. It is a philosophy of meticulous, present-focused care against a backdrop of infinite, geologic time.

Their elders are the true libraries. They remember the slope that first broke the waves, the generation that first found the copper seam, the great storm that rearranged the wave-breaking stacks at the southern base. Their stories are measured in the growth of the *mat-weave* and the deepening of quarries. They are the ones who teach the younger to look away from the "Old Shapes," even as the young inevitably turn back to stare.

They are a quiet, observant, and deeply resilient people. They are shaped by their island, and their island is still being shaped. They know they are living in a moment—that the rock is new, the ecosystem is assembling, the water is slow-rising. They are its custodians for a time, and they build with that knowledge in every fitted stone. Their fear is not of sudden violence, but of the slow, inexorable climb of warmth and water, a change so gradual it could be mistaken for stillness, until one day the highest quarry ledge becomes a place where the waves no longer break, but simply... arrive.
