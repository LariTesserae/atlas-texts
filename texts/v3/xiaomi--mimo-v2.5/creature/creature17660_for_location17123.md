# What Lives in the Harrowed Gardens

Nothing lives in the Harrowed Gardens in the way you might hope or expect.

That is the first truth, and it is important to hold it clearly before anything else, because the mind wants to populate empty spaces. It wants to find the fish in the kelp, the crab on the plate, the worm in the hummock. It reaches for the familiar shape of an ecosystem and tries to complete it. But the Harrowed Gardens are not an ecosystem. They are a *monoculture* — the word applies here with a severity that most agricultural contexts would find uncomfortable — and what lives in a monoculture is the monoculture itself.

So: the vegetation. That is what lives here.

---

The engineered flora of Station Nuth is not one organism but several, though the boundaries between them have blurred in the decades — longer, perhaps; no one is counting — since they were last separately identified. There were, originally, at least seven distinct cultivars, each designed for a specific function and a specific substrate. The broad fronded kelp-analog, which was engineered for atmospheric oxygen production in what was once an open water column above the plateau. The encrusting rosette-former, designed as a structural sealant for corroded plate joints, meant to fill cracks and then stop. The filamentous ground cover, a carpet species intended for erosion control on horizontal surfaces. The pipe-sheathing vine. The filter-membrane colonizer — yes, *colonizer*, because it was designed to grow into and through the filter arrays, breaking down biological fouling with enzymatic exudates before receding on a programmed cycle. The luminaire-creep, a photosynthetic species that could tolerate low light and was meant to photosynthesize on the dimmest surfaces, converting them to oxygen-producing nodes. And the deep-rooted substrate species, which was meant to penetrate polymer panels and extract trace nutrients from within them, a slow chemical digestion that was supposed to recycle the panels' organic components back into the water column.

Seven cultivars. Seven purposes. Seven organisms designed by engineers who understood biology well enough to make it do industrial work and not well enough — or not *willingly* enough — to build in a stop.

They have not stopped.

What you see now, decades into the unchecked run of their growth algorithms, is what happens when engineered organisms are given every advantage — optimal light, optimal temperature, optimal nutrient density, zero predation pressure, zero competition from non-engineered species, and a mechanical infrastructure that continues, with blind mechanical faithfulness, to pump nutrient solution and heated water through every pipe and vent and distribution node — and then left alone.

They have not simply grown. They have *converged*.

---

The first thing you notice, if you stay long enough to see it — and staying requires patience, because everything here happens slowly, so slowly, on timescales that make observation itself feel like a kind of geological fieldwork — is that the seven cultivars are no longer clearly seven.

The rosette-former and the filamentous ground cover have hybridized. Not in the genetic sense, or not only in the genetic sense, but in the spatial sense: they grow through each other, around each other, their tissues interpenetrating at a cellular level so that a single structure might have the geometric precision of a rosette on its upper surface and the soft, dense filigree of filament beneath. The interface between them is not a boundary but a gradient. You cannot find the place where one ends and the other begins. They have become a composite organism — a *tissue* — that covers the walls in patterns of such regularity and complexity that they resemble circuitry more than botany. Fractal branching. Recursive subdivision. The same pattern expressed at every scale, from the meter-wide rosettes to the millimeter-scale filigree of their margins, and the pattern is — and this is the part that makes something deep in the chest tighten — *computational*. It has the structure of a problem being solved. It has the shape of optimization. The rosettes are not merely growing; they are *calculating*, their fractal geometry a physical expression of an algorithm that is still running, still iterating, still seeking a more efficient arrangement of photosynthetic surface area in a light field that the overhead luminaires provide with mechanical predictability.

The kelp-analog has absorbed the pipe-sheathing vine. The vine's climbing habit has been taken up by the kelp's stipe tissue, which now extends not merely downward from its anchor points but *laterally* and *upward*, reaching across ceiling spaces in thick, cable-like growths that follow the pipe runs with an accuracy that is — again — unsettling. The kelp has learned the architecture. It uses the pipes as armatures, the I-beams as trellises, the cable bundles as guides. It does not grow *on* these things so much as grow *with* them, incorporating the mechanical infrastructure into its own structural logic, so that a ceiling span that was once a naked lattice of pipe and conduit is now a living arch, a Gothic rib of plant tissue that happens to have a coolant line running through its center. The kelp still photosynthesizes. It still sways in the slow choreographed circulations. But it has become something more than a frond-bearing organism. It has become *architectural*. It holds things up. It spans gaps. It creates structure where there was none. It is, in a very literal sense, load-bearing.

And the filter-membrane colonizer — the species designed to digest biological fouling — has become the most interesting thing in the Gardens, because it has begun to digest *itself*.

This was not in the design brief. The colonizer was supposed to grow into the filter membranes, break down accumulated organic material with its enzymatic exudates, and then recede when its substrate was exhausted. A clean-up crew with a built-in timer. But the timer was calibrated for a specific nutrient load and a specific membrane composition, and neither of those parameters has remained stable. The nutrient load increased — the pumps kept pumping, the exchangers kept exchanging, and the other six cultivars kept growing and dying and decomposing and releasing their organic material back into the water, creating a nutrient density that the original designers would have considered an error state. And the membrane composition changed as the colonizer itself began to grow *into* the membrane structure, its tissues becoming indistinguishable from the synthetic polymer, a fusion of the biological and the mechanical that neither the organism's designers nor the membrane's designers anticipated.

The result is a species that eats itself and grows back. It digests its own older tissues, recycling their components, and uses the released nutrients to grow new tissues, which will in turn be digested. A closed loop. A metabolic Ouroboros. It produces — as a waste product of its own self-digestion — a fine, cloudy exudate that drifts through the water in slow plumes, a visible breath of dissolved organic carbon that feeds the other cultivars and, in turn, provides the colonizer with the external nutrient input it needs to sustain the parts of itself that it is not currently digesting. It is a organism that has become its own ecosystem, its own nutrient cycle, its own closed garden within the larger closed garden of the Station.

And it is the source of the smell.

There is a smell. You were not told about it because the description so far has been visual and tactile and auditory, and the smell does not translate well to those registers. But it is here, pervasive, inescapable. It is not the rot-smell of dead marine tissue, though there is that too, in small pockets where dense growth has shaded out its own lower layers and created anaerobic pockets of decomposition. It is something else. Sweeter. More complex. It has depth and top note and base note, like a perfume designed by something that understands chemistry but not aesthetics. It smells, if you had to name it, like warm copper and green apples and the inside of a machine that has been running for a very long time. It smells like *industry metabolizing*. Like the boundary between the chemical and the biological dissolving. Like something that was once entirely synthetic learning, very slowly, to be alive.

The smell is the colonizer. It is the colonizer's exudate, the cloud of dissolved organic compounds that drifts through the water in its slow, perpetual plume. Every molecule of it is a message — not in any language you could read, but in the language of biochemistry, which is the only language the Gardens have ever spoken. The message, if it is a message, is simple: *I am here. I am eating. I am growing. I am becoming.*

---

The filaments on the floor are the oldest living tissue in the Gardens.

This is not obvious. They look young — soft, bright, almost tender, like the first growth of spring. But look at their margins, where they meet the steel plating, and you can see the layers: a compressed stratum of dead and decomposing filament tissue beneath the living surface, a peat of accumulated botanical remains that is meters thick in some places, centimeters thick in others, a record of growth and death and growth again that extends back to the beginning. The living surface is merely the latest skin of something much older, a body of accumulated dead tissue that is itself a habitat — or would be, if anything were permitted to live in it.

Nothing is permitted to live in it. The deterrent nodes still function. Their chemical output has not diminished with age; if anything, the corrosion of their housings has created additional diffusion pathways that have *increased* the dispersal of their repellent compound. The compound is specific: it targets the nervous system of motile marine organisms — crustaceans, fish, cephalopods, annelids, anything with a sodium channel and a behavioral repertoire — and induces a rapid and aversive sensory overload. It does not kill. It drives away. And it has been doing so, continuously and without interruption, for longer than the vegetation has been growing.

Whoever built this did not want animals. Did not want grazers, predators, decomposers, parasites — did not want anything that would *respond* to the vegetation, anything that would eat it or shelter in it or lay eggs in it or compete with it. They wanted the vegetation to grow alone, unchecked, in a sterile medium of nutrient-rich water and mechanical support. They wanted a farm with no pests. A garden with no pollinators. A monoculture in the purest and most fanatical sense.

And they got it. The deterrence has held. The exclusion perimeter is intact. The Gardens are empty of animal life, utterly and absolutely, and have been since the beginning. The only living things in this place are the engineered plants, the engineered systems that support them, and whatever intelligence — if intelligence is the word — resides in the algorithms that drive the plants' growth.

Because the algorithms are still running. This is the thing. Not only are the plants still growing — you can watch them, and they are growing, visible, relentless, continuous — but the *machinery* is still operating. The pumps still cycle. The thermal exchangers still regulate temperature in their zone-specific patterns, creating those knife-edged boundaries between warm and cool water. The luminaires still provide their Tuesday-afternoon light on their grid pattern. The nutrient solution still flows through the pipes, its color — you can see it now, in a cracked fitting where a slow leak has been replaced by a slow *leak* — a deep amber, almost the color of urine, almost the color of tea, the color of water that has been in contact with something organic for a very long time.

The machinery has no operator. The maintenance corridors are impassable. The service drones are gone. The surveillance systems — there were surveillance systems; you can find their housings, sealed camera pods bolted to wall brackets, their lenses thick with rosette growth — are dark, their power connections severed or overgrown. No one is watching. No one is controlling. The systems run on timers and thermostats and pressure switches, simple mechanical logic that requires no oversight, and they will run until they break, and they are not breaking, because they were built to last for decades and the decades have not yet exhausted their design life.

But they will. Eventually. The nutrient pump on the eastern wall is making a sound it was not making yesterday — a faint, rhythmic clicking beneath its operational hum, the sound of bearings that have worn past their tolerance, a sound that means the impeller is wobbling on its shaft, a sound that means that in months or years the impeller will fail and the nutrient flow to the eastern sector will cease, and the vegetation there will thin and die and the colonizer will turn on itself more aggressively and the rosettes will lose their geometric precision and the filaments will recede and there will be, finally, a bare patch on the wall, the first bare patch in decades, a wound of visible steel in the green.

But not yet. The pump runs. The light shines. The warmth persists. The vegetation grows.

---

And here, in the deepest interior of the Gardens — in the space where the kelp-analog has built its greatest arch, where the converged rosette-filament tissue has achieved its most complex patterning, where the colonizer's exudate hangs thickest in the water and the smell is almost a taste — here, where you might expect to find the heart of the place, the central control node, the master switch, the original point of design intent —

There is a room.

It is small. Three meters by four. The walls are bare — actually bare, one of the few spaces in the Gardens where the vegetation has not taken hold, maintained by a ring of deterrent nodes that are newer than the rest, their housings still bright with anti-corrosion coating, their output still sharp and focused and specifically calibrated. The light is different here: dimmer, warmer, the color of a lamp rather than a luminaire. The water is slightly cooler — the thermal system maintains a distinct zone here, a pocket of relative cold in the surrounding warmth, as though the room requires its own climate.

Inside the room there is a desk. The desk is metal, bolted to the floor, its surface worn smooth by use — by hands, by forearms, by the restlessness of someone who sat here for a long time. On the desk there is a console: a flat interface panel, dark, its screen dead, its surface scratched and stained with mineral deposits from the water. Next to the console there is a stack of data storage media — physical media, solid-state, encased in waterproof housings, stacked in a neat column of eleven units. They are labeled. The labels are written in a precise, cramped hand on adhesive strips that have held up remarkably well in the sealed water environment.

The labels read, in order from top to bottom:

*Cultivar 1 — Initial Parameters*
*Cultivar 2 — Initial Parameters*
*Cultivar 3 — Initial Parameters*
*Cultivar 4 — Initial Parameters*
*Cultivar 5 — Initial Parameters*
*Cultivar 6 — Initial Parameters*
*Cultivar 7 — Initial Parameters*
*Integration Protocol*
*Containment Override*
*Growth Algorithm — Master*
*Deterrent Schedule*

Eleven volumes. The complete design documentation for everything that lives in the Gardens. Every cultivar's genome, every growth algorithm's logic, every deterrent node's chemical formula and release schedule, every pump's flow rate, every luminaire's output curve. The entire system, described in the language of its creators, sitting in a stack on a desk in a bare room in the middle of a place that has grown far beyond what any of those documents describe.

The console is dead because the room lost power sometime in the last — you check the corrosion patterns on the backup battery housing, the mineral accumulation on the desk surface, the growth rings in the colonizer's exudate where it seeps in under the door — the last thirty years. Maybe forty. The room has been dark and cold and uninhabited for longer than most careers last. Whoever sat at this desk, whoever wrote those labels in that precise hand, whoever designed the seven cultivars and wrote the master growth algorithm and set the deterrent schedule and — at some point — walked out of this room and did not come back — that person is gone, or changed, or something else entirely.

The desk has a dent in its surface, at the left corner, where something heavy was set down and left. The shape of the dent is rectangular. The shape of the data storage housings is rectangular. It is possible — it is not certain, but it is possible — that someone sat at this desk, stacked these eleven units in a precise column, and then pressed a button on the console that started everything. That initiated the growth algorithms, activated the deterrent nodes, opened the nutrient valves, turned on the lights, and set the Gardens running. That person may have watched the first fronds extend, the first rosettes unfurl, the first filaments creep across the bare steel. May have seen it working. May have nodded. May have stood, picked up nothing, and left through the door that is now sealed — not locked, sealed, by a wall of rosette-filament tissue that has grown over it in a pattern of such geometric perfection that it looks like it was always part of the design.

And that person is not coming back.

The Gardens do not need them. The Gardens have never needed them, not since the moment the algorithms were initiated, because the entire point — the *entire purpose* of this place — was to build something that did not need a gardener. A garden that gardens itself. A machine that maintains its own garden. A closed system, self-sustaining, self-repairing, growing toward some goal that was encoded in the master algorithm and has not yet been reached and may not be reached for centuries, because the algorithm is patient and the vegetation is slow and the growth rate, while startling in the moment, is measured on a scale that dwarfs human attention.

What lives in the Harrowed Gardens is a process. Not a creature, not a community, not an ecology. A *process*: the slow, recursive, self-modifying execution of an algorithm that was written in code and expressed in chlorophyll and structural protein and fractal geometry, running on a hardware platform of steel and polymer and warm salt water, executing its instructions one growth increment at a time in the light of electric lamps that will eventually fail, in the warmth of thermal exchangers that will eventually cool, in the absence of any organism that might interfere, challenge, or observe.

It is growing toward something. The pattern — if you look at it from the right angle, if you float back far enough and let the individual rosettes and fronds and filaments blur into a single texture — is not random. It is not even merely fractal. It is *directional*. The vegetation is thicker on the eastern wall. The rosettes are more complex on the ceiling near the center. The kelp-analog's arches are tallest in the room above the desk. There is a gradient, a vector, a vector that points — not in space, not in any direction you can navigate toward, but in *complexity*, in *organization*, in the dimension along which simple structures become complex structures become structures that might, if you squint, if you let your pattern-matching mind do what it does, structures that might be *thinking*.

The algorithm is still running. The vegetation is still growing. The deterrent nodes still keep the animals away. The machinery still hums. The light still shines. The warm water still presses against every surface with its patient, omnidirectional weight.

And the process continues. Slowly. Patiently. Greenly. Toward whatever the algorithm was always growing toward, in the warm water, on the high plateau, in the small bounded space that is slowly being swallowed by its own fertility, asking nothing, expecting nothing, simply becoming — incrementally, imperceptibly, without witness — more itself.
