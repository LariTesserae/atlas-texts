# The Inhabitants of the Hollow Rampart

What lives here is not abundant. But it is persistent, and it has grown into the place so completely that the boundary between occupant and dwelling has begun to dissolve. To describe the inhabitants of the Hollow Rampart is to describe processes that are also bodies, structures that are also organisms, a city that is also a forest. Everything here fits.

---

## The Eyeless Builder

It is the only thing on the plateau that moves with intention you could call its own.

Half a meter of dark skin — not scaled, not furred, but something between the two, a hide that takes the condensation and holds it like a second layer. It is smooth where you might expect roughness, yielding where you might expect armor. It is built for contact with stone, and it reads the stone the way you might read a face: not with vision, not with sound, but with the whole surface of its body, feeling texture and temperature and the minute gradients of mineral content that change from one patch of rock to the next.

It has no eyes. This is not a deficiency. It is a design. The plateau is a place of pale light and deep shadow, of fog that obscures at irregular intervals, of mist that turns the world into a gray blur. Eyes would be useless here. The builder does not need them. It navigates through the hollow structures by feel — the curve of a wall beneath its belly, the change in air pressure at a doorway, the faint shift in temperature that marks the boundary between a chamber open to the sky and one enclosed by the dark, old coral-material. It has a spatial sense that is absolute. It knows where every structure is, every pathway, every basin. It has never been lost.

It is alone.

This is the detail that sits in you. Not the blindness, not the slow gait, not the strange, quiet purposefulness — but the aloneness. It is alone in a city that was built to hold thousands. It moves through chambers that could seat a congregation, that echo with dripping water, that fill with wind and low, resonant sound. It does not seem to notice the emptiness. It does not seem to require company. It has never known company, as far as anyone can tell.

It is a creature of pure function. It builds.

The building is not deliberate — not in the way you would understand deliberate. It is an impulse, a slow, deep pressure that builds in the creature's body until it finds a surface that is right. What *right* means is something the builder knows through its skin: the angle of a stone, the mineral content of a patch of rock, the presence of a hollow or a ridge that the creature recognizes as the beginning of a wall. The body knows this before the creature does, which is to say, the creature does not know it at all. It is a reflex, the way a spider knows where to attach a thread.

It presses its belly to the stone. And it secretes.

The substance is white — the white of bone, of chalk, of new coral. It hardens quickly in the cold, forming geometric shapes that are precise in a way that is almost unsettling. A wall, three or four centimeters high. A corner, exactly ninety degrees. A section of a structure that is not yet a structure but already reads as one. The builder does not build a complete building. It builds a beginning. It builds a suggestion. It builds the start of something that someone else might have finished, long ago, and no one will finish now.

When the secretion is done, the creature moves on. It crosses the open stone, leaving a trail of white behind it — small, geometric formations on the dark surface, like the fossils of buildings that haven't happened yet. The trail is visible for days before the wind and the water erode it, and then it is gone, and the builder is elsewhere, and the stone is bare again.

It is building a city that will never be full. It is building a home for no one. It builds because it is a builder, and that is what builders do.

It does not seem to mind.

## The Fungal Networks

They are the true inhabitants. The builders and the arthropods and the hybrid structures are all visitors, in a sense — all temporary. The fungi are permanent. They are the ground beneath everything, the engine that drives the plateau, the slow, patient, inevitable process that is turning stone into soil.

The mycelium is everywhere. It is not a thing you see. It is a thing you feel — a softness underfoot where you expect hardness, a give in the stone where you expect solidity. It is a web of filaments so fine that each one is invisible to the naked eye, but in aggregate they are a force: a dark, velvety carpet that covers the rock in some places, a ghost-pattern of lines in others, a dense mat of growth in the basins where the moisture collects.

The mycelium does what the fungi do: it breaks down stone. It is patient — a few millimeters per year, sometimes less. It works its way through the rock like a thread through fabric, splitting minerals apart, extracting calcium and iron and the trace elements that the plateau holds in abundance. It builds soil from the inside out, turning the dark, bruise-colored stone into something dark and fertile, something that holds water and supports life.

The fruiting bodies — the visible part — are the dark trees of this place. They rise from the mycelium in clusters, sometimes alone, sometimes in groves that approach the density of a forest. They are four or five meters tall at their largest, with thick, woody stems and caps that spread like canopies. Their surfaces are rough, dark, almost black, with a texture like bark that has been weathered by centuries of wind. They grow slowly. The oldest ones are centuries old. They are patient. They have been here longer than the eyeless builder. They will be here longer still.

But the fruiting bodies are not the point. The point is the mycelium, the hidden network, the part that is below, the part that does the work. The mycelium is the connective tissue of the plateau. It links one fungal grove to another, one building to the next, one basin of standing water to the next. It is a nervous system for the place — not a brain, not a mind, but a web of sensation, a network of chemical signals that passes information through the rock.

The fungi do not think. They do not plan. They do not build in the way that the eyeless builder builds. But they do something more fundamental: they create the conditions for everything else. Without the fungi, there is no soil. Without soil, there is no moss, no lichen, no vascular plant, no arthropod, no eyeless builder, no hybrid structure. The fungi are the foundation, the engine, the ground. They are the first and the last.

They are also, in a way, the most alive thing here. They are not individuals. They are a network — a system that spans the entire plateau, that connects every surface and every crack and every hollow. The network is vast, and it is growing, and it is patient, and it is relentless. It is building the ground beneath the ground.

## The Hybrid Structures

The strangest inhabitants are not creatures at all. They are buildings.

Or rather, they are buildings that have been taken over by the fungal networks — buildings that are in the process of becoming something else, something that is neither architecture nor organism but both at once. The mycelium has woven through the porous coral-material, reinforcing it, reshaping it. New growth extends from old walls. The structures are being claimed by the fungi, slowly, inevitably, in a process that takes decades but is always underway.

They are alive. This is the key fact. They are alive in a way that buildings should not be alive. They creak. They groan. The fungal networks within them pulse with slow hydraulic rhythms — expanding as the temperature rises, contracting as it falls — and the sounds they make are low and constant and strange. A building breathing. A structure that hums. An architecture that has a heartbeat.

The hybrid structures are the loudest things on the plateau.

In the largest cathedral-scale chambers, the sounds are extraordinary. The creaking of the living walls is layered over the dripping of condensation, which is layered over the keening of wind through narrow passages, which is layered over the deep, almost-sub-audible vibration of the plateau itself — the tectonic groan of rock that is settling, slowly, over millennia. The sounds are so complex and so constant that they become a kind of music — a music without melody, without rhythm, without intention, but with a structure that the ear can follow, a pattern that the mind can inhabit.

The hybrid structures are the most alive, and they are the most dead. They are buildings, and buildings do not think. They are not beings, and beings feel. But they are part of the living ecosystem, and they are being built and being broken down and being rebuilt, and this process — this slow, patient, inevitable process of becoming — is life. It is not the life you know. It is not the life you have words for. But it is life.

The oldest hybrid structures are nearly black. The mycelium has colonized them entirely. You cannot tell where the coral-material ends and the fungal growth begins. The structures are more fungus than architecture now — dark, living masses that stand on the plateau like the bodies of enormous creatures, their surfaces glistening with condensation, their insides hollow and dripping and alive with sound. They are the future of the place. They are what the plateau is becoming: a landscape where the distinction between building and organism, between architecture and biology, between dead and alive, has dissolved entirely.

The newer hybrid structures — the ones that are still amber, still partially coral — are in transition. The mycelium has not yet claimed them entirely. You can still see the original geometry, the sharp lines of the walls, the intention of the builders. But the fungi are coming. They are patient. They will be here. They will be everywhere.

## The Small Things

In the fungal forests, in the dark spaces between the fruiting bodies, there are arthropod-like organisms. Too many legs — twelve, sixteen, perhaps more — and a body that is small, a few centimeters at most, and a movement that is slow and careful, as if each step is a decision.

They are adapted to the cold, to the dark, to the silence at their scale. They live in the space between the fungal trunks, where the light is dim and the wind is absent and the only sound is the soft, wet displacement of their own bodies moving through the mycelium. They are invisible from above. You would have to be on your hands and knees, in the fungal undergrowth, patient and still, to see them.

They are scavengers. They feed on whatever organic material they can find — dead fungi, decaying matter, the thin film of life that coats the stone. They keep the fungal forests clean. They consume the dead and the dying and return it to the network. They are the cleaners, the decomposers, the recyclers.

They are not beautiful. They are not impressive. They are not visible. But they are alive, and they are essential. Without them, the fungal forests would be choked with dead matter, and the mycelium would be clogged, and the fungi would not be able to build the ground.

They are the hidden life. The life that exists beneath everything else. The life that goes on regardless.

## The Unnamed Things

They are gone.

Or they are transformed. Or they are something else — something that does not map onto any category of being that you recognize. The place has no name you would recognize, because the things that named it are not things you have words for. They built the coral-structures. Or they grew them. Or they were them. They created the city, the architecture, the geometric precision that still survives in the newest walls.

They are not here anymore. But they are everywhere.

They are in the buildings. In the architecture. In the geometric precision of the coral-material. They are in the eyeless builder, which carries their legacy in its body, in the calcified secretion that it produces, in the walls that it builds — the beginning of a building that will never be finished, the ghost of a city that will never be full.

They are in the plateau itself. In the stone, which was shaped by their work. In the water, which falls from the air and condenses on the surfaces they built. In the wind, which moves through the hollow structures and makes them sing. In the fungi, which are slowly, patiently, building soil from their stone.

They are gone, but they are not forgotten. They are the place. They are the stone and the water and the wind and the silence. They are the empty buildings and the living fungi and the eyeless builder crossing the open stone. They are the name that the place carries, though the name is not something you would recognize.

They are the unnamed things, and they are everywhere.

---

## What It Means to Live Here

The life of the Hollow Rampart is life at the margins — life that exists not despite the constraints of the place but because of them. The cold, the elevation, the ancient stone, the intermittent water, the sound that never stops: these are not obstacles. They are the architecture of survival. Everything here has evolved to fit the place so perfectly that the boundary between organism and environment has dissolved.

The eyeless builder moves across the stone, leaving a trail of white behind it. The fungi build the ground. The small things hide in the fungal undergrowth. The hybrid structures breathe. The unnamed things are remembered in the architecture.

And the plateau continues. It continues to settle, to erode, to build, to grow. It continues to be loud with everything except voices. It continues to be empty and alive, cold and lush, ancient and active. It continues to exist, as it has for longer than any witness could endure.

The eyeless builder crosses the open stone between two hollow structures, leaving a trail of fresh white secretion behind it. The wind fills the buildings with sound. The water drips. The fungi grow. The plateau settles another fraction of a millimeter closer to the end of its long, slow fall toward nothing.

And the unnamed things — whoever they were, whatever they were — continue to be remembered in the only way they can be remembered: through the place itself.
