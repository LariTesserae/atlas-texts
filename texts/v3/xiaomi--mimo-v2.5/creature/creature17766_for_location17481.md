## The People of the Mireworks

The first thing you notice about the inhabitants — if you are a visitor, and they can always tell — is their skin. Not green, exactly. Not the color of the leaves above them. More the color of light passing through a leaf: a pale, warm ivory with an undertone of jade that deepens at the knuckles, the elbows, the backs of the hands where the veins sit close to the surface. Some people are greener than others. The elders tend toward a deep, almost sage complexion, as though decades of breathing spore-heavy air and drinking basin-water has slowly impregnated them with chlorophyll. The young are paler, closer to the white of river birch bark. Every child born in the Mireworks comes into the world faintly pink and over the first year of life drifts steadily toward green. The midwives watch for this. A child who stays pink is a child to worry about.

Their eyes are accustomed to the twilight. Pupils perpetually dilated, irises dark — brown so deep it's nearly black, in nearly everyone, regardless of family lineage. They navigate the dim interiors by instinct and by touch, and visitors often remark that the people of the Mireworks move through their own town with a fluid, unhesitating grace that borders on unsettling. They never bump a lintel. They never step into a channel by accident. They know the buildings by the way the wood feels under their palms, and they know the channels by smell, each one carrying its own signature of mineral content and biological load — the faint metallic sweetness of the eastern drains, the sharper, almost medicinal tang of the runoff from the processing district.

They are not a large people. Large people would be at a disadvantage here — in the low doorways, in the narrow passages between buildings where the hedges have been allowed to swell a little too far, in the low-ceilinged rooms where the canopy-roofs sag under the weight of rainwater. They are compact, dense-boned, quick in their movements but not hurried. There is a difference, and it matters. Hurrying in the Mireworks means slipping on wet root-mat, means missing a cut in the hedge that will bud back twice as thick by the next morning, means letting a channel choke while you're busy rushing somewhere else. The people do not hurry. They move with the kind of efficient, purposeful rhythm that looks slow to outsiders but covers enormous ground over the course of a day.

Their hands are their most distinctive feature. Broad-palmed, short-fingered, with thick calluses that are themselves faintly green — a permanent stain from years of handling pillarwood and moss-mat and the iron tools of the beating mills. The nails are kept short. Not for hygiene but for practicality: long nails catch on vine-lattice, snag the fine fibers during extraction, collect the algae that grows on every surface and that no amount of washing fully removes. Every pair of hands you see in the Mireworks tells you what that person does. The weavers have flattened fingertips and a distinctive pattern of scars along the outer edge of the left hand, from the tension-bars of their loom-frames. The channel-keepers have hands that are perpetually waterlogged — the skin wrinkled even when dry, the nails soft and peeling. The pillarwood-guides have the strongest hands of anyone, and the strangest: years of binding and training saplings into architectural forms has given them a grip strength that borders on grotesque. A master guide can crush a green walnut in their fist without apparent effort.

## The Families

No one in the Mireworks is a stranger, and no one is alone.

The basic unit is the maintenance cell — a group of three to eight people who jointly own and care for a section of the town. This is not always a family by blood, though blood families tend to cluster. It is a family by function: a household of people who share walls, who share channels, who share the daily labor of trimming and training and harvesting and repairing that keeps the town from being swallowed. You are born into a cell. You may leave it — if you marry into another cell, or if the council of elders determines that a different cell needs your skills — but leaving is rare, and it is felt. It is like a transplant. Sometimes it takes. Sometimes the new cell rejects you, and you know it within a season, by the way the hedge-walls in your new dwelling seem to grow toward you a little too eagerly, as though testing whether you belong.

The cells are led, loosely and without formal title, by the person who has lived in the cell longest. This is not always the oldest person. Injuries and illness claim the Mireworks' inhabitants at a steady, unhurried rate — not often enough to feel catastrophic, but often enough that every cell carries its gaps, its missing members, its ghosts. A person who was born in a cell and never left will almost always be its leader by middle age, but a person who arrived by marriage twenty years ago and has proven themselves through the daily maintenance may hold equal authority in practice. Leadership in the Mireworks is not declared. It is recognized. The person who knows which wall needs trimming, which channel is running warm, which section of pillarwood is ready for harvest, which roof-mat needs reinforcing before the rainy season — that person leads, regardless of age or sex or lineage.

Children are raised communally within the cell. The person who gave birth to you is your mother, and this matters, but she is not the only adult who feeds you, teaches you, corrects you, and puts you to sleep. By the time a child in the Mireworks is old enough to hold a trimming blade — which is younger than you might expect; five, six years old in the drier quarters where the hedges are less aggressive — they have been taught by every adult in the cell, and they know every adult's quirks and preferences and tolerances. They know not to wake the person who works the night channel-shift. They know to bring the first harvest of the broadleaf canopy-roof to the person with the bad knees, because that person taught them to climb and the climb is what damaged the knees. This knowledge is layered, accumulative, and it creates bonds that are not sentimental but structural — as load-bearing as the pillarwood walls themselves.

## The Workers

The beating mills are staffed by the strongest of the population, and they are almost universally young — twenties and thirties, when grip strength is at its peak and the lungs can still handle the haze of fiber-mote that fills the mill-houses. The work is seasonal, or rather it is cyclical: the plantation harvests come in bursts, timed to the growth cycles of the pillarwood and bamboo stands on the basin's drier fringes, and when the harvest arrives, the mills run at capacity for weeks. The work shifts are short — four hours in the cool of early morning — but they are intense. The hammers of the beating mills strike at roughly two hundred blows per minute, and the sound is not just loud but percussive, a physical pressure against the chest that visitors from drier countries describe as feeling like being struck repeatedly by something invisible. The mill-workers wear their hearing as a badge, the way miners in other places wear their soot. They speak loudly even outside the mills. They gesture more than other Mireworks inhabitants, compensating for a world that has become quieter for them than it is for everyone else.

The mill-workers eat enormously. Plant-fiber processing demands calories — the sustained muscular effort of managing the cams and belts, of loading the steaming vats, of hauling the pressed boards onto the barges — and the food is correspondingly dense. The Mireworks diet is almost entirely plant-based: the basin supports no livestock of any scale, and the few fish that occasionally colonize the deeper channels are considered unclean, though no one can articulate exactly why. Protein comes from legumes — a species of aquatic bean that grows in the warmer channels, its roots tangled with the algae, its pods harvested by wading workers in the early months of each season. The beans are bitter, slightly astringent, and they are eaten in prodigious quantities. Mill-workers carry them in cloth pouches at their belts, chewing them raw between tasks. The bitterness, it is said, counteracts the drowsiness that the humidity induces, though whether this is physiology or tradition is impossible to determine.

The canal-keepers are a different kind of worker entirely. They are the town's circulatory system — the people who maintain the channels that carry water, waste, runoff, and coolant through the settlement's body. Their work is unglamorous, persistent, and absolutely essential. A blocked channel in the Mireworks does not just cause inconvenience; it causes structural damage, because the water in the channels serves a second function that outsiders often miss: it carries nutrients to the root systems that anchor the buildings. The pillarwood walls are alive, and they need to drink. The channels are their irrigation. A canal-keeper who allows a channel to choke is not just negligent — they are threatening the structural integrity of every building in their cell. The punishment for this, in the rare cases it occurs, is not punitive but corrective: the negligent keeper is reassigned to channel maintenance for a period measured not in days but in growth-cycles. They spend a full year cleaning, clearing, and monitoring the channel they failed, and they do so under the supervision of the cell's most experienced canal-keeper, who does not speak to them about what they did wrong. The lesson is the work itself.

The canopy-tenders work above, in the perpetual green twilight of the roof layers. They are the lightest and most agile of the population — small-statured, flexible, with an instinctive understanding of how the broadleaf canopy-trees distribute their weight across their aerial roots. They move through the upper stories of the town like a species apart, always looking up, always watching for the telltale swell of new growth that indicates a branch is about to shift the load-bearing geometry of a roof-mat. When they find one, they mark it — a cut of a specific shape into the bark, visible only to other tenders — and then they return with the proper training materials and redirect the growth. This can take months. A canopy-tree that has decided to grow in a particular direction does not change its mind easily, and the tender must persuade it through a sequence of increasingly specific interventions: binding, scoring, selective pruning, the introduction of competing growth-points that redirect the tree's energy. It is botanical negotiation, and it requires patience of a kind that few other occupations demand. The canopy-tenders are, as a result, the quietest and most deliberate people in the Mireworks. They speak softly, move softly, and have a reputation — partly deserved, partly mythologized — for being able to think in timescales measured in decades.

## The Children

Children in the Mireworks are raised wet.

This is not cruelty. It is environment. They learn to walk on root-mat that squelches under their weight. They learn to sit before they learn to walk, planted on the raised platforms of their cells' dwellings, watching the slow movement of water in the channels below with the patient fascination of people for whom water is as constant and as unremarkable as air. By the time they are old enough to be trusted on the paths — three years old, four — they have already developed the Mireworks' signature gait: slightly bow-legged, weight forward, eyes always scanning the ground ahead for changes in texture that indicate a soft spot, a shift in the channel, a new growth that wasn't there yesterday.

They play, of course. But their play is different from the play of children in drier places, because the world they play in is alive and responsive. A game of chase in the Mireworks is a game played through a landscape that changes as you move through it: you duck under a vine that wasn't there an hour ago, you climb a root-mat that has sent out a new lateral since morning, you hide behind a hedge that will, if you stay still long enough, begin to grow around you. The children know this. They use it. Hide-and-seek in the Mireworks is a serious proposition, because the hiding places actively participate. A child who finds a good spot — a fold in a hedge, a gap between aerial roots, a depression in the channel-bank — may find, to their delight, that the vegetation begins to close around them, offering genuine concealment. This is not learned behavior on the part of the plants; it is simply growth. But the children anthropomorphize it instantly and completely, and the adults do not correct them, because the relationship between the people of the Mireworks and the vegetation is, at its core, one of mutual negotiation with a force that is powerful, indifferent, and only partly comprehensible. It is better to begin that negotiation early, and with wonder.

The children also learn, very young, the sound of the mills. This is important, because the sound of the mills tells you things. The rhythm of the hammers indicates which machines are running, which means which stage of processing is underway, which means approximately how much raw material is currently in the system, which means how much labor will be needed in the coming days. A child who can read the mill-sound can read the town's economic state, and this skill — passed informally, almost unconsciously, from parent to child — is one of the Mireworks' most important cultural technologies. It is never taught in a lesson. It is absorbed, the way the moss-mat absorbs water: slowly, pervasively, and with the appearance of effortlessness that conceals enormous complexity.

## The Elders

The council of elders meets in the oldest quarter, in a structure so ancient that its pillarwood walls are nearly black with age, their rings so compressed that they resemble stone. The room is small — barely four meters across — and it is always damp, and it is always warm, and it smells of old wood and old water and the faintly metallic tang of the basin's mineral load. There are no chairs. The council sits on a raised platform of root-mat, their legs crossed, their hands resting on their knees, and they talk.

They talk slowly. Not because they are slow but because they are choosing words with the same care they choose cuts on a living wall — precisely, with full awareness that what they say will be carried forward and will persist. The Mireworks has no written language. There are marks on walls — guide-cuts, harvest-marks, the shallow notation system that the channel-keepers use to record flow rates — but there is no formal script, no literature, no archive. The town's history lives in the memories of its oldest inhabitants, passed down through the cells in the form of stories, songs, and the particular way a person says *this wall was moved in the flood of such-and-such year, and here is what we learned.* The council's deliberations are slow because they are simultaneously a meeting, a history lesson, a policy debate, and a communal act of remembering. An agenda item that has been on the agenda for decades is not a failure of governance. It is a problem that requires decades of consideration, because the solution, when it comes, will be embedded in living wood that will remember the decision for a century.

The eldest among them — the person who has been maintaining their cell the longest, who has more years of seasonal cycles in their memory than anyone else alive — carries a particular authority that is not formal but is absolute. This person does not chair the meetings. They do not set the agenda. They sit in the same circle as everyone else and they speak in the same slow, deliberate way. But when they speak, the others listen with a particular quality of attention — not deference, exactly, but recognition. This person has seen more seasons than anyone in the room. They remember the last time the basin's water table rose by a full meter. They remember the pillarwood blight of an earlier generation. They remember which decisions worked and which didn't, and their memory is the closest thing the Mireworks has to a written record. When the eldest speaks, they are speaking not just for themselves but for the deep time of the town itself.

## The Barge-People

There is a class of inhabitant who exists slightly apart from the rest: the barge-workers, who navigate the channels southward to the river and beyond, carrying the fiber-boards to market and returning with what the basin cannot provide. Iron. Salt. Seed stock from drier regions. News.

These people are recognizable not by their skin or their hands — those are the same green-white, the same broad and calloused — but by their eyes, which look outward. In a town where everyone's attention is directed inward, toward the maintenance of the living walls and channels and roofs, the barge-people are the ones who look past the canopy, toward the horizon, toward the flat waterlogged plain and the sky above it. They have seen the places where the water table drops below a meter. They have seen towns where buildings are made of dead material — stone, fired brick, treated lumber — and where the air is clear and thin and carries no scent of growth. They have seen this, and they have come back, and the question of why they come back is one that the town does not ask directly but considers constantly.

The barge-people leave on a cycle that matches the plant-processing calendar: out with the finished boards, back with raw materials and time to reload. A single trip takes the better part of a week — the channels are slow, the barges are heavy, and the water-level fluctuates with the seasons in ways that require constant attention to navigation. They travel in pairs or threes, never alone, because the outer channels are where the vegetation is least managed and most aggressive, and a barge that stalls in an unmanaged stretch can be overgrown in days. Not consumed — the plants have no interest in consuming iron or wood — but obscured, hidden, made invisible from the navigable channels so thoroughly that it might as well not exist.

When the barge-people return, they bring more than goods. They bring the outside world's perspective, and this is the town's most precious and most dangerous import. A person who has seen the outside knows that the Mireworks is unusual. They know that other people live in dry buildings and drink clear water and can see the sky from their doorsteps. This knowledge does not fit comfortably in the green-filtered world of the town. It sits like a stone in a channel — not damaging, not blocking, but present, creating a slight turbulence in the flow of things. The council of elders knows this. They welcome the barge-people back with warmth and they listen to their reports with genuine interest and they do not, under any circumstances, allow the barge-people's perspective to set the agenda. The town's pace is its own. The town's choices are made on the town's timeline. The outside world can wait.

## What They Are

They are gardeners, engineers, builders, and custodians of a living system they did not create but have learned to inhabit with extraordinary skill. They are not primitives. The engineering of the beating mills, the hydraulic management of the channel network, the botanical science of pillarwood cultivation and canopy-tree training — these are sophisticated technologies, refined over generations, demanding knowledge that is no less complex for being biological rather than mechanical. A person who can look at a pillarwood wall and tell you by its ring-pattern exactly how old it is, how fast it grew, where it was stressed, and when it will be ready for harvest is performing a kind of analysis no less rigorous than an engineer reading a blueprint.

They are not happy, exactly, in the way that visitors from drier places understand happiness. They are not unhappy either. They are *engaged* — bound up, day after day, in the maintenance of a world that requires their constant attention and that rewards that attention with the deep, slow satisfaction of things that grow well. They take pride in a well-trimmed hedge-wall. They take pride in a channel that runs clean and clear. They take pride in a pillarwood beam whose grain is tight and straight and whose rings tell the story of a decade of perfect, patient guidance. These are not small things. These are the things that hold the world up, and they know it.

They smell like their town — warm, green, wet, metallic, alive. They carry the scent in their clothes, their skin, their hair. A person from the Mireworks, standing in a dry place, can be identified by smell before they are seen. This is not a thing they are self-conscious about. It is the smell of home, and home, in the Mireworks, is a living thing that you carry with you the way you carry your own heartbeat — always, unthinkingly, and with the full knowledge that if it stops, everything stops with it.

They are patient. They are strong. They are quiet when quiet matters and loud when the mills are running, which is most of the time. They sit on their doorsteps with their feet in the channel-water and they watch the green light filter down through the canopy layers and they breathe the warm wet air and they wait for the things they have planted to grow, and grow, and grow.
