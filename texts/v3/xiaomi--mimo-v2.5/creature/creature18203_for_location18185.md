They are the ones who tend the engines.

They are not what built them — that was another time, another purpose, a surface world’s ambition buried and forgotten. But they are the ones who now know their rhythms. You see them at dusk, which is a cycle of light and dark they keep here below: when the bioluminescence flares and the condensation drips increase, they emerge from the hollow columns where they nest. Their bodies are built for this gloom: eyes are vestigial, mere smooth patches of skin over deep sockets, but their other senses are acute. They feel the vibrations through their feet, through their palms pressed to the vibrating walls of their homes. They smell the chemical shifts in the air, the slow change in the amber drips that tells them when a concentration is ready to be processed.

They are not large, but they are solid. Limbs thickened for climbing, for hauling. Hands not delicate but broad, with multiple opposable digits that can grip pipe wrench or stem with equal ease. Their skin is mottled, pale where it sees no light, stained with chlorophyll and rust where it works. They move with a slow, deliberate economy, speaking little. Communication is mostly tactile, a hand on a shoulder, a tap on a column, a vibration transmitted through metal. In the pools, they sometimes gather to rest, their lower bodies submerged, their senses quiet, listening to the thrum of the great absorbed engine as it resonates in the water and their bones.

They do not think of themselves as stewards. The concept is too clean. They are more like antibodies, or perhaps the hands of a body that doesn't know it's alive. They go about their tasks: tightening a bolt that has been loosened by a root’s growth, clearing a filter clogged with larval casings, redirecting a flow of processed syrup to a new basin where a colony of luminescent fungi is being cultivated. They eat the fungal growths, the larger larvae they pull from the pools, the thick paste of fermented plant matter that collects in certain vats. They drink the cooled, settled runoff from the engines, which is sweet and faintly intoxicating.

They are curious about you, in their way. Not your form, not your origin, but your heat signature, the unfamiliar sounds you make, the way you move through their space without touching the columns, without feeling the vibrations. They observe from catwalks, their silhouettes blotting out the dim blue-green glow for a moment. A child — smaller, quicker, still learning the rhythms — will sometimes drop a thread-dangling snare or a piece of compacted substrate near you, just to see what you do. They are not afraid. Fear is for things that disrupt the balance, and you are, so far, simply another quiet anomaly, like a new fungus growing in an old groove.

Their history is not spoken; it is *in* the place. You see it in the way the oldest among them run their hands over tool marks on the column walls, marks made by hands not shaped like theirs. You see it in the ritualistic way they sometimes polish a panel of corrugated metal, exposing a faded logo or serial number from a world of open sky. They do not know what the symbols mean, but they know the action is important, a way of acknowledging the bones of the house they live in. They tend the engines because the engines keep the moisture moving, and without the moisture, the ceiling would dry, the drips would stop, the pools would vanish, and everything — the columns, the six-legged grazers, the whining swarms, the writhing larvae, their own lives — would unravel.

So they work. They are born in the hollow columns, they learn the thrums, they take their places in the slow, eternal maintenance. When they die, they are carried to the deepest pools and left to become food, their nutrients entering the cycle. It is not a belief. It is a fact, like the smell of sap and ozone, like the punishing noise of the wings, like the warm, sweet darkness that is their only sky.

They do not look up. There is nothing up there but the great, breathing ceiling and its amber rain. Their world is the space between, the forest of columns, the maze of pipes, the living warmth. It is enough. It is complete. They are a part of it, as essential as the engines, as mindless and as vital as the larvae, as permanent as the slow, geological patience of the columns themselves.
