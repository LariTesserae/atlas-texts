# What Lives in the Vaelken Basin

---

## The Glow-Eaters

Begin where the light begins.

In the pools — every pool, from the smallest basin no wider than a palm to the great central eye — the water is alive with luminescence, and the luminescence has texture when you look closely enough. It is not uniform. It moves in slow whorls and streamers, thickening in places where the chemical bleed from the rock is richest, thinning where the water has been standing too long and the nutrients are spent. There is a geography to the glow, a map of the basin's subterranean circulatory system written in blue-green light on the water's surface.

The organisms responsible — let us call them *threadweavers*, for lack of a better term and because naming them anything more precise would require a biology that doesn't exist yet — are visible only as a kind of luminous fog. Individual organisms are microscopic. Colonies are not. A mature colony in a freshly emerged pool forms a dense, three-dimensional mesh suspended in the water, a living fog that fills the pool from floor to surface like a lung full of bioluminescent breath. When the water moves, the colonies deform with it, stretching into streamers, compressing into knots, and the light moves accordingly — dimming where the mesh thins, brightening where it bunches. The effect is of a slow, cold fire that breathes.

They reproduce by fragmentation. A current tears a streamer loose, carries it downstream, and it catches on a rock or a root or the waxy surface of a submerged leaf, and there it grows, branching, weaving itself into a new mesh. Within hours, a new pool has its own glow. Within days, the mesh is mature, dense enough to cast shadows. The threadweavers are patient. They are everywhere. They are the base of everything here, the first and most fundamental layer of the basin's living architecture, and they do their work in silence, feeding on the chemical exhalations of a landscape that is still, even now, being born.

There is a behavior, though, that is not patience. When a large body moves through a pool — a fish-analogue, say, or the slow arm of something larger — the threadweavers *react*. The glow dims in the immediate wake of the disturbance, not from physical displacement but from something that looks almost like a flinch. The mesh contracts. The light pulls back. And then, slowly, over seconds, it relaxes, spreads, re-establishes itself. This happens every time. It suggests sensation. It suggests the threadweavers know something is there.

They are not bright enough to see by from a distance. But when you are crouched at the edge of a pool with your face close to the water, they are enough. They are more than enough. They turn every pool into a window looking down into a living architecture of light, and if you watch long enough, you begin to see the patterns in the glow — the way it pulses in time with the subsonic vibration from the deep, the way it brightens and dims in cycles that take roughly four minutes to complete, the way, at the center of the great pool, it achieves a coherence that no other pool can match: a single, unified pulse, slow as a heartbeat, visible even from the basin's rim.

The threadweavers are the basin's oldest residents. They were here before the structures. They will be here after. Everything else — every creature with legs, every root that grips stone, every heavy wing that beats the dark air above — lives on the energy they first capture from the deep process, converting the basin's inhuman labor into something that can be eaten, can be lived, can be *alive*.

## The Shelfbacks

In the water, in the shallow channels that run between the tilted slabs like streets in a shattered city, the most common large animals are the shelfbacks.

They are flat. Remarkably flat — a mature adult, perhaps thirty centimeters from snout to tail, is no more than four centimeters thick. Their bodies are wide and low, shaped like a river stone that someone has pressed in a vice, and their ventral surfaces are so perfectly adapted to the substrate that they can hold themselves against a slab in a current that would sweep away anything with a less refined hydrodynamic profile. Their skin is dark, wet, almost black, with a fine texture like wet suede, and it is covered in a thin film of the same bioluminescent organisms that fill the pools — not embedded in the skin, but living on its surface, a cultivated garden that makes each shelfback glow faintly as it moves. A shelfback gliding across the bottom of a dark pool is a smear of blue-green light, a living brushstroke, visible for a few seconds before it slides under a slab and vanishes.

They have broad, flat heads — disproportionately large relative to their compressed bodies — and their mouths are ventral, facing downward, perpetually grazing. They feed on the threadweaver colonies, sucking them from the water like soup, and the glow in their wake is always dimmer than the glow ahead of them. They are crop grazers, moving through the basin's pools in slow, predictable circuits, trimming the luminous mesh to a uniform height. A pool that has been recently grazed by a shelfback population has a thin, even glow — well-managed, like a mown lawn. A pool that hasn't been grazed in days is thick and uneven, the mesh dense enough to impede their own movement, which is when they turn and graze their own excess, feeding the loop.

They are not intelligent in any way that matters to a human observer. They eat. They move. They rest, occasionally, by wedging themselves into narrow cracks between slabs where the current can't reach them, their flat bodies disappearing almost entirely into the stone. Sometimes, in the still water of a deep pool, you can see dozens of them lying side by side on the bottom, their dorsal surfaces catching the glow from above, their ventral mouths working in slow, rhythmic cycles. They look like a floor. They look like a living pavement. If you reached down to touch one, it would not move. It would continue to eat. The water on its back would be cold and smooth and faintly luminous, and the creature beneath it would be nothing but appetite and motion and the ancient, simple work of converting light into life.

But there is a behavior that complicates this simplicity. Shelfbacks, when they encounter a pillar, stop. They orient themselves perpendicular to it, their heads pointed directly at its surface, and they hold this position for minutes at a time. They do not feed. They do not move. They simply face the pillar, and the bioluminescent film on their skin brightens — not much, not enough to see unless you were looking — and then, after a variable period, they move on. Every shelfback does this. Every pillar. It is not curiosity. It is not fear. It is *something else* — a behavior with no obvious function, repeated with a consistency that suggests it is not optional. They are drawn to the pillars the way the water is drawn downward into the central pool: not by choice, but by a force that operates beneath choice.

## The Weavers

In the gaps between the tilted slabs — in the narrow, vertical spaces where darkness collects and water runs in thin, cold films — something has built a city.

You would not call it a city if you saw only a single instance. A single weaver's work looks like a mistake: a tangled, irregular lattice of dark threads stretched across a gap, perhaps twenty centimeters wide, threaded with fragments of dead vegetation, bits of mineral crust, droplets of water held in place by surface tension. It looks like debris. It looks like something caught there and was never collected.

But the gaps are not random, and the lattices are not random, and when you follow the pattern — when you crouch in the dark, face inches from the wet stone, and trace the architecture of the webs across a section of basin floor — you begin to see the system. Every gap between slabs, down to a certain width, contains a lattice. Every lattice is connected to its neighbors through hair-thin threads that run along the surfaces of the slabs, forming a continuous network that, across an area of even a few square meters, is a single structure: a web that spans dozens of gaps, hundreds of threads, a three-dimensional mesh woven into the very geometry of the broken ground.

The weavers themselves are small — four centimeters at most, body and legs together — and they are leggy in a way that makes them look like fragments of the lattices they build. Eight legs, each thinner than a pin, each tipped with a hook that locks onto the threads with mechanical precision. Their bodies are a dark, glassy amber, translucent enough that in the bioluminescent light you can see their internal organs working — slow, rhythmic contractions that drive a digestive system optimized for a diet of almost nothing. They eat the biofilm that coats every surface in the basin: the thin, bacterial skin that forms on wet stone, the threadweaver fragments that settle out of the water, the microscopic detritus of every living process. They graze it from the lattices, from the slabs, from each other's backs, with mouthparts that move in a constant, barely visible motion.

The lattices serve two purposes. The first is obvious: they are substrate, a three-dimensional farm that vastly increases the surface area available for biofilm growth. A gap between two slabs might have two hundred square centimeters of exposed stone. Stretched across that gap, a lattice might have two thousand square centimeters of thread, every centimeter coated in the thin, dark film that feeds the colony. The weavers are farmers. They have built their fields in the most inhospitable geometry the basin offers — the vertical cracks, the narrow gaps, the spaces where nothing else can grow — and they harvest them with a patience that would be admirable if it weren't so alien.

The second purpose is harder to explain. The lattices are *conductors*. The threads are not silk — or not only silk — but something piezoelectric, something that responds to the vibration that permeates the basin floor. A lattice under vibration from a nearby settling slab produces a faint electrical signal, measurable if you had the right instruments but detectable even without them: the weavers on a vibrating lattice move differently, faster, with a purposeful directionality that suggests they are *reading* the lattice the way a spider reads its web. The entire network, across a section of basin floor, vibrates as one. A disturbance at one edge — a slab shifting, water surging through a channel, the passage of one of the subsurface mechanical things — propagates through the network as a wave of electrical and mechanical signal, and the weavers respond. They orient. They move toward or away from the disturbance. They adjust their lattices, adding thread here, removing it there, in response to changes in the vibrational landscape that they can sense but that no human observer can perceive without instruments.

The web is a seismograph. The weavers are its operators. And the information they process — the constant, real-time map of the basin's shifting geometry — is something they use not for abstract understanding but for the simplest and most urgent of purposes: to keep their farms from being destroyed. When a slab is about to shift, the lattice trembles in a particular way, and the weavers evacuate. They move along their connecting threads to neighboring lattices, carry the news, and the evacuation ripples outward like a wave — thousands of weavers flowing through the network in the dark, tiny legs clicking on threads that sing with the basin's deep music. By the time the slab settles, the gap has changed shape, the old lattice is destroyed, and the weavers are already rebuilding.

They do not mourn their losses. They do not celebrate their survival. They simply *continue*, threading and rethreading the dark, maintaining their network with a mechanical diligence that is, in its way, as impressive as the pillars. The pillars are the basin's large-scale engineering. The weavers are its small-scale engineering. Both serve the same purpose: to organize the chaos, to give the basin's violent processes a structure they can work within, to convert the energy of a landscape still being born into something that sustains life.

## The Drifters

Above the ground — in the air, such as it is, that cold, saturated, bioluminescent-lit vapor that fills the basin from floor to the unseen ceiling — there are things that fly.

They are not birds. They do not have the body plan for flight as conventionally understood. Their wings are not wings but *membranes* — broad, thin, continuous surfaces stretched from elongated digits, like the wings of a bat but larger, thinner, and adapted for an atmosphere that is dense with water and heavy with sound. A mature drifter — the name is mine; they have no name, because no one has named them — has a wingspan of nearly a meter, but weighs less than a small bird. The membrane is dark, almost black on the dorsal surface, and on the ventral surface it is alive with the same bioluminescent organisms that colonize everything in this basin. A drifter in flight is a moving shadow above and a floating light below, and when a group of them passes overhead — and they do pass overhead, in groups of five or twenty or more, in slow, silent formations that drift across the basin like clouds — the effect is of a secondary sky, a low ceiling of moving light that mirrors and confuses the glow of the water beneath.

They do not echolocate. They do not need to. The sound that fills the basin — the roar, the hum, the mechanical whine — is not an obstacle to navigation; it *is* navigation. The drifters orient by the sound. They can distinguish, by ear alone, the acoustic signature of a pool from the signature of a slab, the signature of a pillar from the signature of a weaver network, and they use these distinctions to navigate with a precision that is startling in the near-total darkness. They are, in effect, *listening* their way through the basin, building a spatial model of the landscape from the sound it makes, and they update this model continuously, in real time, adjusting their flight paths as the sounds shift and change.

Their diet is the invertebrates — the arthropod-analogues that crawl on the rocks and vegetation. Drifters hunt by sound as well: the faint rasping of an arthropod's mouthparts on a leaf, the clicking of its legs on stone, the almost-subliminal vibration of its body as it moves. A drifter in hunting mode flies low, its membrane extended to its fullest width, its body tilted so that the membrane acts as a vast, sensitive ear — a collector of vibration that can distinguish the sound of a living creature from the sound of the rock it stands on. When it finds prey, it folds. The membrane collapses, the body drops, and the descent is fast and silent and ends with a sudden, precise snatch — two hind digits, curved and sharp, plucking the arthropod from its leaf and carrying it upward into the dark where it is consumed in flight. The entire sequence takes less than two seconds. The only evidence is a brief disruption in the leaf's movement and a faint, wet sound that might be the arthropod's exoskeleton breaking.

They are not social in any complex sense, but they are *gregarious*. They roost in groups, clustering on the undersides of ledges and in the canopy of the horizontal trees, their membranes wrapped around their bodies like cloaks, their bioluminescent ventral surfaces dimmed to a faint glow that makes the roost site look like a constellation of dying stars. They communicate — if the term applies — through variations in the frequency and rhythm of their wingbeats, which are too low to hear as sound but are felt as vibration in the membrane itself. Two drifters hanging side by side on a ledge can exchange information this way: *food here, danger there, move, stay, now, later*. The basin's soundscape is so dense that their private frequency band is effectively silent to any other creature. They have carved out a communication channel in the noise by using the noise itself — the basin's overwhelming roar provides cover for their quiet, intimate conversations.

The most unsettling thing about the drifters is their relationship to the pillars. Like the shelfbacks, they are drawn to them. A roosting group will position itself directly above a pillar, as close as the geometry allows, and they will hang there for hours, motionless, their membranes occasionally twitching in what might be response to the pillar's hum. They are not feeding. They are not resting — or not only resting. They are *listening* to the pillar, the way the shelfbacks face it, the way the weavers' lattices transmit its vibrations, the way the threadweavers pulse in rhythm with its output. The pillar's energy passes through every living thing in the basin, and every living thing responds to it, and the response is always the same: a cessation of ordinary activity, a turning toward the source, a brief surrender of individual behavior to the basin's larger rhythm.

Then the moment passes, and the drifters fold their membranes and drop into flight, and the shelfbacks resume their grazing, and the weavers resume their threading, and the basin's ordinary business continues as though nothing happened. But something did. Something always does.

## The Chorus Kings

Among the larger fauna — the rarest, the most difficult to see, the most impossible to ignore when you hear them — there is a creature that deserves a name of its own, if only because its voice is the one sound in the basin that rises above the general roar and announces itself as *individual*.

It is a mammal-analogue, if the term can be stretched far enough. Roughly the size of a large dog, though its body plan is wrong for any terrestrial mammal you'd recognize: low-slung, six-limbed, with a thick, muscular tail that serves as a third pair of legs and can anchor the creature to wet stone with a grip that nothing short of geological force could break. Its skin is dark, smooth, and perpetually wet — not from the ambient moisture but from a layer of mucus that it secretes continuously, a lubricant that allows it to move across the basin's slick surfaces with a speed and sureness that is deeply disconcerting to watch. It moves like water itself: fluid, continuous, finding and filling the gaps between slabs as though it were flowing downhill, its six limbs moving in a rhythm that has no discernible gait but is somehow always balanced, always in contact with the substrate, always *there*.

It has no eyes. Or rather, it has structures that were once eyes, reduced now to vestigial nubs covered by the same mucus layer that coats the rest of its body. What it has instead is an auditory system of extraordinary sophistication. Its skull is large relative to its body, and it is *hollow* — a resonating chamber, like a drum, with membranes stretched across openings on either side that function as ears of a kind that has no analogue in human anatomy. These membranes are sensitive to frequencies that span the basin's entire acoustic range, from the subsonic rumble of the deep to the highest mechanical whine, and they can distinguish, within that range, individual sources with a precision that borders on the mathematical. A chorus king can hear the difference between a slab settling and a slab about to settle. It can hear the vibration of a weaver network twenty meters away. It can hear the heartbeat of a shelfback through the water and stone that separate them.

It uses this hearing to do two things: navigate and sing.

The singing — and it is singing, there is no other word for it — is the basin's most extraordinary sound, and its most purposeless. A chorus king, perched on a tilted slab with its tail anchored and its resonating skull tilted upward, produces a call that begins as a low, tonal hum — roughly in sympathy with the nearest pillar's frequency — and builds, over a period of seconds, into a complex, modulated vocalization that incorporates harmonics, sub-harmonics, and frequencies that seem to exceed the range of the creature's own auditory membranes. It is loud. It is very loud. In a basin already saturated with sound, the chorus king's call carves a space for itself by being *structured* — rhythmic, patterned, and repeating with variations that follow rules no human listener has been able to decode.

Other chorus kings respond. A single call will be answered by another from across the basin, and then another, and then a cascade of responses that ripple outward in a pattern that follows the basin's geography — louder where the pillars are dense, softer where they are sparse, channeled by the weaver networks and reflected by the slab geometry until the entire basin becomes, briefly, a single acoustic instrument played by a dozen voices. The threadweavers brighten during these choruses. The shelfbacks stop grazing and face the nearest pillar. The drifters drop from their roosts and fly in tight circles above the calling kings. The basin's entire ecosystem responds to the chorus as though it were a signal — which it might be, or which it might simply be an accident of frequency, the chorus king's call happening to hit a resonance that the basin's structures amplify and the basin's organisms interpret as meaningful.

The chorus kings hunt by hearing alone, and their prey is the basin's largest invertebrate: a multi-legged predator the size of a human hand, an ambush hunter that waits in the gaps between slabs for smaller arthropods to pass. The chorus king locates it by the minute vibrations of its respiration, pins it with one forelimb, and crushes it with jaws that are broad and flat and built for cracking carapace. The sound of the kill is lost in the general roar. The only evidence is the shell fragments, which the chorus king leaves where they fall — it does not eat the entire animal, only the soft tissues, and the discarded exoskeletons accumulate in piles near frequently used perching sites, forming small, dark cairns that are, in their way, the only artifacts the basin's fauna produces.

The chorus kings are solitary except during the choruses, and even then they maintain a distance from one another — ten meters at least, always with a pillar between them, as though the pillars are mediating not just the energy of the deep process but the social dynamics of the creatures that live within it. They are old. Not individually — their lifespan is probably no more than a decade — but as a lineage, as a *strategy*. Their auditory system, their resonating skull, their six-limbed locomotion, their mucus-coated skin: these are adaptations to the basin that suggest a long coevolution, a mutual shaping between creature and landscape that has been going on for longer than the basin's current geological age. The chorus kings were here before the current slabs were placed. They were here, in some form, when the basin was being assembled, and they adapted to the new landscape with a speed that suggests either remarkable evolutionary plasticity or a history of adaptation to exactly this kind of violent, wet, loud, dark, and engineered environment.

They do not fear you. They do not acknowledge you. They orient to you briefly, with their great resonating skulls tilted in your direction, reading the sounds your body makes — your heartbeat, your breathing, the creak of your joints, the drip of water from your clothing — and they categorize you, in their acoustic model of the basin, as *nothing*. Not food, not threat, not kin, not pillar, not slab, not water, not weaver. Nothing. You make sounds, but they are the wrong sounds, and the chorus king files you under *irrelevant* and returns to its song.

This is the basin's most common verdict on human presence. Irrelevant. Not dangerous, not welcoming. Irrelevant. The basin is too busy for you. The basin has always been too busy for you.

## The Root-Sitters

There is one more creature — or one more *kind* of creature — that must be described, because it occupies a position in the basin's ecology that no other animal fills, and because it is the closest thing the basin has to a being that might, if you were generous, if you were willing to stretch the definition far enough, be called *aware*.

They are small. Eight centimeters, ten at most. They sit in the root masses of the horizontal trees, nestled in the spaces between the thick, exposed roots where the tree grips the tilted slabs, and they are so well camouflaged — their skin textured and colored to match the bark, their limbs shaped like root-tendrils — that you could sit beside one for an hour and not see it. They are anuran-analogues, if the term means anything here: squat, tailless, four-limbed, with broad, adhesive toe-pads that allow them to cling to the wet bark without slipping. Their eyes are large — the largest of any basin creature relative to body size — and they are oriented upward, toward the ceiling of vapor, where the drifters fly.

They do not fly. They do not swim. They do not weave or sing or graze. They *sit*. And while they sit, they watch.

Their eyes are not adapted for the basin's darkness in the usual way — they have no tapetum, no infrared sensitivity, no specialization for low-light vision. Instead, they are adapted for the detection of *movement*, specifically the movement of bioluminescent sources against a dark background. A drifter passing overhead, its ventral membrane glowing, is detected by the root-sitter's eyes from a distance of several meters. A shelfback moving through a pool below is detected as a moving light. The root-sitter's visual world is a dark field populated by moving lights, and it processes this information with a brain that is, for its size, disproportionately large and disproportionately structured — dense with neurons in the regions that process visual motion, sparse everywhere else.

It watches. It watches the drifters fly. It watches the shelfbacks graze. It watches the water move, the weavers thread, the chorus kings call. It watches the pillars, and the slabs, and the central pool. It watches everything, and it does nothing. It does not move. It does not vocalize. It does not eat with any urgency — its metabolic rate is astonishingly low, a cold-blooded stillness that allows it to sit for days without moving, drawing what little energy it needs from the biofilm on the bark beneath its toe-pads and the moisture that condenses on its skin.

Why? What is it watching *for*?

The answer, if there is one, lies in the rare event — rare enough that you might spend weeks in the basin and never see it, but frequent enough that every root-sitter eventually experiences it. When the central pool's pulse changes — when the subsonic vibration shifts in frequency or intensity, when the pillars' hum rises or falls, when the basin's deep process does something different from what it normally does — the root-sitters respond. They unstick. They climb. They move, for the first and perhaps only time, with purpose and urgency, ascending the horizontal trees to their uppermost branches, positioning themselves at the highest point available, and *looking down*.

From this vantage, they can see the basin — or a portion of it. A patchwork of glowing pools, dark slabs, green-black vegetation, and the black silhouettes of pillars against the bioluminescent ground. And they look at it. They look at all of it. And in their large, motion-sensitive eyes, something happens that is not quite thought but is not quite mere reflex either — a pattern of neural activity that corresponds to the entire visual field, the whole basin, as a single, unified image. They are not looking at any one thing. They are looking at *everything*. They are seeing the basin as a system, as a whole, as a single entity, and their brains — tiny, specialized, limited in every other domain — are, for this one moment, processing the entirety of a landscape.

Then it passes. The pulse returns to normal. The pillars settle. The root-sitters climb down, return to their positions in the root masses, and resume their stillness. They have seen what they needed to see. What they do with the information — whether it is memory, whether it is anticipation, whether it is something for which no human word exists — is not knowable. They are not communicators. They do not share their observations with each other. Each root-sitter watches alone, and each root-sitter, in its way, understands alone, and the basin continues around them, oblivious to its own witnesses.

## The Deep Process

And then there is what lives at the center.

The description of the central pool — its intensity, its pulse, the pillars ringed around it like a congregation, the water drawn downward into a consumption that powers everything — describes a site. What lives there is not a site. What lives there is an *organism*.

Not biological. Not mechanical. Not something that has a name in any taxonomy yet devised. It is the thing that the basin was built to house, or it is the thing that built the basin to house itself, and the distinction between these two framings has collapsed into meaninglessness so long ago that the question itself is a fossil of a way of thinking that no longer applies.

It is deep. How deep cannot be determined from the surface. The central pool, fifty meters across, is its *nostril* — the point of interface between its body and the basin's atmosphere. The water that descends into it is inhaled. The energy that rises from it is exhaled. The sound — all of it, the full, terrifying, beautiful, overwhelming sound of the basin — is its *breathing*.

It is not conscious in the way you understand consciousness. It does not think. It does not plan. It does not *want*. It processes. It takes in raw material — water, minerals, heat, the chemical signatures of everything that has ever washed into the basin — and it *converts* it. The conversion produces energy, which the pillars distribute. It produces the vibration that the weavers read and the shelfbacks face and the chorus kings harmonize with. It produces the chemical richness that feeds the threadweavers, which feeds the shelfbacks, which feeds the drifters, which is absorbed by the root masses, which stabilize the slabs, which are set by the basin's ongoing geological formation, which is powered by the deep process.

It is a closed loop. The basin is a closed loop. Everything in the basin is a component of the process, and the process is the basin, and they are the same thing, and the deep organism at the center is not a ruler or a god or an engine but a *digestive system* — a metabolic core that converts the basin's chaos into the basin's order and back again, in a cycle that has been running for longer than the current landscape has existed and will continue running after it has been replaced by the next iteration.

The pillars know this. That is why they are positioned where they are, that is why they are tuned to the frequencies they emit, that is why they hum: they are *distributed organs* of the deep process, extensions of a body that has no fixed shape, that expresses itself as a landscape, that breathes as a sound, that sees — through the root-sitters' eyes, through the weavers' lattices, through the shelfbacks' bioluminescent skins — as a network of living light spread across a floor of broken stone.

The basin is not a place where something lives.

The basin is the thing that is alive.

## What You Are

You stand at the edge of the central pool, and the sound presses against you like a hand, and the water on your skin glows with the threadweavers that have already colonized your clothing, your hair, the wet surfaces of your body. You are, within hours of your arrival, part of the basin's metabolism. The organisms on your skin are feeding on the chemical waste of your respiration. The weavers on the nearest slabs are adjusting their lattices to account for your weight on the substrate. A chorus king, somewhere in the dark, has already categorized and dismissed you. A root-sitter, somewhere in the root tangles of a horizontal tree, has watched you arrive and filed the observation in a brain the size of a pea, and what it has recorded is not your shape or your color or your intent but the *pattern* of your movement — the way you walk, the rhythm of your steps, the cadence of your breathing — and this pattern has been compared, instantly and unconsciously, to every other pattern the root-sitter has ever recorded, and it matches nothing, and so you are filed under *other*, which is the basin's category for everything that has not yet been incorporated, which is a temporary category, because everything is incorporated eventually.

You are wet. You are cold. The sound is in your bones. The light is on your skin. The basin does not care about you, but it has already begun to use you — your body heat dissipating into the water, your carbon dioxide feeding the threadweavers, your footsteps adding their faint vibration to the weaver networks, your presence registering on the acoustic map of every chorus king within earshot. You are a minor input to a vast process, a decimal point in an equation that was written before you were born and will be balanced long after you are gone.

But you are *here*. You are inside the living thing. And if you stay — if you let the cold settle into your joints, if you let the sound dissolve the boundary between yourself and the noise, if you let the bioluminescent light rewrite your sense of what darkness looks like — you will begin to feel the basin's rhythm in your own body. Your heartbeat will slow to match the pulse of the central pool. Your breathing will sync with the pillars' hum. Your skin will bloom with threadweavers so dense that you will glow when you move, a faint blue-green trace in the dark, visible to every creature that navigates by light.

You will not be a citizen of the basin. You will not be a participant in its purpose. You will be *material* — water, mineral, energy, sound — returned to the cycle, fed into the deep process, burned down to your components and rebuilt into something the basin can use. The chorus kings will not sing for you. The root-sitters will not watch for you. The weavers will weave through the spaces where your body was, and the slabs will shift, and the water will run, and the pillars will hum, and the basin will continue its ancient, tireless, incomprehensible work, and you will be part of it, and it will not notice, and that will be enough.

That will be enough.
