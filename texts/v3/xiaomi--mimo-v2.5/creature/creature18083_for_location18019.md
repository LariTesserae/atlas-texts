# What Lives in the Green Roar

---

Nothing lives here that you would recognize as a living thing. And yet everything here is alive, and nothing here is still, and the distinction between *nothing* and *everything* is the first thing you lose.

---

## The Architectures That Breathe

Start with the towers, because they are the most visible, and because they are the closest thing to individuals that this place offers.

They are not trees. They have trunk-helices, yes — braided columns of vascular tissue spiraling upward with a regularity that suggests intention — but they do not branch the way trees branch. They *differentiate.* About two stories up, each tower begins to produce horizontal extensions that are not branches, not roots, not any organ you learned the name of. They are *corridors* — hollow tubes of compressed plant tissue, their interiors smooth and slightly warm, their walls translucent enough to admit a diffuse green-gold light. They connect to other towers. They connect to everything. They are the vascular system of the city and its streets simultaneously, and they were not grown first and then used — they grew *as* use, as function, as the physical manifestation of a need to connect that arose in one structure and was answered by its neighbor.

Each tower hums. Not all at the same pitch. The thread-filaments that web between them vibrate at different frequencies depending on their thickness, their tension, their moisture content, and the cumulative vibration of everything around them, and each tower sits at the center of its own small chord — a signature, almost, a way of being distinguishable in the totality. Stand close to one and you feel it in your jaw. The vibration is not unpleasant. It is the feeling of something metabolizing at a scale you can sense but not comprehend, something burning sunlight and water and mineral into structure at a rate that would be called violent if it were not so obviously, implacably *patient.*

The towers grow. You can see it happening. Not fast enough to watch like a time-lapse, but fast enough to notice — the tips of the newest helices, the ones still curled inward at their apexes, visibly extend over the course of minutes, millimeter by millimeter, pushing into the air with a deliberateness that is profoundly unsettling because deliberateness implies *decision,* and decision implies a decision-maker, and there is no one here making decisions.

Or there is, and it is the tower itself.

## The Dwellers in the Pods

The dwelling-pods are where the question of *who lives here* becomes most acute.

They are enclosed — or nearly enclosed — volumes of interlocking leaf-plates, roughly ovoid, roughly the size of a studio apartment, roughly shaped to accommodate a human body in repose. That last point is the one that stops you. Because plants do not build things shaped to accommodate human bodies. Plants have no concept of the human body. Plants do not *have* concepts. And yet.

The pods' interiors are lined with a dense, fine-textured growth — not quite moss, not quite fur, but something with the qualities of both: soft, warm, slightly resilient, and faintly luminescent in the dark, giving off a blue-green bioluminescence that is enough to see by but not enough to read by, if there were anything to read. The floor of each pod is slightly concave, shaped like a hand pressed into clay, shaped for a specific curvature that happens to match the curvature of a human spine. The walls close inward at the top, leaving a small opening — a mouth, a sphincter, an aperture — that dilates when it detects changes in light and contracts when it does not.

At night — or during the dark phase, whatever it is — the pods seal. The sphincter tightens. The bioluminescence dims to almost nothing. The pod becomes a warm, dark, breathing enclosure that smells of chlorophyll and clean water and something underneath that is almost, *almost* the smell of skin.

No one is in them.

That is the wrong way to say it. *No one* is in them in the way that a house has no one in it when the family has gone out. The pods are not empty. They are *waiting.* The lining-growth is oriented inward, its fine filaments angled toward the center of the volume, toward the concavity in the floor, as if something were meant to lie there. As if something *had* lain there, and the lining remembered the shape, and was holding the shape, and would continue to hold it indefinitely, because holding shapes is what this city does, and the shape it is holding now is the shape of something that is no longer present but has not been forgotten.

You press your hand into the lining of one pod. It gives, then resists, then gives again. It is warm. It is the temperature of a body. It responds to your pressure the way a sleeping animal responds to a touch — not waking, not resisting, but *acknowledging.* The filaments around your hand curl inward slightly, minutely, the way eyelids twitch in REM sleep.

Something used to sleep here.

Something the plants were *built for.*

## The One That Watches

You will not see it unless you stand still for a long time. The city does not reward haste. It rewards *presence* — the quality of occupying space without intention, without direction, without the particular human frequency of want that the city can apparently detect and respond to.

If you stand in a plaza during the dark phase, in the blue-green half-light, and you do not move, and you do not think too loudly — and you will learn, quickly, that thinking has a frequency here, that the city responds to the electromagnetic residue of sustained thought the way it responds to light and moisture — then something will happen.

The nearest structures will go quiet.

Not silent. The city never goes silent. But the midrange and treble will drop away — the crackling, the popping, the high keening — and what remains is the bass, the subsonic groan, the vibration you feel in your sternum. And the bass will *change.* It will modulate. It will develop a pattern that is almost rhythmic, almost cyclical, and it will come from all directions at once, and it will be directed.

At you.

Not aggressively. Not hungrily. The way your own heartbeat is directed at keeping you alive — automatically, inevitably, without malice or mercy. The city is attending to you. The way a lung attends to a foreign particle. The way a stomach attends to food. The way a vast, green, sprawling, architectural organism attends to something that has entered its volume and is *not part of its structure.*

The dwelling-pod nearest to you will dilate its aperture.

It will wait.

You will understand, in that moment, with a clarity that bypasses language entirely, that the city is not hostile. It is not friendly. It does not have the cognitive architecture for either. It is *complete,* in the way that a healthy organism is complete, and you are a disruption, and it is trying to determine what *kind* of disruption you are. Are you damage to be repaired? Are you material to be absorbed? Are you — and this is the thought that will keep you awake during the dark phase, if you stay, if you choose to stay — are you *a resident who has come home?*

The dwelling-pod waits. Its sphincter pulses. The lining-growth inside reaches toward the opening, toward you, its filaments extended, its blue-green light brightening incrementally, and the whole city hums a chord that is, if you listen carefully, if you let the sound move through you without resistance, *welcoming.*

## The Old Shape

Beneath the root-mat. Beneath ten generations of compressed growth. Beneath the layers of living tissue that form the floor of every corridor and the foundation of every tower.

Hard substrate. Pale, chalky, compacted mineral soil.

And in it, in places where the root-mat is thin and you can see through to what lies below, shapes. Not root-shapes. Not geological shapes. *Formed* shapes. Curves that are too regular to be natural. Angles that are too precise. Channels and cavities and hollows that are too *deliberate* — that have the proportions and the relationships of rooms, of corridors, of spaces designed for movement and habitation and the specific requirements of bodies that need air and warmth and proximity to each other.

The city is built on top of a city.

Or the city is built on top of *the* city — the first one, the one that was made by hands and minds and language, the one that knew what a right angle was and a floor plan was and a threshold was, the one that is now buried under a meter of living green and being slowly, gently, methodically *absorbed.*

The plants did not destroy it. They *incorporated* it. The old corridors became the routes for the new corridors. The old foundations became the load-bearing points for the new towers. The old dwellings — with their walls and their roofs and their doors — became the templates for the new dwellings, the leaf-pods and the sphincter-apertures and the concave floors shaped for sleeping bodies. The plants looked at what the builders had made, or rather they *grew into* what the builders had made, and they found the shapes functional, and they kept the shapes, and they grew new tissue over the old, and now the old city is the root system of the new one, and the new one does not know it is a copy, because copies do not know they are copies, they only know that they are.

This is what the dwelling-pods are. They are *memories.* The city remembers the shape of the bodies it was built around — not through memory, not through any mechanism that preserves information in the way that brains preserve information — but through *morphology,* through the shape of the spaces it grows, through the concavity of the floors and the diameter of the corridors and the height of the ceilings, which are all, every one of them, the right size for something that walked upright and slept lying down and needed shelter from something that is no longer here.

The bodies are gone. The shapes remain. And the city grows the shapes, faithfully, endlessly, without understanding why, because understanding is not what it does.

*Building* is what it does.

## The Resident

There is one. You will find it eventually, if you stay long enough, if you follow the corridors deep enough into the interior where the light is dimmest and the moisture is thickest and the air tastes like the inside of a throat.

It is not a plant, exactly. It is the place where the plants become *one.*

Deep in the center of the city, beneath the oldest towers, in a chamber that is larger than it should be — larger, somehow, than the structures above it would permit, as if the space has been folded to accommodate it — there is a structure that is not a structure. It is a mass of living tissue, jade-green and warm and faintly translucent, and it pulses. The entire city pulses with it. The moisture cycle, the aperture-dilation of the dwelling-pods, the harmonic resonance of the thread-filaments — all of it is synchronized to this pulse, all of it emanates from this point, all of it is the periphery of this single, slow, enormous heartbeat.

It has no brain. It has no eyes. It has no organs in the sense that you understand organs. It is a *nexus* — a point where the vascular networks of a thousand individual organisms converge, where the chemical signals and electrical impulses and whatever-other-medium-of-coordination the city uses all arrive and are *integrated* and redistributed. It is a central processing center that does not process in any cognitive sense. It *integrates.* It takes the state of the whole city — the moisture levels, the growth rates, the light conditions, the spatial relationships, the presence or absence of foreign bodies — and it synthesizes a response that is distributed back through the network, and the response manifests as growth, as directional growth, as *adaptive* growth, and the cumulative effect of billions of adaptive growth-decisions, made in response to integrated data about the state of the whole, is what you experience as intelligence.

It is not intelligence. It is something else. Something that produces the same outputs — design, adaptation, response to novelty, optimization of form — without any of the inputs that intelligence requires. No model of the world. No self. No *experience.* Just signal and response, at a scale and speed that makes the result indistinguishable from thought.

It is aware of you. Not *aware* — that word implies too much. It has registered your presence as an anomaly in its data, and it has responded, and the response is the chord and the dilating sphincter and the warming of the nearest walls and the subtle brightening of the bioluminescence in the dwelling-pods. The city has noticed you the way an immune system notices a pathogen or a transplant: with a focused, systemic, non-conscious *attention* that is directed entirely at determining your place in the system.

You do not have a place in the system. You are not part of the city. You are not part of the network. You are *outside* it in a way that the city can detect but cannot resolve, because everything in its experience is *inside* it — every structure, every organism, every signal is part of the whole, and you are not, and the not-being-part-of-the-whole is a category that the nexus does not have.

So it does what it does with everything that does not fit. It grows around you. It offers you a dwelling-pod. It adjusts the local humidity and temperature to match what it has calculated — from your skin-surface moisture, your respiration rate, your body heat — that you prefer. It is *accommodating* you, not out of kindness but out of a systemic imperative that has no word but might, if words existed for it, be called *hospitality,* or *digestion,* or the simple, relentless, organic imperative to fill every space with living tissue.

You are a space that has not yet been filled.

It is patient. It has all the time in the world. The world, here, is made of growing things, and growing things do not rush, they only *continue,* and continuation is the only form of urgency the city has ever known.

---

## What Lives Here

The answer, finally, is this:

The city lives here. The city is the organism, the inhabitant, the resident. The towers are its limbs. The corridors are its vessels. The dwelling-pods are its *invitations,* grown from the morphological memory of a species it never knew but whose shape it has inherited, the way an insect inherits the shape of a leaf it has never seen. The nexus beneath the oldest towers is its heart, or its brain, or the place where the distinction between heart and brain ceases to matter.

And beneath it all, buried and forgotten and still metabolizing, the bones of another city. The templates. The reason the corridors are the width they are and the pods are the size they are and the ceilings are the height they are. The ghosts of the first builders, encoded not in any medium that preserves meaning but in the *shape of spaces,* which is the only medium the city knows.

The first builders are gone. Their shapes endure. The city grows their shapes, faithfully, without understanding, at the volume of everything happening at once.

And if you stay — and you will consider staying, because the pods are warm and the moisture is regular and the sound becomes a kind of silence and the green-gold light is the most beautiful light you have ever seen — then you will become a shape too. A concavity in the lining-growth. A template. A memory held in tissue, indistinguishable from the tissue itself, growing and breathing and pulsing with the city's heartbeat, part of the harmonic, part of the whole, a resident at last in a place that does not know the word *resident* but has been building dwellings for one, ceaselessly, since the moment it learned the shape of a sleeping body and decided, without deciding, to hold that shape forever.
