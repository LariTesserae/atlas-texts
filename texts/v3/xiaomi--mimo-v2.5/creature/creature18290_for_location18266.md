They do not have names. They do not need them. They are not *named* things, but *placed* things, each one a feature of the terrain, as integral and as silent as a channel or a seam.

There are the **Lithophages**, but to call them eaters of stone is to mislead. They do not consume; they *process*. They are low, slow-moving masses, the size and shape of a settled kneading bowl, with a surface like wet mortar. They move through the water films and over the stone in paths that have no urgency, their passage leaving a sheen not of slime, but of a *renewed* wetness, as if they are spreading the essential condition of the place from one spot to another. They are the same pale chalk as the stone, but with a faint, milky luminescence that comes from within. They are always where the water is deepest, and their movement is so glacial it takes a full turning of your head to confirm they have shifted at all. They leave no trail. They *are* the trail.

In the seams between the blocks, where the plants erupt, live the **Spire-Singers**, though they make no sound. They are not birds. They are crystalline filaments, thin as grass stalks, growing vertically from the joints. They branch, dichotomously, into fractal trees of mineral growth. Each tip is a different hue—translucent amber, milky amethyst, a quartz-clear so pure it distorts the air behind it. They do not move. Their "singing" is a light phenomenon: when the directionless light strikes their angled facets, it refracts, not into rainbows, but into singular, solid points of color that project onto the surrounding stone. These points of light *move* as you move, not like a reflection, but like a separate entity tracking your position with perfect accuracy, a silent, colorful surveillance that paints the pale blocks with shifting constellations of violet, gold, and rose. They are not alive in any biological sense. They are light-harvesting structures, *grown* into their precise locations to perform this function forever.

On the exposed stretches of block-field, where the stone is dry of pooled water but still slick with atmospheric moisture, glide the **Geometrists**. They are flat, like skates, with a diameter slightly wider than a human handspan. Their dorsal surface is a perfect, unblemished mosaic of the same calcium compound as the ground, rendering them invisible when still. Their only movement is a slow, drifting glide, propelled by a rhythmic contraction of their ventral surface against the wet stone—a motion that produces no sound and no wake. They trace the geometric patterns laid in the block-joints, following a single interlocking seam for a hundred meters before, with a smooth, deliberate arc, transferring to a perpendicular line. They are mapping. Or perhaps they *are* the map, their movement reinforcing a pattern that was there from the beginning. They never deviate. They never accelerate. They are the basin's way of tracing its own blueprint, a living diagram of its own construction.

The **Aequors** inhabit the shallow depressions that contain the brightest, sky-reflecting pools. They are not fish. They are membranous, semi-rigid discs, held just beneath the water's surface by surface tension and some internal buoyancy. Their bodies are entirely transparent except for a network of veins, dark and branching like river deltas, which pulse with a slow, rhythmic contraction that circulates the mineral-rich water through their form. They cast no shadow; they *are* shadows, dark patterns against the blinding white of the reflected sky. Their purpose, if one could be assigned, is to be depth-marks, to give the eye a reference in a place where perspective is a lie. They anchor the impossible distances. When you see an Aequor, you know that point in the pool is exactly where its place in the pattern requires it to be. They are the punctuation in the basin's silent, spatial sentence.

Above it all, the things you called birds are the **Cantilevers**. They are not creatures of muscle and feather. They are structures of leavered bone, lighter than air, held aloft by the basin's own heavy, saturated atmosphere. They are shaped like broad, jointed wings, but they have no body, no head, no eyes—just the articulated, pale limb, with a secondary, smaller limb folded at its joint like a rudder. They move with the precision of a thrown object, gliding on invisible currents, never flapping, never turning. Their flight paths are arcs and lines that never intersect, a three-dimensional calligraphy drawn against the white sky. They are not navigating; they are *demonstrating* the properties of the air and space they inhabit. To watch them is to see the basin's physics made visible: the thickness of the air, the weight of the light, the strange, unfaltering trajectories of objects built to move through a world without friction or surprise.

You see them all, these placed things, and you understand they are not inhabitants. They are *elements*. They are the basin's flora and fauna, yes, but they are also its measurements, its maintenance, its proof. They are the proof that it is finished. They are the proof that it is working. They are the silent, moving, growing, glowing, gliding proof that the place is exactly as it was made, and that its making was complete. They are the last, most intricate tools in the workshop, still running, their work simply to *be* themselves, in their precise, intended places, in the endless, still, bright, humid, impossible, handmade world.
