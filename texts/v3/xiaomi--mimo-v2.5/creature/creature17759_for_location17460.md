# The People of Vash Tidal

They are not what you would expect.

If you surfaced beside one of them — if you could surface, here at the top of a drowned mountain — the first thing you would notice is that they look wrong for the water. They are not streamlined. They are not sleek. They have the blunt, upright proportions of air-breathers, because that is what they are: lungs, not gills; hands, not fins. Their skin is pale, not with the blue translucency of deep-sea dwellers but with the particular pallor of things that live in bright, cold light without much pigment. Sunlight here, filtered through shallow water, still carries enough ultraviolet to matter, and over generations the population has developed skin that is faintly grey-pink, almost the color of the root-walls themselves — a camouflage that was never selected for on purpose but that persists because the people who blended into their buildings were slightly less conspicuous to the eels.

Their eyes are large. Not grotesquely so, not the adapted-to-darkness eyes of cave fauna, but large in the way of people who have spent centuries in a world where information comes sideways through water — where peripheral detection of movement is more valuable than long-distance acuity. The irises are pale. Grey, or grey-green, or the washed-out amber of water-light through limestone. They see well in this world. They see the schools of fish the way you might see weather approaching. They read each other's faces through the distorting lens of water with an ease that suggests their visual cortex has quietly reorganized itself over many generations to account for refraction.

They are not large people. This is not the water's doing — pressure at this depth is negligible, and the thermocline is barely a boundary. But the food is specific: fish, algae, the starchy inner tissue of certain root structures that can be processed into something like a flatbread if soaked long enough in hot water from the thermal vents at the mountain's base. There is no large game. There has never been large game. The people are lean, narrow-hipped, light-boned — not fragile, but economical. They carry very little on their frames because there has never been a reason to carry much.

Their hands are the most remarkable thing about them.

Not in the way that a surface observer would immediately register — they have ten fingers, five on each hand, the standard mammalian arrangement. But the fingers are longer than you would expect, and the webbing between them extends further than mere genetics would explain. This is not true webbing. It is not membranous. It is simply that the connective tissue between the digits has thickened and extended over generations, creating a paddle-like surface that makes the hand an efficient instrument of water displacement. They swim with their hands more than their legs. The legs are used for bracing against root-walls, for anchoring to stone, for the slow walking-wade through the shallower channels. But propulsion — the fluid, unhurried movement through the open courtyards and between the buildings — comes from the hands. The stroke is distinctive. It looks like someone kneading very large, very yielding bread. It is silent. Everything they do in the water is silent, or nearly so. They have learned — not consciously, not as a technique, but as a bodily inheritance — to move without creating the pressure waves that would alert the eels.

The eels matter. The eels matter enormously.

---

There are perhaps a dozen of the large ones — the territorial eels that nest in the outermost structures. They are not dangerous to the residents in any consistent way. They are territorial, yes, and their mouths are wide, but they are ambush predators by temperament, and the residents are not prey-sized in any way that triggers their strike reflex. The danger is not predation. The danger is that an eel startled into defense will thrash, and an eel thrashing in a gap between root-walls can do significant structural damage. The residents have, over centuries, developed an almost ceremonial relationship with the perimeter eels. They approach slowly. They announce themselves — not with sound, which would be absurd, but with touch. A hand pressed flat against the root-wall nearest to an eel's nest, held there for a long moment, letting the creature feel the vibration of a familiar heartbeat through the wood. The eels learn. They come to associate the vibration with the non-threat of the residents. Some of the oldest eels — and they are old; the residents believe, with some basis, that the largest individuals may be centuries old — will tolerate a resident passing within arm's length. The youngest ones will not. The youngest ones must be given wide berth, and the residents navigate the outer perimeter accordingly, adjusting their routes the way a person on the surface might adjust their walking path to avoid a dog that hasn't been socialized.

This negotiation with the eels is one of the few things in the settlement that looks like hierarchy. The elders — the people who remember which eels tolerate proximity and which do not, who know the nesting cycles, who can read the subtle postural language of a coiled eel in a root-fork — carry a specific kind of authority. Not political authority. Not command. But a quiet, embodied expertise that others defer to in practice if not in name. An elder will not tell you where to walk. But if you watch where the elder walks, you will walk where it is safe.

---

The children.

The children are the loudest thing in the settlement, and the settlement is already loud.

They learn to swim before they learn to walk. This is not a metaphor or a cultural practice; it is the simple physics of the environment. The dry rooms — the air-pocket chambers in the upper reaches of the buildings — are where infants are born and where they spend their first weeks, held against warm bodies in the humid, plant-filtered air. But almost as soon as they can move with intention, they are in the water. The water is everywhere. The threshold between air and water in a living quarter is not a door but a gradient — a sloping root-floor that descends from the dry upper chamber into the submerged lower passage, and the transition is so gradual that a child crawling downslope simply finds itself swimming before it understands that swimming is a separate skill from moving.

They are loud because they have not yet learned the eel-awareness. They splash. They make sounds — the sharp, percussive bursts of half-formed language carried through water, which arrive at a listener's ear with a clarity that makes surface-water speech seem muffled by comparison. Underwater speech is not a human adaptation; their vocal cords are not shaped for it, and what they produce is less language than a system of tonal punctuation — rising inflections for attention, falling for assent, sharp repeated notes for distress or excitement. The real communication happens above the waterline, in the air-pocket chambers, where they gather at night — or what passes for night, the dimming of light as the sun passes behind the mountain — and talk. They talk in the air. They talk in the fluid, rapid, gesture-supplemented way of people for whom spoken language is something you do in a specific, precious, finite medium.

The gestures matter. Because so much of life happens in water, where sound is distorted and facial expressions are blurred by refraction, the residents have developed a sign language — not a formal system, not codified, but a rich, dense, overlapping vocabulary of hand and arm movements that can be performed one-handed while the other hand holds a basket or braces against a wall. The language of the air-pocket chamber and the language of the water are not the same language. They overlap, but they are distinct. The children learn both simultaneously, switching between them the way bilingual children on the surface switch between spoken tongues — unconsciously, contextually, with a fluency that older residents occasionally find difficult to match.

---

They eat together.

The preparation of food happens in the air-pocket chambers, because fire is impossible here — no dry fuel, no way to sustain a flame in a waterlogged environment — and because the processing of the starchy root-tissue requires heat, which comes from the thermal vents at the mountain's base. This is the one regular expedition that every resident undertakes: the descent along the mountain's western face to the vents, where superheated water rises from deep fractures in the limestone and creates pockets of warmth in which certain fast-growing algae thrive. These algae — bright orange, filamentous, almost aggressively alive — are harvested and carried back in baskets, along with chunks of the vent-warmed stone itself, which retain heat long enough to serve as cooking surfaces when wrapped in root-fiber insulation.

The cooking is communal and slow. The root-starch is dense. It must be soaked, then heated, then scraped, then mixed with shredded algae and fish oil — rendered from the small schooling fish, pressed out in woven fiber presses — into a paste that can be dried into flat cakes. The process takes most of a day. It is done in groups, in the largest air-pocket chambers, and it is the primary social ritual of the settlement. People talk while they work. They share news, settle disputes, arrange partnerships for child-rearing, negotiate the complex web of mutual obligation that constitutes governance here. The food is never varied. It is always the same: starch, algae, fish. The residents do not complain about this. Variety is not a concept that has meaning here. What they have is what grows, and what grows is what has always grown, and what has always grown is enough.

They eat the fish raw. Always raw. The small schooling fish are caught by hand — scooped from the water in woven baskets or, for those with the patience, caught individually between two fingers in a motion that is almost identical to the way one might pick up a coin from a table. The bones are removed with fish-bone needles — an irony not lost on anyone, though no one remarks on it because the tools have always been this way. The flesh is pale, almost translucent, mild in flavor, with the mineral taste of the water that raised it. It is eaten immediately or pressed into the starch paste for preservation. Nothing is wasted. The bones are threaded onto fiber cord and hung in the air chambers to dry, where they serve as the raw material for needles, hooks, and the fine scraping tools used in root-fiber processing. The scales — wide, flexible, almost translucent — are used as lenses.

This last fact is perhaps the most surprising thing about the residents of Vash Tidal, and it is the thing that, more than any other, suggests that they are not simply primitives adapting to an unusual environment but people who have, in their own way, thought deeply about the world they inhabit.

They use fish scales as magnifying lenses.

Not for fire-starting — there is no fire. Not for reading, because there is nothing written to read. For *seeing*. The small schooling fish in the settlement have scales that, when removed carefully and held in the right light, bend and focus underwater light with surprising precision. The residents use them to examine the fine structure of the root-walls — to see the health of the living bark, to detect the early signs of disease or parasitic infestation in the plant architecture. They hold the scales up to their eyes the way a jeweler on the surface might hold a loupe. The gesture is unmistakable: the slight squint, the careful distance, the stillness of a person focusing on something small and important.

They have not invented the microscope. But they have arrived, through a completely different path, at the principle of magnification. They know that the world has layers. They know that what looks like a healthy root-wall can, under the lens of a fish scale, reveal a web of fungal filaments slowly hollowing out the heartwood. They know this, and they act on it — pruning, encouraging, redirecting growth in response to what they see. Their architecture is maintained not by engineering but by a kind of botany conducted at the cellular level, observed through the eyes of fish.

---

There is one more thing about them that should be said.

They grieve for their buildings.

When a structure collapses — when the slow thickening of roots finally exceeds the structural capacity of the chamber, when a ceiling sags and an arch gives way and a room that has been dry for decades floods with cold water — the residents mourn. Not loudly. Not with ceremony. But there is a visible quietness that settles over the settlement in the weeks following a collapse. People visit the ruin. They run their hands over the broken root-ends, the exposed heartwood, the raw edges where living tissue has been torn from living tissue. Some of them press their foreheads against the remaining walls and stay that way for a long time.

They do not rebuild. They cannot. The collapsed structure is still alive — its root system is still growing, still extending into the substrate, still reaching toward the light. New shoots will emerge. In a decade, in two decades, a new structure will begin to take shape from the ruin. But it will not be the old one. It will not have the same rooms, the same passages, the same shaped doorways where two root systems were merged and separated generations ago. The particular arrangement of space — the way the light fell through a certain gap at a certain time of day, the way the current hummed at a particular pitch in a particular chamber — is gone. It cannot be grown again. It was unique. It was, in every way that matters, a living thing, and it has died.

The residents understand this. They understand that they live in a city of mortal beings. They understand that every wall they touch is growing, and that growing means changing, and that changing means, eventually, ending. They understand this and they stay. They tend the new shoots. They guide the young roots. They do not rush. They do not mourn for long. The water is cold and the light is bright and the fish are passing through the courtyards in dark, glittering clouds, and the settlement hums its endless, involuntary song, and life goes on in the way that life has always gone on here: slowly, patiently, without certainty that any of it will last.

It is enough. It has always been enough.
