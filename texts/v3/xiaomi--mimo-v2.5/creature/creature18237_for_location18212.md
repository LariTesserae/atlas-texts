# What Lives in The Stacking

---

The people are the least strange thing here, and the most. They are ordinary in the way that weathered wood is ordinary — shaped by exposure, not altered in substance. Three hundred, maybe. The number depends on whether you count the woman in the eastern residential tower who has been walking the same corridor for six days now, not lost but *continuing*, her gait unhurried, her expression one of mild concentration, as though she is listening to something the corridor is saying that only walking at this particular pace will let her hear. She will stop eventually. She always does. She stops in the room where the blue hum of the filtration machinery is loudest and sits down and drinks water from a cup and looks at nothing in particular and then begins walking again. No one finds this unusual. She has been doing it for four years.

The residents move through the district the way a tongue moves through a mouth — finding the ridges and hollows by feel, by repetition, by a knowledge that is muscular rather than cognitive. They know which stairwells expand on which days. They know that the passage between the water processing plant and the interior farms runs shorter in the afternoon than the morning, and they have adjusted their schedules accordingly, not out of frustration but out of a simple pragmatism that does not distinguish between this quirk and any other. *The passage is longer now. Walk faster or wait.* They wait. They are good at waiting.

They are not monks. They are not ascetics. They are not pioneers or survivalists or visionaries. Most of them came here because of a job, or a relationship, or because they had been somewhere else first and that somewhere else had become intolerable in ways that were personal and not worth explaining. A few came because they had heard about the distances. A very few came because they had *felt* the distances — standing at the district's edge during one of the wrongness-events, watching the second landscape overlay the first, feeling the foreign temperature on their skin, and knowing, with the certainty that precedes thought, that the place was unfinished and that being inside an unfinished thing meant the possibility of *participating in its completion*. These last ones are the ones who stay longest and speak least.

They live in small rooms. The rooms are adequate. They contain a bed, a surface for eating, a shelf, a light panel that hums at a frequency that varies building to building and room to room, so that each space has its own particular silence — the absence pitched differently depending on what sound it is not making. The walls are the resinous material that sweats in the cold, and the residents have learned to wipe them down in the mornings, not compulsively but as one wipes a window, because seeing through the moisture to the surface beneath is a small act of orientation. The condensation is thicker in some rooms than others. Residents whose walls weep heavily have, over time, developed a relationship with this fact. They do not resent it. They describe their rooms the way one describes a companion with a persistent habit — *yes, it does that, it's always done that, you learn to work around it.*

The food is good. This is worth stating plainly because it is one of the few things in the district that is uncomplicated. The interior farms produce reliably — leafy greens, a root vegetable the color of rust that has no name outside the district and tastes somewhere between celery and copper, a white fruit that grows in tight clusters and has a sweetness that lingers. Under the wrong-spectrum lights, the farms are beautiful. Residents who work them describe the light as *almost right*, and the almost-rightness gives the growing things a quality of urgency, as though the plants know the light is not quite what they need and are compensating by being more vivid, more saturated, more insistently alive. The workers move between the narrow hydroponic rows with the careful tenderness of people tending something fragile in a place that is not fragile but is *unfixed*.

Children exist here. Not many. Perhaps forty, perhaps fewer, depending on the year and the counting. They adapt faster than the adults, which is to say they stop being surprised sooner. A child of seven who has lived in the district for two years will navigate the spatial inconsistencies with an ease that looks, to newcomers, like magic and, to old residents, like the natural result of learning a language before the syntax hardens. The children draw maps. The maps are always wrong. Not inaccurate — *wrong*, in the way that the district itself is wrong, containing paths that loop through themselves and rooms that appear on the same floor twice and staircases that descend and arrive above. The children exchange these maps the way children everywhere exchange secrets, and the fact that no two maps agree is not a failure of cartography but a record of honest observation. Each child has mapped the district as the district presents itself to *them*, and the district presents itself differently to everyone, and this is not a problem. The children know this. They have always known this.

The buildings breathe.

Not metaphorically. The condensation is exhalation. The structures draw moisture from the forming geology below and from the ambient air above and process it through systems that are not entirely mechanical and not entirely geological and not entirely understood by the engineers who monitor them. The moisture rises through the walls in capillary networks — some of them pipes, some of them fissures in the resinous material, some of them neither — and emerges on the surfaces in patterns that repeat but not precisely. In the residential tower on the eastern edge, the condensation on the third-floor landing always forms in a pattern that residents have, without ever discussing it, come to call the *fern*: a central drip line with smaller drips branching off at regular intervals, each branch producing a secondary branching, and so on, to a scale that requires crouching to observe. The pattern forms and dissolves and forms again over the course of each day. It has been doing this for as long as anyone can remember. No one has timed it. No one needs to. It is enough to know that it happens, the way it is enough to know that breathing happens.

The buildings also *settle*, though not in the way buildings normally settle. They do not sink or tilt or compress under their own weight. They settle in the way a conversation settles — each structure finding its position relative to the others not through force or gravity but through a continuous, silent negotiation that has been ongoing since the first building was placed and that shows no sign of concluding. The overlap between the residential tower and the water processing plant was not an accident or an error. It was a *compromise*. Both structures needed the same coordinates. Neither would yield. So they *agreed* — not consciously, not deliberately, but in the way that water agrees to fill a container, following the path of least resistance into a configuration that satisfies both constraints simultaneously. The result is a shared space that contains both habitation and machinery, both the domestic sounds of a resident closing a door and the industrial hum of filtration, layered without interfering, coexisting in the way that two radio stations can occupy the same frequency if neither one is entirely real.

This is what the beyond-modern engineers mean when they say *consensus physics*. It is not an override of physical law. It is a *renegotiation* — a process by which the material world is persuaded, through means that leave no visible mechanism, to occupy configurations that classical mechanics would reject. The engineers do not understand how it works. They understand *that* it works. They monitor it. They record it. They have produced, over the decades, a body of knowledge about the district's physics that is internally flawless and externally useless — a complete and self-consistent system that describes only this place and predicts only this place and applies to nothing else. They are proud of this. They are also, quietly, frightened by it, because a complete theory that applies to exactly one location is not a theory at all. It is a *portrait*.

The cold continues.

It is the one thing that behaves. The cold comes from the sky and from the ground and from the absence of heat in the spaces between the buildings, and it does what cold does — it settles, it persists, it makes the body aware of itself. The residents dress in layers. The layers are practical, not fashionable. Thermal undergarments of a material that may or may not be produced in the district — no one asks, because asking would require a supply chain and the district does not have a supply chain so much as a *continuity*, a flow of materials that appear when needed and that no one traces to its source. Over the undergarments: wool, synthetic, wool again. The outer layer is always a coat that was never new. Residents arrive with coats that look like they have already been worn for years, and this may be because they have been, because clothing in the district ages differently, the fabric softening and fraying and then *stabilizing*, reaching a state of wear that does not progress further, as though the garment has decided how old it will be and has stopped.

The cold makes the breath visible. In the interior spaces, the breath and the condensation merge, so that each resident walks in a small cloud of their own making that joins the cloud of the building and both become indistinguishable. In the courtyards, where the skeletal trees stand, the breath rises and catches in the bare branches and lingers there, white among white, until the air moves it away. The trees do not respond to this. They do not respond to anything visible. But there are residents — a few, the ones who sit in the courtyards for long hours — who believe the trees are *listening*, that the condensation on their bark carries information in its chemistry, that the water the district exhales is a language the trees have learned to read. These residents have no evidence for this belief. They hold it anyway, in the way one holds a tool that might be useful or might be decorative but that feels right in the hand.

At the edges, where the district meets the forming geology, the ground is different. You can feel it through your shoes — the solidity gives way, not to softness but to *uncertainty*. The surface is firm but its composition changes with each step. Here it is calcium. Here it is basalt. Here it is the unnamed lung-colored stratum that has existed for only eleven years and that geologists cannot classify because it does not match any known material and because every sample taken from it behaves differently depending on where in the district it was removed. The ground at the edges is where the district's argument with itself is loudest. Standing there, you can feel the slow ripple beneath your feet — the geology continuing to form, the tectonic sentence still unfinished — and you can feel, too, a second rhythm, fainter, that does not come from the ground at all but from somewhere above or beside or *through*, as though the spatial distortions have a pulse, a heartbeat, a period of contraction and release that operates on a timescale too long to observe but too present to ignore.

The moss knows this rhythm. The deep-green rosettes on the western walls grow in patterns that correspond to the spatial distortions — thicker where distances are most unreliable, thinner where the geometry is relatively stable, absent entirely in the areas where the second landscape most frequently manifests. The moss does not know this in the way that you and I know things. It knows it in the way that roots know gravity. It grows toward the instability the way plants grow toward light, but not to reach it — to *be near it*, to be adjacent to the place where the district is most actively becoming, where the process that has not finished is most audible, if you can hear the way moss hears, which is slowly, and with the whole body.

The bird has not returned. The wren. Residents mention it occasionally, not with longing but with the same careful tone they use for everything — the tone of people who have learned that observation is the only reliable currency and that surprise is a luxury reserved for those who have not yet understood where they live. *A bird was here. It stayed for a while. It left.* These are the facts. The facts are sufficient.

What is not sufficient — what has never been sufficient, what the district's engineers and residents and moss and trees and buildings and geology are all, in their various ways, *working toward* — is a word for what the place is doing. Not what it *is*. What it is *doing*. The district is a verb without a tense. It is not becoming. It is not arriving. It is not falling apart or falling together. It is *negotiating* — with itself, with its own geometry, with the cold that is the only honest thing, with the residents who have chosen to live inside a process that has no end point and no name.

The residents do not need the word. They have the *experience*. They wake in their small rooms and wipe the condensation from the walls and dress in their layers and step out into the district that shifts and argues and overlaps around them, and they move through it with the ease of people who have made a home inside a question. They do not need the question answered. The question is the home. The not-knowing is the architecture. The cold, which is the only honest thing, makes them alive to it — every nerve alert, every breath visible, every footstep a small act of faith that the ground beneath them will continue to exist in the next moment, even if it does not exist in the same way it existed in the last.

They are alive. The district is alive. The geology is alive. The buildings, with their exhaled moisture and their silent negotiations, are alive. Even the space between things is alive — shifting, deepening, contracting at night when the droplets on concrete become the dominant sound and the shadows of the skeletal trees point toward illuminations that no one has agreed to contain.

Something continues.

It has not finished becoming.

Neither have they.
