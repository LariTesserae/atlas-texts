The silence here is not empty. It is full of water and the slow conversation of stone, and within that, other sounds move like shy animals.

You learn to hear the inhabitants not by their voices but by their traces. The scrape of a tool on rock—a sound so like the natural groaning of the mountain it takes you a moment to recognize its intentionality. The soft *thunk* of a placed stone. The ripple in a pool that isn’t wind-caused, but the footprint of something small and quick darting from one mossy seam to another.

They move like the water does. Quietly, following the paths of least resistance—the flat terraces, the shallow basins, the ledges that hold a bit more sun. You see them, sometimes. Not faces, not yet. First, a shadow detaching itself from the angular shadow of a building. A shape hunched over the edge of a pool, hands working in the shallows, sorting the pale silt or gathering something that glints—mica, perhaps, or the wet, iridescent shell of a beetle. They are dressed in the colors of the place: the grey of dry stone, the rust-pink of the veined rock, the deep, wet green of moss. Their movements are slow, deliberate, conserving heat in the perpetual cool.

Their bodies are adaptations. Lean, efficient, built for climbing and balance on wet stone. Feet that know every slick angle and secure foothold. Hands scarred by tool-stone and the mineral bite of the water, but nimble, capable of tying the thin, tough cordage they make from the fibrous roots of the largest ferns. Their skin has the same wet-sheen quality as everything else in the basin, as if they are never quite dry, as if they drink and weep through their pores like the rock itself.

There are families, but small ones. A child is a rare sight—a brief, startling flash of movement higher up on an unfinished terrace, where the walls are more ruin than shelter. The child does not play in the way you know. It climbs, testing its weight on the slabs. It sits with absolute stillness for long periods, watching a beetle navigate the miniature forest of a moss mat. It learns the language of the place, which is not words but shifts: the slight give of a damp rock underfoot, the moment a fern frond will release its held drop of water, the particular low note the wind makes as it passes between two specific walls. Communication is often just a shared glance toward the sky when the haze thins, or a hand pointing to a new, dark seep of water appearing on a face that was dry yesterday.

They tend the mountain as one tends a slow, breathing animal. They clear the drainage channels not with urgency but with the patience of grooming. They add to the walls, yes. But they also remove—prying loose a slab that has shifted dangerously, or carefully breaking away a section of moss where it threatens to widen a crucial seam. Their work is a negotiation, not a conquest. They do not fight the weeping; they guide it. They do not smooth the rough stone; they learn its roughness.

In the evenings—or what passes for evenings in the bright, hazy light—you hear another sound. A humming. Not from a throat, but from the resonant chambers of the empty buildings. The wind plays the architecture, but sometimes, someone inside will add their own low, continuous tone, harmonizing with the wind-note, making the stone vibrate in sympathy. It is not singing. It is a way of making one’s presence felt in the bones of the place, of adding a human frequency to the mountain’s groan.

They eat what the place yields: the tender, coiled hearts of ferns, boiled in water that tastes of mineral and sky; the fat, protein-rich beetles crushed into a paste; the starchy tubers of a root that grows only in the deepest, wettest cracks. They drink the water, always the water, filtering it through mats of sphagnum moss that make it sweet and cool. Their bodies are nourished by the mountain’s own seepage.

At night—if the haze ever clears to stars, which it sometimes does for a few breathless hours—the settlement is dark. No fires burn for light. They see as the lizard sees, their pupils wide, absorbing the faint glow of the wet surfaces, the silver reflection of the stars in the shallow lake. The darkness is not frightening. It is simply another state of the water and the stone, and they are part of it.

They are few. Perhaps no more than the fingers on two hands. They do not all live in the settlement. Some have made homes in the upper fragments, in caves carved just deep enough to block the wind, where they can watch the approaches to the basin. They come and go like mist. You might see a figure on a high ridge at dawn, outlined against the brightening haze, utterly still, just looking. Watching the light change on the rock, or following the flight of a passing bird, or waiting for the precise moment when the sun will strike a certain pool and ignite it with light.

Their connection to each other is as quiet as their connection to the place. You see it in the way a tool left on a wall is later found neatly stored. In the way the basin’s center is kept clear of debris, a shared, sacred mirror. In the way two of them might pass on a terrace, close enough to touch, and exchange nothing more than a slight tilt of the head, a shared inhalation of the mineral air, before continuing on their separate, slow paths.

They are the mountain’s thoughts, moving at its pace. They are the unfinished project’s custodians, though they might not remember its original name or purpose. The purpose has become the maintenance, the listening, the quiet addition of one more slab to one more wall, the ensuring that the water flows where it should, the patient watching of the moss’s advance.

If you stay long enough, you become another one of their traces. Your footprints in the moss, the slight disturbance in a pool, the warmth you leave on a sun-warmed stone. You become part of the listening. You begin to hear the groaning not as geological but as conversational—a slow, deep exchange between the mountain and its few, quiet inhabitants. They are not masters of this place. They are its latest layer of sediment, settling into the seams, becoming stone, becoming water, becoming the green, ongoing argument between rock and life. And they are still being made, here, at the speed of geology, in the place that has not yet decided what it is, and perhaps, in the end, will decide that it is this: a weeping, unfinished home for those who move like water and listen like stone.
