Living within the hollow are the **Kept**, for so they call themselves in their soft, close-range speech. They are not the original dwellers, but generations removed from those who first found shelter in the thornroot’s fracture. Their bodies reflect this long intimacy with the dark, the damp, and the quiet. Their skin is pale, with a faint, pearly sheen from perpetual humidity. Eyes are larger than those of sun-folk, the pupils dilated to drink in the gloom, seeing in shades of grey and muted root-color. Their hearing is preternaturally acute, tuned to the faint click of a millipede’s mandible or the slow *plip* of a water droplet falling three chambers away. Their voices, as described, are rarely used at full volume; their true language is touch and pressure, a complex tactile dialogue developed over centuries in this soundless world.

They are tender with their environment and with each other. Their movements are deliberate, economical, designed to disturb neither the fauna nor the structural roots. They walk softly on the root-mat floor, their feet calloused and sensitive, feeling the vibrations of the living architecture beneath them. They do not see themselves as owners, but as **tenants** or **guests**—a distinction without practical difference, but one that shapes their entire worldview. They tend the hollow as one tends a sleeping companion: with care, with minimal disturbance, with a deep understanding of its rhythms and its tolerance.

Their sustenance is a threefold pact:
1.  **The Seep:** The thornroot’s weeping water is their drink and the base of their simple gruel (made from a cultivated, shade-tolerant fungal mat they encourage in certain damp corners).
2.  **The Harvest:** They cultivate edible parts of the thornroot itself—the inner bark of specific limbs, which when stripped and beaten becomes a fibrous, starchy food. They also farm the larger, named fungi: *Dew-Caps*, pale and spongy; *Root-Ears*, which cling to the roots in brackets; and *Thread-Mats*, a web of mycelium they coax onto prepared root-bark trays.
3.  **The Fauna:** They do not eat the fauna directly. Instead, they are **farmers of exudate.** They have learned to gently score the root bark with their flint blades in specific, rotating patterns—not enough to harm the root, but enough to stimulate a slight increase in the sugary seepage in that spot. They then cultivate "groves" of the most efficient consumers—the beetle larvae and certain fat worms—on these scored patches. The fauna grows plump on the increased flow. The people then harvest *these creatures* for food, filtering them from the wet root mats and roasting them on heated stones (a rare and carefully managed use of heat, always deep in a clay-lined fire-pit with a long, root-bark chimney that vents to the far upper tangles). The result is a gritty, protein-rich paste, the staple of their diet.

**The Fauna, in their Turn:**
*   **The Clickers:** The blind, pale millipedes (*Phosphogrubus silentii*). They are the herd animals of the hollow. Herded not by people, but by the topography of the root exudate. They move in slow, silent caravans along nutrient gradients. The people consider them sacred, or at least deeply respected. To harm a Clicker is a taboo—unnecessary and wasteful. Their faint clicking is the hollow’s heartbeat.
*   **The Crusters:** The thumbnail-sized, slick beetles. They are the first responders to any new exudate source, their chemical senses exquisite. A sudden convergence of Crusters on a wall signals a new seepage point—a valuable sign for the Kept.
*   **The Threadworms:** The thick and thin worms are the gardeners. They aerate the substrate, process waste, and prevent the clay below from becoming septic. The people leave offerings of fungal scrap for them at the edges of their sleeping platforms.
*   **The Dust:** The swarms of springtails. They are the living weather of the hollow, a shifting, drifting census of moisture and nutrient levels. The Kept read them like a sky: a thick, settled dust indicates calm and stable conditions; a sudden, swirling cloud means a shift in humidity or a nearby root shift. Children are taught to read the Dust before they are taught their own names.
*   **The Named:** A few species have earned specific names through interaction. *Glow-Worms* are not worms but fungal filaments that emit a faint, phosphorescent glimmer when disturbed, used by children to mark passages in the deepest dark. *Leather-Slugs* are the larger slugs, their trails leaving temporary, glistening paths on the root-walls, sometimes harvested for the mild antiseptic in their slime (used on minor cuts).

**The Great Fruit** is an event, a calendar marker. Its appearance signals a peak in the thornroot’s metabolic activity. The warmth it radiates is sacred. The Kept gather not just for the heat, but to be near this concentrated pulse of *life*. They tell stories during these times—stories of the root’s heart, of the sky-world, of the first family to descend. They do not worship the thornroot, but they **commune** with it during the fruiting, feeling its slow, immense vitality through the warmth on their skin. After the fauna has consumed the fruit and dispersed, the Kept collect the emptied husk. The dried rind becomes a valuable material: a waterproof membrane for lining water basins, a smooth surface for scoring tactile messages, or a soft wrapping for infants.

They are a people defined by containment and subtlety. Their technology is one of **adaptation, not domination.** They do not shape the world to their needs so much as they learn the world’s shape and fill the niches it provides. Their history is not written, but felt—impressed into the worn grooves of a root-bark tool, encoded in the specific touch-patterns of an elder’s hand, remembered in the location of the best *Dew-Cap* groves.

To an outsider, the hollow might seem a prison of dark and damp. To the Kept, the outside is the true prison—a place of overwhelming, dangerous openness. Here, every surface is known. Every sound is accounted for. The darkness is not an absence, but a presence—the presence of the root, the water, the many small lives, and the eighteen humans who are just another strand in its dense, quiet, and ceaselessly living web. They are not master of this place. They are a part of its metabolism, a slow-beating component in the vast, hidden body of the thornroot. And in this belonging, they find a peace as deep and constant as the seeping water itself.
