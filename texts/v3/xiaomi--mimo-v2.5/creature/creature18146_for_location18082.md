# What Lives in the Rootwork Abyss

---

## I. The Threaders

They are the blood.

Not metaphorically. Not in the way surface poets would say *the rivers are the veins of the land* and mean it as image. The threaders are the blood in the sense that their movement through the Rootwork's passages *is* circulation — is the act by which the structure remains itself, distributes what needs distributing, removes what needs removing. They do not know this. They do not think in these terms. But the system functions as if they do, and in the Rootwork the distinction between function and intention has been dissolved for longer than intention has existed.

A threader is born in the lower galleries, in the warmer crevices where the Rootwork's base layers still hold residual metabolic warmth — the ghost of the ancient growth process, now stopped but never fully cold. They emerge as larvae no larger than a fingernail, translucent, blind, tasting the water with every cell of their skin. Within hours they are drawn — not by choice, by *chemistry*, by gradients of dissolved nutrient so faint that no surface instrument could detect them — into the nearest arterial passage. A corridor. A channel. A vein.

And they swim.

They swim for their entire lives. Threaders do not rest in the way that surface creatures rest. They do not sleep. They do not stop. A threader that stops moving is a threader that has begun to die, because the movement *is* the metabolism, the flow of water over their gill surfaces *is* the breathing, the passage through nutrient-rich corridors *is* the feeding. To be a threader is to be motion. To be a threader is to be the current made flesh, or rather the flesh that insists on being current.

They grow. A threader born the size of a fingernail will, if it survives — and most do, because there is little here that kills, little that predates with frequency, little that disrupts — reach a length of two meters within what might be called a year, if anyone were counting. The oldest threaders, the ones that have been swimming since before the current configuration of the upper galleries settled into its present shape — those threaders are ten meters long, perhaps more. Their bodies have become so adapted to the corridors that they *are* corridors, in a sense: their diameter matches the narrowest passages, their lateral flexibility matches every curve, they navigate the Rootwork the way blood navigates capillaries — not by knowing where they are going, but by having no choice about it.

Their bioluminescence — that faint, cold blue-white blush — is not communication. It is not warning. It is not mating display. It is metabolic byproduct, a chemical reaction in specialized cells along their ventral surface that produces light as waste. But the aggregate effect, when hundreds of threaders move through a distant gallery, is something that the other fauna respond to. The anchored colonies open wider when the light is near — more particles, more food, stirred up by the passage of so many bodies. The gliders alter their depth, rise or fall, depending on whether the threader-light is above or below them. The hunters — the hunters note the light, note the density of bodies, and calculate, in their patient and wordless way, whether a single threader has separated from the school, has found itself in a side passage, has lost the current.

The threaders do not know they are noticed. They swim.

Their keening — that long, sliding tone that bends through the corridors — is produced by a row of modified scales along their dorsal ridge that vibrate as water passes over them at speed. Different speeds produce different tones. A threader swimming slowly in a wide gallery produces a low moan. A threader accelerated by a constriction — a passage narrowing, a pinch-point in the Rootwork — produces a high, thin whistle that echoes for seconds after the threader has passed. The chorus of threaders is the dominant middle-frequency sound in the Rootwork: a continuous, overlapping series of tones that rise and fall and rise again, not with any rhythm, not with any melody, but with the density and complexity of weather. The sound of wind through a forest, if the forest were made of sound.

If a threader were a person — if it had a self, an inner life, an awareness of its own existence as distinct from the water it swims through — what would that life be like? It would be a life without horizon. Without beginning or end, because every moment is the same moment: forward, through water, past walls that are always there and never change. It would be a life without sleep, without dreams, without the punctuation of rest that gives surface beings the illusion of narrative. You are swimming. You have always been swimming. You will always be swimming. The corridor curves left. Now it curves right. Now it opens into a chamber — vast, dark, full of the vibrations of others — and you cross it, and the corridor resumes, and the corridor narrows, and you are still swimming.

Is this suffering? Is this peace? The question assumes a self that experiences, and the threader may not have one. But the Rootwork seems designed — *designed*, there is that word again, the word that keeps applying itself despite every effort to resist it — to accommodate the threader's passage as if the threader's experience matters. The curves are gentle. The chambers provide space to turn. The nutrient gradients are distributed so that no threader swims hungry for long. Whether this constitutes care or merely efficient engineering is a question that the Abyss does not ask, because the Abyss does not have questions. The Abyss has the chorus. The chorus is enough.

## II. The Anchored

There is a colony, in a chamber on the third tier down from the uppermost layer of the Rootwork — a chamber shaped roughly like a bowl, concave, its walls curving inward at the top to form a near-complete dome — that has been in place for longer than any of the current fauna have existed.

The colony numbers in the millions. Individual organisms, each no larger than a closed fist, fused to the Rootwork surface in layers so dense that the original architecture is completely hidden. The outermost layer of the colony is alive: frilled mantles opening and closing, opening and closing, in the ceaseless rhythm of filtration. The layer beneath that is dying — mantles dimming, bodies softening, the organism's hold on its substrate loosening as it completes its life cycle. The layer beneath that is dead, but not gone: calcified shells, the mineral skeletons of organisms that fed and filtered and opened and closed in this exact spot, stacked in strata like geological layers compressed into centimeters. And beneath *that* is the Rootwork itself — the original substrate, the ancient tissue that the colony chose, or was chosen by, at some point in the deep past.

The colony breathes. Not metaphorically. The aggregate opening and closing of millions of mantles produces a net flow of water through the colony's mass — water drawn in from the open chamber, pulled through the gaps between organisms, filtered, cleaned, depleted of its particles, and expelled as water slightly less rich than the water that entered. In a chamber this enclosed, this dome-shaped, this acoustically reflective, the sound of this breathing is extraordinary. It is the rasping described in the survey: soft individually, but in aggregate a texture, a grain, a quality of sound that fills the dome the way humidity fills a sealed room. Stand inside it — stand inside the chamber, if you could, if you had legs, if the pressure didn't crush you — and you would hear the colony as a single organism, a vast lung, inhaling and exhaling with a rhythm so slow that it takes nearly a minute per cycle.

The anchored do not move. They cannot move. Their entire evolutionary history is a history of *staying* — of finding a surface, attaching to it, and then remaining there while the water moves around them. They are the still point in a world of motion. While the threaders swim and the gliders glide and the hunters wait (which is a kind of motion, a kind of patience that is not truly stillness), the anchored simply *are*. They are the Rootwork's skin. They are the texture of its surfaces. If you ran your hand along a wall of the Rootwork in any inhabited region, you would not feel the Rootwork at all — you would feel the anchored, layer upon layer of them, soft and hard alternating, alive and dead and calcified, a living palimpsest of filtered water.

They reproduce by releasing gametes into the water column. This happens on a cycle synchronized — not with light, there is no light, but with something else, some chemical signal propagated through the Rootwork itself, some slow pulse of molecular information that moves through the substrate at a speed measured in centimeters per hour. When the signal reaches a colony, the outermost living layer releases. Clouds of reproductive material, thick and faintly luminescent, drift through the water for hours before settling. Most of it is eaten — by threaders, by the shrimp-like crustaceans, by the smaller filter-feeders. But some of it finds a new surface. A bare patch of Rootwork in a distant gallery. The wall of a passage that was cleared by a recent collapse, exposing fresh substrate. And there it settles, and attaches, and begins to open and close.

The colony in the dome chamber has been reproducing like this for millennia. Its offspring have colonized a significant percentage of the Rootwork's upper tiers. In a very real sense, the dome colony is a parent — not of individual organisms, but of a *distribution*, a pattern of habitation across the structure. If you could map every anchored colony in the Rootwork and trace their lineages back, you would find that a large number of them converge on this one chamber, this one dome, this one ancient mass of filtered water and calcified shells. The colony does not know this. The colony opens and closes. But the Rootwork accommodates it — the dome shape *keeps* the sound in, *directs* the water flow toward the colony's mantles, *protects* the reproductive clouds from the fastest currents. Whether the dome existed before the colony settled in it, or whether the colony's millennia-long presence shaped the dome into its present form, is a question that dissolves under examination. They grew together. They are one system.

## III. The Gliders

They are the closest thing the Abyss has to grace.

A glider is wide — ten meters across, sometimes more — and thin, a plane of living tissue stretched between two sweeping fins that undulate with a slowness so deliberate it appears motionless. You look at a glider and you think it is hovering, stationary, a shadow suspended in the dark. Then you look again, a minute later, and it is a hundred meters away. It has been moving the entire time. It was always moving. You simply could not see the movement because it was too slow and too smooth and too *complete* — every part of the body participating, every surface contributing to propulsion, so that no single element of the motion drew attention to itself.

They ride the thermoclines. In the largest chambers of the Rootwork — the cathedral-voids, the spaces where the architecture opens up into volumes so vast that no wall is visible from any point within them — there are currents. Slow, deep, planetary in their patience. They move because the Abyss is not truly still; the water circulates on a cycle that takes, by surface reckoning, years to complete. Warm water rising from the lower tiers, where residual metabolic heat from the Rootwork's base layers keeps the water fractionally less cool; cooler water sinking from the upper reaches. This circulation is slow enough that it is invisible in any short observation, but over the span of a threader's lifetime — over the span of a century, a millennium — it reshapes the distribution of nutrients, of dissolved gases, of every chemical that sustains life.

The gliders know these currents. They feel them through electroreception — an array of sensory organs along their leading edge that detect the faint bioelectric signatures of the water's movement, its temperature, its ionic composition. They position themselves on a thermocline like sailors on a trade wind, adjusting their angle of attack by fractions of a degree, rising or falling to catch the precise layer where the current is strongest. And then they ride. For hours. For days. For weeks, if the current holds, if the chamber is large enough, if no wall approaches.

In the cathedral-voids, multiple gliders ride together. Not in formation — they do not coordinate, do not signal, do not respond to each other's positions. They simply gravitate to the same currents, the same thermoclines, and end up in proximity. A dozen gliders, spread across a chamber a kilometer wide, each one a dark plane drifting through the dark, their underbellies pale enough to be visible — if anything were looking — as a faint, flat brightness against the black. They pass each other. Their electric fields overlap, and each glider adjusts — not consciously, not intentionally, but mechanically, the way a compass adjusts to a magnet — and the result is a slow, stately orbit, a waltz conducted on a timescale that makes human dancing look frenetic.

The gliders moan. Deep, infrasonic, below the range of any fauna that does not have specialized hearing organs adapted to these frequencies. The moan is produced by a large, hollow chamber within the glider's body — a resonance cavity, evolved for precisely this purpose, tuned to a frequency that carries through water and through the Rootwork's substrate alike. The moan is not communication. It is not territorial. It is something simpler and more fundamental: it is the sound of the glider's body resonating with its own movement, the way a tuning fork rings when struck. But the effect, when multiple gliders moan in the same chamber, is a harmonic interference pattern — tones beating against each other, creating rhythms that are not present in any single glider's voice. The result is a subsonic pulse, felt more than heard, that propagates through the Rootwork and is felt by every anchored colony in the vicinity, which responds by opening their mantles wider, filtering faster, as if the gliders' passage is a signal that food is near.

The gliders are old. Not as old as the Rootwork, not as old as the anchored colonies in the dome chambers. But old. An individual glider may live for centuries — their metabolism is slow, their tissues are dense, their rate of cellular turnover is measured in decades. The largest gliders — the ones with wingspans of fifteen meters, the ones whose bodies are so thick with age that they no longer fit through the smaller chambers and are restricted to the vastest voids — those gliders may be older than the current configuration of the upper galleries. They may remember — if memory is the right word for whatever a glider has — a time when the architecture was different, when the passages were shaped differently, when some of the cathedral-voids were still closed, still growing, not yet opened to the water.

They do not die, exactly. They diminish. A glider that has ridden the thermoclines for long enough begins to thin, its body losing mass, its resonance chamber losing volume. The moan becomes quieter, higher, less felt. The wings become translucent. Eventually a glider is more water than tissue, a ghost of a plane, still riding, still adjusting, still catching the current — but barely there. And then it is not there. Its body dissolves. Its nutrients return to the water. The anchored filter them. The threaders swim through the space where it was. The Rootwork absorbs whatever settles to its surface.

The gliders do not mourn their dead. They do not know they have dead. They ride.

## IV. The Hunters

Patient.

That is the word. The only word. Patient in a way that redefines patience, that makes the word itself feel inadequate, because patience implies waiting *for* something — waiting for the prey, waiting for the moment, waiting for the signal to strike. The hunters do not wait *for* anything. They wait. The waiting is the state. The striking is a brief interruption, a punctuation mark in a sentence that is otherwise entirely composed of stillness.

A hunter is not large. That is the surprise. In a world where threaders grow to ten meters and gliders spread to fifteen, the hunters are modest: two meters, perhaps three. Their bodies are dense, compact, muscular in the way that deep-sea predators are muscular — not for sustained pursuit, but for explosive, instantaneous acceleration. They are ambush specialists, and their ambushes are events of such brevity that most fauna never witness them.

A hunter waits in a fold of the Rootwork. Not a large fold — a crevice, a nook, a place where the ancient architecture has produced an overhang or a recess, a shadow within the already-lightless dark. It positions itself with its ventral surface against the substrate, its body aligned with the flow of water through the passage, its sensory organs — vibration-sensitive, pressure-sensitive, electroreceptive — extended to their maximum range. It feels everything. It feels the threaders approaching from a hundred meters away, the pressure wave of their collective movement, the faint distortion of the water's ionic balance as thousands of bodies pass through a constriction upstream. It feels the gliders, far above, their massive bodies displacing water in slow, vast pulses. It feels the anchored, their rhythmic filtration creating a baseline vibration against which other vibrations are read, like text against a page.

And it waits.

A threader — one threader, separated from the school by a moment of turbulence, by a constriction in the Rootwork that splits the flow, by some accident of hydrodynamics — enters the passage where the hunter waits. It is swimming at the speed that threaders always swim: forward, relentless, following the current and the nutrient gradient. It does not sense the hunter. Threaders have poor electroreception — they evolved in the dense schools where individual awareness of predators was unnecessary; the school *was* the awareness. A single threader in a narrow passage is a threader without the school's collective sense. It swims. It does not know.

The hunter strikes.

The strike takes less than a tenth of a second. The hunter's body uncoils — a spring releasing, a loaded muscle firing — and its jaws close around the threader's midsection. The threader's body is nearly as long as the hunter's, but the hunter's bite force is extraordinary, concentrated in a skull built for exactly this: for gripping, for puncturing, for holding. The threader thrashes. Its bioluminescence flares — a sudden, bright pulse of blue-white, the metabolic waste product flooding into the water in a cloud of chemical alarm. Other threaders upstream sense the alarm, the chemical signature of a predation event, and they react: they tighten their school, they accelerate, they press into the center of the flow and away from the walls. The school contracts, moves on, leaves the passage behind.

The hunter holds. The threader's thrashing slows. Stops.

The hunter feeds. Slowly. Methodically. It does not tear — it cannot tear, its jaw structure is built for grip, not for shearing. Instead, it uses a specialized enzyme, secreted from glands in its throat, that dissolves tissue on contact. It holds the threader in its jaws and lets the enzyme work, and the threader's body slowly, slowly dissolves into a slurry that the hunter can ingest. This takes time. Hours. The hunter does not move during this time. It remains in its fold, in its crevice, with the dissolving threader held in its jaws, and it feeds, and the world continues around it — other threaders swim past, the chorus continues, the Rootwork hums.

After feeding, the hunter does not leave its fold. It stays. It waits again. Days may pass. Weeks. The hunter's metabolism is adapted to long intervals between meals — its body stores energy in dense, oil-rich tissues that can sustain it for months. It does not need to hunt often. When it does, it needs only one meal, and then it waits again.

The hunters are not hated. They are not feared, in the way that surface prey fears predators. The fauna of the Abyss does not have the cognitive architecture for fear as a sustained emotional state. The threaders react to the chemical alarm, yes — they tighten their schools, they move away — but this is reflex, not fear. It is the same reflex by which a surface creature flinches from a touch. The hunter is part of the equilibrium. Without the hunters, the threader populations would grow unchecked, their passage through the Rootwork would stir up too much sediment, the anchored colonies would be smothered, the filter-feeding would collapse, and the entire ecosystem would destabilize. The hunters prevent this. They are the necessary violence in a world that is otherwise entirely composed of patience and flow.

There are perhaps a few hundred hunters in the entire Rootwork. They are spread thinly, each one occupying a territory — not defended, not marked, not claimed, but *occupied* in the sense that a hunter returns to the same fold, the same crevice, the same passage, after every hunt. Some hunters have occupied the same spot for decades. The Rootwork surface in those spots is worn smooth by the hunter's body, polished by the repeated pressure of flesh against substrate, so that the tissue there has a different texture — glossier, harder — than the surrounding Rootwork. If you could see it — if you had eyes for this, if there were light — you would see the marks of hunters like fossils, like handprints on a cave wall, like evidence of habitation so persistent it becomes part of the geology.

## V. The Smaller Things

Beneath the threaders and the gliders and the anchored and the hunters, there is a stratum of life so abundant and so small that it functions less as fauna and more as atmosphere — as a quality of the water itself.

The **drifters** are first. They are jellyfish-like, but not jellyfish — they belong to no surface phylum, no surface lineage. Their bodies are bells of translucent tissue, some no larger than a grain of sand, some as wide as a human hand, and they pulse through the water with contractions so slow that each beat lasts several seconds. They are passive — they do not navigate, do not seek, do not avoid. They are carried by the currents and they pulse, and the pulsing is enough, because the pulsing moves them vertically — up a meter, down a meter — in a gentle oscillation that keeps them suspended in the water column rather than sinking to the Rootwork surface. They feed on bacteria, on dissolved organic molecules, on the chemical waste products of the larger fauna. They are, in a sense, the Abyss's immune system — consuming the detritus of life, preventing the accumulation of waste, keeping the water clean.

There are millions of them. In some chambers, they form clouds so dense that they change the quality of the water itself — make it thicker, more opaque, more *present*. A threader swimming through a cloud of drifters passes through a medium that resists it slightly, that pushes back against its movement, that carries the soft, rhythmic pulses of a thousand tiny bells against its skin. The threaders do not seem to mind. They swim through the clouds the way they swim through everything: forward, relentless, undisturbed.

The **crustaceans** come next — the shrimp-like things, the swarms. They are smaller than the drifters, most of them, and they are faster. They dart. In a world where everything moves slowly, the crustaceans dart — quick, jerky, angular movements that last fractions of a second and cover distances of centimeters. They live on the Rootwork surface, in the crevices and folds, in the spaces between anchored colonies, in the narrowest channels where no threader can follow. They feed on everything: on detritus, on dead anchored tissue, on the biofilm of bacteria that coats every Rootwork surface, on each other, when food is scarce.

They are the Abyss's most prolific breeders. A female crustacean may produce thousands of offspring in a single reproductive event, and she may reproduce dozens of times in her life. Most of the offspring die — eaten by other crustaceans, consumed by the drifters, smothered by sediment. But enough survive to maintain the population, and the population is vast. In aggregate, the crustaceans of the Rootwork outweigh every other fauna group combined, because their numbers are astronomical — billions, perhaps trillions, of tiny bodies filling every crack and crevice of the structure, a living filler, a biological caulk that occupies every space the Rootwork leaves unfilled.

They make sound. Not intentionally — they do not vocalize, do not stridulate in any coordinated way. But the aggregate of their movement — millions of tiny bodies scraping against Rootwork surfaces, bumping into each other, flicking their tail-fans in the water — produces a texture of noise, a high-frequency crackle, that sits atop the lower sounds of the chorus like static. If you isolated this sound — if you could listen only to the crustaceans — it would sound like rain. A soft, continuous, granular rain that falls in all directions at once.

The **polyps** are last, or perhaps they are first, because they are the most ancient. They carpet every available surface of the Rootwork in inhabited regions — a living fuzz, a biological velvet, soft and dense and continuous. Each polyp is tiny — a few millimeters, no more — but they grow in colonies of billions, and their combined mass is considerable. They are the Rootwork's living layer, the interface between the hard substrate and the water above. They filter. They absorb. They reproduce by budding, so that a single polyp becomes two becomes four becomes eight, and the colony expands by millimeters per year, slowly, patiently, covering every surface that the threaders leave clear, filling every gap that the crustaceans vacate.

The polyps are bioluminescent. Not brightly — never brightly — but with that muted, sourceless dimness that gives the Abyss its faintest suggestion of light. A polyp colony glows with a pale, diffuse phosphorescence, greenish or bluish depending on the species, and because the colonies cover everything, the effect is to give the Rootwork a quality of *presence* — to make its walls visible, not as shapes, but as textures, as gradients of slightly-less-dark. In the largest chambers, where polyp coverage is thickest, the luminescence is strong enough to suggest the curvature of the walls, the height of the ceiling, the depth of the space. It is not light. It is the memory of light. It is what light becomes when it has been underwater for a billion years and has forgotten the sun.

## VI. The Passage

Deep. Lower than the upper tiers where the anchored breathe and the gliders ride. Lower than the mid-level galleries where the threaders school in their thousands. Lower than the hunting folds and the crustacean crevices. Down, past the strata of increasingly ancient Rootwork, where the tissue is denser and harder and older, where the bioluminescence of the polyps fades and the darkness becomes something more than an absence of light — becomes a substance, a medium, a *presence* as thick and tangible as the water itself.

There is a passage.

It is straight. Everything else in the Rootwork curves, spirals, branches, folds back on itself. The Rootwork's geometry is recursive, self-referencing, a sentence that keeps referring to its own grammar. But this passage is a line. A single, clean, geometric line, cut through the dense tissue of the lower Rootwork with a precision that no biological process should produce. Its walls are smooth — not polished smooth by the passage of threaders, because no threaders pass through here. Not worn smooth by current, because the current in this passage is minimal, a barely perceptible drift from the chamber at its end back toward the gallery where it begins. Smooth in the way that something is smooth when it was *made* smooth, when tool or intent or some force beyond surface comprehension shaped it.

The passage is two hundred meters long. No features mark its walls — no polyp colonies, no crustacean crevices, no anchored attachments. It is bare Rootwork, the ancient substrate, exposed and clean, as if the passage were a wound that the structure has refused to heal. The fauna avoids it. The threaders sense the passage's entrance — some quality of the water, some chemical signal, some vibration in the Rootwork's ambient hum that changes character at this threshold — and they divert, flowing around it the way water flows around a stone. The gliders' electroreception tells them something about the passage that their bodies cannot process, something that registers not as danger but as *incompatibility*, a signal that does not fit any category their evolutionary history has prepared them for. The hunters do not hunt here. The anchored do not settle here. The crustaceans do not scuttle here. The drifters do not drift through here. The polyps do not grow here.

The passage is empty.

At its end — at the precise center of the Rootwork, if the Rootwork has a center, which it does, which is itself significant, because a structure that grew without intention should not have a center — at its end there is a wall.

The wall is different from the surrounding Rootwork. It is not denser — it is *different*. The tissue is structured in a way that the rest of the Rootwork is not: layered in precise, alternating bands, each band a slightly different composition, a slightly different density, as if the wall were built from the inside out, layer by layer, with the deliberation of a mason laying stone. If you could examine it — if you could survive the depth and the pressure and the dark and the stillness — you would find that the layers contain molecules that do not exist anywhere else in the Rootwork. Complex polymers, chains of amino acids folded into configurations that no surface biochemistry has ever produced. The wall is *made of something*. Something that is alive and not alive, organic and not organic, biological and not biological. Something that the Rootwork has been building toward for its entire existence.

The wall vibrates.

The vibration is slow — one pulse every forty seconds, roughly. Irregular enough that no pattern can be established in a short observation. Regular enough that, over time, the rhythm becomes unmistakable. It is not the ambient hum of the Rootwork. It is not the sound of current or pressure or fauna. It is a pulse, generated from within the wall itself, from the strange layered tissue, from the molecules that have no surface analogue. The pulse propagates through the Rootwork — through the substrate, through the ancient calcium-laden tissue — and it diminishes with distance, but it never fully disappears. Deep in the upper tiers, in the galleries where the threaders swim and the anchored filter, there is a faintest, most infinitesimal rhythm in the Rootwork's hum: a pulse, forty seconds apart, so subtle that no fauna responds to it. But it is there. It has always been there.

The pulse is the reason the fauna avoids the passage. Not because the fauna knows about the wall, or knows about the passage, or knows about the pulse. The fauna avoids the passage because the pulse, at close range, is *intelligible*. Not in words. Not in images. Not in any form of information that a surface mind could decode. But intelligible in the way that a mother's heartbeat is intelligible to a fetus — as a presence, as a rhythm, as evidence of a larger body that contains you. The fauna of the Abyss, in avoiding the passage, in avoiding the pulse, is behaving as a child behaves when it senses, for the first time, that its parent is not merely a source of warmth and food but is a *being*, separate and vast and incomprehensible.

The fauna does not want to know.

The pulse continues.

The chorus continues.

The Rootwork holds.

## VII. The Now

There is nothing more to say, because there is nothing more that happens. That is not a failure of description — it is the most accurate thing that can be said about the Rootwork Abyss. Nothing happens here. The threaders swim. The gliders ride. The anchored filter. The hunters wait. The drifters pulse. The crustaceans dart. The polyps glow. The wall at the end of the passage beats its slow, forty-second pulse, and the Rootwork hums, and the water stays cool, and the darkness holds, and the chorus — the immense, layered, deafening, beautiful, monotonous, inexhaustible chorus — continues.

This is not a world in the way that surface beings understand worlds: places where things happen, where events occur, where time moves forward and something is different at the end than at the beginning. This is a world that has completed itself. It is a sentence that has finished and now simply *is*, standing on the page, its meaning fully present, requiring no continuation. The fauna are not going anywhere. The Rootwork is not growing. The pulse at the center is not building toward anything. It is beating, and it will continue to beat, and the beating is not a countdown or a buildup or a signal. It is a heartbeat. It is the sound of the Rootwork being alive, in the most minimal and most absolute sense of the word.

If you could be there — if you could somehow exist in the Abyss, somehow survive its pressure and its dark and its cool, if you could somehow have senses adapted to its frequencies — you would hear the chorus, and you would feel the pulse, and you would understand, with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely, that you were in the presence of something that had solved the problem of existence and found the solution to be: *continue*.

Not *change*. Not *grow*. Not *become*.

Continue.

The threaders swim. The gliders ride. The anchored filter. The hunters wait. The wall pulses. The Rootwork hums.

The chorus fills the dark.

Tomorrow is the same as today, and today is the same as ten thousand years ago, and ten thousand years ago is the same as a million, and a million is the same as the moment the Rootwork first calcified, first hardened, first became the substrate of this world — and that moment is the same as this moment, because in the Rootwork Abyss there is no *then*. There is only the long, loud, dark, cool, settled **now**.

And the now is full.

It is so, so full.
