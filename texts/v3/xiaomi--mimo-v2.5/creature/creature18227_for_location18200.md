# What Lives in the Hollow

## I. The Mat

Begin where you cannot see.

Beneath the algae, beneath the moss, beneath the isopods and the springtails and the slug with its slow glacier's purpose, there is a mat. It covers every submerged surface in the basin — a living skin, translucent where it is young and opaque where it has thickened, clinging to gravel chips and limestone chips and the mineral crusts at the water's edge with an adhesion that resists the faint currents the breathing of the compressors sets up. If you could peel it away, which you cannot, it would come in sheets, gelatinous and faintly greenish-grey, holding together with the tenacity of wet paper.

This is a biofilm. The word is clinical and insufficient. It is a *city*.

The base of the mat — the part pressed against stone — is bacterial. Not one species but dozens, perhaps hundreds, arranged not randomly but in functional strata that have organized themselves in response to gradients of chemistry and light so subtle that only the organisms themselves could detect them. At the very bottom, pressed against the limestone where calcium carbonate is still precipitating from the seep, are chemolithotrophs — bacteria that eat stone. They derive their energy not from sunlight, not from organic matter, but from the chemical bonds in minerals, oxidizing iron and manganese that leach from the freshly broken limestone, reducing sulfate that seeps from somewhere in the station's plumbing. They are colorless. They are invisible. They are the foundation of everything.

Above them, in a layer perhaps a twentieth of a millimeter thick, cyanobacteria have begun to establish themselves — photosynthetic, though in this dimness they photosynthesize at a rate that would be imperceptible to anything but themselves. They are the green. Not the green of the moss, which is lush and obvious, but a green so dark it is nearly black, a green that exists at the absolute threshold of what light can support. They stretch toward the crack in the eastern wall the way a plant stretches toward a window, but at a timescale of hours rather than weeks, their filaments reorienting imperceptibly as the faint grey luminescence shifts with the passing of clouds above. They are the bridge between the mineral world and the living world — taking the energy of whatever light reaches this place and converting it into carbon, into sugar, into the organic molecules that feed everything else.

And above the cyanobacteria, in the water itself, a suspension of organisms too small to count: diatoms with glass shells, flagellates spinning in place, ciliates sweeping bacteria into their oral grooves with coordinated rows of hairlike cilia, amoebae extending pseudopods with the patience of the slug above them, engulfing particles one at a time. This is the water that drips from the ceiling. This is the water that the moss drinks. This is the water that tastes mineral and metallic and green. It tastes that way because it is alive in every cubic millimeter, a dilute broth of metabolism so dense that the water itself has become an organ of the mat, carrying nutrients and waste and signal molecules between organisms that cannot move and organisms that cannot stay still.

The mat breathes with the station. When the compressors cycle and the pressure differential reaches through the crack, the water level shifts by fractions of a millimeter, and the mat ripples — not visibly, not in any way you could photograph, but chemically, the oxygen concentrations shifting as fresh water is drawn in and depleted water is pushed out, the bacterial communities responding to these oscillations as though they were tides. They have never known anything else. To them, the compressor station is not noise, not vibration, not the intrusion of industry into geology. It is *weather*. It is the rhythm of the world, as constant and unquestionable as the sun was to organisms that lived in light.

## II. The Mites

Among the isopods, in the moss, there are mites.

You would not see them without magnification. They are arachnids, related to the spiders, but they have abandoned webs and silk and patience for a different strategy. They are round, barely a millimeter across, amber or dark brown, with eight legs that move in a hurried, mechanical gait, as though each leg is operating on its own schedule and the animal is simply the consensus between them. They are, to the isopods, what the spiders are to the springtails — predators — but they are smaller and more numerous and less selective in their diet.

They eat algae scraped from the surfaces of stone. They eat fungal hyphae pulled from the moss. They eat the fecal pellets of springtails, which are rich in partially digested plant matter and bacteria, and in doing so they accelerate the decomposition of waste that would otherwise accumulate in the hollow's tight quarters. They eat each other. They eat their own shed exoskeletons when they molt, recycling the chitin. They eat dead moss. They eat living moss, though not enough to damage it. They eat bacteria directly, grazing on the biofilm at the water's edge with mouthparts designed for scraping.

There are mites in every moss cushion. There are mites in every crevice. There are mites on the isopods themselves — not parasitizing them but simply using them as transport, riding the slow segmented bodies across wet surfaces the way a human might hail a cab. The isopods do not seem to mind, or perhaps they do mind and lack the means to object. The relationship is phoretic: the mites go where the isopods go, and the isopods go everywhere, and so the mites go everywhere, and in this way the mites have colonized every microhabitat the hollow offers without needing to cross a single open expanse of bare stone on their own legs.

They have a life cycle measured in weeks. A female mite lays a clutch of eggs — six, eight, twelve — in a crevice where the moss is thickest and the moisture most constant. The eggs hatch into larvae with six legs, which feed for several days before molting into protonymphs, then deutonymphs, then adults. The entire cycle, egg to egg-laying adult, takes less than a month in the cold of this place. In a warmer world it might take less time. But the cold is patient, and the mites are patient with it, and their populations grow at the pace of moss growth, which is to say slowly, which is to say inevitably.

They are the decomposers that the hollow needs. Without them, dead moss would accumulate faster than bacteria could break it down, and the thin soil that the moss is building would be overwhelmed by undecomposed organic matter. The mites chew it down. They chew it and they digest it and they excrete it as fecal pellets that are themselves colonized by bacteria, and the bacteria break it further, and what remains is humus — dark, wet, rich with nutrients, a fraction of a millimeter deep, holding water and minerals in a matrix that is neither stone nor water but something new. Something that did not exist in this place a year ago. Something the mites are building without knowing they are builders.

## III. The Fungi

In the moss, threading through it like a nervous system, are fungi.

They are not visible as mushrooms. There are no fruiting bodies in the hollow, no caps or stems or gills, no spore clouds released into the air. The fungi here live as hyphae — single-cell-thick filaments, white against the dark green of the moss, branching and rebranching and anastomosing with one another until they form a network that connects individual moss plants into a web of shared resources. A moss plant receiving light from the crack in the eastern wall sends carbon, fixed by photosynthesis, through fungal hyphae to a moss plant in the darkest corner where no light reaches. The fungus takes a fee — a percentage of the carbon, a tithe — and in exchange it delivers water and minerals to the moss that cannot extract them efficiently from bare stone on its own.

This is mycorrhiza adapted to moss rather than to tree roots, but the principle is the same: cooperation mediated by fungus, a marketplace of carbon and mineral where the currency is survival. The fungi are ancient in their strategy. They were doing this before there were trees. They were doing this when moss was the dominant vegetation on land, hundreds of millions of years ago. They are doing it now, in a hollow beneath a compressor station, and they are doing it with the same molecular machinery, the same biochemical handshake, the same quiet commerce that they have always done.

The hyphae also decompose. When a moss leaf dies — and they die constantly, each one lasting a season or two before succumbing to age or cold or the grazing of mites — the hyphae penetrate the dead tissue and begin to digest it from within, extracellular enzymes breaking down cell walls, liberating the carbon and nitrogen locked inside. This is slow work in the cold. A single dead moss leaf might take a year to fully decompose. But the fungi are not in a hurry. They have nothing but time. The hollow is stable, the temperature is constant, the moisture never wavers, and the food supply is replenished with every moss leaf that dies, which is to say continuously. The fungi grow at the pace of the hollow itself — slowly, incrementally, filling every available space with their invisible architecture.

Some of the hyphae extend beyond the moss, reaching across bare stone to the next moss cushion, probing for new territory. They advance at a rate of perhaps a centimeter per month, exploring the limestone surface with the tips of their leading hyphae, which are specialized for this purpose: thin-walled, enzyme-secreting, responsive to chemical gradients in the stone. When they find a crack or a seam where moisture persists, they branch and colonize. When they find dry stone — which in this hollow is rare — they stop and wait, the hyphae thickening slightly, their cell walls reinforcing, entering a state of dormancy that can last months. They are patient. They are the oldest strategy in the hollow, predating the mosses they partner with, predating the animals that shelter among them. They are the infrastructure.

## IV. The Nematodes

In the water — in the pooled water of the basin and in the film of water that coats every surface — there are nematodes.

They are unseeable. They are the most abundant animals in the hollow, more numerous than the springtails, more numerous than the mites, more numerous than the bacteria they feed on. They are transparent, cylindrical, tapering at both ends, and they move with a characteristic sinuous motion that is neither swimming nor crawling but something between — a thrashing progress through water so thin it would be a film on a microscope slide. In the pooled water of the basin, where the depth reaches a centimeter at the lowest point, they are truly aquatic, navigating a world where surface tension is a boundary as real as a wall and a grain of limestone dust is a boulder to be circumnavigated.

Some of them are bacterivores, feeding on the biofilm, pulling bacteria into their mouths with a pumping pharynx that contracts several times per second. They are the grazers of the invisible meadow, keeping bacterial populations in check, preventing any single species from dominating, maintaining the diversity that makes the mat resilient. Without them, the biofilm would become a monoculture — one species outcompeting all others — and the mat would become fragile, susceptible to collapse. The nematodes are the editors. They trim and select and redistribute, and in doing so they maintain the health of a community they cannot perceive as a community.

Others are fungivores, feeding on the hyphae that extend into the water film. They bite through the filaments and consume the contents, and the fungi respond by growing faster, branching more densely, investing in the kind of rapid regeneration that is the fungal answer to predation. This is not destruction. This is pruning. The fungivorous nematodes stimulate the fungi to grow, to explore, to fill more space, just as the grazing of a deer stimulates a meadow to produce more shoots. The hollow's fungal network is denser and more vigorous because of its nematodes, not in spite of them.

A few nematodes — fewer, rarer, harder to find even if you had the magnification — are predatory. They feed on other nematodes. They are larger, their mouths armed with stylets, needle-like structures that pierce the body wall of their prey and suck out the contents. They are the apex predators of the water film, the hawks and wolves of a world measured in micrometers. They keep the grazer populations in check, preventing overgrazing of the biofilm, maintaining the balance that allows the mat to persist. They are rare because they must be. A predator cannot be as abundant as its prey. But it must be present, because without it, the prey would consume everything and then starve, and the system would collapse.

The nematodes reproduce with an efficiency that borders on the obscene. A single female can produce two hundred eggs in a lifetime that lasts three weeks. The eggs hatch, the juveniles feed and grow and molt four times, reaching adulthood in ten days, and they begin reproducing immediately. At this rate, a single nematode could theoretically produce millions of descendants in a year. They do not, because the system is balanced — the predators eat them, the bacteria they feed on fluctuate, the water film dries slightly here and there as the breathing of the station shifts air patterns, killing some, sparing others. But their capacity for reproduction is the engine that drives the entire water-film ecosystem, providing a constant supply of grazers and prey and fecal pellets and corpses that feed the bacteria that feed the nematodes that feed the predators that keep the grazers in check. It is a wheel. It turns in the cold, in the dark, in a basin the size of a dinner plate, and it has been turning since the crack opened and the water found its way in.

## V. The Tardigrades

And then there are the tardigrades.

They live in the moss. They live in the biofilm. They live in the thin film of water that coats every surface in the hollow, which is every surface. They are barely visible to the naked eye — half a millimeter, perhaps, when fully extended, their bodies fat and segmented like microscopic caterpillars, four pairs of stubby legs ending in claws that grip the moss leaves and algal filaments they crawl across. They move slowly, deliberately, with the same patient economy as the slug on the limestone above. They are, in the taxonomy of the hollow, something between the mites and the nematodes — larger than the nematodes, smaller than the mites, occupying a scale of life that is nearly invisible but not quite, a scale where you need a hand lens or a low-power microscope to enter their world.

They feed on algae scraped from moss surfaces with a pair of stylets that protrude from their mouths, piercing individual algal cells and sucking out the chloroplast-rich contents. They feed on bacteria. Some species feed on moss tissue directly, puncturing cells with their stylets and consuming the cytoplasm. They are not harmful to the moss in any meaningful sense — the damage they cause is repaired in hours, and the moss has been coexisting with tardigrades for hundreds of millions of years, long enough to have adapted to their presence, long enough to have built their modest grazing into the cost of doing business.

But it is what the tardigrades can *do* that makes them remarkable, and what makes them, perhaps, the most important residents of the hollow.

When the water film dries — when the station's breathing shifts and a corner of the hollow loses its moisture for a few hours, or when a particularly cold spell causes the condensation to freeze briefly before thawing — the tardigrades survive. They retract their legs, contract their bodies into compact, desiccated cylinders called tuns, and lose nearly all the water in their cells. Their metabolism drops to undetectable levels. Their DNA, normally vulnerable to damage from desiccation, is protected by special proteins — intrinsically disordered proteins that form a glass-like matrix around the genetic material, preserving it in a state of suspended animation. In this state, they can survive not just hours of drying but *years*. They can survive temperatures far below what the hollow ever reaches. They can survive pressures, radiation, the vacuum of space. They have been revived from tuns that were fossilized in dry moss for a century.

When the water returns, they rehydrate. The tun swells. The legs extend. The metabolism restarts. Within minutes, the tardigrade is crawling again, feeding, reproducing, picking up where it left off as though nothing happened.

In the hollow, where the moisture is nearly constant, the tardigrades may never need to tun. They may live their entire lives in a state of active hydration, never calling upon the extraordinary survival mechanism that is their most famous attribute. But the mechanism is there, encoded in their genes, expressed in the structure of their proteins, a legacy of an ancestry that endured far harsher conditions than this hollow will ever produce. They carry their insurance policy everywhere, ready to use it or not, as the situation demands. They are, in this sense, the most resilient animals in the hollow — more resilient than the isopods, which would die if the moisture failed; more resilient than the spiders, whose webs would collapse; more resilient even than the bacteria, some of which would go dormant but not all of which would survive. The tardigrades would survive. They would wait. They would rehydrate when the water came back. They would begin again.

There are perhaps a hundred thousand of them in the hollow. Perhaps more. They are everywhere the moss is, and where the moss is not, in the biofilm, in the sediment, on the surfaces of stones that have no moss yet, pioneering the bare ground with the same patient, stubby-legged progress that their ancestors made on bare rock before there was soil, before there were plants, before there was anything but stone and water and the first tentative experiments of life.

## VI. The Slug's Trail

The slug is still moving.

It has crossed perhaps a third of the hollow in the hours it has been here, leaving behind a trail of mucus that is, in its own way, an ecosystem. The trail is silver when fresh, catching the grey light from the crack in the wall, but it dulls quickly as the water in the mucus evaporates or is absorbed by the stone. Before it dries, however, it is a resource. Bacteria colonize it within minutes, feeding on the organic compounds in the mucus — glycoproteins, minerals, trace amounts of whatever the slug ate before it came here. Nematodes follow the trail, grazing on the bacteria. Mites encounter it and recoil, the adhesive surface trapping their legs if they step in it, forcing them to navigate around it with the irritation of a pedestrian encountering a puddle.

The mucus trail also contains eggs. Not many — the slug has laid a small cluster, perhaps thirty, in a crevice where the moss is thick, each egg a translucent sphere barely a millimeter in diameter, filled with a yolk that is already dividing, already becoming a slug. The eggs are glued to the stone with a specialized mucus that hardens into a protective casing, semi-permeable, allowing gas exchange while preventing desiccation. In three weeks, if conditions remain stable — and they will, the hollow does not change quickly — the eggs will hatch, and thirty tiny slugs, each one a centimeter long, will emerge and begin to eat moss.

They will be vulnerable. The spiders in the hollow are too large to catch an adult slug — too large and too disinclined to tackle something that produces copious amounts of sticky mucus and has no wings to be caught in a web. But the juvenile slugs, fresh from the egg, are soft and small and slow, and if a spider's web happens to be strung across their path, they will become entangled and consumed. The mites will not bother them. The nematodes cannot bother them. The tardigrades are indifferent to them. They will grow, if they survive, at a rate of perhaps a millimeter per week, feeding continuously on moss, building their bodies from the green tissue of a plant that is itself built from the grey limestone and the dim light and the mineral water of the hollow.

Some of the juveniles will leave. They will find the crack in the eastern wall and squeeze through it and emerge into the world above — the world of construction debris and gravel and the concrete slab of the compressor station — and most of them will die there, in the dryness and the exposure and the absence of moss. But one or two might find a damp corner, a shaded spot, a place where rainwater collects, and there they might establish themselves, beginning the process of colonization anew, expanding the range of the hollow's fauna by millimeters per generation, a slow diaspora of slugs radiating outward from this unlikely origin point.

Or they might not. The hollow does not need them to. The hollow is complete in itself, a world in a basin, a universe in a hollow beneath a concrete slab, and whether its children survive to colonize new territory is irrelevant to its persistence. It will be here tomorrow. It will be here next season. The moss will grow. The mites will crawl. The tardigrades will wait, hydrated and patient, in the film of water that coats the world, and the compressor station above will continue to breathe, and the hollow will breathe with it, and the slow, slow work of life on stone will continue, undisturbed, unknown, self-sufficient in its smallness, self-sustaining in its wetness and its dark, complete in every particular, needing nothing from the world above but the crack in the wall that lets the light in and the air move and the world know, briefly and incompletely, that it is there.
