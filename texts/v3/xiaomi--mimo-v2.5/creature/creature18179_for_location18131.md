# What Lives in the Keelworks

---

They live in a place that is still deciding whether to exist, and they have made themselves at home in the indecision.

---

**The small ones** are everywhere. You stop noticing them the way you stop noticing the hum of a refrigerator — not because they become invisible but because your attention learns to set them aside, the way a fish learns to set aside the water. They number in the thousands. Possibly tens of thousands. No one has counted them because counting them would require stillness, and they are never still.

They are called, by the people who once stood where you are standing, **keelbugs**, though this is a name born of convenience rather than taxonomy. They are not insects. Their body plan borrows from insects the way a submarine borrows from a fish — superficially, functionally, and with enough deviation from the original blueprint to make a biologist uncomfortable. They have six legs on the smaller specimens and eight or ten on the larger ones, each leg ending in a pad of something that looks like compressed mineral fiber, which gives them their extraordinary grip. Their bodies are segmented — head, thorax, abdomen in the smallest, though the divisions blur on the larger individuals until the whole organism reads as a single elongated oval, dark and gleaming, like a drop of oil that has learned to walk.

Their exoskeletons are the color of the walls: that near-black volcanic glass, dark violet where the light catches the edges of their segments. Under magnification — and the Keelworks has magnification, tucked into a drawer on the third level beside a tin of solvent and a screwdriver — you would see that the exoskeleton is not chitin. Not exactly. It is a composite: a biological polymer laid down in layers over a crystalline mineral substrate that is chemically identical to the basaltic glass surrounding them. They grow their own armor from the rock itself, extracting silica and metal oxides through mouthparts that function simultaneously as feeding apparatus and mineral processing organs. They eat the walls. They eat the dust. They eat the residue of their own kind when a body falls and does not get back up.

They do not chirp. They do not hum. They do not vocalize in any register that a human ear can detect. The sound they make — that collective skittering, that static hiss beneath everything — is purely mechanical: the tap and scrape of a thousand hard bodies on hard surfaces, the whisper of mineral-footpads on steel, the faint tick-tick-tick of mandibles working against glass. But there is a second layer to it. Stand very still, in the quiet hours when the human machinery pauses between cycles, and you can feel something in the skittering that is not quite rhythm but is adjacent to rhythm. A pattern in the noise. An implication of coordination that does not resolve into obvious signal. It is the sound of a thousand individuals each doing their own thing, except that their own things, taken together, begin to look like a single thing with a thousand hands.

**What they are doing** depends on where you look.

On the upper levels, where the infrastructure is densest and the humans spend their working hours, the small keelbugs are housekeepers. They eat the dust. They eat the corrosion. They consume the thin film of organic residue that accumulates where human skin sheds onto metal surfaces, where food crumbs fall into crevices, where sweat drips onto handrails and leaves behind its faint mineral signature. They are, in this sense, vermin — but vermin of a particular and useful kind, the way a cat is useful in a granary. They keep the Keelworks clean in a way that the humans' own filtration systems cannot, reaching into gaps and joints and conduit bundles where no brush or vacuum can follow. The humans tolerate them. Some of the humans leave scraps for the larger ones, though this is discouraged in the posted regulations on the second level, regulations that are printed on laminated cards that have yellowed and curl at the edges.

On the lower levels, where the infrastructure thins and the rock predominates and the warm updrafts rise from below, the keelbugs do something else.

**The lower keelbugs are larger.** Not dramatically — a dog's body in the largest specimens, though longer, more attenuated, with a leg span that can cover a meter of vertical wall. They cluster on the platforms nearest the shaft, the descending series of natural and artificial shelves that step down into the warm dark. Here the behavior changes. The housekeeping stops. The feeding on human residue stops. Instead, the larger keelbugs work the rock.

They mine it. Not in the way the Keelworks mines it — not with drills and hydraulic presses and chemical extraction. They mine it the way termites consume a dead tree: slowly, methodically, with an industry that is individual in execution and collective in effect. You can watch one for an hour and see it scrape, process, and deposit mineral material in a small growing pile against the wall. Then you can look at the wall itself and see that the pile is part of a larger arrangement — a shelf, a ridge, a buttress of deposited material that has been built up over seasons or years into a structure that has deliberate geometry. Right angles where no natural process produces right angles. Parallel ridges spaced at regular intervals. Curved surfaces that follow the contour of the rock with a precision that suggests measurement, though no organ of measurement has been identified on their bodies.

They are **building** something. Or they are maintaining something that was already built. Or they are rebuilding something that the shifting rock has damaged, adapting the structure in real time to accommodate the slow deformation of the fissure. It is impossible to tell from a single observation. It is possible that it is impossible to tell from a hundred observations. The structures they build are not recognizable as any human thing. They are not shelters, because the keelbugs do not appear to use them for shelter — they sleep, if they sleep, in crevices and cable runs and the undersides of platforms, in the gaps that the infrastructure creates between itself and the rock. They are not food stores, because the keelbugs eat where they work and do not appear to stockpile. They are not nests, because the keelbugs reproduce — if they reproduce — in ways that have not been observed, though small keelbugs appear periodically on the lower levels in clusters of a dozen or twenty, translucent and soft-bodied, their mineral armor not yet hardened, their movements uncertain and clumsy in a way that the adults never are.

The structures are **structures**. That is all that can be said with confidence. They serve a purpose that is legible to the keelbugs and opaque to everyone else, and they are maintained with a diligence that suggests the purpose is important.

---

**The larger fauna** — the centipede-things, the meter-long navigators of vertical shafts — are a different matter entirely.

They are rare. Perhaps a dozen in the entire Keelworks at any given time. They are called, with less affection and more wariness, **runners**, and they are the source of most of the unease that visitors report on their first night underground.

They are not centipedes. They have more legs — twenty, thirty, sometimes more, the count varying between individuals in a way that suggests regeneration, limbs lost and regrown in imperfect copies. Their bodies are longer and more flexible than any centipede, capable of curving around obstacles with a fluidity that makes human joints feel like rusted hinges. Their exoskeletons are the same dark glass as everything else in the Keelworks, but thicker, denser, with a matte quality in the larger specimens that absorbs light rather than reflecting it. In the dim amber illumination of the lower levels, they are functionally invisible. They become visible only when they move, and they move fast.

They eat the keelbugs. This is the straightforward ecological observation: runners hunt small keelbugs, consuming them in quantities that suggest a high metabolic rate. A single runner can strip a section of cable run of its resident keelbugs in minutes, moving along the cable with a speed that is less walking than flowing, its many legs producing that rapid irregular skittering that resolves, when you hear it close, into something too fast and too precise to be random. The small keelbugs do not flee. They go still, pressing flat against whatever surface they are on, their mineral armor catching the light in a way that makes them momentarily harder to see against the glassy rock — a camouflage that works against the human eye but does not appear to work against the runners, which find them anyway, always.

But the runners do not only eat. The runners **move**. They are the only fauna in the Keelworks that traverse the full vertical range of the fissure, from the upper narrows where the rock closes to a person-width gap, down through the inhabited levels, past the machinery and the platforms, and into the shaft below — the indeterminate depth from which warm air rises and into which monitoring instruments have been recently, deliberately reoriented. They descend and they return. The round trip takes hours. No one knows what they do at the bottom.

When they return, they sometimes carry things. Small objects, held in the mandibles of the anterior head segments, too small to identify from the walkways where human observers stand. The objects are deposited on the lower platforms, near the structures the keelbugs maintain, and then the runners descend again. The pattern repeats. The objects are never identified. They are not taken by the keelbugs, which ignore them. They are not taken by the humans, who do not go to the lower platforms often enough or carefully enough to examine them. They accumulate in small, precise piles that are arranged with the same geometric intentionality as the keelbug structures, as though the runners and the keelbugs are engaged in a single project, contributing different materials to a common effort, separated by a scale of body size but united by something that functions, at a distance, like a plan.

---

**The growth on the north wall** is alive. This much is certain. What it is alive *as* is a question without an easy answer.

It is not a fungus. It is not a lichen. It is not a mineral deposit that merely resembles life. It is warm — consistently, measurably warm, several degrees above ambient, which in this cold dry place is a metabolic signature that demands explanation. It is pale — not white, not pink, but a color between the two that is the same color as the roseate dust that covers every surface in the Keelworks. This is not a coincidence. The dust originates here. The growth produces it: a fine particulate exudate released from the surface of its fan-shaped shelves in a slow, constant stream, so fine that it does not fall but drifts, suspended in the still air, settling eventually on whatever surface gravity and air currents guide it toward. The dust is spore. It is also mineral. It is both at once, a reproductive unit that is simultaneously a packet of geological material, designed to adhere to rock surfaces and contribute to the growth's expansion. The growth eats rock. Like the keelbugs, it extracts silica and metal oxides from the basaltic glass, but it does so chemically, through acids secreted from its leading edges, dissolving the rock a molecule at a time and incorporating the dissolved minerals into its own structure. It is slow. A shelf grows perhaps a centimeter per year. The largest shelves on the north wall are older than anyone currently working in the Keelworks has been alive.

The small keelbugs eat it. They graze on it the way cattle graze on grass — cropping the leading edges, consuming the fresh growth that is richest in mineral content, leaving behind the older, denser, more crystalline core material. This is the primary food source for the small keelbugs on the lower levels. The relationship is not parasitic. The growth does not appear to suffer from the grazing. Its leading edges grow back within days, the chemical dissolution of the rock resuming as though interrupted by nothing more than a passing wind. It is possible — it is likely — that the grazing stimulates growth, removing older material and allowing the chemical processes at the rock interface to proceed unimpeded. It is a mutualism. The keelbugs eat the growth. The growth feeds the keelbugs. The keelbugs' waste products — more of that roseate dust, enriched now with organic compounds — settle on the growth and are absorbed by it. A cycle. A closed loop of mineral metabolism, occurring in the dark, in the cold, in a crack in the earth where nothing like this should be able to sustain itself.

But the growth does more than feed keelbugs and produce dust. It also **listens**.

This is the observation that no one has published, because publishing it would require explaining it, and no one can explain it. The growth has no nervous system. It has no sensory organs in any recognizable sense. But it responds to vibration. Place a hand on one of its shelves and press, and within seconds, the leading edges of the growth — the pale, soft, actively growing margins — turn toward the pressure. Not dramatically. Not quickly. A slow curvature, a bending of millimeters, occurring over the course of minutes, so gradual that you would miss it if you were not watching for it. The growth orients toward vibration. Toward the subsonic thrumming of the machinery. Toward the footfalls on the walkways above. Toward the skittering of keelbugs on the rock. And, most notably, toward the warm updrafts from the shaft below. When the air from below warms — when a cycle of whatever is happening at depth produces a pulse of heat — the growth's leading edges lift, orient, and release a burst of dust so dense it becomes briefly visible as a pale pink cloud, drifting downward into the shaft.

The growth is feeding the shaft. Or it is signaling to the shaft. Or it is releasing something into the updraft with the same intentionality — or the same simulacrum of intentionality — that the keelbugs display in their construction and the runners display in their material transport. It is participating. Whatever the project is, the growth is part of it.

---

**The roseate dust** itself. You should know about the dust.

It is everywhere. It is in your lungs now if you have been here more than a few minutes. It is so fine that it passes through the filtration systems designed to remove it, settling on the interior surfaces of the air handlers and the humans' own air masks with a persistence that has caused more than one engineer to swear at length and with feeling. It is not toxic. It is not dangerous. It is simply *present*, the way dust is always present in dry, enclosed spaces, except that this dust is also alive, or part-alive, or the reproductive and metabolic byproduct of something that is alive, and it carries with it a faint warmth — a thermal signature that persists even after the dust has separated from the growth that produced it, as though each particle retains a memory of the metabolism that created it.

In certain concentrations, under certain lighting, the dust does the thing that the rock does: it catches light in a way that the amber fixtures cannot explain. A deep blue-green flicker at the edges of perception, gone when you look directly at it, present only in peripheral vision, only at certain angles. The dust and the rock share a mineral signature. The growth produces dust from rock. The keelbugs produce dust from rock. The dust settles on everything and becomes part of everything, and everything in the Keelworks is slowly, atom by atom, becoming the same substance.

This is not metaphor. Spectrographic analysis of the dust — performed once, by a geologist who left the Keelworks two years ago and does not discuss her time there — reveals a composition that is roughly sixty percent mineral, thirty percent organic polymer, and ten percent something that produced no recognizable spectrographic signature at all. The ten percent is the warm part. The ten percent is what glows.

---

**The humans** are the least adapted thing in the Keelworks and the most tenacious.

They come because the rock contains something worth extracting. What that something is depends on who you ask and how much they are willing to say. The official purpose — posted on the laminated cards, referenced in the mission briefings, inscribed on the commemorative plaque near the upper entrance — is geological research and mineral extraction. A research outpost. A sample-collection facility. A place where scientists study a young volcanic formation and harvest its unusual mineral deposits for analysis on the surface.

This is true. This is not all that is true.

The humans stay because the rock does something to them. Not quickly. Not obviously. Not in a way that shows up on a blood test or a brain scan, at least not in the early months. But after six months underground — after the seasonal rhythm of the surface has been replaced by the amber twilight and the subsonic hum and the constant, peripheral presence of movement in the dark — the humans begin to change. They sleep less. They eat less. They spend more time on the lower levels, standing at the railings of the descending platforms, looking down into the shaft with expressions that are not fearful and not curious and not resigned but are something adjacent to all three. They begin to notice the patterns. The dust drifts. The keelbug structures. The growth's orientation toward the deep. They begin to understand that the Keelworks is not a facility built *in* a fissure. It is a facility built *on* a living system, and the living system was here first, and the living system has purposes that predate and will outlast the humans' presence.

Some of them leave. The Keelworks has a rotation: eight months down, four up. The humans who complete their rotations return to the surface and describe the experience in terms that are careful and clinical and do not quite capture the thing they are trying to say. They describe the silence, which is not silence but the absence of wind. They describe the darkness, which is not darkness but the absence of sky. They describe the feeling of being *inside* something — not inside a building, not inside a mine, but inside a process, the way a splinter is inside a wound. They describe wanting to go back.

Some of them do go back. They serve second rotations and third rotations and eventually stop counting. They move to the upper narrows, where the infrastructure is personal and cramped, where they have carved out small domestic spaces in the rock itself — not rooms, exactly, but alcoves, fitted with bunks and cookstoves and the small accumulated artifacts of lives lived in a place that is fundamentally inhospitable to life. They bring books and music and photographs. They bring things from the surface that make no sense underground: a potted plant (it died in weeks), a photograph of the ocean (it faded in the dim amber light), a child's drawing of a house with a chimney and a sun (it is pinned to the wall of an alcove on the fifth level, next to a schematic for a hydraulic pressure valve, and it is the most human thing in the Keelworks).

They are not prisoners. The exit is open. The upper fissure leads to the surface, four hundred meters up, through a passage that is narrow but navigable, equipped with climbing infrastructure and emergency lighting. Anyone can leave. No one is held. But the exit is *up*, and up is hard, and the Keelworks is *here*, and here is warm, and here is humming, and here the dust settles on your skin and the rock glows at the edges of your vision and the keelbugs pause in their work and turn their dark glassy bodies toward you with what might be recognition or might be indifference or might be something that human language does not have a word for because human language was not designed for this place.

---

**What lives in the Keelworks** is a system. It is not a collection of organisms that happen to share a space. It is a metabolism — a single, distributed, slow-burning chemical reaction that uses the heat of the earth and the mineral wealth of young rock and the strange, half-alive dust and the tireless industry of the keelbugs and the unknowable purposes of the runners and the patient growth on the north wall and the warm updrafts from the shaft below, all of it turning, all of it cycling, all of it converting raw geology into something that is not quite life and not quite industry and not quite art but is all three at once, expressed in structures of deposited mineral and patterns of skittering movement and the blue-green glow of a ten percent fraction that no instrument can name.

The humans are part of it now. They did not intend to be. They came to extract and study and catalog, and instead they have been absorbed — not consumed, not destroyed, but *incorporated*, the way a stone in a river is incorporated into the riverbed, the way a tree in a forest is incorporated into the canopy. They maintain the machinery that the system uses. They monitor the fissure that the system inhabits. They breathe the dust that the system produces. And in the quiet hours, when the machinery pauses between cycles and the humans sleep in their alcoves in the upper narrows and the keelbugs work in the dark and the runners descend into the shaft and the growth on the north wall lifts its leading edges toward the updraft and releases its cloud of warm pink dust into the rising air — in those quiet hours, the Keelworks is most fully itself: a living wound in the earth, still opening, still healing, still deciding what it wants to become, and full of things that have already decided to be here, in the crack, in the dark, in the hum, in the dust, in the slow and patient work of making something from nothing in a place where nothing was supposed to live.

---

They are still working. All of them. The machinery. The keelbugs. The growth. The runners, somewhere below. The dust, drifting. The rock, shifting. The humans, sleeping, preparing for the next shift, reaching for tools that have become familiar in their hands, reaching for the handrails that vibrate with the same frequency as their own pulse, descending to the platforms where the warm air rises and the monitoring instruments point downward and the structures of deposited mineral gleam with a geometry that makes the back of the brain itch in a way that is not quite recognition and not quite fear but is the oldest feeling there is: the feeling of standing at the edge of something deep, and knowing that it is deep, and knowing that it is alive, and knowing that you are inside it now, and that the way back up is long, and that the way down is longer, and that the dust is in your lungs and the hum is in your bones and the glow is in the corner of your eye and will be there when you close your eyes tonight, blue-green, patient, waiting for you to see it directly.

You will not see it directly. It does not work that way. It only works in the periphery. It only works when you are not looking.

Look away. Let the Keelworks do what it does. Let it live in the crack. Let the rock shift and the brackets slide and the keelbugs build their strange geometry and the runners carry their small treasures up from the dark and the growth release its dust into the updraft and the humans tend their machines and sleep their shallow sleep and dream of warm air and violet light and the sound of something vast and quiet working itself out in the stone beneath their feet.

The Keelworks is not a place. It is a process. And the process is ongoing. And whatever lives here — all of it, together, in the amber twilight, in the bone-dry air, in the fissure that is still opening, still forming, still deciding — whatever lives here will continue to live here long after the last human has climbed the four hundred meters to a surface they will not remember wanting to see.

The dust settles. The hum continues. The rock, very slowly, very patiently, tries to close the wound.

It has not succeeded yet.
