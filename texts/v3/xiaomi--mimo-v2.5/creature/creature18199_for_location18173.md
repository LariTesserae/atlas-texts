# What Lives in The Stills

The invertebrates were here first. Or rather — they were here before anyone noticed them, which is not the same thing. They predate the installation by a span of years no one has bothered to calculate, because no one has bothered to identify them. They exist in the deep mud beneath the mineral terraces, in the saturated layers where the water hasn't yet decided whether it's groundwater or process effluent or something older than both. They are small. The largest you've ever found was just under three centimeters, and it was mostly tube — a segmented, translucent casing of compacted silt and mineral that the creature inside had secreted and then, over time, become indistinguishable from. When the field team extracted it, it took them a moment to understand which part was animal and which part was geology. The creature itself was pale in the way that things are pale when the concept of color has never been relevant to their existence. It had no eyes. It had mouthparts designed for something — the lab notes say *sorption* — that suggests it feeds by pressing itself against surfaces and absorbing what those surfaces are made of. Mineral. Organic sediment. The faint electric trace of current passing through water. It fed on the place itself, and it was fed upon by no one, and it was not interesting enough to name properly. The field notes call them *Stills worms*, which is a label, not a name, and no one has objected.

There are hundreds of them. Thousands, possibly. You never see them. You see their burrows — delicate, branching tunnels in the mud, lined with the same precipitated mineral that coats everything else, so that the burrows themselves look like fossil casts of organisms that never existed. The tunnels connect to each other in networks that have no apparent center, no hierarchy, no queen or patriarch or nexus. They are simply *there*, threaded through the substrate like the root systems of trees that grew in a place where trees were never going to grow. Occasionally, when you step in the wrong place and the crust shatters and the mud surges up, you catch a glimpse of something pale and quick retracting into a tube that is, for a fraction of a second, unmistakably alive — the walls of it contracting, the creature inside pulling itself backward with a motion that is less like crawling and more like a muscle flexing inside a bone. Then the mud closes over it, and it's gone, and you're left with the impression of something that has no interest in being seen, that has built its entire existence around the principle of not being a surface-level phenomenon.

---

The rushes persist. This is perhaps more remarkable than the worms, because the rushes are *visible* — they stand above the mineral mat, they reach toward the white sky, they are in some sense performing the act of being alive in a way that the worms will never be asked to perform. And they are losing. You can see it in the way they bow. Not toward light — there is no directional light to bow toward — but under weight. Each blade carries a film of mineral deposit that thickens over the course of weeks until the blade is less a leaf and more a casting of a leaf, a mold of its former self. The rushes compensate by growing new blades from the base, pushing fresh green up through the crusted base of the old ones, and this works for a while, for a season, until the new blades are coated too, and the cycle begins again. They never get taller than about forty centimeters. They never flower. They produce no seeds that anyone has germinated. They are sustained by something — perhaps the faint chemical gradients in the shallow water, perhaps the electrolytic byproducts of the generator's slow crime — but they are not *thriving*. They are *persisting*, which is a different verb, a different posture, a different kind of life. They are the life you lead when the alternative is not death but *geology* — when the place you live is slowly, patiently converting you into a mineral specimen and your only recourse is to keep growing just fast enough to stay ahead of the conversion. The rushes do this. They have been doing it for as long as the berms have held water. They will probably keep doing it long after the generator finally dies, because the chemistry of The Stills is now self-sustaining in ways that the original engineers did not plan for and would not, if they understood, approve of.

A botanist who visited once — years ago, before the access road degraded to the point where only tracked vehicles could make it — described the rushes as *extremophile adjacent*. She meant this as a compliment. She meant that they were displaying a tenacity that bordered on the heroic, surviving in conditions that should have excluded vascular plant life entirely. She took samples. She grew them in the lab under controlled conditions, in clean water with proper nutrients and appropriate light, and within six weeks they were unremarkable — healthy, green, average-heighted rushes of a type you'd find in any temperate wetland. The place was the thing, not the rush. The rush was just what the place looked like when it expressed itself through the medium of chlorophyll.

---

The fly.

There is one fly. Or there is always one fly. No one has established whether it's the same fly or a succession of flies, because no one has been here often enough or carefully enough to tell, and the fly, for its part, does not cooperate with identification. It is small, dark, with wings that have a faint mineral sheen — not the iridescent shimmer of a healthy insect wing, but a dull, opaque glaze, as though the fly has been left in a solution of something and the solution has begun to set. It moves slowly. It lands on surfaces and stays there for long periods, legs motionless, wings folded, doing nothing that resembles the behavior of a fly in any field guide. You have the sense, watching it, that it is *listening* — not with ears, because flies don't have ears in any conventional sense, but with its whole body, with the sensitive hairs on its legs and thorax, reading the vibrations of the place through contact. It moves when the mud breathes. When a gas pocket shifts beneath the silt, sending a low tremor through the mineral crust, the fly lifts off, hovers for a moment in the heavy air, and resettles a few centimeters away, in the same relative position to the tremor's center, like a needle seeking a pole. It is mapping the place. Or it is reading the place. Or it is doing something for which there is no verb because no one has needed to describe this behavior before.

If it dies — and it must die eventually, or it must be replaced by others who die eventually — its body is incorporated. You have found, once, a fly-shaped inclusion in a mineral nodule, the delicate architecture of wings and legs and compound eyes rendered in crystallized calcium, perfect down to the bristles on its legs. It was beautiful in the way that death masks are beautiful — a precise replica of something that was once alive, made by the same process that is making everything else in The Stills into a version of itself. Whether the fly knows this — whether it experiences its mineralization as a slow encroachment or simply as the texture of being alive in this place — is a question you stop asking after the first visit, because the place does not answer it, and the fly does not care that you asked.

---

The caretaker comes every eleven days. You have never met them, but you have seen the evidence: a fresh diesel canister on the concrete pad, its cap unscrewed and then loosely replaced; a handprint in the silt near the generator, the mineral-dusted impression of a glove's palm and fingers, already filling with water but still legible when you arrived; a logbook inside a rust-eaten metal box bolted to the berm, its pages stiff with moisture, its entries written in a hand that has become less patient over time. The early entries are detailed — date, time, fuel level, observations about the mineral terraces, notes on equipment condition. *Generator running smooth. Tank 3 still overflowing. New crystalline growth on north pipe cluster. Will check pressure readings next visit.* The later entries are shorter. *Fuel added. No change.* And then, most recently, a single word in ink that has bled slightly into the paper: *Continuing.*

The caretaker does not maintain the installation in any engineering sense. They do not repair the pipes, or clear the sediment from the pump, or replace the corroded conduit. These tasks, if they were ever meaningful, stopped being meaningful sometime around year four, when the mineral deposits became structural — when the buildup of precipitated material inside the pipes began to perform the function that the pipes themselves had originally performed, channeling water through passages of crystallized calcium instead of corroded steel, and the system, impossibly, continued to work. The caretaker's role is simpler and more fundamental. The caretaker feeds the generator. The generator feeds the current. The current feeds the electrolysis. The electrolysis feeds the chemistry. The chemistry feeds The Stills.

There is a moment, in the logbook, around year seven, where the caretaker seems to have understood this. The entry is longer than the surrounding ones, written in smaller script as though the writer was running out of space or time or willingness to be legible:

*Thought about shutting it down today. Pulled the kill switch on the generator. Thirty seconds of silence — real silence, the kind where you can hear the mud. Then the west terrace started to change. Not growing. Collapsion. The ridge I've been watching for months just came apart, the crystals separating, falling into the pool, the pool turning opaque. Started the generator again. Took about ten minutes for the ridge to reform. Not the same ridge. A new one, same place, different shape. Different chemistry, I think. The water color changed from rust to something more like tea. I don't know what I'm keeping alive here. I don't know if keeping it alive is the right way to describe what I'm doing. But I am doing it.*

The entry after that one is dated eleven days later and reads: *Fuel added. No change.*

---

The invertebrates do not notice the caretaker. The rushes do not notice the caretaker. The fly notices the caretaker — it lifts from whatever surface it's resting on when the boundary is crossed and circles once, twice, three times, in the heavy air, then resettles on the generator housing, as close to the caretaker's hands as it can get without being touched. The caretaker does not swat it. The caretaker has never swatted it. There is a passage in the logbook, buried among entries about fuel levels and equipment condition, that reads: *Fly was on the intake valve again. Don't move it. Don't know why. It likes the warmth.*

The caretaker is the only person The Stills allows inside its boundary who stays for less than an hour. The field teams, when they still came, would stay for days, setting up equipment, taking measurements, arguing about whether the place constituted an environmental hazard or a geological curiosity or both. The caretaker comes, fuels the generator, checks the logbook, stands for a few minutes in the center of the place with mineral-stained water seeping through their boots, and leaves. They never take samples. They never bring equipment. They walk the same path each time — in from the southeast berm, where the crust is thinnest, straight to the generator, then a slow circuit of the perimeter, checking the pipes by touch, pressing a hand to each one and holding it there for a moment, the way you'd check a sleeping animal for fever. Then back to the generator. Then out the way they came.

What the caretaker feels when they stand in the center — whether it is duty, or fascination, or the particular gravity that processes exert on the people who tend them, the way a greenhouse exerts a pull on its keeper long after the keeper has stopped believing in the garden — is not recorded. The logbook does not contain feelings. It contains fuel levels and observations and, recently, a single word that is either a statement of fact or a confession or a form of prayer.

---

The process lives here. The process is what lives here. It has its own metabolism — the water in, the minerals out, the slow conversion of everything within the boundary into more of itself. It has its own rhythm — the mud breathing, the terraces growing, the precipitation blooms shuddering into existence and dissolving and reforming. It has its own immune system — the mineral crust that seals any break in its surface, the chemistry that incorporates foreign objects rather than rejecting them, that converts tools and documents and flies and, if you stood still long enough, probably you, into a version of the place itself. It has its own caretaker, who comes every eleven days and does not name what they are keeping alive because naming it would make it too much like a thing that could die, and the caretaker has, somewhere in the space between year seven and year eleven, stopped being willing to consider that possibility.

The Stills does not grow. That would imply a direction, a size it is moving toward. The Stills *deepens*. Each year the mineral terraces are fractionally thicker, the mud is fractionally more saturated, the chemistry is fractionally more complex. The invertebrates burrow deeper. The rushes, if they could be graphed, would show a very slight downward trend in height, as the mineral burden on each generation increases by an increment so small that only the cumulative effect — measured in decades, in centuries — reveals what is happening. The place is becoming more itself. It is converting the ordinary world around it — the rain that falls on it, the groundwater that feeds it, the diesel that the caretaker brings, the air that enters and is trapped and is processed and is exhaled as condensation on every surface — into the specific, dense, mineral, silent, breathing, growing, patient thing that it is.

Nothing in The Stills is in a hurry. The worms will outlast the generator. The rushes will outlast the worms. The mineral terraces will outlast the rushes, building themselves in the absence of any engine, powered by nothing more than chemistry and time and the patient dissolution of everything the installation was built from. The caretaker will outlast their own willingness to keep coming, and when they stop — or when the next caretaker begins, because there will be a next caretaker, there is always a next caretaker, because the place does not let people leave unchanged — the logbook will contain, on its last page, whatever word the writer chose to end with, and it will be the same word, because there is only one word for what The Stills is doing and what The Stills requires of those who tend it.
