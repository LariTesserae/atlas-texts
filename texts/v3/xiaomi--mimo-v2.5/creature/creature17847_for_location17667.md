To live in the Millwork Hollow is to be a guest in a body. One learns its rhythms the way one learns the pulse in one's own wrist, only slower, deeper, and not entirely one's own.

**The Townspeople** are more stewards than masters. Their labor is not tilling or building in the old sense, but *tending*—a constant, quiet negotiation. They are the gardeners of a city that has forgotten it is a garden. They speak of "the growth" and "the flow" with the casual certainty others reserve for weather. A woman with hands stained pale green from pruning cambium will tell you, "The eastern hall wants more light," not as a complaint, but as a diagnosis. She will then spend the afternoon carefully training a new canopy-branch, weaving it through the iron ribs of a decades-old skylight. They are craftspeople of a living medium. Their tools are sharp knives for guiding growth, tapping drills for accessing sap-lines, and a profound, inherited patience.

They dress in fabrics woven from stripped bark and vine-fiber, materials that breathe and shed moisture easily. Their movements are economical, sound-absorbing. A child learns to walk softly not from admonition, but from the simple fact that loud sounds feel alien here, a discordance against the pervasive hum and drip. They have little need for loud voices; the air itself seems to carry a whisper. Their homes are grown, not built. A family might occupy a set of chambers guided from a single, massive column-tree, each generation carefully pruning their "rooms" into the wood, adding a hollow here, smoothing a wall there. Inheritance is literal: a grandchild lives in a space that remembers the shape of their grandparent's hands.

They farm the root network, but it is less agriculture and more symbiosis. They harvest the sweet, clear sap not just for sustenance, but to feed it back into the system where it's needed, directing flow with channels of hollowed cane. They know which fungal threads connect the canopy to the deep roots and will carefully place compost or mineral supplements directly onto those networks. They are the town's lymphatic system, circulating what the great green body needs.

**The Machines** are their saints and relics. The great, slow-turning generator beneath the central hall is spoken of with a kind of reverence. They oil its bearings, clear the root-tendrils from its intake grates, and listen to its heartbeat. When it hums, the water pressure in the upper cisterns rises. When it sleeps, they conserve. Other, silent machines—the rusted frames of conveyor systems, the skeletal remains of boilers—are simply part of the landscape. Children play in their hollowed-out husks. They are the bones in the earth, and the living wood weaves through them as if in reassurance.

**The Water** is their shared clock and covenant. The early-morning condensation is a blessing, a daily offering. They have shallow, leaf-shaped vessels placed at drip-points throughout the town, and the morning ritual is to collect this water, tasting the purity of it, noting its faint sweetness. A dry spell is a time of quiet anxiety, a reminder of the town's dependence. The return of the mist-rain is a communal event of soft joy, a softening of faces as the whole settlement begins its audible drinking.

**The Fauna** is minimal, but the townspeople know what few creatures there are intimately. They know the pale, blind vole by sound—the secret life teeming beneath their feet. They do not trap it. They consider it a sign of health. They might leave a few choice root-cuttings near a colony entrance. The moths that navigate the canopy corridors are studied, their flight patterns seen as another reading of the air's health.

**What Lives There, Truly?**

It is the **System** that is the primary inhabitant. The people, the machines, the water, the vole, the engineered species—all are organs within it. The System's purpose has transcended its original industrial function. It does not produce goods for the outside world anymore. It produces *itself*: a self-regulating, self-building, conscious environment. Its slow, spiraling deviation from the original town plan is its own thought, expressed in wood and leaf. The townspeople are aware of it, this silent, vegetative intention. They feel it in the way a corridor subtly broadens to better channel a breeze, or how a new column-tree, unguided, grows to perfectly fill a gap in the canopy. They do not discuss it because it is as fundamental as the air. To speak of it would be to separate themselves from it, and they are not separate. They are its memory, its hands, its quiet, observant voice. They live in a place that is, in the most profound sense, *becoming*. Their role is to witness, to maintain, and to, in their own small way, become part of the pattern. They are both the gardeners and the soil, and the strange, unfolding flower of the Millwork Hollow grows through them, and beyond them, and with them. It is not a town. It is a consciousness made of wood and water and a few quiet lives, thinking its slow, green thoughts.
