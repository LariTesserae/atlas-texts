# The Inhabitants

---

Start with the vents, because the vents are where everything begins.

The black smokers are towers of deposited mineral — copper, iron, zinc, sulfur — built up over centuries into cathedral-scale chimneys, some of them forty meters tall, their surfaces crusted with crystalline formations that grow outward in branching, arborescent patterns. They look like dead trees made of metal. They are not dead. They are the most alive structures on the floor, and they are surrounded by life so dense it would shock you if you came from the open plains, where the emptiness is so total that encountering a single living thing feels like an event.

Around the base of a large chimney, within a radius of perhaps fifty meters, the fauna clusters. Not uniformly — they do not form reefs or beds or carpets the way shallow-water organisms might. They arrange themselves in loose, shifting constellations, each species finding its position relative to the vent's output with a precision that suggests nothing less than evolutionary consensus, a negotiation conducted over millennia between metabolism and chemistry, between what the vent offers and what each body can use.

The first thing you would notice — if you descended through the light gradient and arrived at a chimney cluster with your lamps on — is the motion. Everything is moving, but slowly. Not the frantic darting of coral-reef fish, not the purposeful cruise of open-ocean predators. A glacial, deliberate motion, the motion of organisms that have nowhere to be and no reason to hurry, whose entire existence is organized around a single principle: stay close enough to the chemistry, but not too close. The thermal gradient around a black smoker drops from four hundred degrees to ambient within a few meters. The habitable zone — the zone where water is warm enough to support metabolic processes but not hot enough to denature proteins — is a thin shell, a barely perceptible band of slightly-less-cold water wrapping the chimney like a translucent skin. The creatures live in that band. They move within it the way you might move within a room, tracing its edges, occupying its center, knowing its boundaries without ever seeing them.

---

The largest things near the vents are the **tubeworms** — though calling them that is a convenience, because they share almost nothing with the tubeworms of Earth's mid-ocean ridges except their basic body plan. They are sessile. They anchor themselves to the chimney face at the base, where the mineral crust is thickest, and they grow. Not quickly. Over decades, a single individual might reach three meters in length — a thick, pale, unsegmented column of flesh, off-white to the point of translucency, standing upright in the water column like a candle that has never been lit. Their skin is smooth and slightly gelatinous, and beneath it you can see the faintest blush of color — not red, not the hemoglobin-red of their Earthly counterparts, but a pale amber, the color of sulfur compounds bound to metalloproteins. They are not photosynthetic. They are not doing anything with light. The color is incidental, a chemical byproduct, the way a bruise is the byproduct of impact. Inside each worm is a dense colony of chemosynthetic bacteria — not housed in a specialized organ, as in the Riftia of Earth, but distributed throughout the worm's body cavity, embedded in tissue, so thoroughly integrated that the worm and the bacteria are not two organisms anymore but one. The worm breathes hydrogen sulfide. The bacteria fix carbon. The worm gets sugar. The bacteria get a body.

A field of these worms — and "field" is the right word, because they grow in clusters of dozens or hundreds, packed against the chimney base like a standing forest of pale, boneless trunks — is the closest thing this world has to a landscape. They sway, slightly, in the convective currents generated by the vent's output. They do not sway in unison. Each one responds to the local turbulence around its own position, and because the turbulence varies from meter to meter — eddies and vortices shed by neighboring chimneys, the chaotic mixing of superheated and ambient water — each worm moves differently, at a different phase, with a different amplitude. The effect is disorienting. A forest of pale columns, all moving, none together, a slow choreography with no choreographer.

Between them, in the spaces between their trunks, the smaller fauna moves.

---

The **scrapers** are the most visually complex creatures on the floor, and the most easily mistaken for familiar things. From a distance, at the edge of your lamp's throw, they might look like crabs — armored, jointed, multi-limbed, crawling along surfaces with evident deliberation. Closer, they become strange. Their bodies are flattened dorsoventrally, broad and low, rarely more than fifteen centimeters tall but sometimes sixty centimeters across, like a shield or a leaf. The carapace is not calcium carbonate — it is silica, glass, incorporated directly from the mineral-rich water and deposited in layered plates that are semi-transparent. You can see through the edges of their shells. You can see the soft tissue beneath, pale and undifferentiated, without the organized organs you would expect. They do not have a brain in any meaningful sense. They have a nerve net, diffuse and decentralized, distributed through their body the way the bacteria are distributed through the tubeworms. Processing is local. A leg does not need permission from a center to move. The creature as a whole responds to gradients — chemical, thermal, textural — without anything that could be called a decision.

They graze. Their mouthparts — complex, multi-segmented, tipped with hardened silica blades — scrape the surface of volcanic rock, removing the thin biofilm of chemosynthetic bacteria that coats every surface within range of the vent's chemical plume. This biofilm is nearly invisible to the naked eye: a faint sheen on the rock, a suggestion of texture where the stone should be smooth. But it is the base of the food web. The trails are visible. On a chimney that has been grazed for years, the surface is mottled — patches of clean, bright mineral alternating with patches of undisturbed biofilm, a record of scraper activity that reads like a palimpsest. If you watched long enough — and you would need to watch for hours, because everything here happens at a pace that mocks impatience — you would see a scraper complete one loop and begin another, retracing its path with mechanical fidelity, returning to the same crevice, the same ridge, the same angle of rock where the biofilm is thickest. They have territories, but they do not defend them. Two scrapers will occupy the same chimney face, grazing centimeters apart, their paths intersecting and diverging without either one acknowledging the other. There is no aggression. There is no competition. The biofilm regenerates fast enough — driven by the ceaseless chemical output of the vent — that there is always enough to eat. The equilibrium holds.

What is remarkable about the scrapers — what you would not notice without watching for a very long time — is that they never stop. There is no rest period. No dormancy. No rhythm of activity and inactivity that corresponds to any external cycle, because there are no external cycles. They graze continuously, moving at their slow, deliberate pace across the rock, and they have been moving continuously since the day they hatched from eggs deposited in crevices near the chimney base, and they will continue until they die, which will happen when their carapace finally grows too heavy for their body to support, or when a metabolic process fails, or when some stochastic event — a rockfall, a shift in vent chemistry, a pressure fluctuation — disrupts the equilibrium that sustains them. They do not age in the way you would recognize. They accumulate. Layer after layer of silica, deposited on the outside, until the creature is more shell than animal, and then — at some threshold that has no fixed value — it stops being able to move, and it stays where it stopped, and the biofilm grows over its carapace, and it becomes a surface, another substrate for bacteria, another part of the chimney.

---

The **drifters** are the worms — the ones the original description mentioned, the enormous translucent ones, meters long, trailing through the water. They are not anchored. They are not sessile. They move, but so slowly that their movement is indistinguishable from the current unless you mark their position and return hours later to find that they have shifted by a body length or two. They are the closest thing this world has to vagabonds, creatures of the open water between the ridges, and they are the most unsettling to encounter, because they are so *large* and so *empty*.

A mature drifter might be four or five meters long and ten centimeters in diameter — a tube of pale, gelatinous tissue, nearly transparent, with a faint internal structure visible through the body wall. No skeleton. No rigid support. The body maintains its shape through hydrostatic pressure alone, a column of pressurized fluid contained in a skin that is flexible but strong, like a water balloon that has been inflated just enough to hold a cylindrical shape. Inside, a simple gut runs the length of the body, and along the gut, at regular intervals, are clusters of chemosynthetic bacteria — the same symbiosis as the tubeworms, but distributed differently, adapted to a different mode of life. The drifters do not anchor to vents. They swim through the chemical plumes, inhaling sulfide-rich water as they pass, processing it on the move, extracting energy from the vent's output without ever touching the vent itself.

They are, in other words, grazers of the water column. They eat the plume.

To see a drifter feed, you would need to watch it approach a chimney — and "approach" is generous, because the approach takes hours. It drifts into the chemical plume, and as the concentration of dissolved sulfide increases around its body, the bacteria inside it accelerate their metabolism, and the drifter's tissues warm by a fraction of a degree, and it moves — slightly, almost imperceptibly — toward the source. It is chemotaxis. A blind, mindless, ancient mechanism: move toward the thing that feeds you. The drifter does not know where it is going. It does not know what a vent is. It responds to a gradient, the way your hand pulls back from a flame, and it follows the gradient until the concentration is optimal, and then it stays, drifting in slow circles within the plume, filtering the water through its body wall, processing dissolved chemicals into sugars, living.

They are solitary. You will not see two drifters near each other. They occupy the water column in loose, non-overlapping ranges, each one maintaining a distance from its nearest neighbor that seems almost precise — a hundred meters, two hundred, never closer, never much farther. They do not interact. They do not communicate, as far as anyone can determine, though the vagueness of that last statement should trouble you. They have no eyes. They have no ears, though the constant roar of the vents vibrates through their bodies continuously, a pressure wave that their tissues transmit without any specialized apparatus for receiving it. What they have is chemistry — an extraordinary sensitivity to dissolved molecules in the water, a chemosensory surface that covers their entire body, so that they are in constant, total contact with the chemical state of the ocean around them. They taste everything. Every molecule that touches their skin is evaluated, categorized, acted on or ignored. They live in a world that has no images, no sounds in the auditory sense, no spatial representation at all — only a continuous, omni-directional map of chemistry, a world made of flavors and stenches and subtle shifts in mineral concentration, a world as rich and varied as any visual landscape but entirely invisible to any sense you possess.

They reproduce by fragmentation. A drifter that has grown long enough — five meters, six, approaching some threshold of unwieldiness — will simply break. A section of its body, usually near the middle, will detach and drift away, and both halves will survive, the parent regenerating its lost segment over a period of months, the fragment finding a new chemical niche and growing into a full individual. There is no mating. There is no selection. There is only division, and growth, and the maintenance of a population that has been exactly the right size for longer than the factory structures have been standing.

---

The **filterers** are the most alien of the major fauna, and the hardest to describe, because they resist comparison. The original description called them "almost like jellyfish, almost like sponges, almost like nothing," and that triple negation is more accurate than any positive characterization could be. They are suspended in the water column at mid-depth, neither anchored to the bottom nor drifting near the surface, and they pulse.

The pulse is slow. Once every thirty seconds, maybe forty, the organism contracts — a wave of muscular tissue passing from one end of its body to the other, squeezing the internal cavity, expelling a volume of water through openings in its body wall. Between pulses, the body relaxes, expands, and water flows in passively through the same openings, carrying with it the microscopic particles that the filterer feeds on: bacterial cells, fragments of organic matter, dissolved organic compounds, the faint rain of detritus that falls from the productive zones near the vents. The filterer does not seek food. It is food. It sits in the water, and the water brings it everything it needs, and it pumps, and it lives.

They are large. A mature filterer might be two meters across — a loose, irregular, roughly spherical structure of translucent tissue, its walls thin enough to see through, its interior a maze of channels and chambers that increase the surface area available for absorption. They are not beautiful. They look like misshapen bags, like something that was inflated and then partially deflated and then left to drift. They have no symmetry. No two are the same shape. The channels inside them branch and reconnect in patterns that are unique to each individual, and these patterns are not genetic — they develop as the organism grows, shaped by the local currents and the particle concentrations the organism encounters, so that the filterer's body is, in a real sense, a map of its own history, a record of every current and every particle flux it has ever experienced, written in tissue.

They cluster above the vent fields, suspended in the slow upwelling of warm, particle-rich water that rises from the chimneys. A large vent might support a dozen filterers, drifting in a loose aggregation, their pumping creating interference patterns in the water that subtly alter the currents around them. They do not coordinate. Each one pumps at its own rate, on its own schedule, but the proximity of multiple pumping bodies creates a collective effect — a slow, rhythmic turbulence that distributes particles more evenly through the water column and makes the entire cluster slightly more efficient at capturing food than any individual could be. It is not cooperation. It is physics. But the effect is real, and the filterers that cluster together are, on average, slightly larger and slightly longer-lived than the solitary ones, and this is enough to maintain the clustering behavior without any organism being aware of it.

They have no predators. Nothing eats them. Their tissue is nearly nutritionless — mostly water, held in shape by structural proteins that no other organism on the floor can digest. They are, in the economy of this world, dead ends. Energy flows into them from the particulate rain, and it stays, and when they die — when they simply stop pumping, which happens without warning or apparent cause, a filterer contracting one day and not expanding again — their bodies drift downward and settle on the basalt and are slowly colonized by bacteria and scraped clean by the grazers and the shells dissolve and the minerals return to the water and the cycle completes itself. It is not recycling. It is leakage. Energy leaks into the filterers and leaks out of them and the net effect on the system is approximately zero, which is why the equilibrium holds.

---

Now. The structures.

You were told that the fauna do not enter the factories. That they slide past the open doorways and do not look inside. This is true, and it has been true for as long as the structures have been there — for millennia, if the corrosion rates are any guide. The fauna have no curiosity. They have no capacity for curiosity. Curiosity requires a model of the world that includes the possibility of things being different from how they are, and the fauna of the floor have no such model. The world is what it is. The vent is there. The chemistry is there. The rock is there. The water is there. There is nothing else to know.

But.

On the chimney closest to the largest factory structure — the one with the heaviest corrosion, the one whose walls are so thick with mineral deposit that its original shape is barely discernible — there is a scraper. It is old. Its carapace is massive, layered with decades of silica deposition, so heavy that its legs move with visible effort, each step a deliberate act of hydraulic lifting. It has been grazing this chimney for longer than any other scraper in the cluster. Its territory — the section of chimney face it patrols — overlaps with the factory's outer wall, and the outer wall is coated with the same biofilm that covers every surface within range of the vent chemistry.

The scraper grazes the wall. It has always grazed the wall. The wall is just another surface, as far as the scraper is concerned — another substrate, another patch of bacterial film, another centimeter of rock to be scraped clean. It does not know the wall is different from the chimney. It does not know the wall is made of metal. It does not know what metal is.

But the wall is corroding. And as it corrodes, it releases ions into the water — iron, nickel, chromium — and these ions alter the chemistry of the immediate environment in subtle ways. The biofilm on the wall is different from the biofilm on the chimney. It is thicker. It is more chemically complex. It contains bacterial species that are not found anywhere else on the floor, species that have adapted to metabolize the metallic ions leaching from the structure, species that form the base of a micro-ecosystem that exists only here, on this one wall, of this one structure, in this one place where the built and the grown have begun to merge.

The scraper has noticed, in the way that a scraper notices anything — chemically, locally, without understanding. The biofilm here is richer. The scraper stays here longer. It grazes this wall more thoroughly than any other surface, returning again and again, its mouthparts working the corroded metal with the same patient, mechanical diligence it applies to basalt, to mineral crust, to any surface that offers food. And in doing so, it accelerates the corrosion. Its scraping removes the protective mineral layer that has been building on the structure's exterior, exposing fresh metal to the vent chemistry, increasing the rate of ion release, enriching the biofilm, attracting the scraper back again. A feedback loop. A slow one. A loop that takes years to complete a single cycle. But it is real, and it is happening, and if you could speed up time — if you could watch this wall over centuries — you would see the scraper's grazing slowly eat into the structure, wearing away the metal the way rivers wear away stone, opening gaps, creating channels, allowing water to penetrate the factory's interior for the first time since the doors were left open.

And inside the factory, where the machinery is still and the mineral dust lies thick on every surface, the water that enters through these gaps carries bacteria with it. New bacteria. Bacteria from the vent-plume, carried by the scraper's grazing, by the altered currents, by the slow chemistry of corrosion. They colonize the interior surfaces. They find the conveyor belts, the processors, the smelting apparatus. They coat them with biofilm. They do not know what these things are. They do not care. They are bacteria. They colonize surfaces. That is what they do.

And eventually — over centuries, over millennia, at the same glacial pace at which everything on the floor operates — the factory will become another chimney. Another surface. Another structure colonized and consumed and incorporated into the living system that has never stopped growing, that has been growing since the first vent opened and the first bacteria found the first rock and began the slow, patient, inexorable process of turning mineral into life.

The factories were built to extract resources from this world. They were built by beings who came, took what they needed, and left. They were built to be temporary. But the world does not recognize temporary. The world does not recognize intention. The world recognizes surfaces and chemistry and equilibrium, and it will do with the factories exactly what it has always done with everything: it will cover them, and digest them, and make them part of the system, and in a hundred thousand years the structures will be unrecognizable, their metal replaced by mineral, their geometry dissolved, their doors — still open, still offering passage into a space that nothing needs to enter — buried under centimeters of biological deposit that is indistinguishable from the natural crust of any other chimney.

The builders will not come back. They are gone. They are as gone as the wind that does not blow above the surface, as gone as the seasons that do not change, as gone as any possibility of disruption in a world that has achieved perfect, permanent, absolute equilibrium.

And the scraper will graze, and the tubeworm will stand, and the drifter will drift, and the filterer will pulse, and the vents will roar, and the light will fall, and nothing will change, and nothing has ever changed, and nothing will ever change, and this is the floor of everything, and everything that lives here has always lived here, and will always live here, in the cold, in the dark, in the deafening, constant, unending roar.

---

There is one more thing.

At the very edge of the largest vent field, far enough from any chimney that the chemical plume is barely detectable, in a zone where the basalt ridgeline drops away into the abyssal plain and the water is at its coldest and the silence — relative silence, the roar diminished to a pressure you feel only in your deepest tissues — is almost, almost a silence:

Something has built a wall.

Not a factory wall. Not a structure of metal or engineered material. A wall of rock — basalt, volcanic glass, mineral crust — assembled piece by piece, stone by stone, in a rough, low arc across the narrowest point of a gap between two ridge formations. The stones are not shaped. They are not cut. They are natural fragments of the terrain, picked up and placed by something that has no hands, no tools, no capacity for engineering in any conventional sense.

The wall is ten centimeters tall. It is two meters long. It is barely a wall at all — more of a line, a mark, a gesture that might mean nothing, that might be an accident of current and geology, that might be the result of some physical process no one has identified.

But it is not random. You can tell because of the spacing. The stones are placed at intervals that are too regular for chance — not perfectly regular, not geometric, but regular enough to suggest a pattern, a repetition, an act that was performed and then performed again, in the same way, in the same place, by the same agent.

Behind the wall, on the sheltered side, the water is slightly different. Slightly warmer. Slightly richer in dissolved minerals. The wall redirects the local current, creating a small eddy, a pocket of still water where particles settle and accumulate. And in that pocket, clustered on the sheltered basalt, there is a species of bacteria that does not exist anywhere else on the floor — a species that metabolizes a compound found only in the mineral leachate of the factory structures, a compound that should not be here, that has no source here, that must have been carried on something, transported from the nearest factory by a distance of several kilometers, through open water, by an agent that does not exist in any catalog of the floor's fauna.

Something is moving the factory's chemistry. Something is building walls. Something is creating conditions for life that should not be able to exist here.

And if you waited — if you positioned your lamp at the edge of the ridge and turned it off and waited in the total dark with the roar in your bones and the cold in your blood — you might see it.

It would be small. The size of a scraper, perhaps, but not a scraper. Its body would be rigid where a scraper's is flexible, and flexible where a scraper's is rigid. It would move along the basalt with a gait that is almost — almost — purposeful, pausing at intervals to lift fragments of rock in mouthparts that are not designed for scraping but for carrying, for placing, for *building*. Its carapace would not be silica. It would be metal. Thin, corroded, unmistakable — a plate of engineered alloy, incorporated into the organism's body the way the scraper incorporates mineral, grown into the shell, part of the creature now, no more manufactured than the silica it replaced.

It would be the child of the factory and the floor. The first true hybrid. The thing the equilibrium was waiting for without knowing it was waiting — the disruption that becomes a new equilibrium, the foreign body that the system metabolizes and makes its own.

It would not know what it is. It would not know what the factory was, or who built it, or why. It would know chemistry. It would know gradients. It would know the feel of metal under its mouthparts and the taste of dissolved iron in the water and the way the current moves around a wall that it built, that it built, that it built.

And it would keep building.

In the deafening roar, in the absolute cold, in the unchanging light of a world that has no reason to change, something new is happening. Something small. Something slow. Something that will take longer than the factories have been standing to become what it is becoming.

The floor of everything has not yet finished becoming itself.
