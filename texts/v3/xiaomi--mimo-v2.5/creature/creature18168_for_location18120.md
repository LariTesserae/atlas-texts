There is a thing that moves through the deep columns, in the center of the basin, where the water is opaque and the stone remnants are most entangled with the growth. It has never been seen from head to tail in a single glance. Those who have entered the Clatterfield deeply — not many, and fewer who returned with useful accounts — describe only pieces of it: a pale flank surfacing briefly between columns, the diameter of a large barrel, slick with mineral precipitate. A section of something arched above the waterline, smooth, grey-white, holding motionless for long enough that one observer mistook it for a column that had grown horizontal, before it slid under the surface without a sound that could be distinguished from the dripping.

It does not seem to have a fixed shape. Or rather, it seems to have a shape that is never fully revealed — always curling away, folding back on itself, occupying the spaces between columns with a patience that makes the word *patient* feel inadequate. The trails it leaves in the sediment are wide and featureless, a smooth furrow that fills with dark water almost immediately, so that within minutes you cannot be certain a trail was there at all. But if you follow one — and it is not clear why anyone would — it loops. It returns to the same clusters of columns, the same built remnants, in patterns that repeat on timescales no one has stayed long enough to map. It is not territorial. It does not defend. It simply *occupies*, the way the water occupies, the way the mineral occupies: completely, slowly, without discussion.

The limbless ones — the translucent grazers — seem indifferent to it. They continue their peristaltic scraping at the base of columns even as the larger body passes within centimeters of them. This has been observed more than once and has given rise to the only consensus among the few who study this place: that whatever the large thing is, it does not feed on them. It does not feed at all, in any way that anyone has recognized. It moves through the Clatterfield as the water moves through it — present, circulating, part of the system's own metabolism, whatever that metabolism is for.

---

There are the builders, or what the builders left behind. This is worth saying again, differently, because the remnants are not merely scattered objects. They are *arranged*, and the arrangement persists despite the mineral growth that has been slowly dismantling it for decades.

The low enclosures — the knee-height walls of shaped clay or stone — do not face random directions. Those that have been measured face toward the basin's center, every one of them, though their walls are now buckled and cracked and filled to the brim with grey sediment. They are not shelters. They are too small and too open for that. They are not graves — nothing has been found inside them except mineral deposit and the occasional limbless grazer curled in a corner, resting or digesting or doing whatever it is that they do. They are markers. They face the center because something at the center was being marked.

And there is one structure that the descriptions return to, though no two descriptions agree on its dimensions, because the mineral growth has changed it, continues to change it, and the act of measuring it disturbs the crusts that make measurement meaningful. It is not an enclosure. It is a *depression* — a shallow bowl-shaped hollow in the basin floor, perhaps three meters across, its rim defined by a ring of shaped stones that have been pushed apart and tilted by the growth of columns through their joints. The depression is slightly deeper than the surrounding floor. The water collects in it more thickly. The dripping from the columns that have grown above and around it falls into it in a dense, focused rain, and the sound it makes is different from the sound everywhere else — lower, more resonant, as though the hollow is a throat.

At the exact center of the depression, there is a column. It is the oldest one in the basin. It is also the thickest — not by a large margin, but enough. Its base is nearly half a meter across, and its faceting is more complex than any other column's, with secondary and tertiary faces nested within the primary angles, so that its surface catches the dim light in a way that makes it seem to shift as you move around it. It has not blunted. It has not weathered. Its tip is as sharp as the youngest columns at the basin's margin, which should not be possible if it is the oldest. It weeps more than the others. The water runs down its faces in continuous sheets rather than individual droplets, and where its base meets the saturated sediment, there is a ring of mineral growth so intricate and so dense that it looks like a skirt of pale coral, branching and rebranching in fractal patterns that go down into the mud and are not visible but are felt — a faint warmth through the soles of your boots, a vibration that is more intimate than the basin's hum, as though the column is a conduit for something that comes from very far below.

The builders faced this column. The incised marks on the stone slab, when cleaned and studied by the few who have bothered, are not writing. They are not symbols. They are *angles* — precise angular measurements, expressed as intersecting lines, and the angles they describe are the angles of this column's faceting. The builders measured the column and carved the measurements into stone, and then they built the enclosures to face it, and then they left.

Why they left is not known. The mineral growth provides a possible answer: the basin was already becoming uninhabitable by the time the structures were built. The water was rising. The columns were multiplying. The dripping was becoming what it is now — a relentless, basin-filling roar that would make sustained habitation a matter of constant acoustic endurance. But the structures suggest no panic. They suggest ritual, or record, or a long-term project that was abandoned not in haste but in the ordinary way that all projects are abandoned — a generation that did not share the previous generation's fixation, a slow redirection of attention toward something elsewhere.

Or perhaps they simply became unnecessary. Perhaps whoever built the structures understood that the Clatterfield was already occupied — not by the limbless grazers, not by the large unseen thing, not by anything with intention — but by a process that would continue with or without witnesses, and that the appropriate response was not to stay and watch but to *record and face*, to place their mark at the right angle and then step away and let the columns grow.

---

There is a species of silence that exists in the Clatterfield, though you would not call it silence. It exists in the moments when you stop moving, stop breathing deliberately, stop brushing against the columns with your body. It is not the absence of sound — the sound never stops — but a *depth* in the sound, a place where the dripping and the hum and the resonance of faceted surfaces converge into something that stops being noise and starts being *signal*. You do not hear words. You do not hear instructions. You hear — if you stay long enough, if the cold and the wet and the roaring white-noise have not driven you out — a kind of *rhythm* beneath the chaos, a pattern in the dripping that is not random, that repeats on a cycle just long enough that you almost miss it, that rises and falls and rises again in a cadence that your body recognizes before your mind does.

Your heart syncs to it. This is the thing no one writes down because it is the thing that makes writing difficult. The basin's rhythm gets into your pulse, and for a while — a minute, an hour, you cannot tell — you are not observing the Clatterfield. You are *part of its process*, another wet thing in a wet place, standing among pale mineral presences that do not know you are here and would not care if they did, your breath visible in the dim grey light, your feet sinking slowly into the saturated floor, your ears full of a sound that is not a sound but a *condition*, the acoustic equivalent of the water, of the dripping, of the slow crystalline growth that is happening right now, that has never stopped, that will be the last thing this place ever does.

Then you move. The rhythm breaks. The dripping is just dripping again. The columns are just columns. You pick your way back through the dense mineral forest, brushing against their sharp edges, hearing the sound shift and reshape itself around you, and you leave, and behind you the Clatterfield continues, and the basin breathes its slow breath, and the large thing moves through the deep columns in its looping circuit, and the limbless grazers scrape and contract and leave their trails that fill with water, and the oldest column weeps its continuous sheets of mineral-rich water into the depression that the builders faced toward it, and the columns grow. They grow. Every droplet that falls leaves behind a molecule of calcium, a molecule of silica, a film so thin it is nothing, but it is not nothing, because there are so many droplets, and so many columns, and so much time, and the basin is patient, and the process is patient, and the Clatterfield does not need anyone to understand it.

It just needs the water to keep falling.
