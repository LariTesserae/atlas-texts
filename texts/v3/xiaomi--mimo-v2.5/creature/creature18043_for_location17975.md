# What Lives in the Breathing Basin

---

## The Floor

Begin where the ground is not ground.

The root-mat is alive at every scale simultaneously. At the level you can see, it is a continuous, spongy expanse — grey-green, textured like the skin of something that has never been wounded because it has never been challenged. Press your hand into it and you feel warmth: not the warmth of sun-heated stone, but a biological warmth, the exothermic byproduct of constant cellular respiration. The mat is roughly forty centimeters thick at the channels and nearly a meter thick beneath the great buttress-ridges, and every millimeter of it is metabolizing. It is never dormant. It has not been dormant for longer than the concept of dormancy has existed, which may be a very long time, because the concept may have been born here, may have been the first idea this place ever produced, and then promptly discarded as irrelevant.

Beneath the visible surface, in the tangled interior of the mat, lives a universe.

**Nematodes** by the billions — microscopic, transparent, writhing through the waterlogged interstices of root tissue. They are grazers. They feed on bacteria and fungal hyphae, and in feeding, they regulate the microbial community that would otherwise consume the mat from within. They are, in a sense, the immune system of the basin made mobile. They do not think. They do not need to. Theirs is a metabolic intelligence, a body-temperature calculus of chemical gradients that keeps the living floor from becoming a rotting floor. They have been doing this for epochs. There is no variation in their behavior because there is no variation in the conditions they respond to. They are, each one, a perfect answer to a question that never changes.

**Collembola** — springtails — live in the upper centimeters of the mat, in the zone where decay and growth are most active. They are tiny, most no larger than a grain of salt, and they are among the most numerous animals in the basin. Their bodies are pale, almost translucent, and under magnification you can see that they are beautifully adapted to this world of permanent moisture: their cuticles are hydrophobic, allowing them to move through the water films without drowning, to walk on the undersides of water droplets suspended from root fibers. They feed on fungal spores. The fungi they eat are themselves decomposing dead root tissue at exactly the rate new root tissue replaces it. The springtails eat the decomposers. The decomposers break down the dead. The dead are replaced by the living. The living produce the dead. And the springtails, in the center of this circle, keep it from spinning too fast. Remove the springtails and the fungi would overgrow and the mat would choke. Remove the fungi and the dead tissue would accumulate and the mat would suffocate. The springtails do not know they are important. They are simply hungry, and there is always food.

**Mites** — oribatids, mostly, the armored, slow-moving kinds that look like tiny dark seeds — patrol the deeper zones of the mat where oxygen is scarce. They have been here so long that they have lost the capacity for most senses that would be useful elsewhere. They have no need of vision. The mat is uniformly dark below the surface. They navigate by chemoreception alone, reading the molecular signature of healthy tissue versus diseased tissue, and they consume the latter. They are janitors. They are meticulous. Their mouthparts are designed for scraping fungal hyphae from the surfaces of root cells without damaging the cells themselves. This is precision that evolved over millions of years in a world where the only variable is the slow, slow, slow degradation of the oldest cells and the need to remove them before they invite the wrong kind of rot. There are thousands of species of oribatid mites in the mat, and the differences between them are so subtle that they would be invisible to anything but a microscope designed specifically to look for them, and no such microscope has ever been brought here.

And then, threading through all of this — through the nematodes and the springtails and the mites and the bacteria and the fungi and the root cells themselves — there are the **mycorrhizal networks**, the fungal connections between the roots of the great stems and the mat itself. These are not individuals in any way that the word is usually meant. They are the basin's circulatory system made visible if you could see at the right scale: vast webs of hyphae, thinner than a single human hair, linking every plant to every other plant through the medium of the mat. They transfer nutrients. They transfer water. They transfer chemical signals that no one has decoded because no one has been here to try. When a stem on one side of the basin needs more water than its own roots can provide, the network delivers it from the wetter side. When the canopy above one stem is slightly denser and produces slightly more photosynthate than the stem can use, the excess flows down through the roots, through the network, and into the mat, feeding the organisms below. The network does not think. It does not decide. It simply conducts, like a wire conducts electricity, following the path of least resistance from abundance to scarcity. It has been doing this for so long that it has become indistinguishable from the root-mat itself. It is the mat's nervous system, if the nervous system had no brain — if it were pure signal, pure conductivity, pure responsiveness, distributed across an area so old that the hyphae in one part of the basin may be genetically continuous with hyphae in another part, the entire mat potentially a single fungal organism of staggering age and size, the largest living thing in the basin and the most invisible.

## The Invertebrates of the Air and Stem

Above the mat, in the world of stems and canopy, the invertebrate fauna is richer than it first appears, though it is never abundant in the way that invertebrate faunas are abundant in places where things change.

The **beetles** are the most diverse group. There are perhaps sixty or seventy species in the basin, and their specialization is profound. The bark-beetles — though they would not be recognized as such by an entomologist from outside, because they belong to families that diverged from outside lineages before the basin sealed itself — live in the grooves of the great stems. They feed on the mosses, lichens, and fungal mats that grow in the bark. They do not bore into the living wood. The living wood is not their business. The living wood is the business of the basin, and the basin does not permit interference. These bark-beetles have flattened bodies, perfectly shaped to slide into the geometric cracks of the bark. They are the color of the bark — grey-white with darker mottling — and they are so motionless during the long hours between feeding periods that they are functionally invisible. Their antennae are reduced. They communicate, to the extent they communicate at all, through pheromones that are released in concentrations so precise and so consistent that each species maintains a chemical signature as fixed and unchanging as the basin itself.

The **fungus-beetles** are darker, rounder, and live in the damp channels of the floor. They are larger than the bark-beetles — some reaching two centimeters in length — and they are among the few basin organisms that show any visible movement. You can see them walking, if you have the patience to watch for long enough, crossing the grey-green expanse of the mat in slow, deliberate lines, following the seams where fungal fruiting bodies emerge from the root tissue. They eat the fungi. They mate on the fungi. They lay their eggs on the fungi. When they die — and they die at a rate exactly equal to the rate at which new adults emerge from pupation — their bodies are consumed by the same fungi they fed on. There is no waste in this system. Waste implies an output that the system cannot use. The basin has no outputs. It is closed.

The **moths** are the most numerous flying insects, and they are the most startlingly adapted to the basin's unchanging nature. Most lepidoptera outside this place are creatures of seasonality — their lifetimes are timed to the blooming of particular flowers, the drying of particular waters, the warming of particular months. The basin moths have no seasons to time themselves to. Instead, they have synchronized their lifecycles to the basin's own rhythms, which are not rhythms at all but constants. They emerge from pupation on a schedule so regular that it could be predicted to the hour if anyone were here to predict it. They live for exactly seven days. They mate on the second day. They lay eggs on the fourth. They die on the seventh. Every generation is identical to every other generation. There is no adaptation because there is nothing to adapt to. Evolution, in the Darwinian sense, has stopped here — not because the organisms are perfect, but because the environment is perfect, and perfection, once achieved, removes the engine of change. The moths are not beautiful in the way that outside moths are beautiful. They are functional. Their wings are a dull grey-green that matches the canopy undersides where they rest. They have no eyespots, no warning colors, no mating displays. They have pheromones, and these are sufficient. In a world where every individual of your species is in exactly the same place at exactly the same time as every other individual of your species was in the previous generation, you do not need to advertise. You only need to arrive.

The **flies** are few but specialized. One family — perhaps three or four species — has become the basin's pollination service, though "pollination" is a generous term for what happens here. The great stems do not flower in any conventional sense. Their reproductive structures are inconspicuous, emerging briefly from the canopy plates as small, pale clusters of spore-producing tissue. The flies visit these structures, feed on the nutrient-rich exudates, and carry pollen-analogs on their bodies from one stem to another. They are hairy-bodied, like all good pollinators, and their mouthparts are elongated and curved to match the geometry of the reproductive structures. The match is exact. It has been exact for longer than most lineages of fly have existed outside this place. The flies and the stems have co-evolved into a lock and key, and the lock has not changed shape in longer than it takes for mountains to rise and fall.

There are **spiders**, too — though few. Spiders are predators, and predation requires imbalance: a surplus of prey, a scarcity of predators, the constant chase of adaptation and counter-adaptation that drives evolutionary arms races. The basin has no arms races. The spider populations here are maintained at exactly the density the prey populations can sustain, and they have been maintained at this density for so long that the spiders have become something other than what spiders are elsewhere. The basin spiders are slow. They move slowly, they build their webs slowly — when they build webs at all — and many species have abandoned web-building entirely in favor of ambush predation, sitting motionless on the bark of the great stems and waiting for insects to walk within reach. Their legs are shorter than those of their outside relatives. Their chelicerae are proportionally larger. They are, in the language of evolutionary biology, sitting at the endpoint of an energy-minimization curve: they expend as little as possible because there is no reason to expend more. Food comes to them at a steady, predictable rate. They take what comes. They do not chase. They have not chased in a very long time.

## The Amphibians

There are four species of amphibian in the basin, and they are the basin's most visible animals, though "visible" requires that you be patient enough to see things that do not want to be seen and are not trying to hide — they are simply the same color as everything around them.

**The mat-frog** (*if you must name it, and naming is a human compulsion the basin does not share*) is the most common. It is four or five centimeters long, squat, with a head that is indistinguishable from its body and limbs that are short and splayed, designed for sitting rather than jumping. Its skin is the exact grey-green of the root-mat, with a texture that mimics the mat's fibrous surface so perfectly that a mat-frog sitting on the mat is, for practical purposes, invisible until it blinks. Its eyes are small and dark and set high on its head, allowing it to sit with its body pressed flat against the mat and still observe the world above. It feeds on springtails and small beetles, snapping them up with a tongue that moves faster than the eye can follow — the only fast thing about the mat-frog, the one concession to the need to eat. It breeds in the shallow water-films of the channels. Its eggs are laid singly, scattered across a square meter of mat surface, each one tucked into a crevice between root fibers. The eggs develop over exactly sixty-three days. The tadpoles emerge, feed on algal films for exactly twenty-one days, and metamorphose into miniature adults. The timing does not vary. It has never varied. The chemical cues that trigger metamorphosis are produced by the mat itself — by the root-tip exudates that leach into the water-films — and these cues are constant because the mat is constant. The mat-frog is, in a sense, not an organism that happens to live on the mat. It is a mat-organism, a component of the mat's biology, as much a part of the system as the nematodes beneath it or the mosses above it. Remove the mat and the frog has no color, no food, no breeding cues, no reason to exist. The mat would not notice the frog was gone. But the frog would notice, if frogs notice, which is unlikely.

**The buttress-newt** is larger — ten to twelve centimeters — and lives in the crevices of the great buttress-ridges. It is darker than the mat-frog, its skin a mottled grey-brown that matches the bark of the ridges. It is nocturnal, though in a place where light does not change, "nocturnal" means only that it is active during the long dark periods between the brief intervals when direct light falls on its particular ridge — a pattern determined by the geometry of the canopy and the angle of the sun, which does not change because the canopy does not change. The buttress-newt feeds on spiders and large beetles. It hunts by walking — slowly, deliberately, placing each foot with care — along the surfaces of the ridges, pausing at each crevice and crack to investigate with its snout. Its nostrils are acute. It can detect the chemical traces left by a beetle that passed this way hours ago, and it follows these traces with a persistence that is almost mechanical. When it finds prey, it strikes with a lunge that is, like the mat-frog's tongue, the only fast thing about it. The buttress-newt breeds in the deeper pools that form in the interior hollows of the oldest ridges — pools that are warmer than the channels, heated by the metabolic activity of the dense root tissue surrounding them. Its eggs are large, few in number — perhaps a dozen per clutch — and each one is attached to the underside of a submerged root fiber where it is protected from the slow currents of the pool. The larvae are fully aquatic and take a full year to metamorphose. One year. Every year. Always.

**The canopy-salamander** is the most unusual of the four, because it is the only amphibian in the basin that lives above the mat. It is small — three to four centimeters — slender, with long limbs and a laterally compressed tail that it uses to grip the bark of the great stems as it climbs. Its skin is pale, almost white, with a faint greenish cast, and it is perpetually damp, coated in a mucus that prevents desiccation in the slightly drier air of the upper trunk. It lives in the grooves of the bark, in the zone where mosses and fungal mats create a microhabitat of near-100% humidity. It feeds on small flies and mites that live in the same zone. It is, as far as anyone can tell, the basin's only arboreal amphibian — a niche that exists nowhere else in the world, because nowhere else in the world has a tree large enough, old enough, and stable enough to support a permanent amphibian population in its bark. The canopy-salamander breeds in the water that collects in the hollows where branches would be if the stems branched, which they do not, but where the expanding canopy plates create small depressions in the upper surfaces of the lower plates. These depressions fill with condensation. The salamander lays its eggs in them. The tadpoles develop in pools no larger than a human palm, fed by nothing but rainwater and the bacterial films that grow on the plate surfaces. It is an astonishingly precarious arrangement that is, in fact, not precarious at all, because the conditions that create it have not changed in longer than any amphibian lineage has existed. The canopy-salamander is precarious in the same way that a rock is precarious: it is a thing that sits in a place and does not move, and the place does not change, and so the precariousness is theoretical rather than practical, a quality that exists only in the minds of beings who have experienced change.

**The fourth species** — the basin's most elusive amphibian — is the **channel-newt**, and it is the only organism in the basin that has lost the capacity for terrestrial life entirely. It is fully aquatic. It lives in the channels, in the thin films of water that never fully evaporate, and it has adapted to this shallow, temporary — though in the basin's terms, "temporary" means "permanent" — environment with a commitment that borders on the absolute. It is flattened, almost ribbon-like, no more than a centimeter wide but seven or eight centimeters long, and it moves through the water channels with an undulating motion that is eerily similar to the movement of water itself. Its skin is the same grey-green as everything else. Its eyes are vestigial — tiny dark points that can detect light and shadow but not form. It does not need form-detection. The channel-newt's world is a world of chemical gradients and temperature differentials, and it navigates this world with a lateral line system — a series of pressure-sensitive organs along its flanks — that would be recognizable to any ichthyologist, because it is the same system that fish use to detect water movement. The channel-newt has, through convergent evolution or shared ancestry, arrived at a fish-like solution to a non-fish problem: life in water so shallow that a fish could not survive in it. It feeds on nematodes and the eggs of springtails. It is, itself, fed upon by nothing, because nothing in the basin is large enough or fast enough or motivated enough to eat a channel-newt, and so it lives for a very long time — years, perhaps decades — moving through its channels, breathing through its skin, a living ribbon in a world of living carpet.

## The Birds

There are four species of bird in the basin, and they are the basin's most vocal inhabitants, not because they are naturally loud but because they must be. The hydraulic hum of the great stems fills the acoustic space of the basin at all times, a low, pervasive, inescapable roar, and any organism that communicates by sound must cut through it or be unheard. The birds have solved this problem by evolving voices that occupy the frequency bands the stems do not — high, sharp, piercing calls that sit above the bass roar like the glint of sunlight on the water-films above the grey-green mat.

**The first and most numerous** is a small, grey-green bird — slightly larger than a wren, with a compact body, a short, strong bill, and a tail that is held cocked at a jaunty angle that seems, in this place of absolute stillness, almost indecently lively. It is a bark-gleaner, a bird that feeds by walking along the surfaces of the great stems, probing the grooves and crevices with its bill, extracting beetles, spiders, mites, and larvae from the organic debris that accumulates in the bark. Its call is a single, sharp, repeated note — a *tik* — produced at intervals of exactly three to four seconds, as regular as the dripping of condensation from the canopy. The purpose of this call is territory maintenance. In a place where nothing changes, territory is everything: the particular section of a particular trunk that a particular bird feeds on is its section, has always been its section, will always be its section, and the *tik* is the announcement of this fact, broadcast to any other individual that might be within earshot. The bird's territory is perhaps twenty meters of trunk circumference. It defends this territory with its voice and, rarely, with physical confrontation — short, sharp flights at an intruder, which are met by short, sharp flights away. The confrontations are ritualized. They last seconds. They resolve instantly, because the territory boundaries have been established for generations and neither bird has any reason or capacity to question them.

**The second species** is larger — starling-sized — and darker, its plumage a deep grey-brown that matches the shadowed interior of the buttress-ridges where it lives and feeds. This bird is a specialist feeder: it eats only the fungal mats that grow in the deepest bark crevices, and its bill is long, thin, and curved, shaped like a probe designed to reach into spaces too narrow for any other bird's bill. Its call is a liquid, warbling phrase — complex by the standards of the basin, though simple by the standards of outside songbirds — that is produced from a perch on the bark surface, usually at dusk-equivalent, which in the basin means at the point in the sun's daily arc when the canopy above the bird's particular ridge is thickest and the light is dimmest. The song has precisely seven notes, always in the same order, always at the same pitch. It does not vary. It has not varied in the memory of the basin, which is to say, in the memory of the root-mat, which records everything in chemistry and decay-rate and growth-pattern and which has recorded this song, in its way, for as long as the song has existed.

**The third species** is the basin's only aerial specialist — the only bird that hunts on the wing. It is sleek, long-winged, with a deeply forked tail and a bill that is wide at the base and narrow at the tip, shaped like a net. It feeds on moths and flies, catching them in the air between the great stems in a flight pattern that is as mechanical as everything else in the basin: a series of figure-eights, repeated without variation, covering the same volume of air above the same section of mat, at the same speed, at the same time of day, every day. Its call is a high, thin whistle — the highest-pitched sound in the basin, pitched above even the condensation patter — and it serves not as a territorial declaration but as a simple contact call between mates. The basin-swifts, if that is what they are, mate for life, and their pair bond is maintained by this whistle, exchanged in the brief moments when their figure-eight paths cross. The whistle is a metronome. It marks the passage of time in a place that has no other way to mark it.

**The fourth species** is the rarest, and the strangest. It is a small, round, extremely quiet bird — the only quiet bird in the basin — that lives exclusively in the canopy, in the dense tangle of photosynthetic tissue that forms the ceiling of the world. It is never seen from the ground. It is, perhaps, never seen at all, except by other individuals of its own species. Its plumage is green — true green, not the grey-green of the mat or the grey-brown of the bark, but a deep, saturated green that matches the living canopy tissue with a fidelity that suggests it has spent longer in this canopy than anything else has spent anywhere. It feeds on the canopy insects — the small flies and moths that are drawn to the reproductive structures of the canopy plates — and it feeds silently, gleaning them from the tissue surfaces with a bill that is tiny and precise and perfectly adapted to extracting insects from the complex, layered architecture of the photosynthetic plates. Its call — because it must call, because all birds must call, because the imperative to signal conspecifics is written so deeply in the avian genome that even the basin cannot erase it — is a sound so low and soft that it is, in practice, inaudible beneath the hydraulic roar. It is felt rather than heard: a vibration, a tremor in the air, a frequency so close to the frequency of the stems' pumping that it merges with it, becomes part of it, disappears into it. The canopy-bird calls, and only another canopy-bird can hear the call, because only another canopy-bird is listening at the right frequency, with ears tuned by evolution to pick out the one voice that matters from the roar of everything else. This is the basin's most intimate relationship between two organisms: a call and an answer, pitched to the limit of audibility, wrapped in the body-sound of a forest that functions like a machine.

## What Nothing Is

And then there is what does not live here.

There are no mammals. The basin is too stable, too predictable, too complete for mammals, which are creatures of excess and variability, which require change to fuel their restless metabolisms. There are no reptiles. The basin is too cool, too wet, too uniformly the same for reptiles, which need temperature variation to regulate their body heat. There are no large predators of any kind. There is nothing that hunts. There is nothing that flees. The fundamental drama of predator and prey — the engine of so much evolutionary creativity in the outside world — has been resolved here into a simple equation of production and consumption, a metabolic ledger balanced to the last decimal, forever.

There are no plants that are not the basin. There are no organisms that are not the basin. Every living thing in this depression, from the mycorrhizal network in the deepest root tissue to the canopy-bird calling its inaudible call in the highest green, is a component of a single system, a single machine, a single organism that has been running for longer than anything outside can comprehend. The individual species — the mat-frog, the bark-beetle, the channel-newt, the bark-gleaning bird with its three-second *tik* — are not inhabitants of the basin. They are organs of the basin. They are the basin's way of processing energy, cycling nutrients, maintaining the conditions that allow the basin to continue being the basin. They did not choose this role. They were not assigned it. They arrived — through the slow, blind processes of dispersal and colonization and evolution — and they stayed, and they changed, and they became what the basin needed them to be, and the basin became what they needed it to be, and the distinction between need and accommodation, between organism and environment, between the living and the place where the living happens, dissolved into the warm, grey-green, humming, dripping, breathing, permanently, perfectly, loudly alive.

---

They are here. They have always been here. They will be here when the light changes and the hum stops and the dripping slows, which is to say, they will be here forever, because those things will never happen. The basin is finished. The basin is complete. The basin is alive with ten thousand kinds of life, each one exactly as numerous as it needs to be, each one doing exactly what it has always done, each one as permanent and as impermanent as everything else — growing and decaying at the same rate, living and dying at the same rate, humming along in the great, resolved chord of a system that has no unsolved problems because it solved them all so long ago that the solutions have become the system itself.

You stand in the breathing basin. The basin breathes. The basin hums. The basin drips.

Inside it, on it, in it, of it — everything that lives here lives here perfectly, and has lived here perfectly, and will live here perfectly, until the basin ends, which is to say, never.
