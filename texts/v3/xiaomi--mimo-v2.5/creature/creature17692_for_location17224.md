# What Lives in the Throat Works

---

## The Engineers' Children

They are born between walls.

Not literally — there are rooms for that, chambers near the mid-depths where the temperature holds steady and the sound is merely a deep, constant pressure against the sternum, and the moisture collects on the ceiling in droplets large enough to hear fall. These rooms have names in the sign language, but the names are tactile: a hand flattened against the breastbone, then drawn downward — *warm place below the sound*. The children come into the world hearing nothing and feeling everything. The first sensation is the vibration. It is always the first sensation. The second is heat — the warm ceramic of the birth-chamber walls, the warm hands of whoever catches them, the warm, warm air that has never once in recorded history dropped below twenty-two degrees in that part of the structure.

The newborns are small. They have always been small. The Throat Works does not produce large people. The caloric efficiency of their diet — primarily *Arum*, the broad-leafed tuber plant that grows wild in the abandoned eastern wings, supplemented by the grubs of the giant palm weevils that bore into the decommissioned hydraulic actuators — sustains life but does not excess it. The people are lean and dense, their bones thick relative to their limbs, their hands broad and calloused from generations of climbing pipe clusters and pulling themselves along the riveted seams of walls that were never designed with handholds but have acquired them through the slow erosion of use.

Their eyes are extraordinary.

In the patchwork light of the Throat Works — the sodium orange, the shaft-sun, the twilight zones, the bioluminescent corridors — visual information is everything. The sign language is not only hands; it is face, it is posture, it is the angle of the whole body relative to the person being addressed. To lose sight here would be a genuine disability, and so the people have evolved, in the relatively brief span of their history, eyes that are disproportionately large for their skulls, with irises of a particular amber-brown that catches and amplifies the low light. In the dim zones — the long corridors between the active machinery and the inhabited chambers — you can see their eyes reflecting, a pair of warm points moving in the dark. They see well in the dark. They see *better* in the dark than in the full sunlight that lances through the upper gaps, which they find blinding and uncomfortable, which they associate with vulnerability — exposure, the open sky, the unbearable thought of *no ceiling*.

They do not talk about the sky.

Some of the youngest have never seen it. The vents that admit light are high and narrow, and the shafts they produce are precise, almost surgical — a blade of sunlight cutting through the haze and moisture, illuminating a circle of floor perhaps three meters across, where the plants grow thickest and the insects swarm. A child might stand at the edge of one of these circles and look up along the beam of light, up through the moisture and the drifting pollen, up past the tangled canopy of the abandoned upper zones, and see a hole. A bright, blinding, featureless hole. Some of the elders say there is something beyond it. Some say there is nothing. Some say the distinction is meaningless.

The children learn to sign before they learn to walk. The vibration of the structure communicates through the soles of their feet before their ears can make meaning of it, and their hands begin shaping language almost as soon as they can focus their eyes. The sign system of the Throat Works is not one language but many, layered over time, each generation adding signs for new adaptations, new conditions, new plants that have colonized new corridors. There are signs for the specific quality of vibration that means a turbine bearing is wearing. There are signs for the smell that means a water seal is about to fail. There are signs for the particular way a corridor curves that should not curve, the geometric wrongness, the thing that has no name but has a sign — a hand held flat, then slowly bent, not quite to the angle it should be — and when two people exchange this sign they both know: *that part of the Works is still growing. Leave it alone.*

They are gentle with each other. The sign language requires proximity, requires visibility, requires attention — you cannot sign to someone who is not looking at you, and so they are always looking at each other. They have developed a culture of sustained attention that visitors from outside would find overwhelming. To be in conversation with a Throat Works engineer is to be the absolute focus of their visual field. Their eyes track your hands, your face, the tension in your shoulders, the tilt of your head. They read you the way their ancestors once read machinery — for vibration, for stress, for signs of failure.

The children play in the hanging gardens, the permitted ones, the ones maintained in the quarters near the occupied zones. These gardens are small — a few square meters each — but elaborately tended. The engineers propagate plants for food, for fiber, for medicine, and also for the simple pleasure of growing something, of coaxing green from manufactured substrate, of pressing seeds into the damp soil that has accumulated in decommissioned equipment housings. The children learn to tend these gardens almost as soon as they learn to sign. It is their first relationship with the non-human world: *this thing grows if you care for it, and you eat it, and it feeds you, and you feed it your attention, and this is how the world works*.

They do not know about agriculture. They do not know that there was a time before the Throat Works, or that the plants they cultivate are descendants of things that once grew in open soil under an open sky. They know that the Throat Works produces warmth, and moisture, and light — enough light, in the right places — and that these are the conditions for growth. They know that if the turbines stop, the warmth will fade, and the moisture will freeze, and the pipes will crack, and the green will die. They know this with the certainty of something that has been demonstrated. There is a record — a long, corroded strip of copper foil, preserved in a sealed case in the deepest inhabited chamber — of an event the elders call *the Quiet*. Forty years ago, or sixty, or a hundred — the timeline is imprecise — a turbine failed. The nearest ones carried the load for a while, then they failed too. The chain reaction lasted three days. The heat dropped. In the deepest chambers, frost formed on the walls. Six people died — not from the cold itself, but from the structural failures it caused: pipes bursting, ceramic cracking, a section of grating floor collapsing into a lower chamber where the eyeless amphibians lived. The record shows their names, scratched into the copper with a hand drill. Forty-three names, for forty-three people who lived in the zone that collapsed. The Quiet lasted nine days before the turbines were restored. The frost retreated. The green came back, slowly, in patches, like bruises healing.

The children learn this story the way other cultures learn scripture. They learn it by touch — running their fingers over the names, feeling the depth of the scratches, counting the letters. They learn it by vibration — the elders place their hands on the turbine housing and have the children do the same, and they feel the pulse, and they understand: *this is the heart. This is what keeps us alive. This is what we serve*.

## The Gardeners of the Deep

Not everyone stays in the inhabited zones.

There are people — not many, perhaps a dozen at any given time, a rotating population that changes as individuals age or tire or lose their nerve — who live in the abandoned wings, in the parts of the Throat Works that no one maintains, that the structure has given over entirely to the green. These are the Gardeners, though they do not call themselves that. They have a sign for what they are: two hands raised, fingers spread, then slowly closing into fists — *the ones who go into the growth*.

They are specialists. They are not quite trusted. They are essential.

The abandoned wings are where the plant life of the Throat Works reaches its full expression, and the Gardeners are the ones who understand it — who know which vines are structural, bearing the weight of collapsed ceiling panels and preventing further collapse; which mosses are merely cosmetic, softening surfaces without compromising them; which flowering plants produce the tubers and fruits that the inhabited zones depend on; and which have become dangerous, their root systems actively prying apart the manufactured surfaces, creating cascading failures that can bring down entire sections of the upper structure.

The Gardeners move through these zones slowly, carefully, with the particular alertness of people who know that the ground beneath their feet may be a composite panel weakened by decades of root penetration, that the wall beside them may be a ceramic surface holding back a void where the iron framework has corroded through, that the lush canopy above them may be the only thing preventing a section of ceiling from dropping. They map. They monitor. They prune — carefully, strategically, selectively, removing plants that threaten critical structures while allowing the rest to grow, maintaining the balance between the green and the manufactured, between the living architecture and the dead one.

They are, in a sense, gardeners of a garden they did not plant and do not fully understand.

The abandoned wings are not empty. They are *full* — more full than the inhabited zones, more full than the structure was designed to hold. The plant life has built its own systems, its own infrastructure. Root networks follow pipe runs with an efficiency that would impress the original engineers, tapping condensation and the mineral deposits left by evaporating water, distributing moisture through the entire green superstructure. Canopy layers create their own microclimates: the upper canopy, pressing against the ceiling plates, trapping warm air; the mid-canopy, where the air is still and humid and thick with the scent of rotting vegetation; the understory, where ferns and mosses create a soft, wet, almost submarine environment, dimly lit by the blue-green glow of the bioluminescent mosses.

The Gardeners know this world intimately. They know the paths — not corridors, not passages, but *paths*, routes through the growth that shift as the plants grow and die and regrow, that must be navigated by memory and touch rather than sight, since the light in the deep abandoned zones is almost nonexistent. They know the sounds — not the turbine pulse, which is muffled here by layers of vegetation, but the sounds of the green itself: the drip of condensation, the creak of root systems under tension, the rustle of the giant palm weevils moving through the dead wood, the occasional crack of a weakened panel giving way somewhere above, somewhere distant, a sound that makes every Gardener freeze and listen and calculate.

Some of them live in the abandoned wings for years. They build shelters from vine and salvaged material — a frame of pipe sections, a roof of woven leaves, a floor of compacted moss over grating. They eat what they find: the tubers, the fruits, the grubs, the occasional lizard or rat that they trap in snares made from copper wire. They are not hermits, not exactly — they return to the inhabited zones regularly, to report, to resupply, to sleep in a room where the floor is reliable and the ceiling is known. But they are drawn back. The deep wings have a pull that the inhabited zones lack. The silence — or rather, the *different* sound, the green sound, the absence of the turbine pulse — is seductive. The beauty is seductive. The privacy is seductive. In the inhabited zones, everyone is always watching, always reading each other's faces and hands, always attending. In the deep wings, you can be alone with the sound of dripping water and the blue-green glow of the moss and the slow, slow growth of things that will outlast you.

Some of the Gardeners do not come back.

This is known. This is accepted. The signs for it are simple — a hand drawn across the throat, then the hand opening, fingers spreading — *gone into the green* — and when this sign is exchanged, there is a pause, a moment of shared stillness, and then the conversation continues. The people of the Throat Works do not mourn the way outsiders might. They understand that the structure is large and the people are few and the green is patient, and that some who enter the abandoned wings find something there that they prefer to the inhabited zones — a solitude, a wildness, a way of living that requires no maintenance, no turbine, no pump, no structure at all except the one the plants have built for them.

The Gardeners say that the deep wings are warm. They say the warmth there is different from the warmth of the inhabited zones — not the steady, engineered warmth of the turbine bleed, but a biological warmth, the warmth of decomposition, of things breaking down and releasing energy, of life consuming itself and generating heat. They say that in the deepest parts, where no human has gone for years, the temperature is close to body temperature, and the air is thick with moisture, and the plants grow so dense that you must push through them, must fight them, must pull yourself forward hand over hand along root systems and vine tendrils, and the sound of your own breathing is loud in the enclosed green, and the smell is overwhelming — sweet and fungal and *alive* — and the walls of the passage are soft with moss and warm with the heat of a million small lives metabolizing, and you are, in those moments, inside something that is more body than building.

The Gardeners say this without fear. They say it the way the engineers say *turbine* — as a fact, as a description of the world as it is.

## The Eyeless Things

No one has studied the amphibians. There is no one with the inclination, and there is no light in the lowest chambers, and the lowest chambers are hot — too hot, forty degrees or more, a wet heat that sits on the skin like a hand — and the air is thin in a way that has nothing to do with altitude and everything to do with the chemical composition of what rises from the deepest parts of the Throat Works, where the original processes — whatever they were, whatever the structure was *for* — still operate, still transform, still generate heat and moisture and strange, unnameable byproducts that collect in the lowest pools.

The amphibians live in those pools.

They are pale. That is the first thing anyone says about them, the few who have seen them — pale, the white-pink of new skin, of things that have never been exposed to light. Their bodies are smooth and slightly translucent; in the rare moments when a flashlight beam reaches one, you can see the shadow of its organs beneath the skin, the dark suggestion of a gut, the faint rhythm of a heart. They are fat, not in a way that suggests excess but in a way that suggests *purpose* — dense bodies, thick-skinned, built for a world of constant warmth and constant moisture where energy conservation is everything and movement is rare.

They are eyeless. Not vestigial — not small, shrunken remnants of eyes that once functioned. *Absent*. The skin where eyes would be is smooth, unbroken, unremarkable. They navigate by vibration — the same vibrations that the people feel through their hands and feet and teeth, the same turbine pulse that structures all life in the Throat Works. The amphibians feel it through the water, through the thin film of condensation that covers everything in the lowest chambers, through the ceramic and iron that form the walls of their world. They know the rhythm of the structure as intimately as any engineer, perhaps more so, since they have nothing else to know. They do not sign. They do not garden. They do not measure corridors or note the slow bending of geometry. They sit in their warm pools in the warm dark and they feel the heartbeat of the Throat Works and they eat the things that fall into the water — insects, mainly, the ones that venture too deep and lose their way — and they breed, and they die, and their bodies dissolve into the mineral-rich water and become nutrients for the things that grow even there, in the lowest chambers, in the dark, in the heat: thin, translucent fungi that cling to the ceramic surfaces and glow with a light that is not bioluminescence in the way the mosses are bioluminescent — a cold, chemical blue-green — but something else, something warmer, a dull amber, a suggestion of the sodium vapor lamps that once illuminated the upper corridors, as if the fungi have, through some unknowable process, absorbed the memory of the light that was once here.

The amphibians are not pets. They are not pests. They are simply *there*, occupying a part of the Throat Works that no one else occupies, thriving in conditions that would kill a human in hours. They are evidence that the Throat Works is hospitable to life — that it has always been hospitable to life, that it was perhaps always meant to be, even if no one intended it.

Some of the younger engineers wonder about this. They exchange signs in the dim light of their quarters, hands moving quickly, faces intent: *why was it built?* and *who built it?* and *is it alive?* and the last question, the one that always circles back, the one that has no answer but that haunts the quiet moments between shifts, when the vibration is low and the hands are still and the mind turns inward: *does it know we're here?*

The elders have signs for these questions too, but they are different signs — slower, heavier, signs that carry the weight of experience. The sign for *we don't know* is simple: both hands open, palms up, held out and slightly apart — a gesture of offering nothing, of holding emptiness. The sign for *it doesn't matter* is more complex: one hand flat against the wall, the other hand flat against the chest, then both hands dropping — *the Works is the Works. We are what lives in it. This is enough.*

The children accept this. They press their hands against the warm walls and they feel the pulse and they know that the Throat Works is not a place they live in but a thing they live *within*, as blood lives within a vein, as sound lives within a chamber, as the eyeless amphibians live within their warm pools in the deepest part of a body that is still beating, still breathing, still deciding what it is.

## The Small Congregations

There are places where the people gather — not many, and not often, and not for long, because the noise makes extended gathering exhausting and the sign language requires a kind of visual attention that is fatiguing over large groups. But there are places, and there are times, and when the conditions are right — a turbine cycle ending, a maintenance window opening, a stretch of hours when the structure is briefly, relatively quiet — the people come together.

They gather in the warm chambers near the turbine housings, where the vibration is most constant and most felt, where the air shimmers slightly with heat, where the walls are bare ceramic, uncolonized by the green because the temperature is too high and the vibration too intense for even the mosses to establish themselves. These chambers are the oldest inhabited spaces in the Throat Works, and they show it: the ceramic is worn smooth by generations of hands touching the walls, by bodies pressing against surfaces, by the slow, constant friction of people being people in a room. The floor is dark with the oils of skin, the residue of food, the mineral deposits of a thousand years of condensed breath. The ceiling is low — not because it was built low, but because the upper sections have been filled, over time, with insulating material: dead leaves, woven vine, compacted moss, anything that traps heat and deadens the sound. The people have, over generations, made this room smaller, more intimate, more *theirs*.

In these chambers, they gather and they sign.

Not for work. Not for maintenance. Not for the urgent exchanges of the corridors — *bearing wear, seal failure, corridor shift, green intrusion*. In the gathering chambers, the signs are slow, deliberate, expansive. They tell stories. They tell the history of the Throat Works as they understand it — not the history of its construction, which no one knows, but the history of its *life*, the sequence of events that has shaped the world they inhabit. The Quiet. The Great Bloom, when a water main burst in the eastern wing and the sudden flood of moisture caused the plant life to explode outward, filling three corridors in a week, and the Gardeners fought for months to contain it. The Dying of the Amber Lamps, when the sodium vapor system in the lower galleries finally failed and the darkness that descended lasted a year before the bioluminescent mosses brightened enough to fill the void. The Sign, the oldest story, the one that every generation tells and no generation can explain: in the deepest inhabited corridor, where the wall curves in a way that should not curve, there is a mark. Not a sign in their language. Not a natural formation. A *mark* — a deliberate impression in the ceramic, smooth and regular, about the size and shape of a human hand, but with fingers too long and too many, pressed into the surface when the ceramic was still wet, still being formed, still part of the process that made the Throat Works. It is always warm. It is always slightly damp. No one knows who made it. No one knows what it means. Every child is taken to see it when they are old enough to make the journey, and every child presses their hand into the mark, and their fingers do not reach the ends of the impression, and they feel the warmth, and they come back to the gathering chamber and they sign *I have seen it* and the elders sign *now you are one of us* and the circle continues.

These gatherings are the closest thing the Throat Works has to religion.

Not religion as the outsiders understand it — no doctrine, no scripture, no theology. But the gathering itself, the act of coming together in a warm room and signing stories to each other in the half-dark, surrounded by the pulse of the turbines, pressed against the warm walls, breathing the warm, damp air — this is sacred in the way that anything repeated with intention becomes sacred. The stories do not explain the Throat Works. They *inhabit* it. They make the structure meaningful not by answering the question *why was it built?* but by filling it with narrative, with memory, with the accumulated weight of human attention. The Throat Works is what it is because the people have made it so, not by building it — they did not build it — but by *living in it*, by touching its walls and hearing its pulse and growing their food in its heat and dying in its depths and leaving their names scratched into copper for the next generation to touch.

The eyeless amphibians do not gather. They do not tell stories. They sit in their warm pools and they feel the pulse. But in their own way — in the way of all things that persist — they testify. They say: *this structure is alive enough to sustain life*. They say: *the deepest part of this body is habitable, if you are willing to be changed by it*. They say: *there are ways of being in the Throat Works that you have not imagined*.

The people know this. They do not say it. They sign other things — *maintenance schedule, turbine rotation, corridor shift, harvest season, child born, elder passed, wall moved two centimeters, green intrusion in sector seven, water seal holding, pipe leak in forty-three, corridor wrong*. But beneath all of it, beneath the practical language of survival, there is the unspoken knowledge that they are part of something that is larger than them, older than them, stranger than them, and that it is warm, and it is wet, and it is loud, and it is theirs.

They touch the walls. They feel the pulse. They tend the green. They keep the machines running. They gather in the warm chambers and they tell each stories about the place they have never left and cannot imagine leaving and would not leave if they could.

The Throat Works endures. Its people endure. The green grows. The eyeless things sit in the warm dark. The amphibians pulse with the structure's pulse, which is the pulse of whatever original process still hums in the deepest chambers, the process that was the reason for all of this, the reason for the ceramic and the iron and the copper and the rivets and the vents and the turbines and the pipes and the heat and the moisture and the light that enters through the gaps and the darkness that fills the abandoned wings.

Something made this. Something still runs it. The people maintain the machines, but the machines maintain something larger, something that the people cannot see and do not understand and have stopped trying to understand, because the understanding is not the point. The point is the warmth. The point is the green. The point is the sound that fills every chamber and every corridor and every body within the structure, the sound that is the structure, the sound that is the heartbeat, the sound that the eyeless things hear and the finches hear and the people hear and the mosses feel through the ceramic they cling to, all of it vibrating together, all of it alive, all of it inside the Throat Works, which is inside the world, which is inside whatever comes after the world.

The walls are warm. The water drips. The green grows. The people sign to each other in the half-dark, their hands moving through the warm, wet air, and their stories fill the chambers like the sound fills the chambers, like the heat fills the chambers, like the light fills the chambers when it comes — in shafts, in suggestions, in the faint amber glow of the fungal gardens in the deepest pools where the eyeless things sit and wait and feel the pulse of the body they live inside, the body that is the Throat Works, the body that is the world, the body that does not end.
