# What Lives in the Still Bowl

---

They begin in the dark before they have names.

The frog is called nothing. It has never needed a name because it has never encountered another kind of creature that might need to distinguish it from something else. It is one of perhaps forty that inhabit the basin at any given time, though the number shifts by ones and never by twos — a slow, tidal oscillation around a mean that has held for longer than the root systems in the ceiling have been alive. Each frog weighs less than a closed fist. Its skin is translucent-white, not the white of paint but the white of something that has had all its color leached away by centuries of lightless living, so that what remains is the color of the tissue beneath — the pinkish suggestion of vessels, the darker shadow of the gut, the faintest blush of the organ that was once a lung and is now something more like a membrane, a second skin for breathing the saturated air. It is always damp. It could not be otherwise. The air is the air of a mouth held open.

Its eyes are open too, and they are enormous — each one a dark, wet hemisphere that takes up nearly a third of the skull. The pupils are vestigial, contracted to thin slits that respond to nothing because there is nothing to respond to. The eyes serve a different purpose now, or perhaps no purpose at all. They have become feelers, organs of moisture-sense. The frog can detect, through the surface tension of its exposed cornea, the direction from which water moves. It can feel the difference between a drip that will land on its back and one that will land two centimeters to the left. This is enough. This is all that is needed.

The frog sits on a stone that has been shaped by its predecessors. Not carved — shaped in the way that a path is shaped, by the repeated passage of small bodies settling into the same hollow, generation after generation, until the stone remembers the shape of a frog even when no frog is there. The hollow is shallow, smooth, contoured to the curve of the belly. The frog fits into it the way a word fits into a sentence. It is not comfortable. Comfort implies a comparison with discomfort, and there is no comparison here. The frog simply *fits*. It is in its place, and its place is the stone, and the stone is the basin, and the basin is the basin.

It breathes. The throat-pouch inflates and deflates in a rhythm so slow it is almost geological — three seconds in, seven seconds out, a cycle as regular as the dripping of the ceiling. When the pouch inflates, it glows faintly. Not with light — there is no light — but with a subtle bioluminescent chemistry that occurs only in the tissue of the throat, a residual trick inherited from ancestors who used it for something, and that persists here because it costs almost nothing and has not been selected against. The glow is invisible to the frogs themselves. It is wasted light, a conversation held in a room with no one listening. But it is there. A faint, blue-white pulse in the absolute darkness, marking the rhythm of a life that has no reason to advertise itself.

The frog eats when something touches its skin. It does not hunt. Hunting implies motion through space toward a target, and motion is expensive and the targets are few. Instead, the frog waits, and when an insect — a beetle, a small moth, a crustacean that has wandered above the waterline — brushes against its flank, the frog's tongue fires. It is involuntary, reflexive, as automatic as the heartbeat. The tongue is long, sticky, tipped with a pad that adheres on contact. The prey is drawn into the mouth before it can react. The frog swallows. The throat-pouch pulses. The cycle continues.

There is no competition among the frogs for food because the food is everywhere and the frogs are few. A frog might eat once a week, or once a month. It is difficult to say. Time does not announce itself here. The frog's metabolism has slowed to match the pace of the world it inhabits — a metabolism that might be called sluggish in a creature exposed to sunlight, but that is here simply *appropriate*. The frog does what the water does: it moves at the speed of the thing it is made of. It is mostly water, and it moves like water, which is to say that it moves only when moved, and when it stops, it stops completely, and the stillness it enters is the same stillness that fills the basin like a substance.

---

The beetle is a flattened thing, so compressed dorso-ventrally that it could slip into a crack the thickness of a fingernail. Its body is the color of wet limestone — not gray exactly, but the particular shade that gray becomes when it has been soaked for a thousand years. Its elytra, the hard wing-cases, are pitted with tiny depressions that harbor colonies of bacteria, and these bacteria produce a thin film of moisture that keeps the beetle's joints lubricated in the mineral-heavy air. The beetle and the bacteria have lived together for so long that neither can exist without the other. The beetle carries the bacteria. The bacteria keep the beetle from seizing shut like a rusty hinge. This is a mutualism as old as the basin, and it has never needed to be spoken about.

The beetle walks on the walls. It walks upside down, or sideways, or in whatever orientation the surface presents. Gravity is a suggestion here, not a command — the beetle weighs so little and grips so firmly that the distinction between floor and ceiling is academic. It moves slowly, deliberately, following the ridges in the lichen with the precision of a blind reader tracing braille. Its antennae are long, three times the length of its body, and they sweep the surface ahead in wide arcs, tasting the chemical signature of the lichen, the moss, the bacterial films, the trails left by other beetles. The antennae are the beetle's world. Without them, the beetle would be a stone — still and purposeless.

The beetle eats lichen. Specifically, it eats the outermost layer of the lichen crust, scraping it from the rock with mandibles designed for exactly this task — flat, broad, edged with microscopic teeth that rasp the lichen away without disturbing the substrate beneath. A beetle will spend an entire day on a patch of lichen the size of a coin, working it down layer by layer until the bare rock shows through, then moving on. The lichen regrows. The beetle returns. The cycle is as old as the walls.

There are hundreds of beetles in the basin. Perhaps thousands. No one has counted them because no one has been here to count. They are not gregarious. They do not cluster or swarm. Each beetle lives its own slow life on its own slow stretch of wall, and if it encounters another beetle, it passes by without acknowledgment. They are not social creatures. Sociality requires a reason — a shared threat, a shared resource, a reproductive advantage — and the basin offers none of these with sufficient urgency to make sociability worth the metabolic cost. The beetles are alone together, each one a solitary unit in a population that functions as an ecosystem only in the aggregate.

---

The moth is the closest thing the basin has to something ethereal.

It is small — wingspan of perhaps three centimeters — and its wings are transparent, not merely thin but genuinely without pigment, like panes of living glass. The wing membranes are so delicate that they ripple in the slow convection currents of the basin air, bending and flexing in ways that would tear the wings of a moth that lived in wind. But there is no wind here. The air moves only when something moves through it, and nothing moves fast enough to create turbulence. The moth flies at the speed of a falling leaf, and the air parts for it like water around a fish.

The moth does not eat. It has no mouthparts. It emerges from its pupa with a fixed store of energy, and it uses that store to find a mate, and then it dies. The entire adult life of the moth may last a week. In a world without seasons, without day or night, without any external marker of time's passage, the moth lives and dies in what amounts to a single, unbroken moment — a moment that begins when the pupa splits and ends when the wings finally stop their slow, glassy beating on the surface of the pool.

The moth navigates by touch and chemistry. Its antennae are feathery, elaborately branched, each branch covered in thousands of sensilla that detect the chemical plumes released by potential mates. In the still air of the basin, these plumes rise and spread in predictable patterns, following the faint convection created by the temperature differential between the water and the walls. The moth follows a plume the way a river follows its bed — not by choice but by the simple physics of the medium. When it reaches the source, it mates. When it has mated, it lays eggs in the lichen, depositing them in the crevices with a precision born of instinct, and then it settles on the wall and waits for its body to stop.

The eggs are tiny, no larger than grains of sand, and they are laid in clusters of five or six. They will hatch in time — weeks, perhaps months, the timescale is irrelevant — and the larvae will feed on the lichen, growing slowly, molting in stages, each stage producing a larger larva that eats more lichen and grows more slowly still. The larvae are as blind as the adults, as pale, as unhurried. They are the basin's most numerous inhabitants, and they are almost invisible — small, soft-bodied, colorless, pressed flat against the substrate they consume.

---

In the water, the nematodes are everywhere.

They are invisible to anything larger than a magnifying lens — thin, white, wriggling threads, each one no thicker than a human hair, each one living its entire life in a film of moisture so thin that it constitutes a world. The nematodes live in the moss, in the algal mats, in the spaces between the calcified cells of the living crust. They feed on bacteria, and they are fed upon by the small crustaceans, and they die and decompose and feed the bacteria, and the cycle turns again, endlessly, at a scale so small that it might as well be hypothetical.

But it is not hypothetical. The nematodes are real, and they are numerous — millions of them, perhaps, distributed through every wet surface in the basin. They are the foundation of the food web, the base layer upon which everything else is built. Without them, the crustaceans would starve, and without the crustaceans, the frogs would starve, and without the frogs, the insects would overpopulate, and without balance, the system would collapse. The nematodes do not know this. The nematodes do not know anything. They are response machines, each one a loop of stimulus and reaction, eat and reproduce and die, eat and reproduce and die, in quantities so vast that the aggregate of their mindless activity produces something that looks, from a distance, like purpose.

---

The thing in the deep water.

It moves rarely. When it moves, it displaces water in slow, heavy waves that take thirty seconds to reach the basin's edge and another thirty seconds to subside. The water in the basin is still enough that these waves are unmistakable — they are the signature of something large, something with mass, moving through a medium that resists it.

No one has seen it. Light does not exist here, and even if it did, the water is opaque with life — dense with algae, clouded with particulate matter, thick with the shed carapaces and fecal pellets of ten thousand small organisms. The thing lives in a world of total occlusion. It navigates by vibration, by pressure, by the faint electrical fields generated by the muscle contractions of its prey.

It is probably a fish. An ancient lineage, blind, pale, its scales reduced to vestigial plates embedded in skin that has become smooth and mucus-covered, adapted to a life of moving through water so thick with life that it is more soup than liquid. Its body is elongated, eel-like, perhaps thirty centimeters long, perhaps longer — no one knows. Its jaw is wide, its teeth are reduced to ridges, and it feeds by suction, opening its mouth and creating a partial vacuum that draws in whatever is nearby: nematodes, crustaceans, small beetles that have fallen into the pool, detritus, the shed skins of the frogs. It is not a selective feeder. It is an indiscriminate one, and this is why it does not threaten the frog population — it takes so few, and so randomly, that the effect is negligible.

The fish is old. Not old in the way that trees are old, counting rings, but old in the way that water is old — continuous, unbroken, present in every moment without interruption. It has been here as long as the pool has held water, and the pool has held water for a very long time. The fish has never seen its own reflection. It has never seen anything. It exists in a world of pure sensation — pressure, temperature, chemical gradient, the electrical whisper of living things — and it moves through that world with the ease of something that has never known any other.

When it dies, it will sink to the bottom of the pool, and the bacteria will consume it, and the nutrients will be recycled into the water, and the moss will absorb them, and the nematodes will eat the bacteria that feed on the moss, and the cycle will continue without interruption. The fish is a temporary arrangement of matter that will be redistributed, and the basin will not notice the difference.

---

The wall-roosters are small, blind, and damp.

They are mammals — or mammal-shaped, at least, possessing the warm-blooded metabolism and the fur and the live birth that characterize mammals, but so altered by the pressures of the basin that they barely resemble their surface cousins. They are the size of mice. Their fur is fine and dense, matted with mineral deposits that have accumulated over their lifetimes until the fur itself has become a kind of armor — a crust of calcium carbonate and magnesium phosphate that protects against the constant moisture and the abrasive surfaces of the rock. Underneath the crust, the fur is white. The skin beneath the fur is white. The eyes are closed — not sealed shut, but reduced to thin slits beneath folds of skin, useless organs that have been allowed to atrophy because they serve no function in a world without light.

They roost in the deep crevices of the walls, in the hollows where the limestone has been dissolved into chambers just large enough to hold a cluster of four or five individuals. They huddle together for warmth — not because the basin is cold, exactly, but because their metabolisms, inherited from surface-dwelling ancestors, still require a body temperature higher than the ambient, and huddling is the cheapest way to maintain it. Their breathing is slow, their heartbeats are slow, everything about them is slow, calibrated to the tempo of a world in which nothing happens quickly.

They eat the insects. They eat the beetles and the moth larvae and the small crustaceans that climb above the waterline. They navigate by echolocation — not the precise, high-frequency echolocation of bats, but a cruder version, a series of low clicks that bounce off the nearest surfaces and return to ears that have become large, flattened against the skull, designed to catch and process the faintest echoes. The clicks are inaudible to human ears. They are below the threshold of human hearing, felt rather than heard, a language of pressure waves that maps the basin in the minds of creatures that have never seen it.

They breed once, or perhaps twice, in their lives. The gestation is long. The litters are small — two or three pups, blind and naked and utterly dependent. The pups nurse for weeks, growing slowly, their fur crusting over with minerals before they leave the roost for the first time. By the time they are independent, they are already old in the way that everything in the basin is old — adapted, settled, fitted into their niche with the precision of a bone in a socket.

---

And in the walls themselves — in the deepest crevices, in the spaces between the rock and the living crust, in the interstitial world that exists at the boundary between organism and substrate — there are things that have no names because no one has ever seen them.

Isopods, small and white, no larger than a grain of rice, scuttling through the lichen like mites through a carpet. Springtails, tiny hexapods that feed on fungal hyphae in the deepest, dampest recesses of the moss. Tardigrades, the water bears, microscopic and indestructible, curled into their tun states in the thin films of water that coat every surface, waiting — though not patiently, because patience implies awareness of the wait — for conditions that will allow them to uncurl and feed and reproduce. Mites of impossible variety, each species occupying a niche so specific that it might be defined by a single square centimeter of wall surface, a particular species of lichen, a particular moisture level, a particular chemical composition of the mineral crust.

These creatures are the basin's invisible workforce. They decompose. They recycle. They break down the dead and return it to the system in forms that the living can use. Without them, the basin would drown in its own waste, buried under layers of shed skin and dead tissue and unfertilized eggs. With them, the system turns like a wheel — not fast, but continuously, without pause, without hesitation, without the slightest deviation from the path it has always followed.

---

The basin does not know that it is remarkable. It does not know that it is rare, or beautiful, or strange. It does not know that it exists, in the sense that a thing knows itself when it has a self to know. It is a process, not a place — a closed loop of water and stone and life, running on the energy of dripping calcium carbonate and the slow chemical gradients maintained by bacterial metabolism. It has been running for a very long time, and it will continue to run for a very long time, and when it stops — when the limestone finally dissolves, when the ceiling collapses, when the water drains away — it will not stop with a sound. It will stop the way a heartbeat stops: silently, and then not at all.

But that is not yet. That is not close to yet. The basin is stable, and the things that live in it are calm, and the darkness is full and patient and unhurried. The frog sits on its stone. The beetle scrapes its lichen. The moth drifts on its glassy wings. The fish moves through its dark water. The wall-roosters breathe in their crevices, clustered together, their mineral-crusted fur rising and falling in unison. The nematodes feed. The bacteria decompose. The moss grows, imperceptibly, one cell at a time, in rosettes pressed flat against the ancient, rounded stone.

The water drips. The ceiling weeps. The darkness holds.

This is what lives in the Still Bowl: not creatures, exactly, and not a world, exactly, but a conversation — a very old conversation, conducted in a language that has no speakers, about a subject that has no name. It is still going. It has always been going. It will continue, in the dark, in the silence, in the packed and patient density of a place that has never needed to be anything other than what it is.
