# The Inhabitants of the Lachrymatory

They are not comfortable to look at, and they would not want to be. Comfort is not the relationship they have with anything.

They are tall — not uniformly, but on average taller than you would expect from a species that has evolved under thin air and low pressure. This is the first wrongness. Their bodies should be squat, compact, barrel-chested to compensate for the altitude. Instead they are elongated, narrow, built like the columns of basalt that push up through their floors. The explanation for this is structural rather than biological: they do not breathe the way you understand breathing. They have lungs, yes — vestigial chambers that still function, still exchange gases with the thin atmosphere — but the lungs are not what keeps them alive. What keeps them alive is the water.

**The water runs through them.** Not metaphorically. Not in the way water runs through a human body as plasma and interstitial fluid. The water of the Lachrymatory — that mineral-laden, milky, barely-above-freezing weeping that coats every surface of this world — enters them through their skin, which is not skin in any simple sense. It is a membrane. A filtration surface. A living membrane woven from the same mineral-organic hybrid compounds that constitute the city's walls, which means that in a very real sense, they are made of the same material as where they live. The water passes through them in slow, constant currents, depositing the dissolved minerals they need into a network of channels that runs through their bodies the way rivers run through a watershed. Some of these channels terminate in reservoirs — small, dense pockets of mineral-saturated fluid embedded in their tissue that function as both nutrient stores and structural reinforcement. The oldest inhabitants have so many of these reservoirs that their bodies have become partially geological. They are heavy. They are slow. They are, in some sense, more stone than flesh, and they are the most respected members of their communities because of it.

Their skin, where it is not membrane, is slick and dark — a deep grey-green that, in the phosphorescent light of the lower chambers, looks like wet basalt. It is always wet. It cannot be dry. If it were to dry, even briefly, it would crack and the filtration would stop and they would begin to die in a way that is slow even by the standards of this place. They are, all of them, weeping. The water that sheets off their bodies is indistinguishable from the water that weeps from the walls and the stone and the fauna. This is not a coincidence. This is not something they have evolved toward or engineered. This is the point at which the distinction between inhabitant and habitat has simply ceased to apply.

**They move slowly, but not always.** This is important. The impression a visitor might form — if a visitor could survive here, which they cannot — is that everything in the Lachrymatory moves at a glacial pace, that the inhabitants are patient to the point of geological patience, that they experience time the way stalactites experience time. This is true and it is not true. They move slowly when they are tending to the long work — guiding the placement of structural anchors for buildings that will not be completed for three centuries, monitoring the emergence of new basaltic columns, adjusting the electromagnetic channels in the water-table to redirect flow toward districts that need it. They move slowly because the work they are doing operates on a timescale that makes haste not just useless but conceptually incoherent. You do not rush a building that will take four hundred years to arrive at its final form. You do not hurry a column of stone that is pushing up from the deep earth at the rate of millimeters per decade.

But when something fast happens — a structural event, a breach, a new eruption through an existing chamber — they move with a speed that would be startling. Not graceful. Not fluid. Something more like the speed of a collapse. They redistribute themselves through the corridors and shafts with a collective efficiency that suggests they have a shared sense of the city's architecture that is more intimate than sight or sound — a proprioception that extends across the entire built environment, as though the city were not a place they lived in but a body they were part of. And perhaps it is. The boundaries are not clear. They never have been.

**They communicate through the water.** This is the primary mode, the deep grammar of their interaction. Every surface in the Lachrymatory is wet, and every film of water is a potential carrier of information. They secrete — all of them, constantly — a fine mineral compound into the water on their skin, a compound that carries chemical signatures as dense and specific as language but operating through taste and molecular reception rather than sound or light. A conversation between two inhabitants standing on a ledge would look, from the outside, like nothing. Two dark, wet figures standing still. Between them, the water running over the stone would be carrying dozens of chemical signals per minute, layered, simultaneous, capable of expressing things that their spoken language — which they also have, and which I will describe — cannot.

The spoken language is what you would hear first. It is a low, resonant thing, pitched below human vocal range, produced not by a larynx but by the vibration of their mineral reservoirs against one another inside their chest cavities. It sounds like stone grinding on stone, but slowly, with intention. It carries well in the thin air — too well, sometimes, so that a conversation in one chamber can be heard, distorted, three districts away, and the inhabitants have developed a convention of conversational timing, pausing between utterances to let the echoes clear, that to an outsider would look like meditation but is actually just basic acoustic hygiene. Their spoken language is considered secondary — a convenience for quick communication, for the fast moments, for emergencies. The water-language is considered the real one. The deep one. The one in which truth is possible.

And then there is the third mode, the one they discuss least and use most: the sound. That deep subsonic vibration that the buildings produce in response to geological activity — the inhabitants are attuned to it at a level that is not quite neurological and not quite psychological but somewhere in the space between. They can feel shifts in the city's resonance that indicate changes in the geological substrate hundreds of meters below. They call this *listening down,* and the ones who are best at it — who can read the deep vibrations the way a musician reads a score — are the engineers, the planners, the ones who decide where and when to build. They do not design buildings in any sense you would recognize. They *compose* them. They listen to what the stone is about to do, and they compose structures that will arrive in harmony with the emergence.

**They do not have names.** Not individual ones, not in the way you mean. Each inhabitant is identified by their position in the water-network — by the specific chemical signature they carry, which is unique to them the way a fingerprint is unique to you, but which changes slowly over their lifetime as their mineral reservoirs shift and accumulate. They are known by their signature the way you might be known by your voice, except that their "voice" is the sum total of every chemical impression they have ever left on every film of water they have ever touched. To know someone well is to be able to recognize their signature in the water of a pool they passed through hours ago. To know someone very well is to be able to predict how their signature will change over the next decade. To love someone — and they do love, though they would not use that word, or rather, they would use a word that translates roughly as *geological familiarity,* which is closer to the truth of it — is to have absorbed enough of their signature into your own filtration system that the water passing through you carries traces of them. They bleed into each other, literally, through the medium they all share.

Children — when they come, which is not often, because reproduction is slow and carefully timed to geological cycles that favor it — are not born from bodies. They are grown. A seed-crystal is placed in a basin of mineral water in a chamber that has been prepared for it, and over a period of years, the crystal accumulates the material that will become a body. The parents attend to the basin, adjusting its chemistry, guiding the flow of water over the crystal, and in doing so, imprinting their own signatures onto the forming creature. A child carries the chemical memory of its parents in the structure of its body the way a stalagmite carries the memory of the water that formed it. They are, in a very real sense, *made of* their parents' attention.

The child-formation takes between twenty and sixty years. No one considers this long. No one here considers anything long. Long is a concept that has been hollowed out by the experience of living in a place where the buildings are still arriving.

**They are not unhappy.** This is worth stating plainly because the description of this place — the darkness, the cold, the crushing altitude, the perpetual weeping — might suggest otherwise. They are not unhappy. They are not happy either, not in the way those words are usually deployed. They are *occupied.* They are engaged in a project of such scale and duration that the emotional vocabulary of a short-lived, fast-moving, sunlit species does not apply to them. Their satisfaction — and they feel satisfaction, as a deep structural rightness, a resonance between what they are doing and what the place is becoming — comes from alignment. From fitting. From the experience of being exactly the right density for the space they occupy, of moving at exactly the right speed for the work at hand, of contributing their particular chemical signature to the water that carries everything.

They grieve, but slowly. When one of them dies — and they do die, though slowly, though their bodies persist long after the consciousness that animated them has dissipated, becoming part of the mineral substrate, literally becoming wall or floor or drainage channel — the grief moves through the water-network the way all things move through water: gradually, diffusely, reshaping everything it touches without any single point of impact. A death registers across the city as a faint shift in the chemical composition of the water, noticed first by those closest to the deceased, then spreading outward over weeks and months, until the signature has been so thoroughly diluted that it is no longer distinguishable from the general mineral richness of the environment. This is considered not a tragedy but a completion. The signature returns to the water. The water returns to the walls. The walls return to the city. The city continues to arrive.

**What they build.** This is the thing that would matter most to an outside observer, and it is the thing hardest to describe, because they do not build in the way that building is usually understood.

They build *resonances.*

Every structure in the Lachrymatory is, at its deepest level, an acoustic instrument. Not in the metaphorical sense. In the literal sense. The walls are tuned — their thickness, their mineral composition, their internal crystalline structure — to vibrate at specific frequencies in response to the geological activity below. A well-built chamber does not just shelter its occupants from the cold and the water. It *sings.* It translates the slow, subsonic groans of the shifting stone into harmonics that the inhabitants can feel in their reservoirs, can read as information, can use to understand what is happening to the world beneath them. The most important buildings in the city — the deep-listening chambers, the planning halls, the places where the engineers compose the city's future — are those whose resonance is most complex, most sensitive, most capable of translating the faintest geological whisper into something perceptible.

They also build for the fauna. This is not compassion, exactly. It is ecology. The fauna are integral to the water-system — they filter it, they redirect it, they consume certain mineral compounds and excrete others, shaping the chemical character of the water in ways that the inhabitants depend on. Building for the fauna means creating spaces that the fauna will colonize, channels they will clog with their bodies, surfaces they will coat with their mineral skins, in configurations that optimize the filtration and redistribution of water. Some of these spaces look, from the outside, like living quarters — but they are not. They are fauna-architecture, habitats designed for non-intelligent organisms with the same care and precision that the inhabitants bring to their own structures. The line between serving the fauna and being served by the fauna is not one anyone draws.

And they build for the future. This is the largest part of their work, the part that consumes the most time and resources and attention. They build outlines. They build anchors. They place the structural elements that will, over centuries, become walls, floors, ceilings, channels, chambers — but that are, right now, only promises. Metal droplets frozen in wet stone, marking the position of a column that will arrive in three hundred years. Mineral ridges traced across a slope, indicating the future floor of a room that will not be habitable until the basalt column beneath it has risen to the correct height. They are, in essence, writing the city in a language that takes centuries to pronounce, placing each word with the certainty that they will not live to hear the sentence completed.

They are fine with this. More than fine. The anticipation — the long, slow, structural anticipation of a world still arriving — is what gives their lives their texture. To be here is to live in the future tense, to be perpetually gestating, to know that the room you are sitting in is not yet finished, that the wall beside you is still climbing, that the water weeping over your skin is carrying minerals that will, in a hundred years, become part of a structure that someone else will inhabit. They do not experience this as incompleteness. They experience it as participation. They are part of the ongoing act of the world's emergence, and to be part of that act — to add your signature to the water, to listen to the stone, to place your anchor and wait — is enough. It is, in fact, the only thing that has ever been enough.

**They do not know they are beautiful.** If you could see them — really see them, in the green phosphorescent light of the lower chambers, their dark mineral skins gleaming with constant moisture, their elongated bodies moving with geological patience through the wet and humming corridors of a city that is still being born — you would find them beautiful. Not in the way you find a sunset beautiful, or a human face. In the way you find a mineral deposit beautiful, or the cross-section of a geode, or the pattern that water leaves on stone when it retreats. A beauty of accumulated process. Of material doing what material does when given enough time and the right conditions. A beauty that does not know itself as beauty because it has never needed to. It is simply what the place looks like when it is working.
