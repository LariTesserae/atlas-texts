Here is what lives in the Dripping Quarter:

**The People of the Drip**
They are a people of perpetual damp. Their skin is perpetually cool, their hair usually holds a faint sheen of moisture, and their breath is visible even indoors. They move with a deliberate economy of motion, born from generations of navigating slick stone and narrow spaces. There is little loud laughter, but plenty of quiet, dry wit exchanged in the close quarters. Their voices are soft, pitched to carry just far enough in the intimate acoustics of the gap-streets. They are bonded by a shared, unspoken understanding: the water and the stone are not just their environment, but their charge, their partner, and their silent adversary. They are few, but their presence is dense with the weight of accumulated knowledge.

**The Carvers**
A distinct class, though the word feels too grand. They are not elite; their work is the most physical, the most essential, and the least visible. They spend their days in the dim lower levels, in the bellies of buildings, in the throat-like drainage shafts. Their hands are permanently crisscrossed with fine, pale scars from chipping stone and crystal in tight spaces. They wear leather aprons thick as hide, stiff with dried mineral paste and water, and their eyes are accustomed to the near-dark, reading the subtle gleam of a fresh crystal filament by feel and faint light. Their rhythm is the Quarter’s heartbeat: the steady *tink-tink-tink* of hard stone on growing crystal, echoing from the guts of the settlement. They understand the growth in a way others don't – not as a nuisance, but as a living process with its own seasons, its own stubbornness, its own strange beauty. Some of the older carvers might, in private moments, trace a particularly elegant crystal branch with a calloused finger, acknowledging its artistry before they are duty-bound to remove it.

**The Weavers of Air and Water**
While the Carvers tend to the stone's invasive growth, others manage the water's flow. These are the architects of the drainage, the keepers of the channels. They know every gutter, every sluice, every vertical shaft by heart. They use simple wooden paddles and bone hooks to clear debris from grates, re-angle tiles that have shifted, and ensure the constant runoff follows its prescribed path to the water table below. They work mostly at dawn, before the day's activity might shift a stone or block a grate, their movements quiet and sure in the blue-grey light. They listen more than they speak, attuned to the sound of the Quarter—a misplaced gurgle or an unusual silence in a specific channel tells them everything.

**The Glimmer-Workers**
This is not an official name, but a quiet one used for those (often women) who tend the fired-clay tiles. They work in the upper, slightly drier workshops near the kilns. They mix the mineral slip—a slurry of clay and specific, locally quarried rock dust—that creates the glazed, watertight surface. Their work is meticulous. They inspect tiles for hairline cracks, re-glaze and re-fire flawed pieces, and create the new ones needed to replace those worn smooth or shattered by the carvers' tools. The glaze is their pride: pale greens that echo the water-stained stone, cool greys that blend with the rock, and that rare, warm amber made from a clay vein deeper in the cliff. A perfectly glazed tile, set just so to catch the diffuse light, is a small, cherished thing of beauty in a world of utilitarian damp.

**The Keepers of Dry**
These are the elders, the storytellers, the ones who manage the precious, scarce resources that *aren't* perpetually wet. They control the woodpile—a small, covered stack of driftwood and sparse rim-cut timber. They oversee the storage of the Quarter's limited food: dried fish (from a rare, silty pool deeper in the ravine where the water is less mineral-rich), cultivated moss and fungi grown on carefully positioned damp stone, and the occasional tough, water-loving tuber. Their domains are the small, high lofts or the innermost upper rooms where, by cunning placement of overhangs and tiles, they have created the closest thing to "dry" the Quarter knows. Here, they repair the precious wooden tools, stitch the thick, felted wool clothing treated with lanolin to resist the damp, and care for the fire-pots—small, clay vessels holding smoldering ember, the Quarter's only source of open warmth and dry heat. They are the memory of the outside, the keepers of the idea of "sun-baked," even if no one alive has truly felt it.

**The Stone-Children**
Not a separate species, but the term used for the youngest generation. They are born into the damp and know nothing else. They are remarkably sure-footed, slipping and sliding with innate grace. They learn to read the crystal growth instinctively, pointing out new filaments to the carvers with a child's excitement. Their games involve balancing on wall-edges, leaping between rooftops, and mapping the ever-changing network of drainage sounds. They are the future Carvers, Weavers, Glimmer-Workers, and Keepers. Their laughter is the Quarter's highest-pitched drip, quick and bright against the constant low whisper.

**The Shadow-Resident**
And then, there is the crystal itself. It is the most persistent inhabitant of all. It is not sentient, but it *behaves*. It seeks light, following the subtle gradients of temperature and moisture. It explores, branching into cracks, following the path of water. It builds, depositing mineral in fractal patterns. It consumes, filling spaces meant for passage. It is the Fourth Wall, the uninvited guest that is also the very fabric of the place. The people do not hate it. They maintain a respectful, weary truce. They speak of it in terms one might use for a powerful river or a harsh winter—a fact of life to be worked with, planned around, and constantly, gently, resisted. In the carvers' dim workshops, in the echo of their tools, and in the delicate, frozen lightning of a crystal branch left momentarily unchipped in an upper wall, the tension and the symbiosis of the Quarter lives on: people and growth, water and stone, carving and being carved, in a slow, damp, eternal dance.
