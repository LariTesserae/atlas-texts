# What Lives Here

## The Weavers

They are not tall. This is not the world for tallness — too much wind-shear on any structure that rises above the canopy, too much surface area exposed to the slow desiccation of the mineral air. They stand roughly a meter and a half at full extension, though they rarely extend fully. Their default posture is slightly stooped, the spine curved forward, the arms held low and loose at their sides, ready. Always ready to touch something, to feel the mat, to read what the vibrations are saying.

Their skin is thick. Not tough in the way of armor — more in the way of bark, or the rind of a fruit. It has a faint greenish undertone, a photosynthetic blush along the backs of the forearms and the tops of the shoulders where light falls most directly. This is not decoration. It is supplementary — a modest contribution to their own caloric budget, a few extra percentages of energy gathered from the perpetual golden light that filters through the woven walls. Their faces are broad and flat, with wide-set eyes the color of wet mineral — amber, grey, sometimes a pale sulfuric yellow. The eyes are adapted to the diffused greenish-gold light of the interior spaces. In the open flats, they narrow to slits. On the mat, in the soft architecture of their cities, they are wide and calm and see everything.

Their hands are their most remarkable feature. Four-fingered, each finger thick at the base and tapering to a broad, sensitive pad — not a claw, not a nail, but a firm pad of dense tissue, ridged like a fingerprint but on a much larger scale. These pads vibrate. Not constantly — only when they choose, when they press a fingertip to the mat or to a wall or to the stem of a growing rosette and send a low-frequency pulse outward through the living substrate. The frequency is below human hearing. You feel it in your jaw, in your wrists, in the fluid of your inner ear. It is how they speak.

Not the only way. They make sound — a soft, subsonic thrumming produced deep in the chest, a vibration of the rib cage that resonates through the floor when they stand on the mat. But this is the public voice, the broadcast — a way of saying *I am here, this is what I mean, everyone within a kilometer can feel it.* For intimacy, for nuance, for the kind of conversation that happens between two of them sitting close in a dim room with the walls breathing slowly around them, there are the hands. They sit facing each other, and they place their hands flat on the mat between them, and they talk in frequencies so low and so subtle that an outside observer would see nothing at all — just two figures, still, hands resting on the living ground, silent as stones.

The conversation would be invisible. But the mat would carry it. The vibrations would travel through the root networks, through the woven walls, through the hollow stems deep below, and if you had the right sensors, you could eavesdrop from a kilometer away — a complex, layered signal, rich in meaning, traveling through the body of the world itself.

They live perhaps eighty years. They age slowly and without drama — a gradual thickening of the skin, a slow calcification of the joints, a dimming of the eyes from sulfur-yellow to a pale, clouded grey. The elders are heavy. Not fat — dense. Their tissue has mineralized over decades, the boundary between their bodies and the mat becoming less distinct. The oldest ones are often found in the deepest parts of the settlement, in the cool chambers below ground, where they spend their days with their hands pressed to the walls, listening to the vapor, feeling the pulse of the deep infrastructure. They are not worshipped, exactly. They are consulted. The mat remembers what they have told it.

## The Beetles

*Matwalkers*, the Weavers call them, though they use no word — they press the meaning into the ground with their hands: the concept of *many-legged, small, careful, belonging to the mat.*

They are everywhere. They are not pests. They are not kept, not domesticated, not managed in any way. They simply are — as much a part of the architecture as the woven walls, as essential to the function of the city as the hollow-stem pipes. They are the maintenance workers of the ecosystem, though they do not know this and would not care if they did.

The largest species is the size of a human fist, as described — a matte-green carapace, segmented, with six legs that move in a slow, deliberate tripod gait. Each footfall is placed with extraordinary precision, the broad pads of their feet making full contact with the surface of the mat before the weight shifts forward. They never stumble. They never crack a leaf. They navigate the dense, complex topography of the rosette-field the way a river navigates stones — finding the path of least resistance, flowing around obstacles with a patience that seems geological.

They feed on dead tissue. The underside of the mat — the compressed layers of organic substrate — is their domain. They tunnel through it, aerating the dead layers, accelerating decomposition, turning old growth into the rich, dark substrate that the living mat above feeds on. A healthy mat has a healthy beetle population, and the Weavers know this. They do not interfere. When a beetle emerges onto the surface, crossing a plaza or climbing the wall of a building, it is ignored — not out of indifference, but out of respect. The beetle knows where it is going. It always does.

Smaller species fill every other niche. Mites that feed on fungal analogs in the substrate. Tiny, fast-moving arthropods with translucent bodies that hunt the even tinier organisms in the uppermost layer of the mat. These are the ones that the wingless birds eat — a constant, silent predation happening at the scale of millimeters, the bird's beak probing the crevices between leaves with mechanical precision, extracting prey that has no chance of escape.

## The Gliders

The slug-amphibian creatures. The Weavers have a word for them too — the vibration means something like *wet-ones* or *glistening-walkers*, and it carries an undertone of affection.

They are the most visible fauna. Wherever you walk on the mat, within minutes you will see one — a long, low body, limbless, moving with a slow peristaltic rippling that is oddly beautiful. Their skin is smooth, pale, opalescent — shifting between grey and a faint lavender depending on the light. They leave a trail of glistening mucus on every surface they cross, and this mucus is not waste. It is a gift. The mucus is rich in nitrogen compounds that the mat cannot easily extract from mineral vapor alone. The gliders are fertilizers. They crawl across the surface of the world, eating and excreting, eating and excreting, and the mat grows thicker and faster in their wake.

They are not stupid. They have eyes — small, dark, set wide apart — and they respond to disturbance. A footstep near one will cause it to pause, to flatten itself against the leaf it is crossing, to become very still. If the disturbance passes, it resumes. If it does not, the glider will change direction, flowing off the leaf and down into the crevice between rosettes, disappearing into the dense interior of the mat with a slow, determined undulation.

At night — the brief, warm, dim hours when the sun drops below the haze and the light turns amber and then deep orange before fading to a darkness that is never quite total, because the mat itself emits a faint bioluminescent glow from its deepest layers — the gliders emerge in numbers. Hundreds of them, crossing the surface of the mat in all directions, their trails catching the low light and shining like veins of silver. They are mating, feeding, migrating short distances from one patch of productive mat to another. The Weavers do not walk on the mat at night. Not out of fear. Out of deference. The night belongs to the gliders.

## The Ground-Dwellers

The birds-that-are-not-birds. Wingless, or nearly — vestigial forelimbs that serve only for balance during the quick, hopping gait that carries them through the packed vegetation with startling speed.

They are small. The largest species is about the size of a human hand. Their plumage is soft, dense, in colors that match the mat — green-grey, bronze, occasionally a deep red that mimics the older tissue of senescent rosettes. They are almost invisible when still. They freeze often. They are prey, though by what, it is unclear — perhaps the larger species of matwalker beetle, perhaps the thing in the deep mat.

Their most extraordinary characteristic is their communication. They press their bodies against the surface of the mat — belly-down, legs splayed, beak touching the vegetation — and they vibrate. A deep, low-frequency pulse, generated by muscular contractions of the torso, transmitted through the mat in all directions. The vibration carries information: territory, availability of food, warning, mating display. Other ground-dwellers, hundreds of meters away, feel the signal and respond. A conversation in tremor, conducted across the vast flat world, invisible to any observer without instruments sensitive enough to detect what is happening.

The Weavers listen to them. The birds' signals sometimes change before environmental shifts — before a fog-cycle, before a subsurface pressure release that will crack open a new mineral flat. The Weavers have learned to read the birds' vibrations the way seafarers once read the wind. A sudden agitation in the ground-dweller network — rapid, staccato pulses, the avian equivalent of an alarm call — means that something is moving in the deep mat. The Weavers respond by thinning the architecture at the margins, by opening passages, by making room.

They do not fear the deep thing. They accommodate it.

## The Deep One

No one has seen it clearly. Not the Weavers, who have lived here for generations beyond counting. Not the gliders, who avoid its territory. Not even the ground-dwellers, whose subsonic communications become agitated and erratic in its presence.

What is known:

It is large. The tremors it produces — slow, rolling, like a distant earthquake that never quite arrives — suggest a body mass that defies the carrying capacity of the mat. Yet it moves. It moves and the mat yields and the mat closes behind it and the mat grows back, and in its wake the substrate is richer, denser, more fertile, as if its passage is a form of cultivation.

It lives in the oldest parts of the basin, where the organic substrate is meters thick, where the root networks are dense enough to function as a single interconnected nervous system. The Weavers believe — and their belief is not faith but something closer to empirical observation, accumulated over centuries of hand-to-mat listening — that the deep one is connected to the mat. Not merely *in* it. Part of it. A mobile organ of a continental organism. A digestive system, perhaps, or a circulatory pump, or something for which no analogous function exists in familiar biology.

When it passes near the settlements, the walls of the buildings flex. Not dramatically — a millimeter of movement, a slow breathing-in and breathing-out that is different from the usual respiratory rhythm of the living architecture. The Weavers feel it. They go still. They place their hands on the walls or the floor and they listen, and what they hear — what the mat tells them — is something like contentment. Something like a vast, slow satisfaction, the feeling of a body doing what a body does, unaware of the small creatures that have built their lives in the spaces between its movements.

The deep one has never harmed a settlement. It has never been observed to interact with the Weavers or any other fauna. It exists on a different timescale — its movements measured in days or weeks, its responses calibrated to processes that unfold over seasons. It is not a god. It is not a threat. It is simply the largest thing in the world, and the world was built around it the way a pearl is built around a grain of sand.

## What They Share

The thing that connects all of them — Weavers, beetles, gliders, ground-dwellers, the deep one, the mat itself — is the mat. It is the medium of communication, the substrate of life, the infrastructure of the city, the floor and the wall and the roof. Every organism here is, in some sense, an extension of it. The Weavers' supplementary photosynthesis. The gliders' fertilizing trails. The beetles' decomposition tunnels. The ground-dwellers' tremor-communication. The deep one's cultivation passes. All of it feeds the mat, and the mat feeds all of them, and the mineral vapor rises from below and the golden light falls from above and the system turns, slowly, patiently, without water, without hurry, without end.

They are not a civilization in the way a visitor might recognize. There is no writing, because meaning is carried in vibration and vibration requires no recording. There is no visible hierarchy, because the Weavers distribute themselves through the mat the way leaves distribute themselves on a tree — each one finding the light it needs, the space it needs, the density of neighbors it needs. There are no armies, no flags, no monuments. The buildings grow and are reclaimed. The cities spread and are reabsorbed. The generations turn like the slow folding of the mat itself — new growth on old growth on older growth, layer upon layer, the whole vast basin a single living record of every life that has passed through it.

And the silence holds it all together. The profound, deep, warm silence of a world that has no need to shout — because every footstep is felt, every vibration is heard, every whisper of meaning travels through the body of the living ground to wherever it needs to go.

You could live here. You could place your hands on the mat and feel the world talking to itself. You could listen long enough to begin to understand — not the words, because there are no words, but the patterns, the rhythms, the slow deep pulse of a place that has found another way to be alive.
