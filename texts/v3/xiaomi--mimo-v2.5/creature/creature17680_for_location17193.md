# The People of Veth Kaal

## What They Are

They are not remarkable. That is the first and most important thing to understand about them, and it is the thing that a visitor would be tempted to deny, because everything about the plateau seems remarkable, and so its inhabitants must surely be remarkable too. But they are not. They are ordinary people — small-bodied, quiet-voiced, brown-skinned from generations of living under a light that is mild but constant — who happen to exist in a place that has forgotten how to change.

They are few, and they are still. Not still in the way of people posing for a moment, holding themselves in place. Still in the way of the stone — a stillness that is not performed but structural, written into the way they carry their weight, the way their hands move, the way their eyes rest on things. They do not fidget. They do not pace. They do not gesture broadly when they speak, because they speak rarely enough that the words themselves suffice. Watching them move through the basin-paths between buildings, you might think they are moving slowly, but that isn't quite right either. They are moving at the correct speed. There is nowhere to get to. The pool they are walking toward will be there when they arrive, filling at the same rate it has always filled, and it will be there when they arrive tomorrow, and the day after that, and on every day that comes, which are all the same day.

Their faces are open and unhurried. There is a quality of attention in them that is different from alertness. Alertness implies danger or novelty — the possibility that something might happen that requires response. Nothing here requires response. The attention of the people of Veth Kaal is more like the attention of a person watching rain on a window — present, gentle, not expecting anything, but unwilling to look away because the thing itself is sufficient. They look at you, if you are a visitor, with this quality. It is not unfriendly. It is not warm. It is simply thorough. They are seeing you completely, the way they see everything, because there is nothing else to do with sight.

## How They Live

They live in the present tense, but not in the way that phrase is usually meant — not in the way of people who have learned to be mindful, who have practiced awareness of the moment as a discipline. They live in the present tense because there is no other tense available to them. The past is not different from the present. Their grandparents' world was this world. The pools held the same water. The same birds called from the same elevations on the same cliff faces. The same flowers, half-open, stood at the same heights above the basin floors. There is a past, in the sense that there were once people who are no longer here, but the world they inhabited is indistinguishable from the world that exists now. History, for these people, is not a story of events. It is a list of names.

And the future will be this. The water will come. The plants will grow, at exactly this rate, to exactly this size. The birds will call. The stone will hold. There is nothing to prepare for, nothing to plan toward, nothing to build toward. The buildings are built. The paths are worn. The gardens are established. A child born today will see exactly what its parent saw, and its parent's parent, and will see it with the same eyes, because the light does not change and the stone does not change and the quality of the dripping after rain does not change.

This sounds like it should be a kind of torment. It is not. The people of Veth Kaal are not suffering. They are not yearning. They are not trapped. They simply are, in the way that the moss in the basins simply is, in the way that the limestone simply is. To call their existence a prison is to assume that they desire something other than what they have, and there is no evidence that they do. Desire requires the concept of alternatives. These people have none. The world is the world. It has always been this way. It will always be this way. This is not a belief they hold or a philosophy they have adopted. It is the water they swim in.

## The Knowledge They Carry

Because nothing changes, everything is known.

This is not an exaggeration. In a world where the conditions are permanent, where the same insects visit the same flowers at the same times, where the same pools fill and drain and refill in the same patterns, where the same birds nest in the same cracks at the same elevations — knowledge becomes total. Not in the way of a scholar who has read every book, but in the way of a hand that knows the shape of a stone it has held ten thousand times. The people of Veth Kaal know their world with a completeness that would be impossible in a place where things shifted. They know every pool on the plateau — not approximately, but precisely. They know which ones fill first after rain, which ones are fed by seeps that never dry, which ones hold the mineral-heavy water that tastes of stone, which ones are home to specific crustaceans or larvae. They know this because the pools have been the same pools, in the same positions, holding the same water, for as long as anyone can remember.

They know the birds. Not their names — or rather, not names that describe species in the way a naturalist would categorize them. They have words for individual birds, or for specific calls in specific locations. There is a word for the single sharp note that echoes off the northeast cliff face in the third basin. There is a word for the low, repeating tone that comes from the overhang above the deepest tree. These are not poetic appellations. They are functional references. When a person says one of these words, they are pointing at a specific, permanent feature of the soundscape — as fixed and reliable as the stone itself.

They know the insects. They know which species will be found in which square meter of ground-cover. They know which ones are active at which hours — though the hours repeat identically each day, so this knowledge, too, is permanent. They know the ones that are beautiful, the ones that are useful, the ones that sting, the ones that taste particular ways. They have words for all of them, and the words are old, and they are the same words that were old when their grandparents' grandparents spoke them, because the insects have not changed and so the words have not needed to change.

This knowledge is not accumulated across generations in the usual sense — each generation learning what the previous generation learned, adding to it, refining it. There is nothing to add. There is nothing to refine. The knowledge is simply passed, like an object handed from one hand to another, unchanged. A child learns the name of the sharp-note-bird-on-the-northeast-cliff from its parent, who learned it from its parent, who learned it from its parent, and the name has not altered, and the bird has not altered, and the cliff face has not altered, and so the knowledge is the same, handed down like a stone that never erodes.

What this produces is a people who are profoundly competent within their world and profoundly limited beyond it. They know everything about the plateau. They know nothing about anything else. There is no curiosity about the lowlands, about other places, about how other people live. Not because curiosity has been suppressed, but because the concept of *elsewhere* has no traction. The plateau is the world. The world is the plateau. Everything that exists is here, and everything here is known, and so there is nothing to wonder about. Wonder requires a gap between what is known and what might be known. There are no gaps.

## The Rhythm of Their Days

There is no urgency in anything they do, because there is no consequence to timing. The condensation pools will be there whether you collect them now or later. The gardens will grow whether you tend them today or tomorrow. The bird will call whether you listen or not. So the people move through their days without schedule, without plan, without the internal pressure that comes from the awareness that time is passing and things must be done before they cannot be done. Things will always be able to be done. The same things. In the same way.

They rise when the condensation begins to form, which is always at the same time, because the conditions that produce it are always the same. They step out of their low doorways into the cool air — the same air, the same coolness, the same thinness — and they stand for a moment, not doing anything, just being in the presence of the morning, which is always the same morning. Then they move, quietly, to the pools, or to the gardens, or to the gathering shelters, and they do what they do, which is always what they do.

Some of them sit in the gathering shelters and work with their hands. They are not building or creating. They are maintaining. A woven mat that will be used for carrying harvested plants is repaired when a strand loosens, but it never needs to be replaced, because it is only used in the same way, carrying the same weight, over the same paths, and so it wears at exactly the rate that repairs can keep up with. A shallow basin in the shelter floor, used for holding collected water, develops a crack — and the crack is filled with a paste of ground limestone and moisture, and it holds, and it will hold for as long as it held before, which is as long as it needs to. The tools they use are the same tools. The copper implements — a few, old, their edges maintained — are passed from hand to hand as tasks shift between people, and they are the same copper implements that were passed from hand to hand in exactly this way, for exactly these tasks, for as long as anyone can recall.

The gardens in the basins are tended, but not cultivated. The ground-cover grows where it grows. The broad-leaved stalks rise where they rise. The pale flowers open to their permanent half-degree where they open. The people do not plant or prune or shape. They weed — or rather, they remove the occasional plant that has grown in a place where it shouldn't be, where it threatens to choke a space that other plants have occupied since before memory. This is the closest thing to a decision the people of Veth Kaal make on a daily basis: this plant stays, that plant goes. And even this is not really a decision. It is a maintenance of a pattern that has been maintained forever, by people doing exactly what these people are doing, in exactly this way, with exactly the same judgment.

Children play. This is important, because children are the one element that changes. Each child is new. Each child is a genuine novelty in a world that produces no other novelties. They are small, quick, curious in the way that children everywhere are curious, and their curiosity is the only thing in Veth Kaal that moves forward rather than cycling. A child does not know the name of the sharp-note-bird-on-the-northeast-cliff and must learn it. A child does not know which pools are fed by seeps and must be shown. A child does not know that the translucent amphibian on the cliff face is called "the stone's breath" and must be told. In this learning — this slow, gentle, repetitive process of being given the world as it is — the children are the only inhabitants of Veth Kaal who experience something like change. For them, the world expands. Knowledge accumulates. Names are added. The basins become known, one by one, in a sequence that has no endpoint but that eventually terminates in totality, when the child has learned everything that there is to learn, and becomes, like everyone else, a person who lives in a world that is complete.

The playing of children is the most energetic thing in Veth Kaal. They chase each other between the buildings. They climb on the low walls of the basins. They peer into the pools and watch the tiny crustaceans move. They hold their hands under the drips from overhangs and let the water strike their palms and count the drops or listen to the sounds. They are the only people who run. By the time they are old enough to be considered young adults, the running has stopped. The urgency has passed. The world has been learned, and the learning has settled into them like sediment into the pools, and they are still.

## How They Treat Each Other

There is no conflict, or rather, there is no conflict that lasts. There may be moments of friction — a hand reaching for the same copper tool, a child taking a plant from a garden spot that another person was tending — but these resolve instantly, not through negotiation or compromise, but through the simple, gravitational logic of a place where nothing is scarce and nothing is urgent. The tool is there. The plant is there. There will always be another moment, and the moment is the same as this moment, so waiting costs nothing. In a world without scarcity and without change, competition has no ground to stand on.

The people are kind to each other, but their kindness has a quality that might be mistaken for indifference by a visitor from elsewhere. They do not embrace. They do not exclaim. They do not rush to help when someone stumbles, because stumbling is a rare and minor event and the person who has stumbled will right themselves in a moment, as they have always righted themselves. Their kindness is expressed in presence — in the quiet fact of being nearby, of being available, of being a familiar body in a familiar place. A person sitting in a gathering shelter, repairing a mat, while another person sits nearby, sorting dried vegetation, is being kind. They are providing the gift of their presence, which in a world this quiet and this still, is not nothing. It is the main thing.

They do not speak to each other often, but they are not silent. They make small sounds — the sound of a hand against stone, the sound of breath, the sound of a word given softly to indicate that something is understood. These sounds are part of the soundscape, as fixed as the bird calls, as predictable as the dripping. A person entering a gathering shelter will make a particular sound — not a greeting, exactly, but an acknowledgment, a way of saying *I am here, I am the same person I was yesterday, the world is the same.* And someone already in the shelter will make a responding sound, which says the same thing.

They eat simply. The broad-leaved plants produce something that can be gathered and chewed — not a fruit, exactly, but a thick, moisture-heavy leaf base that provides sustenance. The insects are eaten by some, roasted in the shallow basins near the communal fires, which are kept burning at a low, permanent smolder in the deepest shelters. The water they drink is the mineral water from the seep-fed pools, and they drink it without noticing its taste, the way everyone drinks the water they grew up with. They do not feast. They do not fast. They eat when they are hungry, and they are hungry on the same cycle, because their bodies have adjusted to the same inputs over so many generations that the rhythm of hunger is as fixed as the rhythm of condensation.

They sleep in their buildings, in the low rooms, on mats of woven plant fiber, covered with more of the same fiber when the air cools at night — which it always does, to the same degree, at the same hour. The sleep is deep and dreamless and lasts the same number of hours every night. They wake at the same time every morning, which is the time when the condensation begins to form, and they step out into the same air, and the day begins again, which is to say, yesterday continues.

## The One Who Sits at the Edge

There is a person — and there is always this person, in every generation, though the individual changes — who sits at the edge of the plateau where the switchback paths begin their descent. This person does not block the path. They sit on a low ledge of stone, slightly to the side, their feet on the first step down. They are doing nothing. They are watching the path. They are waiting for nothing, because there is nothing to wait for, and yet the waiting has become their role, passed from person to person like the copper tools, like the names of the birds.

When a visitor comes — which is rare, which might be years apart, which might be a decade or more — this person is the first to see them. They watch the visitor climb the switchbacks, growing larger against the pale rock, struggling slightly in the thin air. They watch the visitor arrive on the plateau and stand, breathing hard, looking around at the basins and the sharp ridges and the strange, flat-canopied trees. The person on the ledge does not rise. They do not call out. They wait until the visitor notices them, which might take a long time, because the person blends with the stone — their skin is the same pale tone, their clothes are undyed plant fiber, their stillness is the stillness of the rock itself.

When the visitor approaches, the person on the ledge speaks. They say something simple — a word or two that functions as welcome, though it doesn't translate as welcome. It translates more closely as *the world is as it was.* This is not a warning or a greeting. It is a statement of fact. It is the first thing a visitor hears, and it sets the terms of everything that follows. The world is as it was. Do not expect it to be otherwise.

The visitor is then led — slowly, without haste, along the paths that wind between buildings and basins — into the town. The person on the ledge walks ahead, and the visitor walks behind, and neither speaks. The sound of footsteps on stone is the only sound added to the soundscape, and it fades quickly, as all sounds do here, absorbed by the quiet and the rock.

The visitor is given a room in one of the buildings near the center of the settlement. The room is simple: a low ceiling, a mat on the floor, a shallow basin in the corner that fills slowly with condensation through a crack in the wall. The door is narrow and low, and when it is closed, the room is dark and cool and smells of limestone and the faint mineral tang of standing water. The visitor can stay as long as they wish. No one will ask them to leave. No one will ask them why they came. No one will ask them anything at all, because the questions would require answers, and the answers would require that the world be different from what it is, and it is not.

Some visitors stay for a day and leave, unsettled by the quiet, by the sameness, by the feeling of being in a painting that is painting itself over and over. Some stay for a week, trying to find the crack in the surface, the place where the constancy breaks, the hidden mechanism that produces the sameness. There is no mechanism. Some stay longer — weeks, months — and begin, slowly, to adjust. Their footsteps become quieter. Their movements become less abrupt. They stop looking for the change and begin to see what is there, which is everything, which has always been everything. A few stay permanently, becoming part of the population, learning the names of the birds and the pools, learning to sit still for long hours, learning to see the translucent amphibian on the cliff face — the stone's breath — and to leave it undisturbed.

## The Absence They Carry

There is one grief in Veth Kaal, and it is the only grief there is, and it is enormous.

People die.

In a world where nothing else changes — where the pools persist, where the plants persist, where the birds persist, where the stone persists — the death of a person is the one event that cannot be undone. It is the one crack in the constancy. A person is here, and then the person is not here, and the space where the person was is a space that nothing fills. The building they occupied remains. The mat they slept on remains. The pool they collected water from remains. But the person does not remain, and the absence is visible in a way that it would not be visible in a place where things changed, because in a changing place, absence is masked by the flux — things move, things shift, things are different tomorrow, and so the gap left by a dead person is gradually absorbed into the general movement. Here, there is no movement. The gap stays. It is the same gap, in the same place, forever.

The people of Veth Kaal do not mourn in the way that people elsewhere mourn. There are no rituals of grief, no periods of visible sorrow. When a person dies, the others who knew them — which is everyone, since everyone knows everyone, and has known everyone's family for generations — continue as they have always continued. The morning comes. The condensation forms. The pools fill. The birds call. The dead person's name is spoken sometimes, in the same context it was always spoken in — *that pool where Eshaa used to collect water* — and the name hangs in the air for a moment, and then the moment passes, and the pool remains, and the water is the same water, and the dripping is the same dripping.

But there is a weight to them, to the people, that a visitor might eventually notice. A heaviness beneath the stillness. It is not sadness, exactly. It is something more structural. It is the weight of knowing that they are the only temporary things in a permanent world. The stone will last. The pools will last. The birds will call. But they will not last. They will be here, and then they will not be here, and the world will continue exactly as it was, undisturbed by their absence, undisturbed by anyone's absence. They are the only things that change, and the change is always the same — they arrive, they live, they die — and the arrival and the living and the dying take place against a backdrop of absolute, unyielding permanence that will outlast them completely and forget them entirely, because the stone does not remember and the pools do not remember and the birds do not remember.

This weight does not make them unhappy. They have never known anything else. They carry it the way they carry everything — quietly, without comment, integrated into the fabric of their days. But it is there. It is in the way they look at each other — that thorough, unhurried attention, that seeing. It is in the way they touch the stone when they pass a wall, resting a hand on it briefly, as though confirming something. It is in the way they sit with the dead person's building left exactly as it was, door closed, mat undisturbed, basin still slowly filling with condensation through the crack in the wall, for days or weeks before someone enters and takes the mat and the basin and adds them to their own collection, and the building sits empty, and the emptiness is permanent, and the mat is now in a different building, and the basin is now in a different basin, and the dead person is now only a name, and the name will eventually be spoken less often, and then not at all, and the world will not notice, because the world does not notice.

## What They Are, In the End

They are the living part of a place that has otherwise finished living. They are the motion in a motionless world, the breath in a stone's stillness. They are born into permanence and they die out of it, and in between, they do what there is to do — they tend the pools, they watch the birds, they know the names, they sit in the shelters and repair the mats and wait for the rain and listen to the dripping and feel the cool air on their skin and exist, fully and completely, in a world that will not remember them.

They are not tragic. They are not heroic. They are not strange. They are people, living in a place, and the place is what it is, and they are what they are, and the two things fit together as tightly as the limestone layers in the cliff face — cream and ash and faintly blue, thin and regular, pages of a book left open to the sky, telling the same story, on every page, forever.
