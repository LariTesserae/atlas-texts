# What Lives on the Resin Plateau

Nothing you would recognize as alive.

The green is alive, but not in the way that *alive* is usually meant. It does not startle. It does not hide. It does not compete. What lives here has never needed to do any of the things that living things elsewhere have learned to do in order to keep living, because the conditions that require adaptation have been suspended along with everything else.

The great ferns are the closest thing to individuals. Each one is massive — their fronds, thick as ship timber and translucent at the edges where the steam light passes through them, unfold in slow radial fans from trunks that are really columns of compressed cellulose, wet and hot to the touch, exuding a sap that is not resin but behaves like it: slow, amber, faintly sticky, smelling of crushed minerals and something older than smell. They do not sway. There is no wind. They have no reason to sway. They hold their fronds at the exact angle at which they were holding them when the pause began, and they will hold them at that angle forever, and this is not restraint or dormancy — it is completeness. They are finished. They are exactly as grown as they intend to be, and the process of photosynthesis still occurs in their tissues, still converts light to sugar, still fills their cells with the chemistry of growth, but the growth goes nowhere. The energy has no destination. It accumulates. It saturates. The ferns are *full*, in the way a held breath is full, and the fullness is their permanent condition.

Between and beneath them, the mosses form continent.

Not individual mosses — that distinction doesn't apply here. The moss is a single continuous body, a living skin that covers every surface the ferns don't claim, and it is old in a way that makes the word meaningless. It has been growing — or rather, it has been *continuing* — for so long that it has become the texture of the plateau itself. You cannot see where the moss ends and the stone begins because the moss has infiltrated the stone, has filled its pores, has become mineral in its lower strata and biological in its upper ones, a gradient from rock to life that has no seam. Touch it and your hand comes away wet and faintly warm, as though you have touched something with a body temperature, which you have. The moss maintains a heat that is slightly above the ambient already-saturated air, a differential so small it should be meaningless, but it is consistent, and it is everywhere, and it means the moss is *doing* something — burning its stored energy in a slow, purposeless combustion that produces no motion and no change but does produce warmth, a faint and useless warmth that goes nowhere and is absorbed by the steam and dispersed and generated again, forever, a cycle that exists solely to be a cycle.

The fungal networks are the ones you should be afraid of, if fear were appropriate here.

They are beneath everything. They are the connective tissue of the plateau — not metaphorically, not in the way that people elsewhere speak loosely of networks and connections and webs, but literally, physically, in the way that a nervous system is literal. The mycelia thread through the moss-bed, through the compressed calcium of the towers, through the peat and the mineral soil and the channels of the streams. They are the reason the city looks like a city: the towers rise where the fungal density is highest, because the fungi *build* the towers, extruding calcium and organic compounds in patterns that are architectural in their precision. The arches are their idea. The domes are their idea. The doorways — the dark openings that lead into interiors you have not entered and should not enter — are their idea. The fungi are not intelligent. They do not think. But they do *pattern*, and the patterns they produce at this density and this timescale have become indistinguishable from design. They are the planners without the plan, the architects without intention, and the city they have grown is a monument to what complexity looks like when it has been allowed to iterate without interruption for longer than any living system was meant to endure.

And then there is the bacteria.

The steam is alive. Not with visible life — with bacterial life, with colonies so dense they form a kind of atmospheric ecology, a cloud of microorganisms suspended in the perpetual moisture, breathing and reproducing and dying and being reabsorbed in cycles so rapid that the population is in constant renewal even as nothing else changes. The bacteria are the only things on the plateau that still *move* in any meaningful sense. They drift. They divide. They are the one process that the pause did not fully arrest, perhaps because they are too small for the pause to grip, or perhaps because the pause was never meant to hold them — perhaps they are the mechanism by which the holding is maintained, the constant low-level activity that prevents the stillness from becoming true stillness, the way a humming motor prevents a structure from settling into the exact frequency that would shake it apart. You breathe them in. You have been breathing them in since you arrived. They taste of warm stone and deep water and something faintly sweet that is not sugar but is the memory of sugar, the chemical echo of a metabolism that forgot its purpose but not its taste.

Together — ferns, moss, fungi, bacteria — they form something.

Not an organism. Not a community. Not an ecosystem in any functional sense, because there are no exchanges, no pressures, no adaptations, no evolution, no death that leads to renewal, no predation, no symbiosis that requires negotiation. There is only *presence*. The green things are present. The fungal networks are present. The bacterial clouds are present. They exist in each other's company the way notes exist in a chord — simultaneously, without sequence, producing a resonance that is not music and not silence but something for which the word *hum* might work if *hum* could be scaled to the size of a plateau and loaded with the weight of geological time.

They are aware of you the way a mountain is aware of weather.

This is not metaphor. The moss beneath your feet is reacting to your weight — not by compressing, because it is already fully compressed, but by redistributing its internal moisture around the pressure point, a micro-adjustment that occurs at the cellular level and takes hours to complete and will reverse hours after you leave. The fungi are registering your biochemistry — the CO₂ you exhale, the heat your body radiates, the salts in your sweat — and filing it into their pattern-making the way they file everything, as data without a hypothesis, as input without an output. The bacteria on your skin are reproducing. Your body is, to them, a new territory, warm and wet and rich with nutrients, and they are colonizing it with the same dispassionate efficiency with which they colonize everything, and they will continue whether you stay or go, and it will not matter, and you will carry them with you when you leave, and you will be their only vector, their only movement through the world beyond the plateau, their only chance to go somewhere that is not here, and you will not know this, and they will not know this, and nothing will come of it, because nothing comes of anything here, because here nothing *becomes*.

But the question is not really what lives on the plateau.

The question is what *holds* it.

Because the pause is not natural. The brokenness of reality — the wrong perspective, the shadows without sources, the water that flows in all directions, the impossible altitude that breeds equatorial excess — this is not a condition. It is an *event*, and the event is still happening, has always been happening, is frozen at the exact moment of its occurring. Something *did* this. Something broke the world here and then held the break in place, and the holding is the roar, the constant deafening roar that is not sound but is the *audible texture of a force that will not release*. The plateau is a wound being held open. The city is the scar tissue growing at its edges. The vegetation is the body's attempt to heal, endlessly attempted and endlessly arrested.

Whatever did this is not here in the way that living things are here. It is here in the way that gravity is here — present as a condition, felt in its effects, invisible in its substance. It may be the plateau itself. It may be the broken physics, the shattered perspective, the reality that refused to settle. It may be something that was once alive and became the pause, became the holding, became the effort that the roar is the sound of. You will not find it by looking. You will not find it by staying. You will find it only by noticing that you cannot leave — not that you are trapped, but that the concept of *leaving* does not apply, because the plateau is not a place you have come to but a place that has happened to you, the way a dream happens to a sleeper, and you are not standing on it but *in* it, held with everything else, mid-breath, mid-process, mid-becoming, paused at the exact moment where complexity was at its most intricate and beauty was at its most terrible and the effort of maintaining both was at its most unsustainable and most absolute.

The ferns do not need you. The moss does not need you. The fungi have already measured you and found you irrelevant. The bacteria will use you and forget you.

You are not a visitor here. You are a fluctuation. A brief and local increase in complexity in a place that has too much complexity already and cannot spend any of it. You will leave and the plateau will not change, and the place where you stood will be exactly as wet and warm and full and still as every other place, and the green will continue, and the roar will continue, and the water will continue to arrive from above and pool downward in its impossible rain, and the light will continue to fall with its one faithful ordinariness on the most extraordinary stillness in the world, and nothing will have happened, because nothing ever does, because here nothing ever *will*.
