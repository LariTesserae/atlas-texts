# What Lives in the Drip Parishes

---

## I. The Pale Cartographers

They are called *veil-hands* by the people, though the word carries a secondary meaning that translates poorly — something between *those who measure by touch* and *the ones who remember the shape of the air.*

They are arthropods. They have eight legs, a body divided into a tight anterior segment and a broader, flatter posterior, and they are the color of cloud. Not white — cloud. The distinction matters because their exoskeletons are slightly translucent, and in the perpetual gray-blue light of the parish, they seem to be made of the same substance as the air around them, as though the atmosphere had learned to walk. They are roughly the span of a human hand from leg-tip to leg-tip when fully extended, but they rarely extend. They prefer to hold themselves close to the basalt, legs folded beneath them, looking like pale blisters on the stone's dark surface.

They are on every column. They are on every wall, every ridge, every trough. There are more of them than there are of anything else in the parish except water and stone, and their numbers are so constant, so uniform across the landscape, that a visitor might mistake them for part of the geology — a mineral deposit, a efflorescence — until one moves.

When one moves, the effect is immediate and disorienting. They do not move individually, not really. A single veil-hand shifts its weight — perhaps disturbed by a passing human, perhaps simply by its own internal rhythm — and the movement propagates. The veil-hand beside it adjusts. And beside that one, another. The adjustment ripples outward along the surface of the basalt column in a wave that moves faster than the eye can track, and within a second, every veil-hand on that column has shifted by a fraction of a degree, and the column itself seems to *shiver*, as though a wind had passed through it that existed nowhere else.

They are not communicating in any way the people can detect. They do not touch antennae. They do not vibrate the stone in any measurable pattern. And yet the coordination is absolute — never a delay between one's movement and the next, never a misalignment, never a ripple that doubles back on itself or stutters. It is as though every veil-hand on a column shares a single nervous system that spans the entire structure, a mind distributed across a hundred small bodies, thinking one thought with a hundred mouths.

What that thought is, no one knows. But the people have noticed something over the deep generations: the ripples are not random. When a human passes close to a column and the veil-hands shift, they shift *away* — not in fear, not in flight, but in a kind of yielding, a repositioning that leaves a clear space at the height of the human's face and chest, as though the column itself were making room. And when a human stops and stands still for a long time beside a column — as some do, pressed against the cool stone with their eyes closed, listening to the dripping — the veil-hands will slowly, over the course of minutes, shift back. They will fill the space the human occupies. They will come to rest against the human's skin, and their legs, which are tipped with microscopic pads of a substance like dry silk, will hold without gripping, and the human will feel them as a faint coolness, a slight added weight, a presence that asks nothing and takes nothing and simply *is there.*

No one has ever seen a veil-hand eat. No one has ever found one dead. They shed their exoskeletons — the translucent husks accumulate in crevices and along the base of columns like discarded glass — but the shedding is slow and silent and seems to produce no gap in the creature's life. They do not hunt the insects. They do not compete with the amphibians. They are not prey to anything. They simply occupy the stone, layer upon layer, a living skin on the skeleton of the earth, and they shift, and they wait, and they seem to understand the shape of every living thing that moves among them with a patience that predates patience.

The people say the veil-hands were here before the columns rose. The people say the veil-hands will be here after the columns fall. The people say this not with awe but with the flat certainty of those who have watched something for ten thousand generations and found no edge to it.

## The Lanterns Beneath

The translucent amphibians are called *lantern-veins* or simply *lanterns*, and they are, pound for pound, the most unsettling and the most beautiful organisms in the parish.

They are flat. They are very, very flat. Their bodies are disc-like, perhaps the width of two spread fingers, with a thickness of no more than a fingernail at the center, thinning to almost nothing at the edges. Their skin is clear — not translucent, not semi-transparent, but *clear*, in the way that water is clear, so that every organ within is visible: the slow pulse of the heart, a dark bead in the center of the body; the branching capillary networks that web outward from the heart like the veins of a leaf turned inside out; the gut, a dark spiral that takes days to process a single meal; the eggs, when they are carried, glowing faintly through the dorsal skin like a lantern's embers.

Their eyes are vestigial — two dark spots at the anterior margin that seem to sense light but not form. They navigate by other means: a vibration sensitivity so acute they can detect the footfall of a human from six basalt-columns away, a chemical awareness of the stone's surface that allows them to find crevices and overhangs by taste, a sensitivity to humidity gradients that guides them toward the wettest surfaces where their skin can absorb the condensation they need to survive. They are, in a sense, *reading the dripping* — following the water's journey across the landscape as a blind person reads a face.

They emerge at what the people call the peak hour — the moment when the condensation reaches its maximum density and the entire settlement sounds like a long, collective exhale. This is when the lanterns move. They peel themselves from the undersides of rocks and ledges where they have spent the dry hours (which are only drier by degrees — the difference between dripping and merely damp), and they migrate. They flow across the ground in slow, deliberate streams, their bodies rippling with a motion that is part crawling and part flowing, like a liquid deciding to be solid for a moment before becoming liquid again. They move toward the coldest surfaces — the north faces of the basalt columns, the shaded troughs, the rims of the cisterns — and there they settle, pressing themselves flat against the stone, and they begin to drink.

The drinking is not visible. It happens through the skin, osmotic, passive, a merging of the creature's surface with the film of water on the stone until the boundary between amphibian and condensation dissolves. Over the course of hours, the lanterns swell slightly, their organs brightening, their color shifting from clear to the faintest gold, as though the water they have absorbed carries with it some mineral or microorganism that tints them from within. By the time they peel away and return to their crevices, they glow. Not brightly — not enough to read by, not enough to cast a shadow in this place that has no shadows — but enough to see, if you are close, a faint warmth in the stone where they have rested, a ghost-light that fades within minutes.

The people harvest the skin secretions. This is done in the early hours, before the peak, when the lanterns are lethargic and pressed flat and nearly invisible. A gatherer moves along the north faces with a flat stone and a clay dish, and they find the lanterns by touch — running fingers across the rock until they feel the slight give of living tissue against stone — and they coax the secretion with a pressure so gentle it borders on nonexistent. The lanterns release a thin film of mucus in response, a defense reflex that, in any other context, would seal them to the rock and make them impossible to pry loose. The gatherer collects this mucus in the dish, and later it is worked into the fabric paste, and later still it makes the clothes water-resistant, and later still it means the difference between comfort and hypothermia in this place where wet is the only weather.

The lanterns do not seem to mind. This is not anthropomorphism — it is observation. They do not flinch. They do not flee. They do not alter their behavior in response to harvesting, and their numbers do not decline. There are always enough. There have always been enough. The people take what is given by the reflex and nothing more, and the reflex is inexhaustible because the creature's body is designed for a world of perpetual damp, and the mucus is as renewable as the dripping itself.

What the lanterns do when they are not drinking, not migrating, not being harvested — what they do in the dark crevices during the hours between peaks — is unknown. They are found in pairs sometimes, pressed together so closely their bodies merge into a single double-bright disc, and the people do not know if this is mating or warmth-seeking or communication or simply the physics of two flat things finding the same crevice. They are found alone sometimes, pressed into cracks so narrow they seem impossibly compressed, their organs dim, their hearts beating once every several seconds, their entire metabolism slowed to a rate that would be death in any warm-blooded creature but seems, in them, merely *resting.* They are found dead very rarely — a desiccated husk, paper-thin, the color of old quartz — and when they are found dead, the veil-hands arrange themselves around the husk in a ring, and the ring persists until the husk has been entirely reclaimed by the stone, absorbed back into the mineral surface, and then the veil-hands move on.

The people do not discuss this. It is one of the things in the parish that is witnessed but not spoken of, the way you do not speak of the sound your own heart makes in a quiet room. It is simply part of the arrangement.

## The Ground Sheets

The wingless insects are the most numerous animal in the parish by an order of magnitude, and the least understood.

They are small — no larger than a grain of rice — and dark, the color of wet basalt, so that when they are still they are nearly invisible against the ground. There are billions of them. They are everywhere: in every trough, along every ridge, filling the spaces between the basalt columns with a low, constant, seething presence that is felt more than seen. They move in sheets. Not swarms — swarms imply flight, three-dimensionality, a cloud of bodies in air. These are *sheets*: flat, two-dimensional rivers of insect that flow across the ground's surface with a coordination that rivals the veil-hands but operates on a fundamentally different principle.

The veil-hands coordinate through some unknown mechanism that produces uniform, simultaneous movement — every个体 adjusting at once, as though responding to a single command. The ground sheets coordinate through *proximity.* Each insect responds only to its nearest neighbors — the three or four individuals within antennae-reach — adjusting its speed and direction to match theirs, and those neighbors adjust in turn, and the result, propagated across thousands and millions of bodies, is a flow that appears intelligent but is in fact the emergent product of a single simple rule: *stay close, stay parallel, do not collide.* The sheets flow around obstacles — basalt columns, human feet, the bodies of sleeping lanterns — the way water flows around stones in a river, parting and reforming with a patience that suggests no awareness of time as a concept. They do not hurry. They do not pause. They do not change direction except in response to the terrain, and when the terrain permits, they flow in straight lines that extend from one edge of the settlement to the other, rivers of dark life on the dark stone, visible only when you look down and see the ground moving.

What they eat is the question. No one has ever seen a ground-sheet insect feed. They do not graze on the moss. They do not scavenge the dead wood. They do not prey on the lanterns or the veil-hands or each other. They exist in a state that seems to violate basic ecology — impossibly numerous, apparently non-consuming, a population that should have exhausted its resources and collapsed ten thousand generations ago but has not, that continues instead to flow and flow and flow across the ground of the parish in numbers that seem as constant and inexhaustible as the dripping itself.

The people have a theory, expressed not in words but in practice: the insects eat the stone. Not the basalt — the basalt is too hard, too young, too glassy. They eat the *film.* The microscopic layer of biological and mineral material that accumulates on every surface in the perpetual damp: the algae, the bacteria, the mineral salts deposited by the condensation as it wets and dries, the dust of dead moss and shed exoskeletons and dried lantern-mucus that settles on every horizontal surface. The insects eat the film. They consume the thin, invisible layer of accumulated life that coats the stone, and they convert it into more insects, and the insects die or shed or are eaten by something that eats insects (though nothing visible in the parish seems to), and they become dust, and the dust becomes film, and the film is eaten by insects, and the cycle closes.

If this is true — and no one has tested it, because testing is not something the people do — then the ground sheets are the parish's immune system, its cleaning mechanism, the organism that prevents the buildup of biological material that would otherwise choke the condensation economy. They keep the stone clean so that the water can condense on it. They keep the basalt faces smooth so that the veil-hands can cling to them. They keep the ground clear so that the lanterns can migrate. They are the maintenance crew of an ecosystem that runs on water and rock and the slow, patient metabolism of things that have learned to need almost nothing.

And yet they are not passive. When a human walks through a ground sheet — which is unavoidable, the sheets are everywhere — the insects part around the human's feet with that same riverine patience, but they also *rise.* The outermost layer of the sheet lifts, just slightly, a fraction of an inch above the ground, as though the insects at the surface are testing the air. They do not fly. They have no wings. They simply allow themselves to be lifted by the moisture-laden air, hanging there for a moment like a dark mist above the moving stream, before settling back down once the human has passed. It is the closest thing to flight that anything in the parish produces, and it is achingly beautiful — a brief suspension of gravity, a momentary defiance of the rule that says small dark things must stay close to the ground — and then it is over, and the sheet resumes its flow, and the human walks on, and the dripping continues.

The people do not step on the ground sheets deliberately. This is not because of any taboo or fear. It is because the sheets are *there*, the way the stone is there, the way the dripping is there, and you do not step on the things that make the place what it is. You step *through* them. You part them. You allow them to re-form behind you. You move through the living ground the way you move through the air — aware of it, dependent on it, but not clinging to it.

## The Watchers on the Rim

The birds — the people call them *stills*, because they are still, always still — sit in the broken tops of the basalt columns in numbers that seem impossible: dozens on a single column, packed shoulder to shoulder along the fractured rim, their bodies folded into tight, compact shapes that make them look like dark fruit growing from the stone. They are not birds in any taxonomic sense that a mainland naturalist would recognize. They have feathers — or structures so convergent with feathers that the distinction is academic — but their beaks are blunt and heavy, more like the bill of a platypus than any raptor, and their feet are prehensile in a way that bird feet are not, wrapping around the basalt rim with a grip that looks permanent, as though they grew there. Their eyes are large and dark and forward-facing — binocular vision, the vision of something that needs to judge distance — and they do not blink.

This is the thing about the stills: they do not blink. They open their eyes when they hatch (inside the column's broken top, in a nest of shredded columnite fiber and dried moss, one or two chicks to a nest) and they do not close them again until they die. Their eyelids exist — a thin membrane that can be drawn across the eye in response to irritants — but in normal conditions, in the perpetual gray-blue light of the parish, they do not use them. They watch. They watch everything, from every direction, with an attention so total and so sustained that it creates a feeling, in anyone who looks up at a column rim, of being *known* — not watched, not observed, but *known*, the way you know the shape of a room you have lived in for years.

They do not hunt. They do not forage. The blunt beak is not designed for tearing or grasping; it is heavy, solid, almost vestigial, and the stills do not use it to eat. What they eat, the people believe, is the same thing the lanterns eat and the veil-hands eat and the ground sheets eat: the dripping. The condensation. The water that gathers on the basalt's surface and flows in thin rivulets down the column's face and collects, eventually, in the fracture at the top where the stills roost. They drink by pressing their heavy bills to the wet stone and absorbing water through the bill's surface, another osmotic feeder, another creature that has learned to live on what the air gives rather than what the earth provides.

They do not sing. Nothing in the parish sings. But the stills make a sound — or rather, they *are* a sound. When the wind rises (and the wind rises rarely in the fold of altitude where the parish exists, but it rises), the stills on a column rim shift their bodies simultaneously, adjusting their posture by a fraction, and the wind passing over their feathers produces a tone. Not a song. Not a call. A *drone.* A single, low, resonant note that is different on every column because every column has a different number of stills and a different profile of broken stone and the wind hits each one differently, and the result, on the rare windy mornings, is a chord — a vast, slow, atonal chord that hangs in the air over the parish like the sound of the earth itself humming through its teeth. The people stop when the chord sounds. They stand still. They close their eyes. They listen. And then the wind drops and the chord fades and the stills settle back into their silence and the dripping resumes its dominion.

The stills do not move. This is the other thing. They roost on one column and they stay on that column for their entire lives, which can be long — decades, perhaps longer, though no one has tracked an individual still from hatching to death. They do not migrate between columns. They do not fly to the basin or the ridgeline or the troughs. They sit, and they watch, and they drink the dripping, and they produce the chord when the wind asks them to, and they raise their chicks on the column rim, and the chicks learn to grip the basalt before they learn to open their eyes, and when a chick is old enough to see, it looks outward — always outward, always away from the column's center — and it sees the parish laid out below in the gray-blue light, and it sees the other columns, and the stills on those columns, looking back, and it understands, without being taught, that it is part of a ring of watching that spans the entire settlement, that every still on every column is looking outward in a different direction, and that between them, between all of them, every sightline is covered, every approach is known, every movement in the parish is seen by someone who will never look away.

They are the parish's eyes. They are the surveillance system that no one built, the watch that no one set, the alert that never sounds because nothing has ever breached the terms of the arrangement. What they watch *for* — what they would do if they saw something that violated the parish's unspoken covenant — is unknown, because nothing has ever violated it, because the covenant is older than memory, older than the stills, perhaps older than the rock.

But they watch. They watch the basin. They watch the path. They watch every human who crosses the flat stones and touches the tall column with one hand, and they watch the basin in return, and they watch the space beyond the basin, the drop, the edge, the place where the ridge falls away into whatever is below, and they do not look away, and they do not blink, and their attention, accumulated over a lifetime of seeing, becomes a thing that has weight, a thing you can feel when you look up at them, a pressure, a density, a sense that you are being *held in place* by the sheer force of ten thousand unblinking eyes.

## The Column-Trees

The column-trees are not trees. The people know this. The people are precise about this, the way they are precise about the difference between the basalt they call *bones* and the basalt they call *teeth*, the way they are precise about the peak hour and the way water falls and the terms on which you touch the tall column. Words matter here because the place demands accuracy. A mistake in naming is a mistake in understanding, and a mistake in understanding is a mistake in the arrangement, and a mistake in the arrangement is —

No one says what a mistake in the arrangement is. No one has to.

The column-trees are vines. They begin as seedlings in the crevices between basalt columns, tiny things no larger than a human thumbnail, their root-tips pressed against the stone with a grip that seems geological in its patience. They grow slowly — centimeters per year, a rate that makes the ferns look impetuous — and they grow upward, always upward, spiraling around the basalt column in a left-hand helix that matches the angle of the column's hexagonal faces with a precision that suggests the vine can *feel* the geometry of the stone, can read the angles and the edges and the fracture lines and grow along them as though following a blueprint written in mineral.

Over decades, the vine thickens. It adds layers of woody tissue the way a tree adds rings, but its rings are not concentric — they are helical, following the spiral of growth, so that a cross-section of a living column-tree shows a pattern like a fingerprint, each line a year of slow, spiraling ascent. The wood is dense even when the vine is alive — harder than oak, heavier than ironwood, dark with tannins and mineral deposits absorbed from the basalt it clings to. But it is the dead vine that achieves the material's true character. When the vine dies — of age, of disease, of some failure of the symbiosis with the stone — the wood hardens further, drying over months into a substance that the people call *columnite* and that behaves, in almost every respect, like a biological mineral. It can be struck against basalt without splintering. It can hold an edge. It can be shaped with chert tools, but only with effort, and the shapes that emerge are simple — straight lines, gentle curves, the forms that the material's grain will allow — and they are beautiful in the way that all honest materials are beautiful, in the way that a thing is beautiful when it has not been asked to be anything other than what it is.

The dead columnite persists for generations. It outlasts the living vine by centuries, a dark sheath around the basalt column, the ghost of a partnership that is over but whose physical evidence remains. The tall column in the basin is wrapped in columnite so old that it has turned nearly black, the wood carbonized by time and mineral infusion into something that strikes like iron and rings like stone. The people do not harvest from the tall column. No one harvests from the tall column. The columnite there is left alone, a monument to a partnership that began before the settlement and will end after it.

The living column-trees are left to grow. The people do not cut living vines. They do not shape living wood. They wait for the vine to complete its life — to spiral up the basalt, to thicken, to harden, to reach the column's broken top and curl over the rim like a hand closing on a stone, and then to die, slowly, in its own time, releasing its grip on the stone as the root-tips decay and the spiral loosens and the dead wood settles into its final hardness. Only then is it taken. Only then, when the partnership is over and the vine has become something other than a living thing, do the people come with their chert blades and their patience and their understanding of the grain, and they cut, and they shape, and they make: bowls, pins, loom-frames, the handles of tools, the ribs of the few shelters that need internal support. Everything else is basalt. Everything else is stone. But the columnite provides the things that stone cannot: the curved, the light, the shaped-to-the-hand, the intimate objects that live inside dwellings and pass between generations and accumulate, through use, a patina of human contact that makes them warm in a place where warmth is the rarest thing.

## The Drip-Orchids and the Green World

The drip-orchids grow along the ridgelines like a slow, pale fire.

They are not bright. They are not showy. Their petals are the color of fog — so close to white that they are more accurately described as the absence of color, a transparency that makes them visible only by the water they hold. Each flower is a small funnel, no larger than a human thumbnail, with five petals fused into a cone that narrows to a central spine. The spine channels condensation — the bead by bead accumulation of water on the petal's outer surface — downward, through the flower, into a reservoir at the plant's base: a swollen, translucent bulb of tissue that holds the water with a viscosity that prevents evaporation. The drip-orchid drinks its own rainfall. It manufactures its own ground. It has solved the problem of living in a place where water never falls but never stops arriving, and it has solved it so completely that it does not need soil — only stone, only the cool surface of basalt, only the thin film of mineral and moisture that coats every surface in the parish.

They grow in clusters of five to fifteen, their bulbs pressing against each other like sleeping bodies, their funnels angled upward and outward to maximize the catch. The clusters line the ridgelines — the exposed backs of buried basalt columns — where the condensation is densest and the dripping is most consistent, and they are most visible at the peak hour, when every funnel holds a trembling bead of water at its rim, and the clusters catch the gray-blue light and hold it, and for a moment the ridgeline glows — not with color, but with water, with the visible presence of a substance that is everywhere in the parish but nowhere so beautifully contained as in the mouths of these small, pale, perfect flowers.

They propagate by dropping propagules. Not seeds — the drip-orchid has no pollinator, no vector, no animal partner in its reproduction. Instead, it produces a small, dense body — a miniature version of itself, complete with a nascent funnel and a tiny reservoir — and it drops this body into the channel of condensation runoff that flows along the ridgeline's surface. The propagule is heavy enough to sink in the thin film of water but light enough to be carried by the current, and the current — the slow, gravity-driven sheet of condensation that moves downhill along the stone's surface — carries it to a new location: a trough, a basin, a sheltered crevice where the water slows and the propagule settles and the funnel opens and the first bead of condensation gathers, and the cycle begins again. The people have seen this happen. They have watched a propagule travel. They have followed it, step by step, along the runoff channel, and they have seen it settle, and they have marked the spot, and they have returned months later to find a new cluster, a new constellation of pale funnels on the dark stone, drinking the dripping with the same perfect patience as its parent.

Below the ridgelines, in the troughs and the sheltered hollows between columns, the green world explodes.

It is disproportionate. It is almost aggressive in its lushness. The ferns dominate: enormous, their fronds the length of a man's arm and longer, their trunks thick with old leaf-bases and the shredded remains of fiddleheads past. They crowd the troughs so densely that walking through them means wading, the fronds parting reluctantly and closing behind you with a sound like whispered protest. Their fiddleheads uncurl in a spiral that mirrors the column-trees' growth — the same geometry, the same patient mathematics — and you can watch them, if you stand still long enough, and see the spiral advance, millimeter by millimeter, as the fiddlehead expands and the new frond emerges, wet and bright and almost luminous in the dim light, its surface beaded with condensation that catches the gray-blue glow and holds it.

The mosses are everywhere. They coat every surface that faces the prevailing moisture — every north face, every sheltered overhang, every basin wall — in a layer of green so vivid it seems artificial, a green that belongs to no other place, a green that can only exist in this specific intersection of cold, wet, dim, and mineral-rich. The mosses are the parish's skin. They hold moisture against the stone, preventing the evaporation that would otherwise dry the surface and break the condensation cycle. They filter the dripping, trapping minerals and microorganisms in their dense mat and converting them into organic matter that feeds the ferns, the fungi, the orchids, the entire web of photosynthetic life that exists in this place without sunlight. They are the foundation. They are the thing that makes the green world possible. And they are so numerous, so pervasive, so integral to the parish's functioning that the people do not notice them the way a person does not notice air — until they are gone, until a section of stone is exposed by a rockfall or a freeze, and the raw, dark, uncolonized surface stares up like a wound, and the dripping beads on it uselessly, rolling off without being held, without being caught, without feeding the green, and the people see the stone and remember that the moss is not decoration but infrastructure, not beauty but survival.

The fungi are the parish's decomposers. They are pale, ghostly, almost translucent, and they grow on everything: dead wood, living wood, stone, the shed exoskeletons of veil-hands, the desiccated husks of dead lanterns, the fecal pellets of stills (which fall like dark rain from the column rims), the shed skin of humans. They grow on the growing and the dead alike, with an indifference to the boundary between the two that is either terrifying or liberating depending on your perspective. Their caps are translucent — you can see the capillaries within, the branching network of hyphae that transports nutrients from the substrate to the fruiting body, a mirror image of the lanterns' visible circulatory system, as though the same blueprint had been used for both animal and fungus, as though the parish ran on a single design principle repeated at every scale.

The people eat the fungi. They eat the drip-orchids' reservoirs when water is scarce during the driest stretches (which are only less wet, but the difference matters). They eat the fiddleheads of the ferns, boiled in clay pots over small fires of columnite shavings (which burn hot and clean and leave almost no ash). They eat the mosses — not the moss itself, which is bitter and fibrous, but the organisms that live within it: tiny crustaceans, nematodes, rotifers, the invisible majority of the parish's biomass, which is microscopic, which has always been microscopic, which thrives in the moss mat the way the veil-hands thrive on the basalt, occupying every surface and every crevice and every film of moisture with a density of life that is felt but never seen.

The green world is the parish's wealth. It is the only wealth. Everything else — the basalt, the dripping, the stillness, the silence — is the context in which the green world can exist, the conditions that must be met, the terms of the arrangement that the place imposes and the living things accept. The green world is what the arrangement is *for.* It is the reason the dripping does not stop, the reason the rock continues to rise, the reason the column-trees spiral and the orchids funnel and the lanterns glow and the stills watch. It is the parish's purpose, expressed in chlorophyll and cellulose and the slow, green patience of a fern frond uncurling toward a light that never arrives as sun but arrives, always, as the soft gray-blue luminance of the cloud that is the sky and the lamp and the rain that never falls.

## The People

There are perhaps two hundred of them. They live in thirty clusters, each cluster a handful of families, each family a few generations deep, each generation a link in a chain that stretches back further than anyone can count and forward further than anyone can predict. They are small. They are lean. They are the color of the stone — dark-skinned, dark-haired, their features angular and sharp in a way that mirrors the basalt's fracture planes, as though the place had shaped them in its own image over the deep generations. Their hands are their most remarkable feature: broad, strong, with fingers that seem slightly too long, as though evolution had favored reach and grip in a place where every surface is wet and every handhold matters.

They do not raise their voices. This is not a cultural choice — or not only a cultural choice — but a biological adaptation. Generations in a place of enforced quiet, where the primary sound is dripping and the secondary sound is the chord of the stills and there is no third sound, have tuned their vocal apparatus to low, close-range communication. They speak in murmurs. They speak in gestures. They speak in the tilt of a head, the angle of a hand, the placement of a body in a doorway or along a ridgeline path. The parish's quiet has been written into their throats and their ears and the architecture of their social lives, so that a raised voice is not taboo but physically uncomfortable, like a bright light in a dark-adapted eye.

They dress in the coarse, water-resistant fabric that the looms produce — dark, heavy, treated with the lantern-mucus paste until it gleams faintly in the gray-blue light. The fabric is not warm. Nothing in the parish is warm, except the human body, and the fabric's purpose is not warmth but dryness: it sheds the condensation, keeping the skin beneath it relatively free of water, preventing the slow, dangerous cooling that comes from living in perpetual damp. The people wear it close — wrapped around the torso, tied at the waist, layered over the legs — and they wear it always, even in sleep, even in the shelters, because the damp is everywhere and the body's warmth is finite and the margin between comfort and danger is thin and known and respected.

They eat simply. Fern fronds. Fungi. The microscopic crustaceans strained from the moss. The occasional insect — ground-sheet insects, harvested by sweeping a cloth across the ground and collecting the dark harvest — which are protein-rich and taste, people say, like wet stone and something green. They eat column-tree propagules when they can find them, though these are rare: the vine reproduces slowly, dropping a single propagule every few years, and most of these are carried by the condensation runoff to locations where they cannot grow. They do not eat the veil-hands. They do not eat the stills. They do not eat the lanterns. These are not prey. These are neighbors. These are the other parties to the arrangement, the living beings that make the parish what it is, and eating them would be like eating the stone or the dripping — a violation not of law but of physics, a denial of the terms on which the place exists.

They build low. They build of basalt. Their walls are dry-stacked, no mortar, the stones fitted together with a precision that comes from ten thousand generations of practice, the joints filled with living moss that holds the stones in place and breathes with the moisture and makes the walls themselves part of the living landscape. Their roofs are their most important technology: concave surfaces of shaped stone and packed clay, angled to catch the condensation, funneled through carved spouts into clay pipes that carry the water to the cisterns. Every dwelling is a water-collection device. Every family is a node in the network. The water flows from roof to pipe to cistern, and from cistern to cup to mouth, and from mouth to ground to stone to air to stone again, and the cycle is the economy, and the economy is the governance, and the governance is the arrangement, and the arrangement is everything.
