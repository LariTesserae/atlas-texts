# The Fauna of the Throat

---

## The Beetles

They are called — but no, they are not called anything. No one has ever named them, because no one who has encountered them has ever needed to distinguish one from another. But they have a name among themselves, if they have names at all: they are the quiet ones, the still ones, the dark commas in an amber sentence.

Their carapaces are not merely black. They are the black of wet basalt, of the surface of a deep pool seen from above, of the space between stars where no nebula intrudes. Each one is a dome, perfectly smooth, with a midline suture so fine it is less a line than a decision — the place where two halves of the same thought meet and choose not to separate. Under the amber light, the carapaces pick up a faint warm sheen, like oil on water, and for a moment you can see that the black is not uniform but layered: a depth of lacquer, of successive coats applied with the patient machinery of chitin deposition, each layer thinner than the last, building toward an opacity that has no bottom.

They sit on the roots. Always on the roots. Never on the floor of the chamber, never on another creature, never suspended in the space between. The root surface is where they belong — they grip it with tarsi so fine they are invisible at this distance, claws that have found purchase in the microscopic texture of root bark, and once they have found that purchase they do not release it, not to move, not to mate, not to flee. Their legs are folded close to their bodies in a configuration that suggests both rest and readiness, though readiness for what is unclear. They have not moved their legs in a long time. The joints may be stiff. The muscles may have atrophied to the point where movement is possible but expensive, and in a place where nothing changes, where every need is met by the mere fact of being here, expense is a concept without meaning.

You notice that they are arranged not randomly but with a pattern that approaches regularity without achieving it. If you drew lines connecting them — beetle to beetle, root to root — you would see that the distances between them follow a distribution that peaks at roughly fifteen centimeters. Not exactly fifteen. Sometimes twelve. Sometimes twenty. But fifteen is where the weight falls, and this is not coincidence. It is something else — a spatial grammar they follow without knowing it, the way a flock of birds maintains spacing without any bird knowing the spacing of the whole. They are distributed the way spores are distributed. They are distributed the way raindrops are distributed on a windshield. There is an order underneath the apparent randomness, and the order is not intelligence. It is chemistry. It is the diffusion of some signal you cannot detect — a pheromone, perhaps, released in quantities so small that only a beetle's antenna can taste it, or something simpler, a volatile compound in the root bark itself, present in concentrations that vary by location, that the beetles avoid or seek without any conscious preference, the way water seeks the lowest point.

Their clicking is the most persistent sound they produce, and it is not produced *by* them so much as *through* them. The spiracles — small, paired openings along the lateral margins of the abdominal segments — open and close with each breath, and each opening releases a tiny puff of gas, a small turbulence in the still air, and each closing produces a click. Not all spiracles click. Only certain ones, the ones with edges slightly misaligned, the ones where the chitinous valve has worn unevenly over time, the ones where the tolerances have degraded to the point where the seal is no longer clean. These are the old spiracles, the ones that have been opening and closing for years, decades perhaps, and they have developed a wear pattern that makes them audible. The younger spiracles — if the beetles are still growing, if they still moult, which is uncertain — are silent. The clicking comes from age. It comes from use. It is the sound of a door that no longer fits its frame.

If you listen closely — and the space permits close listening, because nothing else demands your attention, because the dripping is constant and the light is constant and the warmth is constant — you can distinguish individual beetles by their click signature. Each beetle has a rhythm, a tempo set by its metabolic rate, and the tempo varies slightly from beetle to beetle because no two beetles have exactly the same metabolic rate. One clicks at roughly four clicks per second. Another at three and a half. Another, older, perhaps, clicks at barely two, each click a separate event with real silence between them, the clicking of a clock that is running down. None of them click in phase with any other. The total effect is what the passage describes — a glittering static, a white noise of discrete events — but the total effect is also, when you disassemble it, a census. Each click is a beetle. Each beetle is alive. And the fact that you can hear them — that their breathing is audible, that it fills this space, that it constitutes a significant fraction of the ambient sound — tells you something about the silence they live in. It takes an extraordinary quiet for a spiracle to be loud.

They do not feed. This is the most remarkable thing about them, and the least remarkable, because in a place where nothing changes, feeding is optional. They have mouthparts — mandibles, maxillae, a labium, the standard equipment of a coleopteran built to process solid food — but the mouthparts are vestigial, or nearly so, reduced over generations of disuse to small, elegant structures that no longer open fully, that move sometimes in a ghostly chewing motion that produces nothing, that processes nothing, that is a reflex looking for a stimulus that never arrives. They may absorb moisture through their tarsal pads. They may take in dissolved minerals through the thin membrane of their abdominal pleura. They may feed on the biofilm of bacteria and archaea that coats every root surface, a living skin on the living skin, invisible to you but rich with life, a civilization of single-celled organisms going about their business beneath the notice of everything larger. The beetles may graze on this. They may simply sit in it, and let it come to them, and absorb what seeps through. The distinction between feeding and existing has collapsed here. To be in this place is to be fed by it.

They reproduce, presumably. They must, or they would not still be here. But you see no evidence of it — no pairs, no eggs, no larvae, no evidence of any event other than continued presence. Perhaps they reproduce very slowly, one generation per decade, or per century. Perhaps they bud, like the plants they live in, producing a copy of themselves from some undifferentiated tissue, a small carapace swelling from the abdominal surface, growing, darkening, eventually separating with a small, clean wound that heals in the amber air. Perhaps they simply appear, the way water condenses on a root surface — slowly, imperceptibly, one day not there and the next day there, as though they were always there and you simply failed to notice.

## The Salamanders

They are the most visible creatures in the chamber, and the most exposed, and this seems to cost them nothing.

Their translucency is not the translucency of glass or ice. It is the translucency of something alive — a scattering of light through tissue, a softening, an opacity that lets through enough to reveal but not enough to define. You can see their organs, but you cannot read them. The heart is a dark bead, pulsing at a rate that is slow by your standards and fast by the beetles' — perhaps sixty beats per minute, perhaps forty, varying with temperature, with hydration, with some internal state that has nothing to do with the chamber and everything to do with the deep history of whatever lineage produced this shape. The gut is a pale coil, visible as a slightly denser region of tissue in the abdominal cavity, and it is always moving, always processing, always converting whatever these creatures eat into whatever they need. You cannot see what they eat. You cannot see their mouths open. They cling to the undersides of root arches and breathe in their precise rhythm and their gut processes something, and that is all you know.

Their eyes are small, dark, placed laterally, and they do not move. This is not unusual for amphibians — fixed eyes are common in species that do not hunt by sight — but here it creates an effect that is difficult to describe. The eyes are present. They are functional. They receive light. But they do not track. They do not fixate. They see you, if they see at all, the way a window sees the street — as a total field, undifferentiated, a continuous wash of light and dark that carries information but not focus. When you move, the eyes do not follow. When you are still, the eyes do not rest. They are always seeing and never looking, and this gives them an air of profound indifference that is not aloofness, not disdain, not any attitude at all, but simply the absence of the neural architecture required to care.

Their skin is wet. Not damp — wet, continuously, uniformly, as though a thin layer of water has been applied and reapplied so many times that it has become a feature of the skin itself rather than a condition of the environment. The water on their skin is not the water on the roots. The root water is external, accumulated, dripping. The salamander water is generated — secreted by glands in the dermis that produce a mucus so dilute it might as well be water, a substance whose primary function is to maintain the skin's permeability, because this creature breathes through its skin as much as through its lungs, perhaps more, and dry skin is dead skin and dead skin is death. The mucus is a technology. It is the most sophisticated thing in the chamber, more refined than the root's peristalsis, more complex than the beetle's spiracles, because it is a negotiation between the organism and its environment, a boundary that is also a bridge, a barrier that is also a membrane. Through this skin, oxygen diffuses inward. Through this skin, carbon dioxide diffuses outward. Through this skin, water moves in both directions, regulated by hormones that respond to the precise osmolarity of the chamber air. The salamander is a valve. The salamander is a filter. The salamander is a living interface between itself and the Throat, and it maintains that interface with a molecular precision that would shame any engineer.

Their feet are remarkable. Each toe is tipped with a small, expanded pad — not adhesive, not suction, but something in between, a surface textured with microscopic ridges that increase the contact area between toe and root, that exploit the water film between them, that create a grip based on surface tension and capillary adhesion rather than mechanical force. They can hold their entire body weight with two toes. They can hold their entire body weight with one. They hang from the undersides of roots in postures that seem precarious — belly-up, limbs splayed, head hanging — but are perfectly stable, locked in place by physics that has not changed since they first evolved this grip and will not change until the roots themselves dissolve. They could hang here forever. They may be hanging here forever. The distinction between a very long time and forever is not meaningful in a place where nothing changes.

And their breathing. The breathing is worth returning to because it is the only thing in the chamber that *rhythms*. Everything else — the dripping, the clicking, the subsonic pulse — is texture, is texture layered on texture, is the accumulation of unsynchronized events. But the salamander breathing is a *beat*. Two seconds in. Three seconds out. A ratio of 2:3 that holds without deviation, without fatigue, without any of the drift you would expect from a biological process. It is too regular. It is regular in the way that a quartz crystal is regular, in the way that a pendulum is regular, and this regularity is either the most extraordinary feat of homeostatic control in the chamber or the most extraordinary feat of evolution, because something has shaped these creatures to breathe in exactly this way, at exactly this rate, and the reason it has shaped them is either that this rate is optimal for gas exchange at this temperature and this oxygen concentration — a biochemical sweet spot, a peak in a fitness landscape that has not shifted in millennia — or that the rate is no longer optimal but was once, and the creatures are locked into it, committed to it, unable to deviate from it because deviation was never selected for, because in a place where nothing changes, selection pressure is zero and the last adaptation to be favored becomes permanent.

They do not interact with each other. There are perhaps fifteen of them in the chamber — you have not counted, because counting them requires looking up, and looking up requires disengaging from the root-level world where your attention has settled, and disengaging is difficult in a place that offers no reason to move your eyes. But if you look — if you systematically, patiently look — you see them: each one alone, each one in its own position on its own root, each one maintaining its own breathing with its own precision, each one a separate instance of the same design, as alike as drops of the same water and as isolated. They do not vocalize. They do not display. They do not court. They do not fight. They do not groom each other or themselves. They do not do anything except breathe and cling and exist, and this is enough, this is complete, this is a life that requires nothing more than the maintenance of its own physiological processes in a space that supports those processes without effort.

## The Worms

They are easy to overlook because they are half-present — half in the root and half in the water and half in the light, which means they are three halves of something that has no whole.

They emerge from crevices in the root bark — from the spaces between root hairs, from the cracks that form when a root grows faster than its outer layer can accommodate, from the wounds left by whatever has tried to eat the roots and failed. They extend perhaps two centimeters into the chamber, or three, or five, and they stop, and they wave — not actively, not with purpose, but passively, swayed by the movement of the water film, by the vibrations of the root's peristalsis, by air currents so faint they register only as a suggestion of motion, a ghost of a breeze. Their bodies are segmented, pale, almost white, with a faint pinkish undertone that suggests blood beneath the skin, or something analogous to blood, a circulating fluid that carries oxygen and nutrients through a simple, open body plan that has been successful for longer than jaws have existed.

They are, in all likelihood, annelids — or something that converges on the annelid body plan, which is the same thing said differently. Segments repeat. Each segment carries its own set of setae — tiny, bristle-like structures that grip the walls of the crevice they inhabit, anchoring them against the pull of water or the slow, persistent drift of gravity. Each segment is semi-autonomous, capable of independent contraction, and this is how they move: a wave of contraction that passes from posterior to anterior, ratcheting the body forward through the crevice, pushing deeper into the root or pulling outward into the chamber depending on which way the wave travels. They can retract completely in less than a second — a fast, fluid withdrawal that leaves nothing behind, not even a ripple in the water film. They can emerge again just as quickly, uncoiling from the darkness of their crevice with a slowness that belies the speed of their retreat, as though caution and curiosity are distributed unevenly across their behavioral repertoire, with caution weighted heavily at the leading edge and curiosity weighted heavily everywhere else.

What do they eat? The root bark, possibly — or the biofilm on the root bark, or the bacteria within the biofilm, or the dissolved organic molecules in the water that seeps from the root surface. They are decomposers, or grazers, or filter-feeders, or all three, their mouthparts — a muscular pharynx that can be everted through the prostomium, the first segment — capable of gathering material from the water film with a combination of suction and adhesion. They feed slowly. They feed in the way that everything in this chamber does everything: without hurry, without waste, without any indication that feeding is different from breathing is different from existing. The boundary between nutrition and life has been erased by the continuity of supply. There is always food. There is always water. There is always the same temperature, the same humidity, the same chemical environment. The worms do not need to search. They extend, they feed, they retract, and the rhythm of this cycle is set not by hunger — hunger requires scarcity, and scarcity is absent here — but by some internal timer, a clock that ticks at its own rate and has no reason to change.

There may be hundreds of them. The crevices in the root bark are numerous, and each crevice may contain one or several, and many crevices are too small to be seen from the center of the chamber, or too shadowed, or too deep. You see the ones that emerge far enough to catch the light. You do not see the ones that stay in the dark. The total population of the Throat — the total number of individual organisms of all species — is a number that no one has counted and no one needs to count, because the number does not change, or changes so slowly that the change is undetectable within a human lifetime, and a human lifetime is not a unit of time that means anything here.

## The Web-Things

They hang in the spaces between root-members, in the open air of the lattice, and they are almost invisible until the light catches them.

Their webs are not silk. Or rather, they are silk in the same way that a waterfall is water — the material is the same, but the form and function have diverged so far from the prototype that the common name is almost misleading. The webs are veils. They are curtains of moisture, stabilized by a protein matrix so fine that it has no visible structure, a molecular net that holds water in suspension the way a sponge holds water, by capillary force, by surface tension, by the simple fact that the holes in the net are smaller than the surface tension of water can bridge. Each web is a filter. Each web is a dam. Each web is a three-dimensional lattice of invisible thread that captures and holds water droplets in a configuration that is both random and beautiful — a constellation, as the passage says, a scatter of bright points on a dark field, a galaxy in miniature, held in place by nothing more than the physics of adhesion.

The creatures that build them — or maintain them, or inhabit them, or are them — are difficult to see. They are small, smaller than the beetles, smaller than your thumbnail, and they are the same color as the web, which is the same color as the water, which is the same color as the light. They are amber. They are glass. They are the chamber itself, miniaturized and given legs and an abdomen and a spinneret that produces a dragline of protein so thin it has no measurable diameter, a thread that is less a physical object than a modification of the air it occupies, a line drawn through the volume of the chamber by something that knows exactly where the volume is and exactly how to use it.

They sit at the center of their webs, or off-center, or at the margin, and they wait. What they wait for is unclear. There are no flying insects in the Throat. There are no prey items of any kind that move through the air between the roots. The web-things do not catch anything. They do not feed on anything that you can see. Their webs are not traps but reservoirs — they collect water, they hold it, they perhaps allow it to be absorbed through the web-thing's own cuticle, a passive hydration strategy that uses architecture instead of physiology. Or perhaps the webs collect dissolved nutrients from the water — organic molecules, mineral ions, the chemical signatures of the root's exudates — and the web-thing absorbs these nutrients through the same cuticle, through the same veil of moisture, filtering the Throat's water one droplet at a time.

Or perhaps the webs serve another function entirely. Perhaps they are sensory organs — vast, thin antennae that detect vibration, that feel the pulse of the roots, that translate the subsonic hum of transpiration into a language of motion and tension that the web-thing can interpret. Every vibration in the chamber — every falling drop, every peristaltic wave, every click of a beetle's spiracle — sends ripples through the nearest web, and the web-thing feels these ripples, reads them, knows from them the state of the chamber, the position of every drop, the timing of every pulse. This would make the web-thing the most informed organism in the Throat, the one that knows the most about its environment, the one that has the most complete picture of the space it inhabits. And it would mean that the web-thing is not waiting at all, but *listening* — listening with a body that is almost entirely ear, almost entirely receptor, a body that has traded the capacity for action for the capacity for awareness, that has become a sense organ in the shape of an animal.

They are alone. Each web is separate. Each web-thing has its own territory, its own volume of air, its own collection of water droplets. The webs do not touch. The web-things do not interact. The spacing between them is greater than the spacing between the beetles, greater than the spacing between the salamanders, and this is perhaps because a web requires space to function, requires air on all sides, requires the volume of the lattice to hang in. A web crowded by another web is a web compromised. The web-things are distributed the way trees are distributed in a mature forest — each one occupying the space it needs, each one shading its own small territory, each one alone against the light.

## The Water-Thing

Something moves in the water film on the roots. Something small, something visible only when you are close, when you are kneeling on the root-floor and pressing your face near the surface and watching the thin layer of water with the attention it does not know it deserves.

It is a wake. A V-shaped disturbance that travels through the surface tension of the water film, and at the apex of the V — or slightly behind it, or slightly to one side, as though it is not steering the wake but merely traveling in the same direction — there is a body. It is smaller than the worms. It is smaller than the beetles' legs. It is a pale, translucent thread of muscle and nerve, no more than a few millimeters long, and it moves with a purposeful, undulatory motion — lateral waves passing from head to tail, each wave propelling it forward through the water film by fractions of a millimeter, the locomotion of a snake scaled down to the invisible, the same physics of thrust and drag operating at a scale where surface tension is a wall and viscosity is a ocean.

It may be a nematode. It may be the larva of something — of the web-things, perhaps, or of the beetles, or of something that has no adult form in this chamber, that completes its development here and then remains, unable to leave, unable to metamorphose into a form capable of traversing the root network to the surface, wherever and whatever the surface is. It may be a permanent larva, a neotenic form, a creature that has evolved to remain juvenile forever because the conditions of the Throat reward juvenility — the warmth, the moisture, the absence of predation, the absence of competition, the absence of change.

It moves without sound. In a chamber filled with sound — the dripping, the clicking, the hissing, the hum — this creature moves in total silence, and the silence is not an absence but a presence, a small pocket of stillness that travels through the water film like a hole traveling through fabric, a negative space that is defined by the sound it displaces. When it passes beneath a dripping root, the drip's sound changes — not in volume but in timbre, a subtle shift caused by the disruption of the water film's surface tension, a modulation so slight that only the most attentive listener would notice. It is a whisper in a room full of shouting. It is a thought in a mind full of noise.

It travels the length of a root and disappears. It emerges on another root. It travels the length of that root and disappears again. It is always moving, always going somewhere, always arriving and departing, and this is the only motion in the Throat that has a destination, that seems to be headed toward something, that implies a purpose beyond the maintenance of an equilibrium. The water-thing moves because it must. It moves because movement is its metabolism, its respiration, its feeding strategy all at once, because every moment of motion exposes new surface area to the water film, new opportunities for absorption, new volumes of nutrient-rich liquid to pass over its cuticle. It is a creature made of the act of traveling. It does not stop. It cannot stop. To stop is to absorb nothing, to process nothing, to become one more still thing in a chamber full of still things, and the water-thing has refused this, has evolved this refusal into its very shape, has become a body that is nothing but motion, a vector without magnitude, a direction without a destination.

It is the only creature in the Throat that you could call free.

## The Ecology of Nowhere

Here is the thing about all of them, all of these creatures, considered together: they are a community that does not interact.

This is not a paradox. It is a definition. A community is defined by proximity, not by relationship, and these creatures are proximate. They share a space. They share a climate. They share a food source — the water, the biofilm, the dissolved organic matter that the roots exude as a waste product of their own metabolism. They share a temperature, a humidity, a light level, a sound environment. They share everything except contact. They share everything except consequence. The beetles do not affect the salamanders. The salamanders do not affect the worms. The worms do not affect the web-things. The water-thing affects nothing and is affected by nothing. Each species is an island, and the archipelago of the Throat is a collection of islands that have never known a bridge.

This is stable. This is the stability of total isolation within total proximity. This is the stability of a parking lot at three in the morning, as the passage says, but it is also the stability of a library, of a cathedral, of any space where many beings coexist in silence, each one absorbed in its own process, each one complete within itself, each one needing nothing from any other. The Throat is a space of absolute coexistence without interaction, and this is rare, and it is beautiful, and it is the kind of beauty that does not move you but settles in you, the way sediment settles, the way the water settles on the root surfaces, the way everything in this place settles into the exact position it has always occupied and will always occupy.

They are alive. They are profoundly, thoroughly, undeniably alive — each one carrying a genome, each one executing a developmental program, each one maintaining a set of physiological processes that would cease if conditions changed and has no reason to cease under current conditions. They are alive in the way that a crystal is alive, if a crystal could metabolize. They are alive in the way that a river is alive, if a river could heal. They are alive in the way that the Throat itself is alive — quietly, persistently, without urgency, without drama, without any of the narrative arc that we associate with life in the world outside this chamber.

They will continue. The beetles will click. The salamanders will breathe. The worms will extend and retract. The web-things will hang and listen. The water-thing will move, will always move, will move through the water film on the roots of the Throat in a path that is not a circle and not a line but something else, something without a name, something that visits every root surface in the chamber and returns to each one at an interval that is constant but not predictable, a path that fills the space of the water film the way light fills the space of the chamber — completely, evenly, without remainder.

They are the tenants. They are the residents. They are the inhabitants of a space that was not built for them and does not need them and does not know they exist. And they are content — not with the human sense of contentment, not with the emotional state, but with the biological sense, the thermodynamic sense, the sense in which a system at equilibrium is content because it has nothing left to seek, nothing left to gain, nothing left to become.

The Throat is complete. Its fauna are complete. Everything that should be here is here. Everything that should not be here is not.

The drops fall. They have always been falling.

The beetles click. They have always been clicking.

The salamanders breathe. In two. Out three. In two. Out three.

The water-thing moves through the water film on the roots, and the roots pulse beneath it, slow and deep and patient, carrying water upward through miles of wood and bark and soil, carrying light from a sun that these creatures will never see, feeding the vast, silent, industrial organism that made this chamber and forgot it and continues, without thought, without intention, without end, to be alive.

You could stay.

Everything here is staying.
