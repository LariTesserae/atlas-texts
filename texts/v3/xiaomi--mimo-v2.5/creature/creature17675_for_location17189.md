# The One Who Works Below

There is a person. There has to be, because nothing else builds like this.

They are not here now — that much is obvious from the way the water has settled into stillness around the edges, from the faint film of fine sediment that has begun to gather in the undisturbed corners of the grinding stone's depression. An hour, maybe two, since anyone stood in this room. But they will return. Everything about this place says *will return* rather than *has left.*

They are small. The ceiling is low, the passage lower, and the shelf is set at a height that would suit someone who does not tower — someone who works with their hands close to their body, elbows in, economy of movement dictated by the space itself. Their hands are their primary instruments. You can read them in everything: the fingerprints in the clay, the particular rhythm of the chisel marks on the stone, the way the dowels have been shaped — each one slightly different, each one a record of a particular afternoon's patience. They have been doing this long enough to develop a method but not so long that the method has become invisible. The work still carries the evidence of thought in every surface. They are learning as they build. They are still, in some fundamental sense, deciding what this place is.

They are warm-blooded. The warmth in the stones is not geothermal — there is no sulfurous edge to the water, no mineral haze — but something maintained, something tended. Perhaps a fire somewhere in the deeper passages, banked and sheltered, its heat conducted through the stone or diffused by the constant flow of water. Perhaps something else. But the warmth is necessary. This person works here for hours. They need the water to hold them the way a room holds someone. Cold would make the stone unforgiving, the clay unworkable, the hands clumsy. The warmth is not a luxury. It is infrastructure.

They are alone.

This is the thing the room insists on most forcefully. Not the absence of animal life — that is chosen, deliberate, a maintained boundary — but the singular scale of everything. One shelf. One grinding stone. One hammer. One relief carved into one wall. The space was designed for one body to move through without negotiation, without accommodation. If there were two, they would constantly be in each other's way. This is a workshop for a single pair of hands, and those hands belong to someone who has made a practice of solitude — not as punishment, not as deprivation, but as a condition of the work itself.

They come from above. The passage leads deeper, yes, but the passage is recent and the light still falls through it from the chamber, which means the person has not yet moved their center of gravity downward. They descend into this place from the surface. They are building something larger than this room.

The concentric circles on the wall are not decorative. Look at them again. The lines radiate outward with regular spacing, and if you trace the concentric rings from center to edge, there are seven of them, each one incised to a slightly different depth. This is a diagram. It could be a map — of the structure above, of the waterway, of the chambers that exist or are planned. It could be a measurement of something: tides, pressure, the passage of light through the water over days or seasons. It is a record, and like everything here, it is still being made — the outermost ring is lighter than the others, its edges less certain, as if the person was working on it recently and had not yet decided how far it should extend.

The dowels on the shelf are not tools in themselves. They are components. Sanded, pointed, uniform enough to fit into pre-drilled holes in other pieces of wood — holes that don't exist yet in this room but must exist somewhere, in some assembly that is being designed here and constructed elsewhere, or that will be assembled in this space once the person has finished preparing the individual parts. They are making something modular. Something that can be taken apart and reassembled. Something that might need to be hidden.

The grinding stone has been used. The pale dust caught in its grain is fine and white — limestone, or bone, or something else that powders under abrasion. If it is stone dust, it could be for making pigment, or for mixing with the clay to change its properties. If it is bone dust, that means there is a surface above that yields bone — not fish, not shell, but something larger, something terrestrial. The grinding stone is a bridge between two worlds: the dry world where things have skeletons, and the wet world where this person has chosen to work.

They keep the channels clear. This is not casual maintenance but a discipline. The water must flow — not just for oxygen, not just for warmth, but because the flow is part of the process. The channels are not drainage alone; they are circulation. The room breathes because they made it breathe. When the channels clog, the water stills, and when the water stills, the sediment begins to fall, and when the sediment falls, the work is compromised. So they clear the channels. They kneel on the stone floor, perhaps with a shaped dowel, and they push accumulated grit through the grate of fitted pebbles, and they do this regularly enough that the channels show no signs of obstruction. This is the least glamorous part of the work and it is done with the same care as everything else.

They are patient in a way that suggests a different relationship with time than the one most people carry. The relief carving alone — seven concentric rings, each one incised with a hand-chisel into fitted stone blocks, working underwater in a space barely large enough to turn around in — could take days. The grinding stone's depression has been worn by weeks of use. The clay has been brought down in wrapped bundles, portion by portion, rather than in a single large supply, which suggests either that it is scarce where they gather it or that they prefer to work with small amounts and keep the rest stored somewhere above. Either way, they are not rushing. They are not racing toward a deadline. They are building this place at the speed that the place itself demands, and the place demands slow.

The woven fiber that wraps the clay bundle is worth examining. It is not crude — the weave is regular, the knots deliberate. This person can weave, and they learned it somewhere, from someone, which means they were not always alone. There is a community, however distant, however estranged, that taught them the things they know: how to split stone, how to weave fiber, how to grind, how to build. They left that community or were sent from it, but they carry its knowledge in their hands, and the woven fiber is the most visible evidence of it — a small, intact piece of the world they came from, used here to wrap the raw material of the world they are making.

They will return soon. The hammer is still wet. The grinding stone still holds dust. The channel flow is steady, which means no one has interrupted it. The plants in the corners are undisturbed. The light is still fierce and bright, which means it is still the middle of the day above. Whatever draws them away from this place — whatever pulls them back up through the water to the surface — it is brief. A meal. A task. A necessary errand in the world of air and open sky.

And when they return, they will duck through the low passage, or descend through the ceiling opening — either way, moving slowly, letting their body adjust to the pressure and the warmth — and they will stand in the shifting light, and they will pick up the hammer or the dowel or the grinding stone, and they will continue. There is no one to watch them. There is no one to judge the work. There is only the water, the light, the slow green growth in the corners, and the deep pulse of the tide pressing down above, and the person, alone, building something that has not yet revealed what it is.

They are not hiding from something. They are building toward something. The difference is in the tools, the care, the diagram on the wall. This is not a refuge. It is a foundation. And the thing that will rise from it — whatever it turns out to be — will rise in its own time, at the speed of hand-shaped stone and channel-carved water and one person's patient, deliberate, unhurried work.

The thin green stalks sway. The light moves across the walls. The channels murmur. The passage breathes.

Everything is waiting for them to come back.
