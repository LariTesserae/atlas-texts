# What Lives in the High Seeps

---

Start small. Start where almost everything starts here.

**In the seams.**

Between the vesicles in the basalt — the tiny glassy bubbles left behind when gas escaped the cooling lava — there are worlds that measure themselves in fractions of millimeters. Bacterial colonies. Cyanobacteria mostly, and the name is misleading because they aren't cyan at all: they are olive-black, or rust-red, or the deep and startling orange of iron oxide, depending on what the water brings them. They live in sheets so thin you'd mistake them for a stain on the rock, a shadow in the shallow where the water barely moves. But they are alive in the oldest way anything on this plateau is alive — older than the moss, older than the stone's surface, older than the water's patient campaign against the rock. They were here first. They found the seeps before anything else did, before the first spore landed in the mineral film, and they built the foundation for everything that came after.

They don't move. They don't need to. They are photosynthetic and chemosynthetic both — they take what light reaches them through the mist and they take what the hot groundwater carries up from below, iron and sulfur and trace metals that have no business being this close to the surface, and they *use* it, the way everything here uses everything: completely, without waste, without hurry. When they die, they leave behind thin mineral crusts that become the first soil this plateau has ever known. They are building the ground under everything else's feet.

At night, some of these mats glow. Not brightly — you'd have to be close, crouching, with your cheek near the wet stone — but there it is, a faint and eerie phosphorescence, blue-green, the same color as the sea from an airplane window. Bioluminescence. The bacteria are signaling, or metabolizing, or doing something that biologists have named and argued about and still don't fully understand. It is not beautiful in the way you'd expect. It is beautiful in the way a heartbeat is beautiful when you can see it through skin — evidence of a process so fundamental that beauty is almost beside the point. The mats glow and the water moves over them and the glow distorts and re-forms and the sound of ten thousand seeps continues overhead, indifferent, enormous, and in the morning the glow is gone and the mats are olive-black again, feeding, persisting, making soil one dead cell at a time.

---

**Above them, in the moss, there are cities.**

Not metaphorical cities. Actual complex societies of organisms so small that a human knee, descending into the hummock-field, brings down like a falling sky.

The springtails first. Collembola. They have no business being as numerous as they are, and yet the numbers are staggering — tens of thousands per square meter, possibly more, in the upper layers of the sphagnum where the water is present but not constant, where there is air to breathe and fungal filaments to graze and decomposing plant matter to process. They are hexapods, technically, six-legged, though they look like nothing with legs at all at first glance. They are tiny — a millimeter, two millimeters — and they are white or translucent or, in some species, a startling metallic blue that catches light in the moss the way dew catches light, which is why you never notice them unless you know to look. They spring. That's the thing about them. They have a forked appendage tucked under their abdomens called a furcula, held in place by a latch, and when they are startled — by a vibration in the moss, by the footfall of a bird, by the slow weight of water-saturated sphagnum shifting — they release it and are *launched*, tumbling through the air of their micro-forest like seeds, like confetti, like something with no gravity. You part the moss with your fingers and a thousand of them spring at once and for an instant the air above the hummock shimmers with them, and then they are gone, settled back into the green, already feeding again.

They are grazers. They eat fungal hyphae — the microscopic white threads that thread through every centimeter of moss, connecting plant to plant, breaking down old growth, recycling minerals. They eat decaying organic matter. They eat each other's feces, which is not as strange as it sounds because their feces are rich in partially digested fungi and bacteria and represent a more efficient meal than the raw material they started with. This is how the ecosystem turns: through consumption and re-consumption, nothing wasted, nothing lost. A springtail dies and its body is eaten by mites and nematodes and fungi and bacteria, and those are eaten by other springtails, and the nutrients cycle and cycle, and the moss grows a fraction of a millimeter taller, and the hummock builds, and in a decade the depression where it started is a meter deep with accumulated peat, and the springtails have moved up with it, always living in the top layer, always rising with the slow growth of their world.

The mites are there too. Oribatids mostly — slow, armored, round as seeds, moving through the moss interstices with the unhurried purpose of something that has been doing this for four hundred million years, which is about how long mites have existed, and which is longer than anything else here except the basalt's minerals and the bacteria. They are the decomposers of last resort. They eat what nothing else has broken down — the toughest fungal walls, the most recalcitrant plant fibers — and they excrete it as fine particles that become, essentially, soil. They are soil-makers. The first real, workable, structurally complex soil on this plateau — soil that a fern can root in, that a grass can anchor in — exists because mites chewed it into being. They do this without seeing it, without knowing it, without any sense of the enormity of what they are doing. They are small and numerous and blind and they are *building the ground*.

There are nematodes too, though you would never see them without a microscope. Roundworms, microscopic, living in the water films on moss leaves and in the saturated peat. They are bacteria-feeders mostly, sweeping through their thin aqueous world gulping cells, and they are preyed upon by larger nematodes that have mouthparts and hunt, and those are preyed upon by tardigrades — water bears — which are the apex predators of this scale, eight-legged and stubby and absurd, moving through the moss with the ponderous gait of something that knows it is armored against almost everything. Tardigrades can survive desiccation, extreme cold, the vacuum of space. They survive the freeze-thaw cycles that happen here when the mist freezes on the rock at night and the sun melts it at dawn. They survive by curling into barrels — tun states — and waiting, and their waiting is a kind of patience that makes the moss's patience look impulsive.

And this is the thing about the moss-plain that a standing human cannot see: it is not a carpet. It is not a blanket. It is a *continent* — vast, vertically stratified, populated at every scale by organisms that have been evolving solutions to the problems of wetness and cold and mineral saturation for longer than most of what we think of as "nature" has existed. The sphagnum is the architecture. It builds itself upward, layer by layer, dead tissue below and living tissue above, and every layer is inhabited, and the whole structure breathes and holds water like a sponge because it *is* a sponge, biologically, structurally, functionally — each leaf a tiny cage of dead cells that hold water by capillary force, each stem a wick pulling moisture upward from the saturated base. A single sphagnum plant can hold twenty times its dry weight in water. The hummock-field holds an ocean.

**Above that, at the scale of a person standing, there are the flies.**

Not many. A few species. Small, dark, purposeful. They patrol the temporary ponds where the water pools in shallow basins on the flat rock — ponds that last days or weeks before the water finds a new path and drains them, ponds that are ecosystems unto themselves, complete and transient. The flies lay eggs in these ponds. The eggs hatch into larvae — wrigglers, they're called, though some species have more elegant names — and the larvae feed on bacteria and algae and decaying organic matter that has washed into the basin, and they grow, and they pupate, and they emerge as flies, and by then the pond may be gone, may be a damp stain on the rock, and the flies go looking for the next one. They are nomads of impermanence. They live their entire lives around water that doesn't persist, and they have adapted to this with a flexibility that looks, from the outside, like recklessness — eggs in a pool that might last a week — but is actually precision. They know the water chemistry. They know what's viable. The females taste the water with their feet, literally, chemically, reading the mineral content and bacterial load, deciding whether this basin is worth the investment. Some are. Some aren't. The ones that aren't are left dry, and the ones that are become the brief, teeming, self-contained worlds that a kneeling human can watch with the slow fascination of something being born and dying and being born in the space of an afternoon.

The flies are food for the birds. But only when the birds are here, and the birds are rarely here.

---

**The birds.**

A pipit. A wheatear. Occasionally something else — a wagtail, perhaps, drawn by the water, the same way wagtails are drawn to water everywhere, as if they cannot help themselves, as if the sound of running water is a compulsion rather than a lure. They come because the insects exist, and the insects exist because the water exists, and the water exists because the earth is still hot beneath this place and the rock is still new and porous and has not yet sealed itself against the upward push of deep pressure. The chain is short and simple and it is the only food web this plateau has.

The pipit is a small brown bird, streaked, unremarkable in plumage, remarkable in persistence. It stands on a rock and it bobs its tail — an up-and-down pumping motion that is either a signal to other pipits or a way to startle insects into movement or simply a thing it does because its body requires it — and it scans the moss-plain with bright, dark eyes that see ultraviolet, that see the world in frequencies no human can access, that pick out the movement of a springtail the size of a dust mote against the green-on-green of the sphagnum. It is a bird built for open places, for wind, for the vast empty expanses where there is nowhere to hide and so it doesn't try to hide. It sits on the highest rock it can find and it sings — a thin, reedy, descending song that the mist catches and carries and distorts — and the song means *I am here, this is mine, I am alive and I am here* — and it means it for as long as it is true, which may be a season or a year or several years, though most pipits don't live long, and the ones that breed here in this new, loud, wet, exposed place probably live shorter lives than most.

The wheatear is bolder, more compact, with a face that looks — if you're generous — alert and intelligent and — if you're not — suspicious. It arrives on the plateau along a migration route that has existed for thousands of years, long before the basalt cooled, and it stops here because there are insects and the insects are easy to catch in this open terrain where there is no canopy, no underbrush, nothing to fly behind. It hunts by perch-and-pounce, standing on a rock, watching the moss, dropping down to seize something — a fly, a moth, a beetle — and returning to its perch to eat it. It is a visitor. It does not stay. It treats the plateau the way a traveler treats a rest stop — useful, unmemorable, to be left behind when the wind is right and the migration clock says *go*.

But sometimes, in the margins, in the brief moments when both species share a rock or a stretch of moss-plain, there is something that looks like recognition. Not of each other — they don't care about each other — but of the situation. Two small birds in a vast, wet, new place, both far from anywhere that feels permanent, both here because the insects are here because the water is here because the earth is still cooling beneath them. They sit. They don't interact. They watch different directions. And the sound of the seeps continues around them, and the mist lifts and settles, and eventually one or both of them leave, and the rock is empty again, and the springtails in the moss below resume their silent springing.

---

**And in the structures — in the abandoned pumping stations and the half-submerged processing facilities — there are other lives.**

Not many. A bat, perhaps, roosting in the rusted steel of a pipe bracket, sleeping through the day while the seeps whisper around it. It is a small bat — a pipistrelle, probably, one of the species that can live almost anywhere, that treats buildings and bridges and abandoned factories as artificial caves, and that will, with equal equanimity, roost in a cliff face or a corrugated metal ceiling. It emerges at dusk — not that there is much true dusk here, the light goes from bright to dim to dark with a speed that the mist mediates and softens — and it hunts the flying insects over the moss-plain, eating them on the wing, navigating by echolocation through a world that is, for it, a landscape of sound, a three-dimensional map drawn in echoes. The constant water-sound of the seeps must be confusing. The bat must have learned to filter it, to hear through it, to find the click of a moth's wing or the whine of a midge within the vast white noise of geological water. It is a small, furry, improbable thing — a mammal with wings, hanging upside down in a place that was built to extract minerals and is now slowly being reclaimed by moss — and it lives here because it can, because the insects are here, because the niche exists and life fills every niche it finds.

There may be spiders. Small ones, in the structures, where the geometry of steel and concrete creates sheltered corners that the moss hasn't reached yet. Sheet-web weavers, probably, or cobweb spiders, the kinds that build in human-made structures because the structures provide the rigid scaffolding their webs require and the moss-plain provides the insects that stumble into those webs. A spider in an abandoned pumping station is a thing of small, intricate domesticity — it has its corner, its web, its steady diet of flies and springtails that have wandered too far from the moss, and it will live and reproduce and die there without ever knowing that the building it inhabits was once a place of loud machinery and human purpose. The humans are gone or they were never here long enough to count, and the spider is here now, and the web catches light through a broken window, and outside the mist drifts and the seeps whisper and the moss climbs the wall another centimeter.

---

**And the moss itself.**

You cannot talk about what lives here without talking about the moss as a living thing — not as landscape, not as ground-cover, not as a feature of the terrain, but as an organism, a participant, a being with its own agenda and its own timeline and its own form of intelligence, which is slow and chemical and architectural and utterly indifferent to the animals that live within it.

Sphagnum moss is not one thing. It is at least seven species here, possibly more, each adapted to a slightly different moisture level, a slightly different mineral concentration, a slightly different position on the hummock. The hummocks are built by the mosses themselves — *Sphagnum* species that grow upward, that create their own elevation, that lift their living tips above the saturated base and leave behind a column of dead tissue that compresses and acidifies and holds water and becomes, over decades, peat. The moss is a builder. It is building itself a city of hummocks and hollows, and the city has its own hydrology, its own climate, its own internal gradients of wetness and temperature and light that create microhabitats within microhabitats within microhabitats.

In the wet hollows between hummocks, different species grow — liverworts, *Marchantia*, their flat thalloid bodies spreading like spilled green paint, each one dotted with cup-shaped structures called gemmae cups that hold tiny clones of the parent, waiting for a raindrop to splash them free and carry them a few centimeters to a new patch of rock or soil. It is the simplest possible form of reproduction — drop, settle, grow — and it works, because the mist never stops and the drops are always falling and the distances that need to be covered are always small. The liverworts are ancient. Their body plan hasn't changed in four hundred million years. They were here — or their ancestors were here — when the first plants colonized land, and they are here now, on this brand-new rock, colonizing it with the same flat, wet, patient persistence they have always had, and they will be here long after the basalt has worn down to clay, because they were here before clay was invented.

Ferns. Not many, but present. Low-growing species, bracken, maidenhair, the kind that don't need deep soil because they can root in a crack, in a crevice, in a fold of moss where a few millimeters of decomposed sphagnum have accumulated. They unfurl their fronds in the mist — the fiddleheads, tightly coiled, covered in brown scales, rising from the moss like small green fists opening slowly — and they reach for the light, which is abundant here, diffused by the mist but always present, always bright. The ferns are the tallest things on the plateau. Nothing else competes. They grow to half a meter and they are, by local standards, giants — the trees of this ecosystem, the canopy, the dominant life-form visible from a standing human's perspective. They sway in the constant breeze that moves across the open rock and they shed spores that drift on that breeze and land in wet places and germinate and grow, and in a year or two there is a new fern, and in five years it is mature, and in twenty years it has created enough shade and leaf-litter and root-structure to allow other things — a grass, a wildflower, something more demanding — to take hold beside it. The fern is a pioneer of pioneers. It goes where the moss has gone before it and it makes the place ready for whatever comes next.

---

**And at night, in the mist, a different life.**

The microbial mats glow. The bats hunt. The springtails retreat deeper into the moss to escape the cooling air — not because they are cold-blooded exactly, but because their activity is temperature-dependent, and the drop in temperature slows them, and they become still, and the world they inhabit becomes quiet in a way that mirrors the surface world's quiet, though the surface world is not truly quiet — the water never stops, the seeps never pause, the sound of geological patience is continuous and unchanging and it persists in darkness the way it persists in light, the way it persists through ice and heat and the long slow centuries of the plateau's transformation.

A mite, deep in the moss, is eating a fungal hypha. It has been eating for hours. It will eat for hours more. Its mouthparts — chelicerae, tiny, intricate, designed for this single purpose — work against the hyphal wall with a persistence that is, at its own scale, indistinguishable from determination. It does not know that it is making soil. It does not know that the soil it is making will, in a century, support a grass that will, in a century after that, support a rabbit that will, in a century after that, support a fox. It knows only the hypha, and the moss around it, and the slow dark movement of water through the spaces between dead cells. This is enough. This has always been enough.

The plateau breathes. The water flows. The moss grows. The springtails spring. The pipit bobs its tail on a wet rock in the enormous light. The bat listens through the noise of the earth. The mite eats. The fern unfurls. The bacteria glow their faint and ancient glow in the mineral water, and they have been glowing, in one form or another, for three billion years, and they will be glowing, in one form or another, for three billion more, and in the meantime the basalt weeps and the mist falls and the moss builds and the plateau becomes, millimeter by millimeter, the place it is going to be, and everything that lives here lives there too — in the future, in the process, in the slow and patient and relentless becoming that is the only permanent thing about this place.
