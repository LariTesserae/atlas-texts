# Inhabitants of the Seepworks

---

## I. The Crustaceans

They are the first things you learn to see, because they are everywhere and they are the color of almost-nothing.

Pale. Not white — white is a color, and these are less than that. They are the color of water that has been water for a very long time. You notice them first as a displacement, a place where the surface of a shallow pool seems to stutter, and then you see the legs, dozens of legs beneath translucent carapaces, moving in a rhythm so slow it looks like breathing. They are called — by no one, because no one is here to name them — something like *sieves*. Their mouthparts are fans. Their bodies are half-gut, half-shell, and the gut glows faintly with the minerals they extract from the water: a thread of dull copper or iron-orange visible through the carapace like a vein in quartz.

They cluster. Not in swarms — they have no eyes to swarm for, no visual sociality — but in *accords*. They gather where the water is richest, and they arrange themselves with a spacing that is almost mathematical, each individual maintaining a precise distance from its neighbors. Touch one, and the disturbance ripples outward through the group: not a flight response but a *reorganization*, each crustacean shifting incrementally to restore the geometry. They do not fear you. They register you as a pressure event, a change in the water's behavior, and they accommodate you the way a riverbed accommodates a stone.

They are ancient. Not this species, perhaps, but this *form* — this body plan, this way of being a living thing in this place. You can see it in the mineral deposits on the oldest walls: fossils, or the ghosts of fossils, impressions of fan-mouths and segmented legs pressed into calcium like a signature pressed into clay. The crustaceans have been sieving this water since before the iron was placed, since before the pipes were laid, since the Seepworks was only stone and the patience of the aquifer above. They were here first. They will be here after.

The largest ones are the size of a spread hand. The smallest are the size of a fingernail. They do not grow so much as *accumulate* — layering calcium onto their shells the way the walls layer it onto the pipes, so that the oldest individuals are encrusted with the same mineral glaze as the stone around them. A very old sieve is indistinguishable from a rock until it moves. Some of them, you suspect, move so rarely and so slowly that the distinction has ceased to matter.

## II. The Eel-things

In the deep channels — the ones that pitch downward at angles too steep for the crustaceans, where the water runs fast and cold and carries a taste like a battery held to the tongue — there are the eel-things.

They are not eels. They are not, by any reasonable taxonomy, even fish. But they are long, and they move through the water with a lateral undulation that is close enough to eel-like that the name persists in the silence where no one speaks it. Their bodies are narrow, flattened slightly from side to side, and their skin is the remarkable thing. It is not scales. It is not slime, though there is a quality of slickness to it. It is a *membrane* — thin, almost iridescent, and active. It is an organ of deposition. As the eel-thing moves through mineral-heavy water, its skin extracts metallic compounds from the solution and precipitates them outward, leaving behind a trail of faint metallic sheen on whatever surface it passes over. Copper on limestone. Iron on iron. A thin, glistening smear that catches the bioluminescent light and holds it for hours before the water washes it away.

This is not waste. This is not excretion. The eel-things are *building something* with this secretion, or rather, their bodies are building something that their minds — such as they are — know nothing about. The trails overlap in the old channels, layer upon layer, creating a substrate of metal deposit on the stone that changes the chemistry of the water that flows over it, which changes the behavior of the mineral extraction, which changes the deposit. A feedback loop with no intention behind it, producing complexity the way a river produces a delta. The channels the eel-things have occupied longest are coated in this metal-sheen to a depth of centimeters. They feel different underfoot — warmer, somehow, though they are not — and they ring faintly when you tap them with a knuckle, a sound like striking a very small bell.

They have eyes. Small, vestigial, sunk deep in the skull beneath skin that has grown over them. They do not use them. They navigate by the electric field of the water itself — by the faint currents generated by mineral gradients, by the slow pulse of the infrastructure that still hums beneath the stone. They are blind in the way that a compass is blind: they do not need to see north to find it.

They grow large. The channels limit them, but where the channels open into cisterns or flooded chambers, the eel-things can reach lengths that surprise you. Two meters. Three. Coiled in the dark water of a deep basin, their skin depositing metal in slow spirals on the basin floor, building a record of their presence in a language of copper and iron that will outlast them by millennia.

## III. The Legged Things

You hear them before you see them, and what you hear is wrong.

A scrape. Not the scrape of a creature moving through a space — not the incidental friction of a body against stone — but a *mechanical* sound, rhythmic and precise, like the ticking of a clock made of chitin. It comes from the spaces between the pipe-racks, from the narrow gaps where infrastructure meets infrastructure and creates corridors too tight for anything with bulk but wide enough for anything with patience and the right number of joints.

The legged things have too many legs.

This is not poetry. This is not metaphor. They have more legs than arthropodic convention should allow, and the excess legs are not vestigial — they move, they bear weight, they coordinate with the others in a gait that is fluid and purposeful and deeply unsettling to watch. Eight legs. Twelve. Some of the larger individuals appear to have sixteen, arranged in pairs along a body that is narrow and segmented and armored in overlapping plates of dark, chitin-like material. Their heads — if the anterior end is a head — bear no eyes, no antennae, nothing you would recognize as a sensory organ. They find their way through the dark by touch, by vibration, by the minute displacement of air in the narrow spaces they occupy.

They are scavengers. They eat what the crustaceans cannot process — the hard mineral casings, the dead organic matter that accumulates in the grease-traps and sediment basins, the carcasses of other fauna. They eat the infrastructure, too, or at least they eat what the infrastructure accumulates: the mineral crusts, the old grease, the calcium deposits that form on everything in this place. They are, in their way, *maintenance*. They keep the passages clear in the same mindless, persistent way that the crustaceans keep the water clean.

They build nothing. They maintain nothing. They simply move through the Seepworks in their precise, clicking gait, and where they pass, the accumulation is less.

Some of them are very old. You can tell by the plates — layered, thickened, worn smooth on their undersides by decades of contact with stone. An old legged thing is heavy with calcium. It moves slowly. The clicking of its legs against the floor is deeper, more resonant, like the tick of a much larger clock. It does not hurry. It has never needed to hurry. Nothing in the Seepworks hurries.

## IV. The Bats

They roost in the upper cavities — the dissolution chambers high in the walls where the air is warmer and drier and the stone is pocked with footholds shaped by ten thousand years of claw and wing.

They are small. Their wingspans barely exceed the length of a forearm. Their fur is the pale, nearly luminous gray of cave-adapted creatures, and their eyes — they have eyes, unlike most of the Seepworks' fauna — are large, wet, and useless. They navigate by echolocation, sending out clicks so rapid and so quiet that you cannot hear them even in the silence between drips. To your ear, their flight is soundless. A pale shape crosses the bioluminescent light, and then it is gone, and there is no sound to mark its passage.

They feed on the wet-paper insects and on small crustaceans that venture into the upper passages during the breeding season, when the pools rise and the sieve-things climb. They drink from the mist that hangs in the upper air — not from pools but from the atmosphere itself, catching droplets on their wing membranes and licking them clean in flight.

Their guano forms stalactites.

This is not metaphor either. The bat guano, mixed with the mineral-heavy water that seeps through the upper cavities, calcifies. It builds. Over decades and centuries, the droppings of generations of bats become columns of calcium and organic compound, some of them nearly a meter long, hanging from the ceiling of a roosting chamber like inverted stalagmites. They glow faintly in the bioluminescent light — not blue-green but amber, the color of deep old organic matter compressed into stone. They are beautiful in the way that all slow processes are beautiful: not for what they are but for the depth of time they contain.

The bats do not know they are building. They do not know the columns are there. They return, night after night — if it is night; there is no night here — to the same chambers, the same roosts, the same holes in the same stone, and they add, imperceptibly, to the slow architecture of their own waste. The roosting chambers are the most organic spaces in the Seepworks: soft-walled, warm, carpeted in centuries of guano and shed fur and the tiny bones of the dead. They smell of ammonia and mammal and the faint sweetness of decomposition.

You do not enter these chambers. You hear the bats from below — a faint, collective rustle, like pages turning in a distant room — and you leave them to their work.

## V. The Nesters

Something lives in the hollows of the dead machinery.

You find evidence of it everywhere: the grease-traps cleaned out, the dust displaced, the interior of a valve housing wiped of the mineral glaze that covers everything else. And sometimes, if you are quiet, if you stand long enough in a passage where a large pump or turbine sits dead and calcified, you see it.

It is soft. That is the first thing. In a world of stone and metal and calcium, it is *soft* — a body without armor, without shell, without any of the hard protections that the Seepworks seems to demand. It is pale, like everything else, but its pallor is different: it is the pallor of something that has never seen light, not the functional pallor of the crustaceans' transparency but the vulnerable pallor of skin that has never been called upon to resist anything but moisture.

It is roughly the size of a cat, but it is not shaped like a cat. It is shaped like a *hand* — or like a cluster of hands, a knot of digits that can extend and retract independently, each one tipped with a pad of soft, sensitive tissue that it uses to read the surfaces it moves across. It has no eyes. It has no ears. It navigates by touch and by a sense you cannot identify, a sensitivity to the vibrations of the stone that is so acute it can detect the movement of water through a wall three meters away.

It makes a nest. It gathers the old rags and grease-soaked fibers and mineral-stained cloth that accumulate in the machinery's crevices, and it arranges them into a depression — a bowl-shaped hollow in the grease and dust — and it curls into them with a slowness that is almost geological. It sleeps. It wakes. It extends a digit into a seam in the metal and holds it there, motionless, for minutes or hours, reading the vibrations of the infrastructure the way a doctor reads a pulse.

It is alone. The nesters — if there is more than one, and the evidence suggests there is not — do not aggregate. Each one has its machine, its hollow, its nest. They are solitary in the way that certain deep-sea creatures are solitary: not by preference but by the mathematics of their world. There is not enough to support more than one. The energy budget of the Seepworks is tight, and the nesters exist on its margins, feeding on the bacterial films that grow on old grease, on the fungal threads that colonize dead organic matter, on whatever small crustaceans they can coax into range with the vibration of a single extended digit.

They are old, or they are the descendants of something old. You cannot tell. They do not accumulate mineral deposit the way the crustaceans do. They do not leave trails the way the eel-things do. They leave almost nothing — a nest, disturbed, that will be re-made; a hollow in the grease, shaped to a body; and then they are gone, deeper into the machine, into a passage you cannot follow because it was never a passage, only the space between components, designed for maintenance by hands that have been dead for longer than the nesters have been alive.

## VI. The Drifters

In the bioluminescent zones, in the faint blue-green light that falls from the shallow glass trays mounted at junctions and doorways, there are insects.

They are delicate. Their wings are thin as wet paper — thinner — and they hold them open at rest, flat, like pressed specimens in a case that no one will ever open. They are translucent. In the blue-green light, they are barely visible at all: you see the light through them, and then you see the slight distortion the light undergoes as it passes through their bodies, and then you understand that there is something there.

They feed on the bioluminescent cultures. Or they feed on the biofilm that forms on the glass trays. Or they feed on the bacterial mats that grow on the surface of the pools nearest the trays. It is difficult to tell, because their mouthparts are adapted to scraping surfaces, and they move from tray to pool to tray again in a circuit that may be feeding strategy or may simply be the only pattern available to a creature with no predators and no need to avoid anything.

They are numerous. In the blue-green zones, there are dozens — hundreds — of them, drifting through the light in slow, aimless arcs. They collide with each other and do not react. They land on the walls and leave tiny marks in the mineral glaze — minute scratches, visible only when you look very closely — and these marks accumulate over years into a texture of their own, a micro-topography of wing-scars on stone.

They have no defense. They have no alarm call, no startle response, no flight-from-threat behavior. When you move through a bioluminescent zone, they drift aside the way dust drifts aside — not because they see you but because the air displacement of your passage pushes them. They settle again behind you, resuming their circuit, their slow grazing of the light.

They die, and their wings — those impossibly thin wings — do not decay so much as dissolve. They become part of the water, part of the mineral content, part of the film on the pool surfaces, part of the culture on the trays. The cycle is tight and complete. Nothing is wasted in the Seepworks. Nothing can afford to be.

## VII. The Unnamed

There is something in the pipes.

You hear it — or rather, you feel it — when the water pressure shifts. A displacement within the conduit, a disruption in the flow that is too large for a crustacean and too deliberate for an eel-thing. The pipes shudder, just slightly, and the water in a nearby pool rises by a millimeter and then falls, and for a moment there is a sound that is not water: a click, a scrape, a sound with *weight* to it, moving through the infrastructure like a thought through a mind.

You never see it. You see its effects: the cleared pipes, the free-flowing channels where others are calcified shut, the maintenance of a circulation that should have failed centuries ago. Something is moving through the arterial network of the Seepworks, something that fits the pipes, something that knows the pipes, something that has been doing this for so long that the doing has become indistinguishable from the pipes themselves.

The infrastructure works. Not all of it — much is dead, sealed, irrelevant. But the core channels — the deep ones, the ones that feed the cisterns and the bioluminescent trays and the pools where the crustaceans cluster — these flow. The water moves through them with a regularity that is not geological, not gravitational, not passive. It is *pumped*. It is *maintained*. Something moves through the pipes, and the water follows, and the Seepworks breathes.

It is large enough to create a pressure wave. It is small enough to fit inside a pipe that you could wrap your arms around. It is fast enough to traverse a channel in moments. It is patient enough to do this every day, every cycle, every season, without rest, without variation, without any sign of fatigue or cessation.

You will never see it. You will feel it in the stone beneath your feet, in the slight tremor that passes through the handrail when you grip it, in the way the water in a pool far from any channel ripples for a moment and then is still. It is the heartbeat of the Seepworks, and it does not know you are here, and it does not care.

## VIII. What Remains of Them

The people built.

They built in a place that was already occupied — by the crustaceans, by the eel-things, by the legged things and the drifters and the unnamed thing in the pipes. They built *among* them, the way you build a house in a forest, aware of the deer and the birds and the insects but not governing your architecture by their preferences. They sealed what they could. They channeled what they had to. They bolted iron to limestone and watched the calcium creep over their work, and they did not stop, and they did not leave — not for a long time.

The evidence of their presence is structural. It is in the riveted plates and the bulkheads and the lamp housings and the iron grating laid over dissolution cavities. It is in the channels carved and recarved to direct the flow. It is in the bioluminescent trays, though these may predate the people or postdate them — the cultures seem to propagate on their own, spreading from tray to tray along the water's surface, colonizing new glass where old glass has cracked.

There are no bodies. There are no personal effects. There are no tools or clothing or records. The people were practical, and when they left — if they left — they took what mattered and abandoned what didn't. Or they didn't leave. Or they are still here, in some form, in some passage you haven't found, in some chamber whose door is sealed with mineral deposit and fungal growth and the slow, patient weight of centuries.

The fauna does not remember them. The crustaceans sieve the water as they always have. The eel-things deposit their metal trails on the same channels the people carved. The legged things click through the same gaps between the same pipe-racks. The nesters curl in the hollows of the same dead machinery, and the bats roost in the same chambers, and the drifters drift through the same blue-green light.

The Seepworks does not distinguish between what was built and what grew. The iron is part of the stone now. The channels are part of the geology. The people are part of the ecology, their work woven so deeply into the fabric of this place that to remove it would be to unravel the structure entirely. They are here the way the aquifer is here: invisible, foundational, the reason everything above them exists.

And the water moves. And the machinery turns. And the fauna teems in its silence, and the light glows its faint blue-green, and the Seepworks breathes.

It breathes.
