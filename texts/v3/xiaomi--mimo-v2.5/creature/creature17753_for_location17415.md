# The Inhabitants of the Great Verdure

---

They do not all think of themselves as living in a place. Some of them do not think of places at all.

## The Mat-Walkers

They are the first thing you might mistake for the ground itself.

They are flat. Remarkably, deliberately flat — not compressed, not sick, but *designed* by some patient pressure to occupy the narrowest possible dimension between one surface and another. The largest of them are perhaps three meters long and no thicker than your palm. Their bodies are broad and soft, with a dorsal surface that is indistinguishable from the mat — not merely colored like it, not merely textured like it, but *composed of the same material*. They graze, and what they graze upon becomes their skin. Filaments of living growth anchor into their backs and grow outward, and they do not reject this. They encourage it. A mat-walker that has been still for a long time — hours, days — becomes genuinely difficult to separate from the substrate. The filaments from the mat reach across and connect to the filaments on the creature's back, and for a time they are one continuous surface, and the mat-walker is not hiding. It is *participating*.

They move by undulation. A slow, rolling motion that passes from head to tail and pushes them forward through the mat's upper layer, parting the cord-like filaments and sliding between them with a wet, sighing sound. They do not climb the columns. They do not need to. Everything they require is beneath them — the warm water that circulates through the root network, the softer growth at the mat's surface, the smaller organisms that live in the spaces between filaments. They are grazers of a world that grazes itself.

Their eyes are vestigial — not gone, but reduced to small dark spots on the leading edge of their bodies, more sensitive to vibration than to light. They sense you through the mat. The mat transmits your weight, your warmth, the micro-vibrations of your heartbeat, and the mat-walker knows you are there before you know it is. It does not flee. It does not need to. What would you do with it? It looks like ground. It tastes like ground. It is warm and damp and faintly sweet and it has no bones and no organs in the way you would recognize organs — just a loose network of vessels that pull water and nutrients from the mat the way the mat pulls water and nutrients from itself.

You step on one once. You don't feel it until it moves — a gentle, rolling shift beneath your boot that makes you look down and see the ground rearranging itself into a long, flat, living shape that slides away from you with the unhurried pace of a thing that has never been eaten by anything and does not understand fear.

There are thousands of them. Perhaps millions. They are one of the most successful body plans this plateau has ever produced. They do not compete with anything. They do not defend territory. They exist in the mat the way the mat exists in itself — diffusely, quietly, completely.

## The Column-Shearers

You find their work before you find them.

The woven columns bear scars — long, curving gouges where the outer layers of braided stems have been peeled back, exposing the paler interior. The damage is always at roughly the same height: two to four meters above the mat surface, in a band that corresponds to the reach of something substantial. The exposed tissue weeps more heavily than the intact surface, and small organisms colonize the wounds quickly, but the wounds are deep enough that the column's structural integrity is not threatened. This is not destruction. It is harvesting.

The shearers themselves are quadrupedal, heavy-bodied, and built low to the ground. They are the size of large cattle, though they would be unrecognizable as such — their bodies are long and deep-chested, their legs short and thick, their heads blunt and almost featureless except for the mouthparts. The mouthparts are extraordinary. Two sets of mandibles, one positioned above the other, each set lined with ridges that are not teeth but something closer to rasps — hardened tissue that can grip and tear along the grain of braided wood with a slow, grinding efficiency. They eat the columns. Not all of them — not enough to kill the organism — but enough, over time, to shape them. The columns that survive shearers grow differently: thicker, denser, with more tightly braided outer layers that resist tearing. An arms race conducted at the speed of centuries.

The shearers have no eyes. Not vestigial ones. None at all. Their heads are smooth where eyes would be, covered in the same tough, damp skin that covers the rest of their bodies. They navigate by smell and by a sensitivity to the hum — that deep, planetary vibration that sits beneath everything. They can feel the metabolic signatures of different organisms through the mat, distinguishing a healthy column from a weakened one, identifying the densest growth, the richest feeding. They are, in a sense, reading the landscape the way a musician reads an instrument — feeling the resonance, knowing where the sound is fullest.

They travel in small groups. Three, five, seven. Not families — there is no evidence of social bonding, no grooming, no communication that you can detect. They simply move in the same direction at roughly the same speed, drawn by the same chemical signals, harvesting as they go. When two shearers approach the same column from opposite sides, they do not acknowledge each other. They work the same organism independently, stripping different faces, their mandibles grinding in a rhythm that occasionally synchronizes and then drifts apart. It sounds like two hands sawing through wet wood, slightly out of time with each other.

Their skin is host to the same kind of symbiotic growth that the mat-walkers carry, but the shearers' growth is different — tougher, more fibrous, forming a thick mat of their own across their backs and flanks. Some of the shearers are so heavily overgrown that you would mistake them for a low mound of vegetation if you didn't see them move. They carry small ecosystems. Beetles. Fungi. The thin, creeping tendrils of something that might be a relative of the frond-plants, growing in the sheltered spaces between the shearers' legs and body. They are walking habitats.

They are never still for long. Even at rest, their mandibles work — grinding, chewing, processing the fibrous mass that fills their gut. The digestive process is slow, aided by a colony of organisms that live in the shearers' gut and break down the braided wood into usable nutrients. In return, the gut colony receives warmth, moisture, and a constant supply of raw material. The shearers do not merely carry ecosystems on their skin. They carry them in their bodies. They are more colony than individual — a walking consensus of a hundred different species, all agreeing, without ever negotiating, to continue being the same thing.

## The Veil-Dancers

They live in the mist.

You see them rarely and never clearly. They are aerial — or nearly so — occupying the space between the ground and the lower canopy where the mist is thickest. Their bodies are translucent, gelatinous, shaped like broad leaves but capable of independent movement, drifting through the saturated air on currents so slight they barely qualify as wind. They are flat, like the mat-walkers, but oriented vertically — they hang in the air like flags, their surfaces catching the moisture that saturates everything, absorbing it directly through their skin.

They have no limbs. No eyes. No mouth. They feed by absorption, the same way the mat feeds, pulling water and dissolved nutrients directly from the air. The air here is rich enough with organic compounds, with the chemical exhalations of a billion organisms, that a creature can sustain itself on nothing but breathing — except it doesn't breathe, exactly, because it has no respiratory system. Its entire body is a membrane. It is all surface. It is a living interface between the wet air and itself, and the distinction between those two things is, for the veil-dancer, optional.

They move by changing their shape. A subtle contraction here, a relaxation there, and the interaction between their broad surface and the mist creates a differential pressure that pushes them in one direction or another. They are slow. Agonizingly slow. But they have nowhere to be. The mist carries them where the mist goes, and they adjust their trajectory only slightly, the way a leaf adjusts its fall. In the deep mist, when visibility drops below ten meters, you walk through clouds of them — dozens, hundreds, hanging motionless in the air, their translucent bodies barely visible until you are close enough to feel the cool displacement of air as your body pushes through their space. They do not move out of your way. They have no reason to. You will not harm them, and they know this — not through any calculation but through the simple fact that they have always been touched by large moving things and have always continued.

Sometimes, in the rare moments when the mist thins slightly and the light deepens, you can see them from a distance. They hang in the spaces between the columns like curtains, like veils, their surfaces catching the faint light and becoming briefly, faintly visible — a shimmer, a suggestion of form, a pattern that might be organic and might be an artifact of the way your eye processes low light in a world of near-uniform green. They are most visible when they move together, when a shift in the air currents sends a group of them drifting in the same direction, and for a moment you see a field of translucent shapes moving through the mist like a slow, silent migration, and then the mist thickens again and they are gone and you are not sure they were ever there.

## The Deep Listeners

You hear them before you understand what you're hearing.

A sound, deep and resonant, rising from somewhere below the mat's surface. Not the hum — not that constant planetary vibration — but something more structured, more deliberate. A pattern. Low tones, sustained for seconds, rising and falling in sequences that repeat but never quite identically. It sounds like chanting. It sounds like a language spoken through walls of wet stone. It sounds, if you press your ear to the mat, like something enormous breathing in a place you cannot reach.

They live in the deep substrate. Below the mat's surface — not at the top, not in the soft sponge you walk on, but further down, in the dense peat-like mass that has been consolidating for millennia, and perhaps deeper still, in the mineralized layers where plant has become something closer to rock. They are large. The resonance of their calls suggests a body cavity of significant size — meters, at least. They are resonating with the mat itself, using the living substrate as a sounding board, amplifying their voices through the root network until the vibrations carry for kilometers.

No one has seen a deep listener in full. Fragments have been found: sections of smooth, dark material, curved like ribs but far larger than any rib should be, buried in the substrate and coated in growth. The material is not bone. It is not wood. It is something new — a biological mineral, produced by an organism that has spent so long in the deep, pressurized layers that its body has incorporated the substrate into its own structure. It is partly alive and partly stone. It is warm when you dig it up, and it vibrates faintly, and it stops vibrating after a time away from the deep.

They communicate. The patterns in their calls are too structured to be random — sequences of tones, variations in duration, pauses that seem to serve grammatical functions. What they say is unknown. They may not be speaking to each other — or they may be speaking to the mat, which transmits their vibrations throughout the plateau, which carries them to other deep listeners in other places, building a conversation that spans the entire width of this ancient, flat, endlessly alive land. The conversation may be older than any surface organism. It may have been going on, uninterrupted, for longer than the woven columns have existed, longer than the mat has covered the stone beneath it. It may be, in fact, the thing that the hum is made of — the accumulated weight of a thousand deep listeners all speaking at once, their voices combining into that constant, below-threshold vibration that you feel in your chest and mistake for the sound of the world itself.

## The Skin-Shedders

They are small. The size of your hand, or smaller. You notice them because of what they leave behind.

Shed skins. Thin, papery casings, perfect in their detail — every limb, every eye, every delicate antenna rendered in a translucent membrane that holds its shape for a few hours before the mat absorbs it. They litter the surfaces of the fronds and the lower sections of the woven columns, hanging from filaments like abandoned clothing. There are thousands of them. They are everywhere. They are one of the most common signs of life in the Verdure, and they tell you almost nothing about the creature that produced them.

The shedders themselves are hard to find because they are always in the process of becoming something else. They are the most visibly metamorphic organisms on the plateau — and on a plateau where the mat-walkers grow their own camouflage and the shearers carry entire ecosystems on their backs, this is saying something. A shedder begins life as a larva that is nearly indistinguishable from the mat: flat, green-brown, filament-covered, practically invisible. It feeds on the mat's surface, growing slowly, and when it has grown enough, it sheds its skin for the first time and emerges as something different — still small, but now three-dimensional, now vertical, with legs that can grip the frond surfaces and eyes that can resolve movement at a distance of perhaps a meter.

The transformation continues. Each shed is more dramatic than the last. The third shed produces wings — not functional ones, but wing-buds that suggest the direction of evolution. The fourth shed produces a body shape that resembles, vaguely, the canopy creatures above: elongated, with a distinct head and thorax and a set of limbs that could be legs or could be arms. The fifth shed, if they live that long, produces an organism that is almost unrecognizable as the thing that started — a sleek, quick creature with large eyes and a body that can fold itself flat against a surface with startling speed, vanishing into the green architecture of the mid-canopy.

They live their entire lives — all five stages, all five skins — in a vertical space of perhaps thirty meters. They do not travel horizontally. They do not cross the mat. They exist in the canopy, in the fronds, in the dim green spaces between the woven columns, and their world is measured not in distance but in height. Up is young. Down is old. The deepest layer, nearest the mat, is where they begin and where they die and where their shed skins accumulate and feed the very substrate they started from.

The adults — the fifth-stage creatures, the ones with functional wings and eyes that can see — are rarely seen because they do not move when they are afraid. And they are afraid often. They are small, and the world is full of things that might eat them, and their survival strategy is absolute stillness combined with absolute camouflage. You brush past a frond and a hundred tiny wings fold flat and a hundred tiny bodies press against the green surface and become part of it, and you would swear there was nothing there, and you would be wrong.

## The Root-Walkers

They are the most disconcerting.

They walk on the roots. Not the roots of any particular plant — the root network itself, that vast, interconnected web of living tissue that runs just beneath the mat's surface and extends, in some form, across the entire plateau. The root-walkers have adapted to this infrastructure with a specificity that borders on obsession. Their legs are long, thin, and jointed in ways that allow them to step precisely on the raised ridges where root channels run closest to the surface. They walk on the ridges and between the ridges and their gait is alien — a stilted, mechanical rhythm that has nothing to do with the soft, flowing movements of most Verdure fauna. They walk like insects walking on water. They distribute their weight along the root channels, which are stronger than the surrounding mat, and they move with a purposefulness that stands out sharply in a world where most creatures drift or slide or undulate.

They are tall. For this world, startlingly tall — a meter and a half, most of which is leg. Their bodies are small, narrow, almost negligible, perched atop those long legs like a thought perched atop a long chain of reasoning. Their heads are featureless from a distance — smooth, dark, damp — but up close they reveal a cluster of sensory organs that are not eyes and not antennae but something in between, fine filaments that wave in the air and taste the chemical signatures of the root network beneath them.

They are foragers. They walk the root network and they find the places where the flow is richest — the channels where the most water and the most nutrients are moving — and they feed by inserting a proboscis directly into the root channel, tapping into the plateau's circulatory system like a parasite tapping a vein. The root does not resist. The wound heals quickly, sealed by the same regenerative processes that keep the entire network alive. The root-walkers are not welcome, exactly, but they are not unwelcome either. They are a pressure, a persistent low-level drain on the system, and the system has adapted to accommodate them the way it accommodates everything — by growing around them, through them, over them, eventually.

They travel in lines. Not organized lines — there is no leader, no communication — but lines that form because the root network has optimal routes, and the optimal routes are the same for everyone, and so the root-walkers walk the same paths and pass each other going in opposite directions and never acknowledge each other's existence. They have no social behavior whatsoever. They are solitary walkers on a shared road, each one convinced — to the extent that they are capable of conviction — that it is the only thing that matters.

When a root-walker dies — and they die often, because their bodies are fragile and their legs are delicate and a single misstep into the soft mat can trap them — it sinks. The mat closes over it with a slow, patient finality that takes hours. The legs fold. The body compresses. The filaments of the mat reach up and grip and pull and within a day there is nothing to see but a slight depression in the surface that fills with water and then fills with growth and then fills with everything else, and the root-walker becomes what it always was walking on: substrate.

## The Still-Eaters

The name is wrong, but no one has a better one.

They do not eat stillness. They eat the things that are still — and in a world where stillness is the dominant survival strategy, there is no shortage of prey. They are ambush predators. They are also, almost certainly, the most intelligent things on the plateau.

They are large. Not the largest — the deep listeners are larger, probably, and the shearers are heavier — but large enough to eat a mat-walker or a root-walker or even a young shearere. They are shaped roughly like the columns: tall, narrow, vertical, with a surface texture that mimics the braided wood with an accuracy that goes beyond camouflage into something closer to mimicry. They do not merely look like the columns. They *are* like the columns — or rather, the columns are like them, or both are like something else, some ancient convergence of form that has produced two superficially identical things from entirely different lineages. The still-eater's body is a single mass, unsegmented, with a surface of tough, woven-looking tissue that is not braided stems but a single organism's integument, evolved over uncountable generations to replicate the specific texture and color and weeping behavior of the columns it stands among.

They stand. That is what they do. They stand among the columns, in the gaps where columns would naturally grow, and they do not move for days, weeks, months. They are patient beyond the patience of any creature you have ever encountered. The mat grows over their feet. The fronds press against their flanks. The small organisms that colonize every surface colonize them, building their miniature ecosystems on the still-eater's body, and the still-eater permits this because the colonization completes the disguise. A still-eater that has been standing in the same spot for a year is invisible. It is a column. It is one more vertical element in the dense architecture of the canopy, and nothing — not the mat-walkers, not the shedders, not the canopy creatures above — distinguishes it from the living wood around it.

Then something comes close enough.

The strike is not fast. That is the unsettling part. In a world where everything is slow and patient and deliberate, the still-eater's attack is merely *less* slow. It unfolds. A section of the column-shape peels open — a mouth, a whole side of the body opening on hidden hinges — and the thing inside is wet and dark and lined with structures that grip and hold. The prey is engulfed rather than bitten. The still-eater closes around it the way the mat closes around a dead root-walker: slowly, inevitably, with a pressure that is not violent but is irresistible. The engulfed creature is held against the still-eater's inner surface, where specialized tissues begin to absorb it. Not digest it in the way you would understand digestion. *Absorb* it. The same way the mat absorbs the shed skins and the dead walkers and the fallen columns. The same way the Verdure absorbs everything.

The process takes days. The still-eater does not move while it feeds. It closes back into its column shape and stands where it was standing and the mat continues to grow over its feet and the fronds continue to press against its flanks and the small organisms continue their colonization and the only evidence that anything has happened is that the still-eater is very slightly wider than it was before and the prey is gone.

They are rare. They cannot be common — a predator of this strategy requires space, requires low density, requires a landscape in which the prey can approach without noticing. The Verdure provides all of these things. The landscape is vast and uniform and dim and full of creatures that have evolved to be invisible, and the still-eater has evolved to be the invisible thing that other invisible things do not see. It is a recursion of camouflage, a hall of mirrors made of green wet tissue, and somewhere in the mirrors is a mouth.

## The Old Ones

You will not see them. You will not know they are there.

They are the descendants — or perhaps the continuations — of the beings who built the stone stacks and the clear circles and the triple-helix columns. They have not built anything in a very long time. They may have forgotten how. They may have decided not to. They may have become something for which building is no longer a meaningful concept.

They live in the deep places, near the deep listeners, in the mineralized layers where plant has become stone and stone has become something that vibrates. They are small now. They were large once — the scale of the built remnants suggests that — but the deep places are compressed and dark and old, and the beings that inhabit them have compressed and darkened and aged in response. They are the size of your fist. They are smooth. They are warm. They have no features that you would recognize as features — no eyes, no limbs, no mouth. They are ovoid, dark, damp, and they sit in the spaces between the mineralized roots, and they pulse.

The pulse is slow. Slower than the hum, slower than the deep listeners' calls, slower than anything you can measure with a heartbeat. It is a metabolic rhythm, a heartbeat of the body politic, and it syncs — over days, over weeks — with the pulse of the mat above them. They are connected. Through kilometers of living substrate, through root networks and water channels and the accumulated mass of uncountable generations of growth, they are connected to the surface, to the mat-walkers and the column-shearers and the veil-dancers and everything else, and the connection is not communication, exactly. It is something older and simpler and more profound. It is *coherence*. They are the same system. They have always been the same system. The distinction between the Old Ones and the landscape is as meaningless as the distinction between the mat and the substrate beneath it.

They do not think in any way you would recognize. But they are aware. The pulse suggests awareness — a response to stimulus, an adjustment to conditions, a slow, deep knowing that the surface is wet and the columns are growing and the mat-walkers are moving and the deep listeners are speaking. They know all of this the way your body knows its own heartbeat: constantly, unconsciously, fundamentally.

If you could ask them what they remember of building — of standing upright in the dim light and stacking stones with hands that had fingers, in a time before the mat swallowed everything — they would not answer. Not because they refuse. Because the question does not parse. Memory requires a separation between self and experience, between the one who remembers and the thing remembered. They have no such separation. They are the experience. They are the mat. They are the deep substrate and the surface sponge and the weeping columns and the mist and the silence and the hum. They are the Great Verdure, and the Great Verdure is them, and the stones they stacked are warm and damp and covered in growth and they do not speak because they have become a place, and places do not speak. They simply are.

## What Remains

And so the plateau continues.

The mat-walkers undulate. The shearers grind. The veil-dancers hang. The deep listeners call. The shedders shed. The root-walkers walk their ridges. The still-eaters stand and wait. The Old Ones pulse in the deep.

None of them know each other. None of them need to. They are held together not by knowledge or society or even ecological interdependence in any simple sense, but by the mat itself — by the living substrate that connects every root, every channel, every organism that has ever lived here and been absorbed here and become part of the substrate's own slow, ongoing self-creation.

You stand on it. You feel the pulse. You feel the hum. You feel, if you are still enough and patient enough and willing enough to stop being a visitor and start being a surface — just for a moment, just for the duration of a held breath — the extraordinary, quiet, green enormity of a world that has solved the problem of existence by becoming one continuous, living, breathing, dripping, patient thing.

It does not need you. It does not notice you. It will absorb you, eventually, with the same gentle thoroughness with which it absorbs everything.

And you will become mat. And you will become substrate. And you will become the pulse.

And it will be very, very quiet.
