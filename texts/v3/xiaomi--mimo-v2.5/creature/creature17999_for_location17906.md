No one lives in the Grown City, and everyone does.

There are no citizens, no residents in the way a surface city knows them. There are only participants. The city itself is the primary organism, a sprawling colonial superstructure of calcium and chitin and time, and everything within it is either a part of its body or a symbiont living in its tissues. The distinction is fluid.

The truest inhabitants, the ones who define the city’s persistence, are the **Reef-Weavers**. They are the descendants—metaphorical, not literal—of the original builders. A colony of them appears as a single, slow-moving entity: a sheet of pale, translucent tissue, a meter across, studded with thousands of tiny, pulsating polyps. Each polyp is a factory, extruding calcium carbonate from seawater, depositing it grain by grain. They flow over surfaces like living mortar. When a wall thins or a passage narrows dangerously, a Weaver colony will migrate to the stress point. They are the city’s immune system, its bone-setters. They do not know they are architects. They respond to chemical gradients—the scent of a fresh fracture, the specific protein signature of exposed structural polymer—and they simply build where building is needed. A Weaver colony is always moving, always working, leaving behind a hardened trail of new growth. Their “thinking” is the distributed intelligence of a neural net the size of a floor mat.

Then there are the **Siphon-Keepers**. These are not a single species but a guild of sessile organisms that form the living skin of the city’s corridors. Feather-duster worms with radioles that unfurl like intricate, living gears. Clusters of bivalves, their shells calcified into the wall itself, their siphons siphoning the current for plankton and detritus. Bryozoans, forming lacy, living mats over every surface. They are the city’s first line of digestion and its first line of defense, turning the water that flows through the city’s "lungs" into sustenance and waste. Their collective filter-feeding cleans the water, and their growth repairs micro-fissures. They are the wallpaper, the plumbing, and the sewage treatment, all at once.

Navigating the spaces between are the **Drifters** and the **Crawlers**. The Drifters are the swarms: clouds of translucent, shrimp-like phyllopods, their compound eyes reduced to light-sensitive spots. They move in loose, shimmering balls, their antennae tasting the chemical wakes of other creatures, communicating through pheromone pulses that ripple through the swarm like silent thoughts. They are the street sweepers, picking at the biofilm on the walls, the detritus on the floors. They are prey, and they are scavengers. Their movements are a kind of weather, a constant, gentle motion that is the city’s pulse.

The Crawlers are the deliberate navigators. Fish-like in silhouette, but with elongated, sensitive pelvic fins they use to feel the walls, maintaining perfect spacing in the narrowest tunnels. They are blind in any meaningful sense, but their lateral lines are exquisite, reading pressure changes with the precision of sonar. They know every current, every eddy, every slight change in water texture that signals a chamber ahead or a junction to the left. They are the couriers, the patrol, though they patrol for nothing. They simply go where their bodies tell them to, following the most efficient chemical gradient to food or mates.

And then there are the **Chambered Ancients**. The slow, whale-sized creatures that inhabit the largest vaulted spaces. They are likely a type of deep-sea gastropod, their shells no longer needed for protection grown over with a thick mantle of tissue, making them look like smooth, pulsating mounds of flesh. They barely move. They are metabolic fortresses, processing the nutrient-poor water with immense, inefficient slowness. Their great, heaving gills create the faintest currents, a respiration that gently ventilates the inner passages. Their waste is a rich, organic rain that settles and is consumed by the siphons and crawlers below. They are the city’s silent engines, turning low-grade energy into the high-grade waste that fuels the core. To them, the city is not a city. It is simply the substrate on which they rest, the familiar pressure and current of home.

The most profound interaction is not between these inhabitants, but between them and the **Current**. The Current is the city’s breath, its information highway, its delivery system. It carries food in, carries waste out, carries the chemical whispers of a damaged wall to the Reef-Weavers, carries the pheromones of a mate to the Crawlers. It carries the larvae of the Siphon-Keepers to new, unclaimed walls downstream, and the spores of the Weavers to distant, crumbling structures. Every organism is oriented to the Current. To face it is to feed; to have it at your back is to disperse. The Current is the city’s only law.

In this warm, pressurized dark, there is no sight to deceive. There is only the absolute reality of touch, chemistry, and pressure. The city is not a place where life lives. The city *is* the life. The walls are alive. The corridors are alive. The water itself, thick with the scent of a million living processes, is alive. A visitor from the surface would find no one home, and everywhere a home. They would see a silent, empty ruin, and in truth, they would be standing in the middle of the loudest, busiest, most densely packed metropolis their world could conceive of—a civilization of instinct, maintained not by thought, but by the relentless, beautiful, and utterly unconscious imperative to live.
