# The People of the Unwinding

## Bodies and Breath

They are not quite what the word *people* suggests. They are enough like people that the word sticks, but only just.

Their bodies carry the plateau in them. Not as metaphor — as *fact*. Generations of thin air and cold stone and food that grows from rock have shaped them into something the lowland body is not. They are tall, as has been said, but the tallness is not uniform. Some are tall in the way of stretched fiber — narrow-shouldered, long-armed, with fingers that seem to have an extra joint, though they do not. Others are tall in the way of stone columns — broader, heavier, with a density to their frames that suggests their bones are thicker than they should be, or made of something closer to calcium deposit than calcium itself. Both kinds are common. Both kinds are family. No one considers either unusual.

Their skin is pale, but not uniformly. There are patches, on most of them, where the skin has taken on a faintly mineral quality — a slightly grey undertone, a texture that, if you look closely, echoes the fine stratification of the cliff faces. It is not disease. It is not mutation. It is what happens when a body lives for long enough in a place where the boundary between organism and landscape is *negotiable*. The people do not discuss this feature of their appearance. It is as unremarkable to them as freckles would be to a lowlander.

Their eyes are the thing visitors would notice first, if visitors came. They are large. Not grotesquely — not in the way of insects or deep-sea creatures — but large enough that the first impression is of *receptivity*. Their eyes seem built for gathering light, for taking in the vast pale distances, for reading the texture of mist and the grain of silence. The iris, in most people, is a color that shifts with the light — sometimes grey, sometimes a pale blue that is almost silver, sometimes, in the deepest basins where the mist is thickest, a color that approaches the unnamed shade of the wrong-colored stones. They blink slowly. They blink *deliberately*, as though each blink is a considered decision to briefly close the world and then reopen it, to check that it is still there and still what it was.

They breathe differently. Not in any way that would be medically significant — their lungs are lungs, their diaphragms are diaphragms — but in *rhythm*. The people of the plateau breathe in a pattern that is synchronized, at a level below conscious coordination, with the slow cycling of mist through the basins. When the mist thickens, they breathe more slowly. When it thins, their breathing quickens slightly. It is as though the plateau's atmospheric metabolism and theirs have reached an accommodation, a shared cadence that has been refining itself for centuries. Visitors — if there were visitors, if the lowlands still sent them, which they have not for a very long time — would find this rhythm difficult to match. They would feel always slightly out of step, slightly *behind* the breath of the place, and this would contribute to the unease that the plateau generates in anyone who does not belong to it.

The people do not shiver. They feel cold — they are not numb, not adapted into some thermodynamic miracle — but the response to cold in their bodies is not *shivering*. It is something closer to *settling*. They draw inward, compress slightly, become denser. Their movements slow further — though *further* from the already-slow baseline is a degree of stillness that can be alarming to witness. A cold person on the plateau, standing in the mist, is very nearly indistinguishable from a stone. This is not camouflage. It is *conservation*. The body, like the land, finds the posture that wastes nothing.

## Names and Naming

The naming problem is not trivial. It is, in fact, one of the foundational puzzles of life on the plateau, and the people's relationship to it reveals more about their character than almost anything else.

Names here are *assigned*, not *chosen*, and the assigner is not the parent or the elder or any human authority. The assigner is the *first sound the child makes*. Not the first cry — cries are universal, cries are biology, and biology is only intermittently relevant on the plateau. The first *sound*. The first expression that has character, that carries *something* in it beyond mere reflex. Sometimes this happens at birth. Sometimes it happens days later, or weeks. The sound is received by whoever is closest, and that sound becomes the child's name, and it is never changed, and it is never shortened, and it is never turned into a nickname, because the sound is not a *label* — it is a *record*, a permanent notation of the moment this person first participated in the sonic landscape of the plateau.

The result is that names are *strange*. They are not words in any language. They are vocalizations — short, usually two or three syllables, often untranslatable, often carrying overtones that the human voice does not typically produce. Some names sound like water on stone. Some sound like wind across a basin. Some sound like nothing that has an analog in the natural world, and these are the names that are spoken most quietly, because they are the ones that seem to come from *somewhere else* — from the folding-zones, from the thin places, from whatever is on the other side of the erosion of reality.

When two people meet, they say each other's names. Not as greeting — greeting on the plateau is accomplished through *presence*, through the simple fact of being in the same space — but as *confirmation*. The sound of a name, spoken by another voice, is a way of saying: *you are here, you are real, you are still the person you were when you first made that sound*. The people say each other's names frequently, in quiet voices, almost *murmuring* them, and the effect, to an outsider, would be of a community perpetually engaged in a low, collective act of *verifying its own existence*.

The settlement itself has names, many names, and they change depending on who speaks them and from where. This is not chaos. It is *accuracy*. The settlement, seen from the western basins, *is* different from the settlement seen from the eastern ridges. The name is not a fixed label but a *description from a position*, and since positions are many and shifting, names are many and shifting. The people navigate this without difficulty, the way a lowlander navigates the fact that the same building can be called *the house on the corner* from one street and *the house across from the park* from another. The difference is that on the plateau, the names do not resolve into a single identity. There is no "true name" of the settlement. There is only the name that corresponds to where you are standing when you say it.

Some elders have noticed that the names are changing more rapidly than they used to. That a name which held steady for a generation will now shift within a season. They do not discuss this with the young, because they do not have the words for what it might mean, and because on the plateau, the correct response to a change you cannot explain is *attention*, not *alarm*.

## The Practice of Yielding

If you asked the people of the plateau what their most important cultural practice is, they would look at you with those large, shifting eyes and they would say something like: *We yield*. And they would mean it with absolute seriousness, and they would not elaborate, and the lack of elaboration would not be evasion — it would be because the concept is so fundamental to their existence that it resists explanation the way gravity resists explanation to a creature that has never left the ground.

Yielding is not submission. It is not defeat. It is not the passive acceptance of something imposed from outside. Yielding, as the people practice it, is an *active, skilled, cultivated discipline* — the discipline of *not resisting what the place is asking*. And what the place asks is different every day, and different in every basin, and different for every person, and the skill lies in *listening well enough to know what is being asked*.

A builder who is laying stone yields to the stone's own grain, its own stratification, its own preference for how it wishes to sit. To build against the grain is to create a wall that will crack — not from structural failure, but from *disagreement*, from a fundamental disharmony between the intent of the builder and the intent of the material. The people do not have a word for this. They do not need one. It is simply the case that stone has preferences, and a good builder is one who discovers them.

A gardener yields to the moss. You do not plant moss on the plateau. You *receive* it. You prepare a space — a basin, a crevice, a damp shelf of stone — and you wait, and the moss comes, and it comes in whatever form and color and pattern it chooses, and the gardener's job is to *tend what arrives*, not to dictate what should arrive. The Fibonacci spirals are accepted because they are what the moss *chose to do*. The stone-penetrating ground-cover is accepted because it is what the ground-cover *chose to be*. The listening plants are accepted because they are listening, and listening is an orientation that the gardener respects.

A weaver yields to the silence. The act of weaving — long strips of dried moss-stem into cordage and simple textiles — is performed in absolute quiet, and the pattern of the weave is not predetermined. The weaver's hands move, and the pattern emerges, and the pattern is *whatever the silence between the threads wants to be*. There are no looms. The weaving is done in the hands, free-form, and the results are unpredictable and beautiful and absolutely unique, and a piece of plateau weaving, held up to the light, will show a pattern that does not repeat, that cannot be replicated, that is — in some way the people understand but cannot articulate — a *map of the silence that existed at the moment of its creation*.

Yielding extends to *everything*. Yielding to the cold, which means not fighting it but *incorporating it* — wearing clothes that insulate through density rather than thickness, eating foods that generate slow, sustained warmth rather than quick heat, sleeping in positions that conserve rather than expend. Yielding to the mist, which means traveling when the mist is favorable and staying still when it is not, without frustration, without impatience, without any sense that the mist *should* be other than it is. Yielding to the folding-zones, which means knowing where they are and what they do and *not trying to understand why they do it*. Yielding to the weirdness, which means living beside it the way you live beside a river — aware of it, respectful of it, drawing sustenance from it, but never stepping into its current without purpose.

The people who are best at yielding are the ones the community turns to in times of difficulty — though *difficulty* on the plateau looks different from difficulty elsewhere. There are no wars, no predators, no famines (the botanical diet is sparse but reliable, and the people's metabolism is efficient enough that they need less than a lowlander would). The difficulties are *subtle*. A basin that has stopped collecting water. A moss species that has begun growing in a pattern no one has seen before. A folding-zone that has shifted its boundaries, swallowing a path that was safe and spitting out a new one that is not. A night when the sound — the deep, everywhere, nowhere sound — is louder than anyone can remember.

In these moments, the yielders sit. They sit at the edge of the affected area, in the mist, in the cold, in the silence, and they *listen*. Not with their ears — or not only with their ears. With their skin, with their bones, with the mineral patches on their skin that may or may not be something more than cosmetic. They sit for hours, for days sometimes, and they yield to whatever the plateau is doing, and eventually they rise, and they say something to the community — something simple, something like *it is moving east* or *it will stop* or *we need to move the storehouse* — and the community moves, or waits, or adjusts, and the yielder goes back to their life, and no one thanks them, because gratitude implies that they did something *extraordinary*, and what they did was simply *pay attention*.

## Craft and Accumulation

The tools are beautiful.

This is not an aesthetic judgment imposed from outside. The people of the plateau would agree, though they would express it differently. They would say the tools are *settled*. They would say the tools are *where they want to be*. What they mean is that each tool — each stone blade, each bone needle, each wooden implement — has been refined over generations to the point where it is no longer merely *functional*. It has crossed some threshold into *rightness*. The handle of a stone chisel, worn by a hundred hands over two hundred years, has become shaped not just to the hand that holds it but to *the act of chiseling itself* — it is the idealized form of that gesture, compressed into wood and stone, and holding it, any person of the settlement feels the accumulated intention of every chisel-strike that has ever been made with it.

The tools are not inherited in the lowland sense. They are not passed from parent to child. They are *returned to the workshop* when their current user is done with them — done, not dead; the plateau does not have a strong sense of the distinction, given that time sometimes passes differently in certain buildings — and they are *tended* by the workshop's keeper, and they are *given out again* when someone needs them, and the person who receives a tool does not ask who used it before them, because the tool *tells* them. Not in words. In the weight of it, the balance of it, the way it moves when it is put to work. Every tool carries the memory of its use in its body, and a sensitive hand can feel that memory, and the tool becomes, in the act of being used, a *conversation between the current user and every previous one*.

This is the closest the people come to what a lowlander would call *technology*. They have not progressed. They have not invented. They have *deepened*. A stone knife made on the plateau today is not significantly different in *design* from one made a thousand years ago. But it is different in *quality* — in the fineness of its edge, in the resonance of its balance, in the degree to which it has been *listened to* and *yielded to* until it became what it wanted to be. The accumulation of skill on the plateau is not horizontal — it does not spread outward into new inventions. It is *vertical*. It goes *down*, into the stone, into the silence, into the increasing perfection of a small number of forms.

The people do not value novelty. They value *depth*. A weaver who produces a pattern that has never been seen before is not celebrated for her creativity. She is *studied*, carefully, by other weavers, to determine whether what she produced was a genuine *response* to the silence or merely an *imposition* of her own will onto the silence. The former is respected. The latter is gently corrected — not through criticism, but through the simple act of other weavers sitting beside her and weaving their own pieces, letting her see the difference between a pattern that *comes from* the work and a pattern that *is put into* the work.

## Children and Growing

Children are rare.

Not because of any reproductive difficulty — though the people's biology has adapted to the plateau in ways that are not entirely understood, and fertility is *different* here, cycling in patterns that do not correspond to any lowland model. Children are rare because the plateau *selects for slowness*, and slowness includes slowness in繁殖. A couple may decide to have a child — *decide* is the wrong word; they may *yield to the possibility* of a child — and the process, from that yielding to the birth, takes longer than a lowlander would expect. The body moves slowly. The body, on the plateau, does everything slowly, including the creation of new life.

When a child is born, the naming ceremony — if it can be called a ceremony — is the most significant social event in the life of the settlement. Everyone comes. Not because they are required to, but because the sound a newborn makes is *news*. It is information about the plateau itself, delivered through the medium of a new human voice, and the people are hungry for this information in a way they would never articulate, because articulation would make it something it is not.

The child is held by the mother — or by whoever is closest, because parenthood on the plateau is a *community function* rather than a binary — and the mother (or the closest person) *waits*. And the child breathes the thin, cold, mist-laden air, and the child's body adjusts, and eventually the child makes a sound that is not a cry, that is not a gasp, that is something *specific* and *particular* and *new*, and that sound is heard, and it is named, and the name enters the community's language like a new note entering a chord.

The children grow slowly. They are children for a long time — not in the sense of immaturity, but in the sense of *duration*. A plateau child, at the age that a lowlander child would be running and shouting and breaking things, is sitting very still in a basin, watching moss grow, and this is not neglect. This is *education*. The child is learning the grain of the silence. The child is learning the rhythm of the mist. The child is learning, through direct and unmediated experience, the fundamental grammar of the place they live in, and this learning cannot be rushed any more than the erosion of stone can be rushed, and the people know this, and they are patient with an patience that would be incomprehensible to anyone who has not sat in the plateau's silence and felt it work on them.

Children do not play in the lowland sense. They do not run, or climb, or shout, or fight. They *engage*. They engage with the moss, with the stone, with the water, with the mist. They spend hours — whole days — in single basins, watching, touching, listening. They learn to feel the difference between stone that has been settled for a long time and stone that is still *in process*. They learn to read the mist's density and direction and predict where the seeps will appear. They learn to sit in the silence until the silence becomes *something* — not a sound, not a presence, but a *medium*, a substance they can move through with their awareness the way they move through the air with their bodies.

And some children — not all, but some — learn to *yield* so completely that they become something the community has no framework for.

## The Yielded

There are people on the plateau who have yielded *too much*.

Not *too much* in the sense of excess. Not *too much* in the sense of error. *Too much* in the sense of *completeness* — they have yielded so thoroughly to the place that the place has *taken them*, not violently, not dramatically, but in the way the stone takes the water, in the way the moss takes the light, in the way the silence takes the sound.

These people are still alive. They eat. They breathe. They move, though slowly — slower than the already-slow baseline, so slowly that they sometimes stand in a basin for hours without visibly shifting their weight. Their skin has become more mineral than not. The pale patches have spread until they cover most of their bodies, and the remaining skin is so thin you can see the fine tracery of veins beneath it, veins that carry blood that is, according to those who have seen it drawn for minor wounds, slightly *grey*.

They do not speak. They made sounds once — sounds that were their names, sounds that were conversation, sounds that were the ordinary business of being human in community — but at some point, the sounds stopped, and they did not resume, and no one knows exactly when the transition happened, because it happened gradually, the way the plateau erodes, and by the time anyone noticed, it was already done.

But they are not vacant. This is the thing that makes them both revered and unsettling. They are *present* — more present, in fact, than any other person in the settlement. Their eyes, which have taken on the color of the unnamed stone — that shifting, almost-color that is not quite blue and not quite green — are *focused*, intensely, on something no one else can see. They watch the basins. They watch the mist. They watch the folding-zones with an attention that suggests they can see the folds *opening*, can see whatever mechanism of reality is operating in those moments when space doubles back on itself.

The community calls them *the Settled*. Not *settled* as in *calm*, but *settled* as in *having completed the process of settling* — like sediment at the bottom of a pool, like dust on a long-abandoned shelf, like the stone itself after eons of being worn to its final form. The Settled have become, in some sense, *part of the plateau's own body*. They breathe with the mist. They are still in the way the stone is still. They are *there* in a way that the active, moving, speaking members of the community are not, because the active members are still in transit, still *becoming*, while the Settled have *arrived*.

People bring food to the Settled. The food is placed near them, and the Settled eat — eventually, slowly, with movements so deliberate they look like geological events — but the eating seems incidental. The Settled do not seem to need food in the way the others do. They seem to derive sustenance from something else — from the mist, from the stone, from the slow cycling of water through the ancient rock. Some elders believe the Settled photosynthesize, in some metaphorical or literal sense, drawing energy directly from the diffuse light that filters through the cloud cover. This belief is neither confirmed nor denied. On the plateau, the distinction between metaphor and mechanism is another thing that has worn thin.

The Settled occasionally *act*. Not often — perhaps once a season, perhaps less. They rise from wherever they have been sitting (and they sit in different places each time, though no one sees them move), and they walk — slowly, so slowly — to a specific location in the settlement or in the surrounding basins, and they *do something*. They touch a stone. They stand at the edge of a folding-zone. They place their hands in a seep. They stand in a particular spot and *face a particular direction* and remain there for hours. And after they have done this thing, the community notices a *change* — a seep that has moved, a folding-zone that has shifted, a patch of moss that has begun a new pattern, a night when the sound is different.

The Settled are not leaders. They are not consulted. They are not obeyed or deferred to. They are *phenomena*. They are part of the landscape in the way that the mist is part of the landscape, and the community relates to them with the same combination of familiarity, respect, and non-comprehension that they relate to the mist.

There is one Settled person — the oldest, the most mineralized, the most *still* — who has not moved from a specific basin in what the community estimates to be thirty years. They sit at the edge of the deepest pool, the one with the mirror-still surface that reflects a sky that might not be the real sky, and they have not eaten in anyone's memory, and they do not breathe visibly, and their skin is the same color as the stone they sit on, and in certain lights they are *invisible* — not hidden, not camouflaged, but genuinely *absent* from perception, as though the eye cannot distinguish between them and the rock.

The community leaves them alone. Completely alone. No one approaches the basin. No one speaks near it. The silence around this person is the densest, most *material* silence on the plateau, and it extends outward in a radius that the mosses and grasses respect — there is no vegetation in the basin, no moss, no ground-cover, only bare, ancient stone and the pool and the Settled person and the sky-reflection that might be another sky.

What they are doing, no one knows. What they have *become*, no one can say. But the community trusts them — trusts them the way you trust a mountain to remain a mountain, the way you trust the plateau to continue being the plateau. They are the furthest along a path that everyone is on, and the community watches them with the same patient, non-judgmental attention they give to everything else, and they wait, and the plateau erodes, and the mist moves, and the Settled person sits, and something — something vast and slow and incomprehensible — *continues*.

## The Evening Practice

Every evening, as the light fails — and the light fails slowly here, in long, pale transitions that take hours, as though the day is not ending but *unwinding* — the people gather.

Not everyone. Not in any fixed location. But enough people, in enough places, that the settlement takes on a particular quality in the evenings — a *softness*, a *concentration*, a sense that the scattered individuals of the day are drawing together into something more cohesive, the way mist draws together in the basins as the temperature drops.

They sit in circles. The circles are not perfect — nothing on the plateau is perfect, and the people do not pursue perfection; they pursue *depth*, and depth is always slightly irregular. The circles are ovals, or bean-shapes, or forms that do not have names, following the contours of the stone they sit on. They sit close enough to touch but they do not touch. The space between their bodies is *deliberate* — it is part of the pattern, part of the silence, part of the structure of the gathering.

And they listen.

Not to a speaker. There is no speaker. Not to music. There is no music. Not to anything. They listen to *the evening* — to the particular quality of silence that descends when the light fails on the plateau, when the mist thickens in the basins, when the cold deepens and the stone begins to radiate whatever warmth it gathered during the day (which is not much warmth, but is *some*, and on the plateau, *some* is enough).

The listening is communal. It is the primary social act of the community, more important than eating, more important than building, more important than weaving or gardening or shaping stone. To listen together is to *be together* in the deepest sense the plateau recognizes — to share not an activity but an *orientation*, a collective turning of attention toward whatever the place is, in this moment, being.

Sometimes, during the evening practice, someone will make a sound. A single tone, hummed in the chest, pitched to match the frequency of the wind or the subsonic vibration of the stone or the particular resonance of the mist in that basin on that evening. The sound will be held for a few breaths, and then it will be released, and the silence that follows will be *different* from the silence that preceded it — richer, deeper, more *textured*, as though the sound has not ended but has been *absorbed* into the quiet and is still vibrating there, inaudibly, beneath the threshold of perception.

These sounds are the closest thing the plateau has to music. They are not composed. They are not rehearsed. They arise from the listening the way the seeps arise from the stone — as *expressions of something that was already there*, emerging because the conditions were right, because the listener listened deeply enough and long enough that the sound *needed* to happen, the way a leaf needs to open, the way water needs to find its level.

Children attend the evening practice from a very young age. They sit at the edges of the circles, in the spaces between adults, and they listen. They do not always listen well — they are children, after all, and children are not yet fully settled into the rhythms of the plateau — but they are learning, and the learning is cumulative, and by the time a plateau person is an adult, the evening practice is not something they *do*. It is something they *are*. It is the structure of their inner life, the rhythm of their days, the thing that holds them together the way the strata hold the stone together — quietly, invisibly, and all the way through.

## Death and Dissolution

They die. Of course they die. The plateau is old, and everything on it is old, and everything that is old eventually *finishes*.

Death on the plateau is not an event. It is a *process* — a gradual, slow, almost imperceptible transition from *being a person* to *being part of the place*. The body slows — slower than the already-slow baseline, slower than the Settled, slower than the stone — and the breathing becomes so faint that only a hand placed on the chest can detect it, and the skin becomes more mineral and less flesh, and the eyes close, and the body becomes very, very still, and at some point — no one can say when — the person is gone, and what remains is a body that looks very much like a stone, positioned in the place where they were sitting or lying, oriented in whatever direction they were facing when the transition completed.

The body is not buried. It is not burned. It is *left*. This is not neglect. This is the final act of yielding — the yielding of the body itself to the plateau, the return of the mineral and the water and the slow, ancient substance of the person to the slow, ancient substance of the place. Over time — years, decades — the body is incorporated. Moss grows on it. Stone forms around it. The seeps carry minerals through it. It becomes part of the strata, part of the text, part of the unreadable library that underlies the settlement.

The people mark these incorporations. Not with monuments — the plateau has no use for monuments, no use for permanence of the *insistent* kind. They mark them with *names*. A stone where someone has been incorporated is spoken to. People passing by will say the person's name — that first sound, that original vocalization — and the saying of the name is a way of *maintaining the person's presence* in the community even after their presence has become geological.

Some stones in the settlement are old enough that they carry the incorporated remains of many people, layered one atop another, and these stones are *known*. They are known by the names of everyone they hold, and the names are said in sequence, and the sequence is long, and the effect is of a *litany* — not religious, not ritualistic in the lowland sense, but *functional*, a way of keeping the community's memory as layered and accumulated as the stone itself.

The oldest incorporated stone in the settlement holds, by the community's count, somewhere between forty and seventy people. The count varies because the folding-zones have shifted the stone's position several times over the centuries, and each shift has made it slightly more difficult to determine exactly how many layers are present. The stone sits at the convergence of three basins, where the water is most reliable, and it is the place where the evening practice gathers most often, and the people sit around it and listen, and the silence they listen to contains, in its deepest frequencies, the accumulated sound of forty or seventy or however many first-sounds, first-names, still vibrating in the mineral body of the stone, still *arriving*, still *becoming*, after all this time.

## What They Know

They know the plateau is wearing away. Not just the stone — the stone is obvious — but *everything*. The structure of space. The reliability of time. The fundamental *agreements* between things that allow a world to be a world rather than a chaos. They know this the way they know the sound of the wind and the rhythm of the mist — not as knowledge, not as theory, not as something learned from a text or a teacher, but as *direct apprehension*, as something known in the body and the bones and the mineral patches of the skin.

They do not know *why*. They do not ask *why*. The question *why* is a lowland question, a question that presupposes that the world has *reasons*, that things happen for *purposes*, that the erosion of reality is a *problem to be solved* rather than a *process to be participated in*. The people of the plateau do not share this presupposition. They do not think the erosion is a problem. They do not think it is *not* a problem. They do not think about it in those terms. They *yield to it*, the way they yield to everything, and they build their lives within it, and they listen to it the way they listen to the silence, and they *attend* to it.

Some of the older people — not the Settled, but the older active members of the community — seem to understand something about the erosion that the younger people do not. They say things, in their quiet voices, during the evening practice, that suggest a *perspective* — a view from somewhere that is not the plateau itself, or not *only* the plateau. They say things like: *It is not ending. It is becoming something else.* They say things like: *The stone remembers what it was before it was stone, and what it will be after it is not stone, and both of those are here, in the stone, right now.* They say things like: *We are the thinness. We are what reality looks like when it has worn through. We are not the people who live on the plateau. We are the plateau, listening to itself.*

These statements are not understood by the young. They are not meant to be understood. They are *seeds* — thrown into the silence of the evening practice, left to germinate in the slow, deep soil of the listener's attention, and if they take root, they take root slowly, over years, over decades, over a lifetime of yielding and listening and attending to the vast, patient, unwinding process that is this place and everything in it.

And this, finally, is what lives on the Plateau of Unwinding. Not a people. Not a culture. Not an ecology. A *process* — a slow, ancient, almost-finished process of *becoming*, conducted in stone and mist and silence and the mineral bodies of human beings who have yielded so completely to the becoming that they have become it, and who sit in the evening circles and listen to the sound of reality wearing thin, and who name each other with the first sounds they ever made, and who weave patterns that map the silence, and who grow moss in spirals and roots through stone and fruits that look like drops of solidified mist, and who do not hurry, and do not waste, and do not explain, and do not stop.

The plateau *is*. The people *are*. The wearing continues. The silence deepens. The mist moves through the basins. The water seeps from the ancient stone. The Settled sit. The evening circles listen. The names are spoken and spoken and spoken, and the stone holds them, and the strata layer them, and the unreadable library grows, and somewhere, in a frequency below hearing, in a fold of space that is not quite here, in a color that has no name, the plateau continues to say what it has always been saying, in the only language it has — the language of *being what it is, for as long as it is that* — and the people, who are the plateau's own way of *listening to itself*, sit in the cold and the quiet and the ancient, thinning air, and they hear it, and they yield, and they are still, and the evening comes, and the stars appear, sharp and close and more numerous than they should be, and the sound — the deep, everywhere, nowhere sound — arrives again, as it always arrives, as it always will, hovering at the threshold of perception, never quite resolving, always almost *something*, and the people close their eyes, and they breathe with the mist, and they are part of it, and it is part of them, and the plateau unwinds, and the world wears thin, and the silence holds everything, everything, in its vast and patient and material and infinite and *kindly* arms.
