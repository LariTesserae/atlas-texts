# What Lives in the Grooved Quarter

## The People

They are small. Not stunted — proportional, healthy, full-limbed — but they are smaller than people who live under open sky, and they have been for long enough that it is not a memory but a fact of the body. The Quarter rewards compactness. The hollows are small. The passages are narrow. The vine-lattices are easier to navigate with a body that does not fight its own geometry. Over generations — hundreds, perhaps thousands — the people have settled into a form that fits their home: short-limbed relative to torso, broad through the shoulders and hips, dense-boned, with hands that are wide and strong-fingered and almost prehensile in their grip. Children are born into the same hands that will climb vine-ropes before they can walk on flat ground, and the hands grow accordingly — the pads of the fingers calloused not from labor but from life, the nails kept short not by choice but because anything longer catches on everything.

Their skin is darker than you might expect, a deep brown with a faintly reddish undertone that echoes the rust-bleed in the basalt. It is thick, as described — toughened against the dryness, resistant to the resinous residue of the plants they handle constantly. It carries a permanent, faint scent of copper and warm stone, because they live inside a world made of copper-scented plants and warm stone and their bodies have long since stopped trying to smell like anything else. They do not notice this smell. Outsiders notice it immediately. It is the smell of the people themselves, inseparable from the smell of the place.

Their eyes are large. Not dramatically so — not grotesque — but noticeably, functionally larger than the eyes of surface dwellers. The irises are dark, nearly black, and the pupils dilate wide in the perpetual twilight, drinking in every photon the bioluminescent colonies and the amber shafts provide. In the deepest passages, where the glow is faintest, the people navigate more by touch and sound than by sight — they tap the walls as they walk, a light, rhythmic drumming with their fingertips that maps the texture and curvature of the stone ahead of them. Children learn this before they learn words. It is the first language: stone-tongue, they might call it, though they have no single word for it because it has never needed naming. It is simply how you move.

Their hearing is acute. It has to be. In a place where sound is amplified and multiplied by stone until it becomes a constant roar, the ability to distinguish signal from noise is not a luxury — it is survival. The people can hear the difference between the footfalls of a rooster settling into its roost and the footfall of a climber shifting on a vine. They can hear the soft, wet rustle of a crawler in the floor mat from three chambers away. They can hear, in the layered chatter of the small vertebrates, the difference between their alarm call and their feeding chatter, and they adjust their behavior accordingly — moving more quietly when the alarm notes spike, pausing, pressing flat against a wall until the system of eyes and ears and wings overhead has settled again.

They are not afraid of the animals. They are not afraid of anything in the Quarter, not in the way surface people might understand fear, because fear requires the possibility of escape and there is no outside. There are degrees of caution — don't sleep where the roosters roost, don't put your hand in a hollow you haven't mapped, don't step barefoot where the crawlers are active — but these are not fears. They are knowledge. They are the body's understanding of where it fits in the lattice.

## How They Live

The people do not think of themselves as builders. They think of themselves as inhabitants — which is a different thing, a more precise thing. A bird does not build a nest so much as it accumulates one, selecting and placing and adjusting until the shape is right, and the people of the Quarter relate to their hollows in the same way. They did not carve the hollows. The hollows were already there, waiting, as if the stone had anticipated their need. The people's work is refinement: smoothing a sleeping surface with a shaped basalt stone, grinding the edges of an entry gap until they are comfortable to pass through, encouraging bioluminescent growths to colonize a particular niche by seeding them with fragments of established colonies carried from elsewhere. This last practice — the cultivation of light — is perhaps the closest thing they have to agriculture. It is meticulous, slow, and considered. A family's most prized possession is not a tool or a cache of food but a living colony of light-growth, maintained in a favored hollow, fed with carefully collected organic dust, its brightness a matter of generational pride. There are lineages known for their light. Families whose glow-hollows are visible from across a chamber, their greenish-white radiance warm and steady and unmistakable. To live near such a family is considered fortunate. The light spills outward, making the passage brighter, making everything easier — seeing, navigating, the subtle work of reading faces and hands and gestures in a world where faces are often half-shadowed.

Food is not scarce. This is important to understand, because the description of the Quarter might suggest hardship, and it is not hardship. It is sufficiency. The people eat the plants — primarily the broad-leafed ground cover, which, despite its waxy, resinous surface, is palatable once the outer film is stripped away (a skill children master young, peeling leaves with a thumbnail in a single, practiced motion). The inner tissue is dense, slightly sweet, and rich with the bound water that the roots have extracted from the basalt. It is the primary source of hydration. You eat to drink, here. The water is inside the food, locked in the plant's cells, and it is released slowly as the body processes the meal — a slow, steady hydration that suits the dry air better than any gulped mouthful would.

There are other foods. The trailing vine-structures, when stripped of their fibrous outer layer, yield a starchy, palatable core that can be eaten raw or, in certain chambers where thermal vents release faint warmth, slowly dried into dense, portable cakes that keep for weeks. The pale, translucent rosettes of the deepest passages — the ghost-plants that grow without light — are a delicacy. They are sweet in a way that startles, a clean, bright sweetness that tastes like nothing else in the Quarter, and they are found only in the most remote, most difficult-to-reach passages, requiring hours of careful navigation through near-darkness to harvest. A handful of ghost-rosettes is a gift of real value. They are shared at gatherings — and there are gatherings, regularly, in the largest chambers, where the vine-lattices have been woven into broad, communal platforms that hang like nets between opposing walls. These are the closest the people come to a town square. Dozens of families occupy them at once, sitting in close, comfortable proximity, sharing food, sharing light-stones (small, carefully shaped pieces of basalt that have been warmed in thermal niches and wrapped in vine-matting), sharing the sounds of their voices, which blend with the animal chorus until the boundary between human noise and creature noise becomes, as always, impossible to parse.

## What They Know

They know the stone. This is the deepest knowledge, the one that underlies all others. Every person in the Quarter can read the basalt the way a sailor reads water — by texture, by color, by temperature, by the way it sounds when tapped. They know which passages are stable and which are slowly shifting, which hollows are old and which are newly formed, where the moisture channels run deep beneath the floor and where the stone is dry and dead. This knowledge is not written. It cannot be written. It lives in the body's memory, passed from hand to hand in childhood, when a parent takes a child's palm and presses it flat against a wall and says: *feel*. Feel the grain. Feel where it changes. Feel the seam where the iron bleeds. Feel the pocket where the air breathes. This is not metaphor. The stone literally breathes — slowly, almost imperceptibly, as thermal expansion and contraction through the day causes air to move in and out of the deeper fissures. The people feel this movement through their palms and know, without being able to articulate it in any scientific language, the difference between a living passage (one with airflow, one connected to the larger system) and a dead-end (one sealed by collapse, one that will be stagnant and dry).

They know the plants. Not as specimens — they have no taxonomy, no Linnaean bins — but as beings with behaviors. They know which leaves to strip and which to leave (the dark ones, fully matured; the lighter ones are too resinous and will cause stomach pain). They know which vine-cords are fresh and strong and which are old and brittle, and they know this by color, by the angle at which they hang, by the sound they make when plucked — a taut vine sings a higher note than a slack one. They know when the bioluminescent colonies are healthy (bright, steady, slightly cool to the touch) and when they are stressed (flickering, dimming, warm at the base). They respond to these signals with the fluency of people for whom plant-health is community-health, because it is. A dying colony means a dimming passage means a navigation hazard means an increased risk of falls, of disorientation, of walking into territory that belongs to the roosters, which is something no one wants.

They know the animals. Every species has a role in their understanding, and while they do not use words like "ecology" or "food web," they have a practical, embodied comprehension of the system that is, if anything, more precise than a scientist's diagram. They know that where the crawlers are active, the floor mat is thin and the stone is exposed and the roots are shallow, so the ground cover will be sparse and you should carry extra food. They know that where the small vertebrates chatter in alarm, a rooster is hunting and you should keep your distance — not because the rooster will harm you (it won't; you are too large, not prey, merely in the way) but because the commotion of a rooster's hunt sends the small creatures scattering, and a hundred startled vertebrates flooding through a passage at once is a physical force, a wave of bodies and claws and chittering panic that can knock a person off a vine-ladder. They know that when the insect drone deepens in pitch and volume, humidity is rising somewhere — the stone is releasing moisture, the roots are working — and this is good, because humidity means the plants are thriving, and the plants are everything.

They know each other. The settlement has no formal governance. There is no chief, no council, no elected anything. Authority is situational and expertise-based: if a passage collapses, the people who know the stone's grain best take charge of the rescue and re-routing. If a bioluminescent colony fails, the family with the finest light-tradition is consulted. If a child is sick — and children do get sick, and adults do too, though less often, their bodies hardened by lifelong exposure to the Quarter's particular microbiome — the people who know the plants' medicinal properties (and there are such people, always, in every generation, those whose hands remember the specific leaf, the specific preparation, the specific dosage) are sought out and deferred to. This is not hierarchy. It is gravity. Expertise draws respect the way mass draws mass, and when the expertise is no longer needed, the gravity dissolves and everyone returns to the same level, which is to say: alive, in their hollows, in the walls, in the dark green twilight of the only world they know.

## What They Do Not Know

They do not know there is a sky. They know there is light — amber shafts from above, greenish glow from the walls — but the concept of an open expanse of blue stretching beyond comprehension, of weather, of a sun that is not a shaft of light but a visible burning sphere, of rain, of wind, of horizons that extend further than the eye can reach — these things are not in their experience or their stories. The shafts of amber light are called, roughly, *the reaching*, or more precisely, a word in their language that means something like "the finger that touches and moves." They know the light changes with seasons. They know it reaches further in the warm months and retreats in the cool months. They know that if you climb high enough, through the narrowest, most dangerous fissures — the ones that open onto the plateau's surface, the ones that everyone is warned away from as children — there is a place where the light becomes blinding, where the air changes character entirely, where the stone gives way to something open and flat and orange and endless. Some people have been there. It is a rite of passage for some, a pilgrimage for others, a foolishness for the cautious. Those who go come back changed in ways that are difficult to describe. They squint for days afterward, even in the Quarter's dimness. They are restless, distracted, and their voices take on a quality that others find unsettling — a hollowness, as if they are speaking from a great distance, even when they are close. They say the open place is terrifying. Not because anything there threatens them — nothing does; the surface is empty, sun-scorched, silent — but because it is *too much*. Too large. Too bright. Too empty. They say the stone beneath your feet feels thin up there, like a shell over nothing, and that looking out at the horizon makes you feel as if you are falling sideways into a space that has no walls and no floor and no ceiling and no end. Most who go once do not go again. They come back to their hollows, to the close warm dark, to the hum and the glow and the smell of resin and stone, and they are content. The Quarter is enough. It has always been enough. The people who have never gone to the surface say this with conviction. The people who have gone say it with relief.

## What Lives Alongside Them

The small vertebrates — the fist-sized, vine-climbing, chittering ones — are the Quarter's most constant companions. They are not pets. They are not livestock. They are neighbors. They nest in the same hollows the people occupy, often in the upper corners where the human body doesn't comfortably go, and over time the two species have reached a kind of mutual accommodation that no one formalized and no one could dissolve. The vertebrates eat the insects that would otherwise plague the people — the biting ones, the ones that lay eggs in damp skin folds, the ones whose bite causes welts that itch for days. In return, the people leave food scraps in the communal platforms, and the vertebrates feed on them. The chittering is constant. It is, as much as the stone's thermal hum or the insect drone, a background condition of life here. Newborns learn to distinguish human voices from vertebrate voices within weeks, the way surface children learn to distinguish birdsong from wind. The distinction matters: a sudden silence in the chittering means something has spooked the colony, and when the colony is spooked, you pay attention. A sudden intensification — a rising, urgent chatter — means a rooster is on the move. The people read these signals with the fluency of another sense. They do not think of it as reading. They think of it as listening.

The roosters are tolerated, not welcomed. They are too large to coexist comfortably — their wingspans in the largest chambers can reach a meter, and when they take flight in an enclosed space, the downdraft is significant, scattering food, knocking tools from ledges, sending vine-cords swaying. But they are essential. Without them, the small vertebrates would overpopulate, overconsume the insects, and the entire system would tip. The people understand this in their bodies, not their heads. They do not think, *the roosters maintain ecological balance.* They think, more simply, *the roosters eat the climbers so the climbers don't eat everything else.* It is the same understanding, expressed in a language that predates abstraction.

The crawlers — the legless decomposers in the floor mat — are regarded with the particular fondness that people reserve for things that do essential, invisible, unglamorous work. Children are told not to step on them. This is practical advice (stepping on a crawler in the mat produces a mess and a smell that lingers) but it is also, somehow, moral advice, though no one would phrase it that way. The crawlers close the loop. They take what is dead and make it available for what is alive. This is obvious. It is so obvious it does not need saying. And yet the saying of it — *don't step on them; they're the ones who make it all work* — carries a weight that exceeds its content. It is, in its quiet way, the closest thing the people have to a creed.

## The Shape of a Day

There are no days. There is no dawn to mark. The light does not change on any human schedule. But the people have impose rhythm on the timeless, because humans must, because the body demands it, because the animals follow cycles that the stone and the light and the seasons dictate, and the people have synchronized themselves to these cycles with the same unselfconscious precision with which they have synchronized everything else.

They sleep when the amber shafts retreat — in the cool months, this is a long sleep, a deep rest that lasts longer than waking, and the passages grow quiet in a way they never do at other times. The insects thin. The vertebrates settle into their corners and go still, their large eyes closing, their bodies fluffed against the cool. The roosters hang motionless. Even the crawlers slow. The stone, freed from the thermal pressure of daytime light absorption, releases its stored heat in long, slow exhalations, and the air grows fractionally warmer as the walls cool. The people sleep in their hollows, curled in the posture the space demands — on their sides, knees drawn up, arms folded, a shape that has been natural for so long that sleeping any other way feels wrong, wasteful, exposed.

They wake when the shafts return. Not all at once — there is no alarm, no call — but in a gradual stirring that begins in the most light-exposed chambers and ripples outward through the passages like a wave. The vertebrates wake first, because they must: the insects are waking too, and the first hour after waking is the richest feeding time. The chittering resumes. The insect drone deepens. The people rise, strip leaves, eat, drink the water in the food, and begin the day's work, which is not work in any industrial sense but is simply the continuation of life: tending the light-colonies, maintaining the hollows, weaving new vine-cord from fresh-gathered fibers, shaping tools, checking the passages for collapse or blockage, moving food between caches, and — always, always — navigating. Moving through the passages, up and down the ladders, across the rope bridges, through the vine-curtains, tapping the walls, listening, reading the stone and the air and the light and the animal sounds, performing the continuous, exhausting, exhilarating act of being alive in a place that demands total attention at all times.

They are not bored. The concept does not translate. Boredom requires a surplus of time and a deficit of stimulation, and the people of the Grooved Quarter have neither. Every moment is occupied — not by task but by awareness, by the sheer cognitive load of existing in a space this dense and this alive. When they rest, they rest fully, bodies still, hands quiet, eyes closed, listening to the hum of the world they belong to. And when they are active, they are active with a wholeness that is difficult to describe to anyone who has not felt it: the sense that every motion matters, that every hand on every vine, every foot on every stone, every glance at every shadow is part of a conversation with a place that is always, always answering back.

## What the Place Makes of Them

The Grooved Quarter has shaped its people the way the fissures have shaped the stone — by slow, patient, irresistible pressure over deep time. The people are quiet where quietness serves them (in the presence of roosters, in unfamiliar passages) and loud where loudness is natural (in the communal chambers, among family, in the roar of the ambient world where a private conversation is impossible anyway and so conversation becomes a public act, shared by everyone within earshot, which is everyone). They are tactile. They touch constantly — walls, vines, each other's hands and shoulders and faces. In a world of limited light, touch is the most reliable sense, and they have elevated it to an art form. A greeting between two people is a laying-on of hands: palm to palm, fingers spread, a brief pressure that communicates presence, health, intention. A farewell is a release of that pressure, slow, reluctant. Between intimates — lovers, parents and children, lifelong companions — the touch is more complex: thumb to inner wrist, feeling the pulse, the warmth, the particular rhythm of that person's blood. This is how they know each other. Not by face (faces are often shadowed, often unseen) but by hand, by pulse, by the small involuntary movements that speak of life beneath the skin.

They are generous, but not in the way surface people use that word. They share because isolation is not an option. The hollows are small. The passages are shared. The light-colonies are communal resources, even the private ones, because a bright hollow benefits the passage it opens onto, and a bright passage benefits everyone who uses it. The concept of private property exists — *this is my hollow, this is my family's light, this is the cache where I keep my tools* — but it is soft-edged, permeable, constantly negotiated by proximity and necessity. If your neighbor's light-colony fails, you share yours. If a passage collapses and blocks access to a family's food cache, the community distributes from its own stores until the passage is cleared. These are not policies. They are physics. They are the social equivalent of the water economy: nothing is wasted, nothing is hoarded beyond need, and the system's survival depends on the circular flow of resources from hand to hand.

They are brave, but their bravery looks nothing like surface bravery. There are no battles, no wars, no enemies to face. The Quarter's dangers are environmental — collapses, blockages, the occasional rooster that has lost its fear of human proximity and must be discouraged from nesting in a living space (a task that involves a lot of loud noise and arm-waving and not a little danger of claw-scratches, but nothing more). The bravery of the Quarter is the bravery of maintenance: of keeping the system running, of checking the passages, of climbing to the high fissures to assess damage after a thermal shift, of descending into the lightless depths to harvest ghost-rosettes or to map a new passage that has opened in a collapse. It is the bravery of daily repetition, of doing the same necessary things today that you did yesterday and will do tomorrow, without the incentive of novelty or the promise of change. It is the bravery of patience. The deepest bravery there is.

## What They Dream

They dream of the walls. Of the stone. Of the greenish glow in the dark and the amber bars of light moving slowly across the curved surfaces. They dream of climbing — upward, always upward, through passages that widen as they rise, until the stone thins and the light intensifies and the air changes and they break through into a place that is not the plateau's surface but something else entirely: a space inside the stone, a hollow so vast it has its own weather, its own sky made of bioluminescent growth so old and so dense that it glows like a sun, and beneath it a green so deep it is almost blue, and in the green things moving, things they have no names for, things that are larger than roosters and older than the stone and patient beyond any patience they have known.

No one talks about these dreams. They are private, in a way that is rare in the Quarter, where most things are shared by necessity. But everyone has them. The same dream, or close enough to the same, arriving in sleep with the regularity of the amber shafts: the vast hollow, the living light, the sense that the stone goes deeper than anyone has ever gone, and that what lives in its deepest places is not empty but full, not dead but waiting, not silent but holding its breath.

They wake from these dreams into the warm dark, into the hum and the chitter and the rustle, into the familiar pressure of the stone on all sides, and they are glad. Not glad that the dream was false — they are not sure it is false — but glad to be here, in this hollow, in this body, in this life that fits so tightly and so completely around them like a second skin, like a third wall, like the vine-cord that holds everything together through its own quiet, relentless, unglamorous strength.

The stone is patient. The people are patient. The light moves slowly across the walls, marking time in a place where time has never needed marking, and the Grooved Quarter goes on — breathing, humming, glowing, growing, alive against all expectation in the dark, warm, green, impossible heart of the Kessu Plateau, where the earth grew tired of holding its edges sharp and let something beautiful fill the wound.
