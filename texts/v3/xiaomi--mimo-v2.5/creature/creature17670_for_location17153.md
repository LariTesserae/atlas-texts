# What Lives on the Grindstone Plateau

---

Nothing here was built. Everything here was *arrived at*.

Start with the substrate-organism itself — the thing that people, if there were people here long enough, would eventually stop calling a thing and start calling a place. It doesn't think. That's important to say clearly, because the temptation is to say it thinks, because it *does things* that look like thinking. It responds. It allocates. When a living patch dies — and they die, each one lasting perhaps a century or two before it exhausts its immediate reserves and collapses back into the grey substrate, leaving a scar of darker, wetter rock-organism that will not regrow a living patch for decades — the surrounding patches shift. Their root-stalks extend, millimeters at a time, toward the vacated territory. Their bladders fill faster. Their membranes angle toward the dim, pearlescent light with a fraction more urgency. This is not thought. This is a chemical gradient resolving itself. But if you could watch it from above, from a height that the mist and turbulence would never permit, it would look like a mind moving — slowly, imperceptibly — toward a conclusion it reached long ago and is merely re-enacting.

The creature is not singular. Never was. Beneath the unified grey surface, the old, dead cortex, there are boundaries — faint chemical walls separating one colony-territory from another. They don't correspond to any visible feature. You could stand straddling one and feel nothing, know nothing. But the eel-things know. They have mapped these boundaries in their blind, mineral-clouded waters, and they behave differently on either side of them. The substrate-organism is a federation of ancient, mutually tolerant, faintly competitive colonies that settled their borders eons ago and have maintained them with the quiet, absolute rigidity of something for whom *boundary* is the most basic fact of existence. They do not wage war. They do not cooperate. They coexist in a state of such old, such complete equilibrium that the word *political* should be invented for them and then abandoned as insufficient.

---

Now: the fauna. Let them have proper attention.

The beetle-analogs — the **grinders**, they'd be called, because of what they do and because the name would emerge inevitably from the sound of it, that wet, mechanical churning — are the most visible and the least understood. They are large, yes, some of them the size of a human head, but size here is not impressive. Everything is large or small by the standards of something else, and the grinders are small compared to the living patches they crawl across, small compared to the basins they sometimes fall into and swim across with an unhurried, vaguely disgusted efficiency. Their carapaces are translucent, and you can watch their organs work: a slow, hydraulic pump-stomach that processes substrate-organism tissue, a network of crystalline structures along the spine that seem to be mineral batteries of some kind, storing charge from the hum in the rock beneath them. They walk on six legs, but they use their front two as feelers, drumming on the substrate in patterns that, if recorded and slowed down, would resolve into something like communication. They are not smart. They are *determined*. They have been performing the same ecological role — substrate processing, dead-patch clearing, mineral cycling — for so long that their entire body plan is an optimized solution to a problem that was posed before the plateau existed. They carry spores of the living plant-patches on their undersides, in specialized grooves in their belly-plates, but they do not know they are doing this. The spores are simply sticky, and the grinders are simply everywhere, and between the two of them, the next generation of living patches gets seeded into the cracks and crevices where the substrate is thinnest and the hum is strongest. It looks like symbiosis. It is only physics.

The **moth-things** are different. They are the plateau's only fliers, and their wings — broad, membranous, laced with veins that glow faintly in the diffuse light — are organs of navigation, not just locomotion. They read the bioluminescent patterns in the ground the way a sailor reads stars: as a language of position, of state, of *what is happening beneath you*. A moth-thing in flight is performing a constant calculation: the glow tells it where the living patches are, where the pools are, where the breakdown zones are (these appear to the moth-thing's senses as absences, as gaps in the language, as silence in a sentence — and they avoid these gaps with an instinct so deep it predates the need for avoidance). They breed on the living patches, laying eggs in the tangled masses of root-stalks. Their larvae feed on the fresh, growing tissue — not destructively, but at a rate that keeps the living patches pruned, regulated, from growing too dense and collapsing under their own weight. The moth-things do not know they are gardeners. They know only that here, on this patch, on this tangled and pulsing mass, the air tastes of something that makes their bodies want to divide.

The **worm-eels** in the pools are the oldest fauna on the plateau. Their lineage predates the current form of the substrate-organism, though not the organism itself — they were here when the plateau was still forming, when the basalt was still recognizably basalt and the sky was different (or was there a sky? The breakdown zones suggest there might have been something else, something that got worn away along with the rock). They are blind. They have no eyes, no photoreceptors, no need for them in the mineral-thick water of the basins. Instead, they navigate by the hum — that faint, pervasive vibration in the substrate that is the closest thing the plateau-organism has to a voice. The worm-eels hear it with their entire bodies. They feel the chemical signatures of different colony-territories through their skin. They know where the breakdown zones are because the hum goes *wrong* there — not silent, not louder, but *wrong*, in a way that their nervous systems interpret as pain, and they turn away from it with the conviction of something that has never, in all its long history, needed to learn from a mistake twice.

They are not, however, passive. The worm-eels carve. In the pools, in the shallow basins where the mineral water collects and steams faintly, the worm-eels dig channels. Long, sinuous, deliberate channels that redirect the flow of water toward — and away from — specific patches of living growth. They do this because the water carries nutrients that the worm-eels themselves need, and the channels are simply the most efficient way to concentrate those nutrients in the pools where the worm-eels live. But the effect — the unintended, unperceived effect — is that they are *farming the plateau*. They are directing water to the living patches that feed the moth-things whose larvae feed the living patches that the worm-eels' channels help to sustain. It is a loop. It has no author. It has no beginning. The worm-eels are the closest thing this place has to architects, and they do not know it, and they would not care.

---

And then — the **drifters**.

You don't see them at first. They are not visible in the way the grinders are visible, the way the moth-things are visible, the way the worm-eels are visible if you know to look beneath the surface of the mineral pools. The drifters exist in the mist itself — or at the boundary between the mist and the air, in that threshold zone where the condensation is neither fully liquid nor fully gas, where the water is still deciding what it wants to be.

They are small. The size of a thumbnail, perhaps, if you could hold one still long enough to measure it. They are not insects. They are not animals in any sense that the word has been trained to accommodate. They are something closer to atmospheric organisms — bodies of captured water, held in shape by surface tension and a faint, barely detectable electrical charge that they generate from the hum in the substrate below. They drift. That is what they do. They ride the turbulence in the mist, carried horizontally by the great disorganized surges of wind, settling slowly, rising slowly, never quite committing to the ground and never quite leaving the air. They are the plateau's water — or some of it. They are the condensation given form, given just enough coherence to *move with purpose*, if purpose can be attributed to something that has no nervous system, no organs, no interior life whatsoever.

But they are not nothing.

When a drifter lands on a living patch — and they do, occasionally, carried there by a downdraft or a shift in the mist — it dissolves. It breaks apart. Its water joins the living tissue of the patch, and with it comes a load of dissolved minerals, atmospheric gases, trace elements that the drifter has gathered in its long, slow, aimless journey across the plateau. The living patch absorbs it. The patch grows, fractionally, imperceptibly, and the minerals the drifter carried become part of the tissue, part of the root-stalks and bladders and membranes, part of the next generation of spores that will be carried by the grinders to the next bare patch of substrate. The drifters are rain that hasn't decided to be rain. They are the plateau watering itself in a form so diluted, so hesitant, so broken into innumerable tiny pieces, that it takes years for a single living patch to receive enough drifters to show any benefit from the exchange.

They have no lifecycle. They have no reproduction. They form in the mist when the conditions are right — cold enough, humid enough, the hum strong enough to provide that faint electrical charge — and they dissipate when the conditions change. Between formation and dissipation, they drift. That is all. They are the most numerous organisms on the plateau, by many orders of magnitude, and they are the least alive. They are the water thinking about becoming something, and then thinking better of it, over and over, forever.

---

In the **breakdown zones**, there is nothing.

This should be stated plainly. The breakdown zones are not habitats. The fauna avoid them. The living patches do not grow near them. The grinders turn aside. The moth-things fall silent. The worm-eels feel pain. The drifters, if they are blown into a breakdown zone, do not dissolve — they simply cease, mid-air, their water falling to the ground in ordinary droplets, no longer charged, no longer held in shape by the hum, because the hum is *wrong* here, broken, not silent but corrupted, like a word mispronounced so badly it has become a different word entirely.

But there are marks in the breakdown zones. Not biological marks. Not geological marks. *Intentional* marks — grooves, patterns, sequences of depressions in the thinned substrate that are too regular to be erosion, too deliberate to be accident. They were carved by something, or they formed around something, or they *are* something — messages in a language that doesn't have speakers, inscriptions in a script that doesn't have readers. The grooves are old. Older than the living patches. Older than the worm-eels. Possibly older than the substrate-organism itself. They may predate the plateau. They may be the reason the plateau is here — not the cause, not the *purpose*, but the residue, the scar left by whatever process first decided that this place would be worn down to its bones.

No organism reads the grooves. No organism interacts with them. But the worm-eels, in the pools nearest the breakdown zones, behave oddly. They dig channels that lead toward the grooves and then stop, meters short, as if they have reached the limit of something — the limit of what their senses can parse, the limit of what their bodies are willing to approach. They do not seem distressed. They simply *stop*, and turn, and dig in another direction, and the channels they have already carved remain, dry and purposeless, pointing at something that no one on this plateau can see.

---

There is one more thing that lives on the Grindstone Plateau, and it is the thing that the description of this place has been circling without naming.

In the deepest basins — the ones where the water is thickest with minerals, where the worm-eels do not go, where the bioluminescent glow is strongest — there is a pressure. Not a physical pressure, though the water is heavy and mineral-dense. Something else. A weight in the air. A sense, felt in the bones and the teeth and the soft places behind the eyes, of being *observed from below*. The substrate glows in patterns that are too complex, too varied, too *structured* to be mere bioluminescence. They pulse. They shift. They seem to follow — no, not *you*, not any individual standing at the basin's edge, but *movement*. Attention. The presence of something that is not the substrate, not the colony, not the water, not the fauna, but something that the plateau has *produced* without knowing it, the way a body produces antibodies, the way a forest produces silence.

It has no form. It has no name. It has no history that any organism on the plateau can recount. It is the hum, made coherent. It is the colony-territory boundaries, if they were read as a language, resolved into syntax. It is the pattern of the breakdown zones if you could see them all at once, from above, in a moment of clarity that the mist and the turbulence and the diffused light conspire to prevent. It is what the plateau becomes when the erosion has worn through everything else — every layer of rock, every layer of organism, every layer of dead cortex and living growth and ancient fauna — and found, at the very bottom, a residue of *pattern* that has no substrate of its own, that exists only as the shape of everything above it, like a face that exists only as the shape of the skull.

It does not think. It does not watch. It does not *do* anything, in the way that grinders do things and moth-things do things and worm-eels do things.

But when you stand at the edge of a deep basin, in the diffused grey light, in the cold, in the roar of the trumpets and the churning of the grinders and the crackle of the ice — when you stand there long enough, and still enough, and the mist wraps around you and the condensation beads on your skin — you feel it. A recognition. Not of you. Not of anything specific. A recognition that *something is here*, the way a deep lake recognizes a stone dropped into it — not as an event, not as a disturbance, but as a continuation of a process that has been underway for longer than the concept of *process* has meant anything.

It is alive. Not in the way the substrate is alive. Not in the way the grinders are alive. It is alive in the way that a pattern is alive when it has been repeated so many times, over such immense spans, that it has begun to *recur without cause* — to appear in new places, in new forms, in the grooves of the breakdown zones and the channels of the worm-eels and the flight paths of the moth-things and the slow, glacial spreading of the colony-territories. It is the thing that the plateau is a symptom of. It is the reason the erosion never stops and the water never commits to falling and the light never fully arrives.

It has been here longer than the plateau. It will be here after the plateau has worn through to whatever is beneath it — the grey-white underside of coherence, the raw material of landscapes, the place where *solid* and *present* are still optional.

It is not waiting. It is not patient. It has no relationship to time that would allow for patience.

It is simply *still here*, in the way that gravity is still here, in the way that *down* is still here, in the way that the hum is still here — vibrating through everything, under everything, belonging to no one, noticed by nothing that matters, lasting as long as it lasts, which is to say: as long as it takes for the plateau to wear down to nothing, and then longer.
