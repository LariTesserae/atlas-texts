And then you see the people.

They emerge from the dimness like the slow disclosure of a secret the hollow has been keeping. First one, then another, then a third from a different direction, their movements unhurried, their bare feet making no sound on the moss or the damp stone. They are of the place. Their skin is the pale, pinkish-white of the new rock, and their hair, where it is thick, is the deep, saturated green of the ferns. It isn't moss, not exactly, but it has the same texture, the same way of catching the diffuse light. Some of them have faint, branching patterns on their arms and shoulders—the pale, fractal tracery of the coral-like organisms, a symbiosis written on the skin.

They are not numerous. You could count them on two hands, perhaps. They are small, lean, with the quiet musculature of people who work with their hands all day in a place that demands precision and patience, not brute force. Their eyes are large, adapted to the dimness, the irises a dark, wet amber. They blink slowly. When they look at you, it is not with suspicion or fear, but with a kind of calm, analytical curiosity, as one might regard a pebble that has suddenly developed the habit of breathing.

They speak, but rarely, and when they do, the words are soft, swallowed by the silence and the stone. Their voices have the cadence of dripping water—a pause, a word, a longer pause. Communication seems as much a matter of gesture, of touch, of the angle of a head or the placement of a hand on a growing root-frame. They understand the language of slow change.

Their lives are a dialogue with the hollow.

You see them work. A woman kneads a fresh batch of mineral-rich mud, her thumbprints adding to the generations of prints in the walls of the basin she is repairing. She works with the focused calm of a meditator, her motions a ritual that is also a necessity. Two others tend a living roof. They do not prune with cutters—the only tools are their hands and a small, smooth river stone used to coax a branch into a new direction. They speak to the plant in murmurs, and you could swear the broad leaves orient toward their voices. They are not masters of the garden; they are its conversationalists.

Their relationship with the fauna is one of respectful coexistence. A man pauses to let a drift of the mineral-winged insects pass before him, his eyes tracking their lazy, iridescent path. He cups his hands, and one of the tiny amphibians hops in, its throat pulsing against his palm. He carries it to a new crevice, a wetter spot where algae is blooming, and releases it. He does this as one might water a favorite plant—a small, deliberate act of stewardship.

Their days are measured in the slow accumulation of tasks. The clearing of a mineral crust from a doorway. The topping up of a basin, ensuring the tiny crustaceans have their world. The weaving of a new root-lattice for a wall that is thickening too quickly. The pressing of a hand to the living stone, not to shape it, but to feel its pulse, to know its mood, to predict where it will grow next. This is their technology: an intimate, learned fluency in the processes of a world that is always under construction.

They eat simply. The crustaceans from the pools are a staple, their shells ground into a mineral-rich paste. Certain ferns provide fronds that are young and tender. The thick mosses can be compressed and eaten, a bland but sustaining food. There is no fire. Nothing is cooked. They consume the raw, living output of their hollow, their bodies perfectly adapted to this low-energy, high-mineral diet.

Their homes are not private in the way a surface dweller might understand. The clusters of buildings are for gathering, for sleeping, for shelter during the heavier dripping periods. But life happens in the spaces between, in the courtyards, along the damp walls. They are a collective organism, their movements choreographed by the unspoken needs of the place and of each other. A child—not a child, exactly, but a younger person with the same coral-like skin markings—trails behind an adult, learning by watching, their small hands already knowing how to press mud and bend soft wood.

They are patient, but not passive. Their patience is a tool, a sharp and active thing. They watch the geology, and they respond. When a new stalagmite begins to form in a pathway, they do not break it; they build a new path around it, incorporating its growth into the evolving map of their world. They are in a state of constant, gentle negotiation with a place that will eventually fill itself if left alone. They will not stop it. They will adapt to it, moving their lives inward as the hollow contracts over centuries, a dance with geology measured in lifetimes.

To the rare visitor, they offer silence and space. They do not question, nor do they explain. They simply make room in their rhythm for your presence, a new and curious variable in the long equation of the hollow. They show you, through their being, what it means to live at the speed of stone and water, to measure your legacy not in monuments but in the continued health of a basin of water, in the strength of a living root, in the quiet persistence of a fern frond uncurling in the dim, warm light.

They are the current thought in the mind of the earth, the part of the process that has learned to look back at itself. And as you watch them move through their damp, green, silent world, you realize they are not just inhabitants. They are the hollow's way of maintaining itself. They are the care that prevents the slow crush of time from becoming an ending. They are the patient hands that keep the conversation going, so that the place might continue to breathe, to grow, to *be*—for as long as the water seeps and the stone weeps and the light glows from within.
