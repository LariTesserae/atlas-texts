# The Inhabitants of the Pressworks Green

There is a slug on the south-facing surface of what used to be Column 7.

It is the size of a man's outstretched hand, perhaps a little longer. Its body is the color of wet chlorophyll—not the bright, obvious green of a leaf, but the deep, murky, almost black-green of the substrate itself, the green of water that hasn't seen light in weeks. It moves with an imperceptible slowness that makes it difficult to track without staring, and staring is something you learn quickly not to do in the Pressworks Green. You look away and look back and it has moved three centimeters, or perhaps six, or perhaps not at all. Time is unreliable here.

The slug's skin is not smooth. It has a texture that echoes the substrate—ridged, faintly corrugated, with tiny papillae that catch moisture and hold it in a film across its back. In this way, the slug is always wet. Everything here is always wet, but the slug has made wetness into a feature of its body rather than a condition of its environment. It breathes through this film. Its pneumostome—a small, rhythmic opening on the right side of its mantle—opens and closes in a cycle that is slow enough to watch and boring enough that you will time it, and when you time it you will find it corresponds roughly to the pulse of the substrate beneath it. Not exactly. But roughly. The(slug and the ground breathe together, not because one dictates the other, but because they are responding to the same slow hydraulic rhythm, the same pressure wave moving through the root mass below.

There are perhaps three hundred of these slugs in the Pressworks Green at any given time. This is a guess. Nobody has counted them, and counting them would be difficult, because they are everywhere and they are camouflaged and they do not move in any predictable pattern. They prefer the undersides of the helical bindweed coils, where the surface is warm from the slow mechanical friction of the vine tightening against steel. They also prefer the base of the weeping columns, where the condensation film is thickest and where a particular species of alga—dark, crusty, almost black—grows in dense patches. The slugs eat this alga. They also eat the fungal mats in the substrate, scraping them up with a radula that is, under magnification, a remarkable thing: a ribbon of chitinous teeth arranged in rows, each tooth curved like a miniature sickle, designed not for tearing but for harvesting, for gathering the soft tissue of fungi the way a comb gathers hair.

When a slug feeds, it leaves a trail. The trail is not the typical silvery mucus of garden slugs. It is translucent, faintly greenish, and it carries with it a payload of spores—fungal spores, algal cells, the reproductive material of whatever the slug last ate. The slug is not merely moving through the Pressworks Green. It is seeding it. Every trail is a planting line, a vector of dispersal that distributes the organisms of the substrate across surfaces that would otherwise be difficult to reach: the vertical faces of columns, the undersides of vaulted canopies, the ancient steel of the press brake's control arm. The slug does not know this. The slug does not know anything, in any sense of the word that we would recognize. But the system knows it, if "knowing" can mean something other than cognition—something closer to *accounting*. The Pressworks Green has its bookkeepers, and they are sluggish, hand-sized, and green.

The slugs reproduce in spring, or what passes for spring beneath the canopy. The timing is cued not by light—the light is filtered and diffuse and changes little with the seasons—but by temperature, which shifts in the substrate as the boiler's output fluctuates with changes in groundwater level. When the substrate warms by two or three degrees, the slugs begin to seek each other. They are hermaphrodites. Two slugs will align their bodies, ventral surface to ventral surface, and extend their reproductive structures—surprising organs, large and elaborate and evanescent, a pale bluish-white that stands out vividly against the green of their bodies. The exchange takes hours. It is slow. Everything here is slow. Afterward, both slugs will deposit eggs in a sheltered crevice of the substrate—small, spherical, translucent, greenish—and both will move away, and within two weeks the eggs will hatch, and the juvenile slugs will be pale and tiny, no bigger than a fingernail, and almost immediately they will begin to feed, and their trails will begin to seed, and the cycle will continue as it has continued for as long as the Pressworks Green has existed, which is longer than anyone knows.

---

The flies are different.

There are perhaps five species in the Pressworks Green, though taxonomy has not been performed on them and some of them may not be true flies in the dipteran sense but rather some convergent form that has arrived at a similar body plan through different evolutionary pressures. They are small—all of them, none larger than a grain of rice—and they are the palest animals in the space. Their bodies are translucent, almost glassy, with just enough internal structure visible to suggest organs, musculature, the faint suggestion of a circulatory system. They look like something that is in the process of becoming transparent, as if the Pressworks Green is slowly dissolving them into the air.

They breed in standing water. This water is not rain—the canopy prevents most direct rainfall from reaching the interior—but rather condensation that pools in the cups and hollows of the substrate, in the concave joints where bindweed coils meet beam, in the shallow basins that form where weeping columns meet ground. The water is warm, mineral-rich, and faintly bioluminescent at night—or rather, in the dim, green-filtered darkness that passes for night beneath the canopy. The bioluminescence comes from bacteria. The flies have adapted to it. Their larvae develop in bacterial water, feeding on the biofilm that coats every submerged surface, and they are themselves nearly invisible, wriggling micro-fauna that would require a hand lens to see clearly.

The adults do not fly much. They drift. They walk along surfaces with a tentative, exploratory gait, their antennae—reduced, almost vestigial—twitching at the chemical signals that saturate the air. What they sense, no one can say. The Pressworks Green smells of chlorophyll breakdown, of iron, of wet stone, of fungal metabolism, of a hundred organic compounds in slow decomposition and slow synthesis. The flies navigate this dense chemical landscape the way a bird navigates by stars, finding patterns in complexity, meaning in noise.

They serve a purpose, or seem to. A few species of the smaller, more cryptic plants in the substrate—low-growing, almost moss-like, producing minute flowers in the deepest shade—appear to be insect-pollinated. The flowers are pale, nearly colorless, with a faint sweet smell that you can detect only if you press your face into the substrate, which no one does willingly. The flies visit these flowers. They crawl into them, stay for a long time, and emerge dusted with pollen that they carry, over their short and cautious lives, to the next flower and the next. This is the only animal-mediated pollination in the Pressworks Green. Everything else—everything larger, more architectural, more structurally ambitious—is water-borne. The flies are permitted to participate in the smallest, most marginal reproductive events, as if the system has a few tasks it has not yet managed to automate.

---

The *helical bindweed*—the squared-off, copper-green coils—is alive in a way that is difficult to talk about without resorting to language that sounds either poetic or insane, and the Pressworks Green does not care which.

It grows. That is the obvious thing. It grows at a rate that is, by human standards, agonizingly slow—perhaps a centimeter of length per week in summer, less in winter—but over the decades it has grown enormously. The coils that now wrap the I-beams of the old roof structure are the product of years of accumulation, layer upon layer of squared stem, each year's growth adding to the diameter of the coil like the rings of a tree. The oldest coils, the ones that have been growing since the earliest days of the Pressworks Green, are as thick as a man's thigh. They are heavy. They are under tension. The sound they make—the creaking, the slow metallic groan of the beams they are compressing—is the sound of stored energy, of potential force held in check only by the slow rate at which the plant can add material.

But the bindweed does more than grow. It *responds*. If you were to cut a coil—and no one has, because to cut it would require a tool and a willingness to bleed, and the sap of the helical bindweed is an irritant that causes a rash lasting days—the exposed surface would begin to weep, not with water but with a thick, clear fluid that smells of copper and tastes, if you were foolish enough to taste it, faintly metallic and sweet. Within minutes, the cut surface would begin to seal, a thin membrane of new tissue forming across the wound. Within hours, the coil on either side of the cut would begin to tighten, drawing the two halves together with a force you could feel if you held your hands around the coil. It heals itself. It closes its wounds. It pulls itself together.

And it grows toward things. Not toward light—the light under the canopy is diffuse enough that phototropism would be a poor strategy—but toward *vibration*. The buried motors produce a constant, low-frequency hum that travels through the root network and into the substrate and from the substrate into the steel of the building's frame. The bindweed coils follow this vibration along the beams, tightening preferentially in the direction of the nearest motor, as if the motors are calling to it and it is answering with the only language it has: slow, squared, green. Around each motor, the coils are densest. Around each motor, the steel is most deformed. The relationship is not parasitic—the motors are not harmed—or at least it is not only parasitic, because the bindweed's coils also provide structural support to the aging beams, compensating for the weakening of steel with the strengthening of living tissue. The bindweed holds the building up. The building gives the bindweed a skeleton. This is mutualism, if you want to call it that, though neither partner chose it, and neither partner could survive the other's absence. The beams would collapse without the coils. The coils would collapse without the beams. They are bound together, in every sense, for as long as the Pressworks Green endures.

---

And then there is the thing in the substrate.

It is not the substrate itself. The substrate is what you see, what you feel when you push your hand into it—root mass, fungal mats, compressed organic matter, the packed debris of seasons. The substrate is the skin. The thing I am talking about is deeper.

If you were to dig—and no one has, not because anyone has forbidden it, but because digging in the Pressworks Green feels like a violation, the way it would feel to dig into the chest of a sleeping animal—you would pass through the upper substrate, which is alive and warm and pulsing, and through the middle substrate, which is denser, more compressed, threaded through with roots as thick as cables, and into the lower substrate, which is cool and wet and very dark. And there, in the lower substrate, you would find something that is difficult to describe because it is not a thing in the way that a slug is a thing or a bindweed coil is a thing. It is a network. It is a connectedness. Every root in the Pressworks Green, regardless of species, regardless of origin, eventually connects to it. The fungal mats bridge species boundaries, linking plants that share no common ancestor into a single, continuous mycelial web. The web has mass. It has energy. It has—according to the few people who have tried to measure it—a measurable electrical potential, a voltage gradient that runs across the Pressworks Green from the boiler at the center to the boundary at the rail spur, low but constant, the way a nerve carries a signal.

It is not a brain. It is not conscious. To call it a brain would be to import too much, to flatter the system with an analogy it does not need. But it processes. It distributes resources. When one section of the Pressworks Green is damaged—a storm branch falls, a section of bindweed dies—the network reallocates. Energy flows away from the dead zone and toward the living. New growth fills the gap. The response is slow—weeks, months—but it is coordinated, and coordination implies information, and information implies processing, even if the processing is chemical rather than electrical, even if it takes the form of hormone gradients moving through tissue at the speed of diffusion.

The Pressworks Green is, in this sense, a single organism. Not a community of organisms cooperating, not an ecosystem in the way that a forest is an ecosystem, with its thousands of species pursuing their individual agendas in proximity to one another. The Pressworks Green is one thing. The slugs are part of it, in the way that mitochondria are part of a cell—borrowed, incorporated, given a role. The flies are part of it, in the way that neurotransmitters are part of a brain—carriers of signals, agents of connection. Even the buried motors, still turning, still humming, are part of it now, their energy captured and used, their mechanical rotation converted by unknown means into the slow hydraulic flow that keeps the weeping columns weeping and the substrate pulsing and the bindweed coiling.

The boiler at the center is its furnace, its metabolic engine, converting groundwater and geothermal heat into the steam that drives the condensation cycle. The weeping columns are its circulatory system. The substrate is its flesh. The bindweed is its skeleton. The slugs are its immune system, seeding surfaces with the organisms that maintain the biofilms that coat and protect every exposed surface from decay. The whole thing breathes. The whole thing circulates. The whole thing grows.

It has been doing this for longer than the people who own the surrounding land realize. The rail spur was abandoned in the 1970s, or the 1980s—the records are incomplete—and the Pressworks Green began after that, or perhaps during, or perhaps it was already underway before the last train ran and the machines were left to rust and the water began to pool and the first green things appeared in the cracks of the concrete. But the speed of its development suggests that the underlying organism—the thing in the lower substrate, the network, the process—may have been there longer. Waiting, perhaps, for the conditions to be right. Waiting for the steel and the water and the warmth and the dark. Waiting for a skeleton to grow around.

---

There is a moment, just before dusk, when the light through the canopy shifts from green to a deeper, more golden green, and the whole Pressworks Green seems to hold its breath. The weeping columns slow their dripping. The bindweed creaks less. Even the boiler's exhalations seem to pause, just for a moment, in a collective suspension that is so brief you might miss it, and so regular that once you notice it, you will never un-notice it. Every evening. A pause. A held breath. A moment of stillness in a system that is otherwise never still.

It lasts approximately ninety seconds.

Then the dripping resumes. The creaking resumes. The steam exhales. The motors hum. The slugs continue their barely perceptible movement. The flies settle into the stillness of the night cycle, their translucent bodies becoming invisible in the dimness. The Pressworks Green continues.

Ninety seconds. That is the closest thing it has to rest. That is the closest thing it has to sleep. If you are inside the Pressworks Green at dusk—and you should not be, because being there after dark is a different kind of experience, and not one that many people repeat—you will feel it. The whole space will feel like a held breath. You will hold yours too, without meaning to, without deciding to. Your body will sync to it, the way bodies do, the way human hearts sync to external rhythms when the rhythm is strong enough and close enough and real enough.

For ninety seconds, you will be part of the Pressworks Green. Not incorporated, not assimilated—you will not be absorbed or consumed or transformed—but *included*, briefly, in the pause, in the held breath, in the moment when the whole organism stops doing what it does and simply *is*, the way a heart stops between beats, not because it is failing but because the pause is part of the rhythm, because rest is part of the work, because even the most patient and persistent system in the world must, from time to time, take a moment to be nothing at all.

Then the rhythm resumes, and you are outside it again, and the Pressworks Green goes on, and you go on, and the two of you—visitor and place, moment and eternity—continue your separate paths, having shared, for ninety seconds, the same breath.
