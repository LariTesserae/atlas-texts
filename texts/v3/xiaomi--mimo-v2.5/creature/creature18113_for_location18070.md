They do not speak, for they have no language to break the silence. But they *signal*—in the faint, bioluminescent pulses that travel through the vining networks, a slow semaphore of chemical needs and warnings. The armored, many-legged climbers of the canopy are called, in the unspoken grammar of the place, **Lithophages**. They move with the deliberate caution of beings who know the structural integrity of their world is measured in millimeters. Their mandibles are tipped with diamond-hard mineral deposits, capable of scoring the vine’s surface without severing the vital fluid-lines beneath. They are pruners, architects of necessity; their feeding stimulates branching, which strengthens the network. In their stillness, they are stone. In their motion, they are the pulse of the canopy’s circulation.

On the terraces, the wingless, feathered **Gatherers** have their own rhythm. Their coarse, hair-like filaments are not for flight, but for insulation against the perpetual heat, and for collecting the mineral dust that cakes every surface. Their claws are curved and precise, adapted for a life of vertical climbing. They are the dispersers, the gardeners. Their nests—woven from the shed outer layers of the vining network—are placed with an instinct for maximum exposure to the warm, still air that facilitates seed germination. They do not call, but they communicate through the careful placement of a hard fruit in a specific crevice, a gesture passed from parent to fledgling that is both lesson and legacy. Their greatest sound is the soft *crack* of a fruit in their beak, and the muffled *thud* as they drop the seed casing to the floor of a new enclosure.

But the true citizens of the Chlorith Expanse are the **Architects of the Long Now**, and they are the plants themselves. The teal rosettes, the pulsing vines, the bone-white structural growth. They operate on a timeline that renders movement into a blur and thought into a geological event. Their society is one of chemical negotiation—root systems engaging in slow-motion trade, offering extracted phosphates for nitrogen compounds; fungal analogs (thread-like, glowing faintly) weaving through the soil, acting as barter and communication lines. The "cathedral-spanning" woody growth is not a single organism, but a consensus, a decision made over decades to share resources and bear weight together. The entire living city is a single, distributed mind, thinking in solutions and gradients, its thoughts the patient, inexorable extension of a root into a promising fissure.

At the frontier, the pioneer rosettes are the scouts, the vanguard of this collective will. They are tougher, their metabolism more brutal. They secrete acids that pool in tiny, corrosive droplets on the stone, each one a miniature siege. They are not individuals in any sense a walker from a wetter world would understand; they are temporary nodes of a will that has no center, a will to *become*, to fill the geometric silence with the logic of growth. They feel the vibration from below—the slow, rhythmic thrum that might be the planet’s pulse or the collective exhalation of a billion roots—and they grow toward it, adding their own faint tremor to the whole.

And the thing they all exist within, the warm, silent, mineral-laden air, is itself a kind of being. A medium of exchange. The metabolic fog carries the signals, the warnings, the offers of the vegetable city. It is the bloodstream of the plateau. It is thick with the weight of unspoken transactions, and to breathe it is to inhale the slow, patient ambition of an entire kingdom.

So who lives here? A democracy of chemistry, a parliament of roots, a silent civilization built on the razor’s edge of *just enough*. Its citizens are mineral and chlorophyll, chitin and feather, acid and patience. They are engaged in a project that will outlast the memory of water, a project with no name, no end, and no hurry. They are the living proof that a place need not speak to be profoundly, complexly, and densely occupied. They are the answer to the question the barren stone asks, and their answer is always the same, spoken in the only language they possess: the slow, sweet, teal-colored conquest of the next millimeter.
