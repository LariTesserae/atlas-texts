# What Lives in the Still Basin

---

## The Frogs

They are the voice of the basin, or the basin is their voice — the distinction has no meaning here.

They are not one species. They are many, though how many is a question that cannot be answered because taxonomy requires variation, and variation requires time, and time is not something the basin possesses in any usable sense. What it possesses is *repetition*, which is not the same thing, though it wears the same skin.

The largest are the wound-colored ones. They sit on the basalt columns — always on the basalt, never on the clay — and their skin is the deep, wet red of something freshly opened. Not blood-red. Deeper than that. The red of muscle tissue when it is first exposed to air, before it has had a chance to oxidize and darken. They are broad-bodied, almost absurdly so, wider than a man's fist, and they flatten themselves against the warm stone with an intimacy that makes it unclear where the frog ends and the basalt begins. Their throats pulse. The pulse is visible from a distance — a bright, pulsating sac of translucent tissue that inflates and deflates on a rhythm that never varies, that has never varied, that will never vary because variation is a thing that happens in time, and the basin has no time.

The call they produce is not a croak. It is a sustained trill, high-pitched and metallic, like a finger drawn slowly along the teeth of a comb. It lasts exactly as long as it lasts — never a fraction longer, never a fraction shorter — and when one frog pauses (as it must, because the sac must deflate, because the air must be drawn back in), there is never silence, because the frog beside it is calling, and the frog beside that one, and the frog on the column below, and the frog half-hidden in the curl of a fern fiddlehead fifty meters away, all of them calling in a phasing pattern that overlaps so precisely it sounds like one continuous voice. It is one continuous voice. It is the basin's voice.

The smaller frogs are chartreuse — the pale, almost luminous green of new growth on a plant that grows in the dark. They sit on leaves. Broad leaves, the ones that cover the ground in overlapping carpets, the ones that pulse slowly, expanding and contracting in their respiratory rhythm. The frogs sit on them and the leaves pulse beneath them, and the frogs do not move to compensate, do not adjust their grip, because the movement is so slow and so constant that it is not movement at all. It is terrain. The frogs ride the breathing of the leaves the way a stone sits on the ground.

These smaller frogs do not call. They watch. Their eyes are enormous relative to their bodies — protruding orbs of liquid black that catch the dim, directionless light and hold it like drops of oil. They face in every direction simultaneously, because one eye points one way and the other points another, and together they construct a field of vision that encompasses nearly everything. They see you. They see everything. They do not react to what they see because reaction requires the capacity for surprise, and surprise requires the expectation of something different, and nothing here is ever different.

There are frogs that live in the water. Dense-bodied, dark-skinned, almost black, with bellies of pale gray that pulse faintly with each breath. They float in the tepid pools with only their eyes and nostrils above the surface, and they breathe through their skin as much as through their mouths, absorbing the warm mineral water directly into their bloodstream. They are nearly invisible in the dark water — two eyes, two nostrils, nothing else. They do not call. They do not move. They wait, in the way that everything in the basin waits: not for something to happen, because nothing will ever happen, but in the permanent, patient state of readiness that precedes an event that has already occurred.

The tadpoles are enormous. Not metaphorically enormous. Not large-for-tadpoles enormous. They are the size of a man's hand, their bodies round and heavy with stored energy, their tails broad and muscular. They move through the warm water with slow, powerful sweeps, leaving visible wakes that ripple outward and reach the edges of the pool and then return, diminished but persistent, like memory. They breathe air. They surface periodically, their blunt snouts breaking the water with a sound so soft it is more felt than heard — a tiny displacement, a momentary flattening of the surface tension — and then they sink again, trailing a single bubble that rises and pops and is gone. They have been doing this. They will always be doing this. The process of their metamorphosis — the slow reshaping of body, the absorption of tail, the emergence of legs — is not occurring. It is not frozen, either. It simply does not exist. The tadpoles are tadpoles. The frogs are frogs. The two states coexist in the same water, in the same instant, neither one becoming the other.

## The Beetles

They are the basin's geology made animate — or its geology is its anatomy made still. The distinction collapses here, the way everything collapses into everything else when there is no time to keep them apart.

The mineral-eating beetles are the most numerous. They are small — the size of a thumbnail, most of them — and dark, their carapaces the same deep, glassy black as the basalt columns they inhabit. They move across the stone in steady, unhurried lines, their mandibles working constantly, grinding away at the mineral surface with a sound that is one of the basin's permanent features: a dry, high-pitched clicking, like the ticking of a very small clock. They eat the stone. Not metaphorically. They consume it, scraping away microscopic particles of basalt with jaws harder than the material they process, and the particles pass through their bodies and are excreted behind them in tiny trails of dark dust that do not accumulate. They cannot accumulate. The basin does not allow accumulation, does not allow loss, does not allow change. The dust is there and it is not there. The stone is eaten and it is not eaten. The beetles click and move and click and move, and the basalt columns remain exactly as they were — sharp-edged, glassy, gleaming wetly in the dim light — because the basin is a single moment, and in a single moment, nothing is consumed.

Or perhaps they are building it. Perhaps the clicking is construction, not demolition. Perhaps the particles they excrete are being deposited, rearranged, incorporated into the stone from a different angle. This would explain why the columns never diminish. This would explain why new columns appear — not over time, because there is no time, but *within* the moment, as though the basin is constantly in the process of creating itself, simultaneously building and maintaining the architecture of its own existence. The beetles are its builders. They are the only things here that seem to be *doing* something, though what they are doing may be nothing more than existing in their particular way, which happens to look like labor.

The larger beetles — the ones the size of small stones — are different. They do not eat the basalt. They climb it. They ascend the columns in slow, methodical spirals, their six legs gripping the glassy surface with pads that seem to adhere without effort, and when they reach the top they stop and remain there, motionless, their antennae extended into the thick, wet air. They are sampling something. The humidity, perhaps. The mineral content of the vapor. The infrasonic vibrations of the geothermal heat rising through the clay. Whatever they are sampling, they sample it for exactly as long as they sample it, and then they descend, following the same spiral path, and when they reach the base they begin again. The same column, always the same column. The same spiral, always the same spiral. The beetles do not choose. They do not decide. They execute a pattern that was established at the basin's emergence and has not deviated by a single degree since.

The stacked beetles — the ones that form inverted columns beneath the broad leaves — are the basin's most visible defiance of things that should not work. A beetle walks upside-down along the vein of a leaf, its legs gripping the surface with an ease that suggests gravity is a suggestion it has politely declined. On its back — on its *ventral* surface, technically, since it is inverted — a smaller beetle clings. On that beetle's back, another. And another. The column hangs there, four, five, six beetles deep, each one smaller than the one below it, and none of them falls. The physics do not work. The center of mass is too far from the leaf surface. The grip of a single beetle, even a beetle with adhesive pads, cannot support the weight of six beetles. And yet. The column hangs. The beetles are still. They do not feed, do not mate, do not move in any way. They simply *are*, in their impossible arrangement, a living stalactite of chitin and compound eyes, reflecting the dim light in dull, iridescent flashes. If you touched the column, the beetles would not scatter. They would remain exactly as they are, because scattering requires the capacity for change, and the basin has no capacity for change. You would be the one who changed. You would be the one who disrupted the pattern. The basin would remain.

## The Insects of the Air

They are everywhere above. They are the air's texture, its grain, its substance.

The gnats and mosquitoes — the things with no common name, because common names require common experience, and no one has common experience of this place — form clouds above the water pools. Not swarms. Not flocks. *Clouds.* The distinction is in the movement. Swarms move with direction, with intent, with the unified purpose of a collective mind. Clouds simply *are* — diffuse, shifting, formless masses that hover above the water's surface at a height of perhaps a meter, their wings producing a single sustained hum that is so constant and so uniform it ceases to register as sound. It becomes a property of the air itself, like humidity, like heat. The air hums. The air has always hummed. The air will always hum.

The insects within the cloud are small, nearly invisible, their bodies no larger than a grain of rice. But their wings catch the light — what little light there is, filtered through the vapor ceiling into a dim, reddish-brown murk — and reflect it in tiny, continuous flashes, so that from a distance the cloud appears to be made of light. Not bright light. Not the light of fire or electricity. The light of wet minerals. The light of basalt crystals when they catch a glint. The cloud shimmers, and the shimmer is the only movement in the basin that could be called random, because each insect moves independently, following no pattern, obeying no leader, driven by nothing more than the simple, mechanical imperatives of a body too small to contain intention. And yet the cloud maintains its shape. It holds at the same height, the same density, the same distance from the water's surface. It has always been there. It will always be there. The randomness cancels itself out, the way millions of random fluctuations cancel themselves out in a system that has reached equilibrium. The cloud is equilibrium. The cloud is the basin's way of being still by being in constant, pointless motion.

The larger insects — the ones with wings — move through the upper vegetation in slow, deliberate arcs. They are not birds. They are insects with the wingspan of birds, their wings broad and membranous and patterned with veins that echo the green rivers of the leaf-veins below. They fly with a slow, almost lazy beat that barely disturbs the thick air, and when they land on a stem or a leaf or a basalt column, they fold their wings with a precision that suggests engineering, each wing tucking against the body in an exact alignment that has been repeated without variation since the basin's emergence. They do not feed. They do not seek mates. They land, they fold, they open their large, dark, compound eyes to their full extent, and they *watch*. The watching is absolute. There is no flicker of attention, no momentary distraction, no shift of focus. The eyes are fixed, the body is still, and the insect watches the basin with a patience that is not patience, because patience implies waiting, and waiting implies anticipation of a future event, and there is no future here. There is only the permanent, crystallized present of a moment that will never end.

Among the leaves, in the spaces between the broad ground-covering carpets, smaller insects move in patterns so intricate they resemble calligraphy. Ants — or ant-like things — trace paths across the clay that are identical from one cycle of attention to the next, their antennae touching the ground at the same intervals, their bodies following the same curves around the same obstacles. They carry things. Bits of leaf. Fragments of fungal shelf. Particles of the clay itself, which they transport from one location to another with no apparent purpose, because the particles are returned to their original positions by the basin's impermeable stasis. The ants carry and the carried is carried back and the ants carry again. The labor is its own purpose. The purpose is its own labor. They are the basin's circulatory system — not moving blood, but moving matter, tracing the pathways of an organism that has no body and no organs and no need for transport but moves itself anyway, because moving is what it *is*.

## The Birds

They are wrong for this place, and the wrongness is the point.

They are too large for insects. They are too silent for predators. They move through the upper canopy — the tangled layers of vine and fern and broad-leafed canopy that form a second ground above the first — in heavy, slow arcs, their wings beating with an effort that suggests weight disproportionate to size. They are not heavy. They are not light. They are *dense*, the way everything here is dense — the air, the water, the clay, the light itself, all of it thickened by the basin's saturation into something that resists movement and rewards stillness.

Their wings are too broad. That is the first thing you notice. For their body size, the wings are enormous — not the efficient, aerodynamic wings of a bird adapted to flight, but wide, flat, almost leaf-shaped appendages that catch the thick air and push through it rather than cutting it. They fly the way a hand moves through water: slowly, with resistance, with a deliberateness that suggests the act is possible but not natural. They do not soar. They do not dive. They move from perch to perch in straight, flat lines, and when they land, they settle heavily, gripping the branch with feet too large for their legs, and they *stare*.

The eyes are the thing.

They are large — disproportionately, unsettlingly large — and dark, and reflective, and they do not blink. They face forward, unlike the eyes of most birds, which are positioned on the sides of the head for panoramic vision. These eyes face forward, like the eyes of a predator, and they lock onto whatever is in front of them with a fixity that is not predatory because it does not *want* anything. A predator's stare is a calculation: distance, speed, angle of approach, likelihood of success. These eyes do not calculate. They simply receive. They take in light and they hold it, the way the basin holds everything — without letting go, without releasing, without transforming what it receives into anything else.

The birds do not forage. They do not hunt. They do not build nests or preen their feathers or perform mating displays or defend territories. They perch and they stare, and occasionally — at intervals so long they cannot be measured — one of them will shift its weight from one foot to the other, a movement so small it might be a tremor, might be a reflex, might be nothing at all. When they open their beaks, it is not to call. It is to breathe. The basin's air is thick enough that these birds — if they are birds — need to breathe often and deeply, their throats expanding visibly with each inhalation, their ribcages swelling beneath feathers that are dark and wet and permanently slicked down against their bodies, as though they have never been dry, as though dryness is a concept that does not exist in this place.

They watch the basin. The basin watches them. Neither one sees anything new.

## The Eels

They live in the water, but they do not belong to it.

The pools are warm, tepid, mineral-heavy — not water in any conventional sense but a suspension of dissolved basalt, clay, and the chemical byproducts of a geothermal system that has no outlet and no inlet, that simply exists as a closed loop of heat and mineral and moisture. The eels move through it with a slow, sinuous undulation that barely disturbs the surface, their bodies long and dark and smooth, covered in a mucus that is indistinguishable from the water itself. They are nearly invisible until they surface, which they do with a deliberateness that suggests choice — though choice, like everything else, is a concept that the basin does not support.

When they surface, they breathe air. They open their mouths — wide, wider than their heads, revealing interiors of pale pink that are the only non-green, non-brown, non-black color in the basin — and they draw in the thick, wet atmosphere with a slow, deep inhalation that lasts exactly as long as it lasts. The sound they make is a soft, wet gasp, almost human in its quality, and it is one of the basin's less frequent sounds, which means it is noticeable, which means it is startling, which means it lasts only a moment before the basin's constant roar reclaims it.

Their eyes are large. Everything's eyes are large here. The eyes are adapted to the dim, directionless light, expanded to the maximum possible size to capture every available photon, and they are reflective — not with the bright, tapetum-lucidum flash of a nocturnal predator, but with a dull, metallic sheen, like the surface of the basalt itself. The eels see in the dark water. They see the tadpoles, the mineral dust, the submerged portions of the basalt columns, the fungal growths that cluster at the water's edge and extend their pale shelves just below the surface. They see everything. They do not react to what they see. They surface, they breathe, they sink, and the water closes behind them without a ripple, because the water, like everything else, remembers its own shape.

There are smaller eels — or the same eels, smaller, because size is not a property that changes here but a state that coexists with other states. They nest in the crevices of the basalt columns beneath the water's surface, their bodies wound around the stone in tight spirals, and they do not move for long periods. Long periods. The concept is meaningless. They are still and then they are moving and then they are still again, and between the stillness and the movement there is no transition, no acceleration, no moment of decision. There is only the basin, and the basin contains both states simultaneously, the way it contains the frogs and the tadpoles, the builders and the built, the moment of emergence and the eternity that follows it.

## The Sinuous Things

They are the largest things in the basin, and they are the most silent.

They move through the undergrowth — the dense, wet, layered tangle of ferns and vines and broad leaves that covers the clay between the basalt columns — and their passage is marked only by the parting of the vegetation. The ferns flatten. The vines sway. The broad leaves rock on their stems, sending slow waves of motion through the green carpet that propagate outward in diminishing circles. And then the vegetation closes behind them, and the basin is exactly as it was, and the only evidence of a passage is the faint, residual tremor of a leaf still settling back into its position.

They are not visible. That is, they are not visible in the way that visible things are visible — as distinct shapes with defined edges and identifiable features. They are visible the way a shadow is visible: as an absence, a disruption, a momentary darkness in a place where the light is already dim. If you look directly at one, it is not there. If you look at the space where it was a moment ago, it is still not there. It exists in the peripheral, in the suggestion, in the feeling of displacement that precedes and follows its passage through the undergrowth.

They are large enough to flatten the ferns. Large enough to leave wakes in the water pools. Large enough to produce vibrations in the clay that can be felt through the soles of bare feet — a deep, rhythmic pulsing, like a heartbeat, like the basin's own heartbeat, though the basin does not have a heart because the basin does not need a heart because the basin does not circulate because the basin does not move because the basin is a single, frozen, eternal moment.

They breathe. You can hear it, if you are very still, if you can separate the sound from the basin's constant roar. A slow, deep inhalation, drawn through a mouth wide enough to engulf a frog, though they do not engulf frogs, though they do not eat anything, though they do not do anything except move through the undergrowth in slow, purposeless circuits, their bodies pressing the vegetation aside and allowing it to close, pressing and closing, pressing and closing, a rhythm as regular as the frogs' trills, as regular as the beetles' clicking, as regular as the basin's own breathing.

They are warm. Everything is warm. But they are warm in a way that is different from the clay's warmth, different from the water's warmth, different from the stone's warmth. They are warm the way living things are warm — a warmth that is generated from within, that radiates outward, that meets the basin's ambient warmth and merges with it until the boundary between the creature's heat and the basin's heat dissolves. They are the basin, and the basin is them, and the distinction between the two was never made and never will be.

## The Fungal Colonies

They are the basin's memory — though the basin has no memory, because the basin has no past.

They grow at the base of everything. The basalt columns, the broad-leafed plants, the vine-sheathed structures that wind upward in their precise helical patterns — all of them are skirted at their base by pale, fleshy formations of fungal tissue that spread outward in shelf-like tiers, each shelf layered on top of the one below it until the original surface — the stone, the clay, the stem — disappears entirely beneath a carapace of living matter.

They are white. Not the white of snow or bone or paper, but the white of things that have never been exposed to light — the pale, almost translucent white of subcutaneous tissue, of the interior of a mouth, of the inside of an eyelid. They are the color of the basin's underside, its hidden surfaces, the parts of itself it does not show to its own dim light. And they are fleshy — not the dry, papery flesh of mushrooms you might find in a forest above, but a dense, wet, almost animal flesh that yields to pressure and then reforms, that is warm to the touch and damp with the basin's pervasive humidity, that smells of mineral and rot and growth all at once.

They are not decaying. Nothing decays here. They are not growing. Nothing grows here. They are *being*, in the same way that everything else is being — in the permanent, crystallized state of their own emergence, frozen at the exact moment of their maximum extension. The shelves are full. The shelves are complete. They have always been complete. They will always be complete. The fungus is a finished thing that presents itself as an ongoing process, a structure that looks like growth but is actually architecture, permanent and immutable and absolute.

At the edges of the colonies — at the margins where the white tissue meets the bare stone or the bare clay — there is a fringe of fine, hair-like structures that extend outward into the air like the cilia of a single enormous cell. They are nearly invisible, thinner than thread, and they sway in the basin's imperceptible air currents with a motion so slight it might be imagined. They are sampling the air. They are measuring its humidity, its mineral content, its temperature, its density. They are gathering information in a language that has no speaker and no listener, a monologue of chemical signals that is broadcast into the basin's atmosphere and absorbed by the basin's atmosphere and never received by anything that could interpret it. The fungus speaks. The basin hears. Neither one understands. Neither one needs to.

Some of the fungal colonies have developed structures that resemble organs. Not biological organs — not hearts or lungs or brains — but functional structures that perform specific tasks within the colony's economy. There are chambers at the base of the larger colonies, hidden beneath the outer shelves, where the air is warmer and wetter and more mineral-dense than the already-warm, already-wet, already-mineral-dense air outside. These chambers are incubation sites. For what? No one knows. The chambers contain nothing that is being incubated. They are empty, but they are not vacant — they are *prepared*, in the way that a nest is prepared, in the way that a womb is prepared, in the way that the basin itself is prepared: for something that has already arrived and will never arrive, for a moment that is always imminent and never occurs.

## The Waters' Inhabitants

The pools are not deep. They cannot be deep, because the ground does not permit depth — it holds, as the description says, it *holds*, and the water it holds is a surface phenomenon, a film of warm, mineral-laden liquid that covers the clay without penetrating it. But the pools are *complete*. They contain everything they need to contain, and what they need to contain is life.

The tadpoles have been described. The eels have been described. But there are smaller things — microscopic things, or nearly so — that live in the water's upper layer, in the thin, warm film where the water meets the air. Protozoa. Bacteria. Things too small to see without instruments, too small to count, too small to name. They are there. They have always been there. They are the water's consciousness — not a metaphor, not a fantasy, but a literal, biological reality. The water is alive, the way soil is alive, the way the air is alive. It teems. It hums with metabolic activity at a scale too small to perceive but too large to ignore. The water is not just water. The water is an ecosystem.

The surface of the pools is coated with a thin, iridescent film — a biofilm, a living skin of microorganisms that covers the water's surface the way the fungal shelves cover the stone. It is beautiful, in the way that the basin is beautiful: deeply, oppressively, inescapably beautiful, in a way that makes beauty feel like a threat. The film catches the dim light and refracts it into colors that do not belong in this place — greens and purples and golds that appear and disappear on the water's surface as the film shifts and settles and shifts again. It is the basin's only source of color that is not green, brown, or black. It is the basin's way of being beautiful without being alive.

Beneath the film, in the water's body, other things move. Not the tadpoles, not the eels — smaller things, the size of a fingernail, translucent, their internal organs visible through their skin as dark shadows that shift and pulse with their movement. They are shaped like leaves. They move like leaves, drifting with the water's imperceptible currents, their bodies rotating slowly, their transparent fins catching the light and refracting it. They are the water's vegetation, though they are animals, though the distinction does not matter here because the distinction between animal and vegetable, between living and nonliving, between the basin and what the basin contains, is a distinction that requires time to maintain, and the basin has no time.

They are born in the water and they die in the water and they are born again in the water and they never leave it because leaving it is a concept that does not exist in a place where everything is already everywhere, simultaneously, permanently, in a state of maximum occupancy that never diminishes and never increases because it is already full and fullness is its own limit.

## The Unnamed

There are things in the basin that have no category.

A shape moves through the water — too large for an eel, too smooth for a frog, too regular in its movements for anything that moves by instinct. It surfaces for a moment, and what breaks the water's surface is not a head, not a snout, not any identifiable anatomical structure, but a *curve* — a single, dark, glistening curve of body that rises above the water and then descends again, displacing a volume of liquid that is precisely equal to the volume of the creature that displaced it. No more. No less. The water returns to its exact previous state. The creature does not reappear.

Something clings to the underside of a vine at a height of three meters above the ground. It is dark, and it is shaped like a hand — not a human hand, not an animal's hand, but a hand-shaped arrangement of tissue, five-fingered, with digits that curl slightly inward at their tips. It does not move. It does not breathe. It does not react when the basin's humid air shifts around it. It might be a plant. It might be a fungus. It might be a thing that the basin invented on the spot, at the moment of its emergence, and then forgot, leaving it clinging to its vine in the perpetual dimness, waiting to be noticed by something that will never notice it.

In the clay, at the base of a basalt column where the fungal shelves have not yet reached, there is a hole. It is round. It is precise. Its edges are smooth, polished to a sheen that the surrounding clay does not possess, as though something has been entering and exiting it repeatedly, wearing the walls smooth with its passage. But nothing enters it. Nothing exits it. The hole is empty, and it has always been empty, and the smoothness of its walls is not evidence of use but evidence of *intent* — the basin's intent, perhaps, to create a space for something that has no occupant, a home that has no resident, a purpose that has no fulfillment.

Something hangs from the vapor ceiling. It is distant — high up, in the brownish murk where the vapor is thickest — and it is dark, and it is large, and it sways with a slow, pendulous rhythm that matches the basin's respiratory pulse. It might be a vine. It might be a root. It might be a body. It might be the basin's answer to the question of whether there is anything above the vapor, anything beyond the dim, directionless glow, anything in the source that presses warmth down upon the clay. The hanging thing reaches toward the ground but never reaches it. It hangs at the boundary between the basin and whatever is above the basin, and it sways, and it is still, and it is both at once, the way everything here is both at once.

## The Basin Itself

The basin is alive.

Not in the way that a forest is alive, or an ocean, or a desert. Those are ecosystems — collections of living things that interact with each other and with their environment in ways that produce emergent properties greater than the sum of their parts. The basin is not a collection. The basin is an *organism*. A single, vast, saturated, teeming, dripping, crawling, breathing organism that contains within its boundaries every form of life that has ever occurred to it, and none that has occurred to anything else.

The basalt columns are its bones. The clay is its flesh. The water is its blood. The vapor is its breath. The frogs are its voice. The insects are its thoughts — small, numerous, purposeless, collectively producing a pattern that no individual component is aware of. The eels are its nerves, carrying signals through the water in chemical languages that have no grammar. The sinuous things are its heartbeat — deep, rhythmic, felt more than heard, felt in the ground, felt in the water, felt in the dense, wet air that fills every space and touches every surface and never, ever stops.

The basin is not old. The basin is not young. The basin is *emergent* — caught forever in the act of becoming, frozen at the threshold between non-existence and existence, its geology embryonic, its life overwhelming, its water eternal. It was born a moment ago. It was born forever ago. Both of these are true, and neither one is more true than the other, because truth requires time, and time is the one thing the basin does not have.

The basin breathes. The plants pulse. The frogs call. The beetles click. The water drips. The eels surface and sink. The sinuous things move through the undergrowth, pressing the ferns aside. The birds perch and stare. The fungus grows without growing. The clay settles without settling. The basalt gleams, wet and dark and glassy, and nothing changes, and nothing decays, and nothing is born, and nothing dies, and the basin exists, in its totality, in its saturation, in its absolute, unyielding, perpetual *presence*.

It is the most alive place that has ever existed. It is the most still.

You cannot stay here. But something can. Something does. Everything does. The basin is its own inhabitant, and it lives in itself, and it will never leave, and it will never change, and it will never end, because it never began, because it is always beginning, because the beginning is the only thing it will ever be.

The basin breathes.

It has always been breathing.

It will always be breathing.
