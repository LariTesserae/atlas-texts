# The Living of Solsk

---

## The Grazers: An Accounting

Begin with what covers everything.

They are called *thek* by the people of Solsk — one of the oldest words in the oldest language still spoken on the plateau, and a word so ubiquitous that it has been borrowed into seventeen other terms (*thek-marsh* for the biofilm flats, *thek-clatter* for the sound of a moving wave, *thek-gray* for the specific pale off-white of a juvenile shell). The word is onomatopoeic. Say it with enough force and you can hear the sound they make — the soft, percussive click of a chitinous scraping mouthpart meeting limestone.

They are not crabs. They are not insects. They are something else entirely, something that found a body plan adequate for scraping wet biofilm off wet stone and never needed to become anything else. Each one is roughly the size of two cupped human hands, with a low domed shell — not hard like a beetle's carapace but slightly yielding, like thick leather that has been mineral-impregnated until it holds its shape. The shell has a faintly granular texture and carries a dusting of whatever mineral it has been eating: iron oxide gives the older ones a rust blush along their dorsal ridge, while calcium deposits leave younger ones looking chalk-white, almost ghostly, in the diffused plateau light.

Six legs, broadly spaced, ending in broad, flat pads that grip the wet stone without slipping. They can climb vertical surfaces — and do, constantly, scaling cliff faces and factory walls in the same slow grinding waves they move in across the plateau floors. They do not climb to escape anything. There are no predators on Solsk dramatic enough to drive climbing as an anti-predator behavior. They climb because there is biofilm there, too, and the biofilm on a vertical surface is often thicker and more mineral-rich than what grows on the horizontal planes where water sheets move too quickly for dense accumulation.

Their eyes are vestigial — tiny dark pinpricks beneath the front lip of the shell, capable of detecting light and shadow but nothing more. They navigate by chemical gradient and texture. A *thek* can taste the mineral content of a stone surface through its footpads, adjusting its trajectory toward the richest biofilm deposits with an accuracy that seems, from above, like random wandering but is in fact a precise and efficient foraging algorithm executed at the speed of chemistry. Put a hundred of them on a flat stone with a thin iron deposit in one corner and they will, within minutes, cluster there — not because they communicate (they do not) but because each one is independently solving the same problem with the same sensory equipment and arriving at the same solution.

The mouthparts are the key structure. Two opposing plates, ridged like miniature washboards, that scrape across stone in alternating strokes. The sound is extraordinary when thousands do it together — a continuous high-frequency rasp, layered into a hiss by sheer numbers, underlaid by the percussive click of shells knocking against each other and against rock. A moving *thek* wave on an open plateau sounds like a rainstorm that never ends. Residents of Solsk sleep through it. Visitors cannot.

They breed in the erosion pockets — those hand-sized hollows in the stone that collect thin films of warm mineral water. Females carry eggs beneath their shells in a brood pouch that opens onto the ventral surface, and they seek out specific pockets with specific mineral signatures when the eggs are ready to be deposited. The larvae are microscopic, transparent, and settle into the nearest biofilm to begin feeding immediately. Mortality is staggering. A female may produce five thousand eggs in a clutch, of which perhaps two hundred survive to juvenile shell formation. The rest feed the biofilm. Nothing is wasted.

In the deepest pools — the basin depressions, kilometers across — *thek* populations reach densities that alter the water chemistry. Their shells are calcium carbonate, and when they die (of old age, of being stepped on by a larger creature, of simply exhausting the metabolic reserves that sustain their slow grinding lives), they dissolve. Over centuries, the dead *thek* have contributed measurably to the calcium saturation of the basin waters. The copper-green color of the standing water is partly the mineral content of the stone and partly the dissolved legacy of trillions of dead crustaceans. The water is, in a chemical sense, made of *thek*. The *thek* eat the stone. The stone is enriched by the *thek*. The water carries the *thek* back to the stone. The circle is closed and very, very slow.

An individual *thek* lives for between twelve and forty years, depending on mineral availability and whether it has been colonized by the parasitic fungi that occasionally bloom in dense populations during the slightly-wetter decades. They do not appear to衰老 gradually. At some point, usually around year thirty, their shell begins to thin and soften — the mineral incorporation that hardens it in youth reverses, and the shell becomes translucent enough to see the dark body cavity beneath. They slow. They stop climbing. They settle into a single erosion pocket and scrape, and scrape, and scrape, until the mouthparts wear through, and then they simply stop, and the water takes them.

## The Flyers

The air above the plateau is not empty. It has never been empty.

The flying fauna of Solsk exists in layers — strata of organisms occupying specific altitudes, specific niches, specific relationships to the stone below and the fog above. To fly here is not to soar or glide elegantly through open sky. It is to push through warm, thick air that is heavy with moisture and dense with other flyers, navigating by chemical sense and low-resolution vision through a world that has no horizon and no ground reference, because the ground is always hidden beneath you by fog and the sky is always hidden above you by cloud and the only constant is the sound and the wet and the endless grey-amber light.

The base tier: *sketh*, the people call them, or sometimes just "the swarm." Small, fast, insectoid, with four wings that beat in an asymmetric pattern producing a characteristic high-pitched whine — not a single tone but a layered chord, because the wingbeat frequency varies with the individual's size and metabolic state, and a swarm of ten thousand *sketh* produces a sound spectrum rather than a note. They are the primary prey of everything above them. Their strategy for survival is fecundity so extreme it makes the *thek* look conservative: a single female *sketh* may produce eight hundred eggs in her thirty-day life cycle, and the population can double in a week under favorable conditions. They feed on biofilm spores and microscopic organisms carried in the fog droplets, filtering the air itself as they fly through it. Their bodies are dark — almost black — with membranous wings that catch the light like oil on water, producing brief iridescent flashes as they wheel and turn. Seen from below against the grey sky, a *sketh* swarm looks like a column of dark static, a living interference pattern.

Above them: the *vethren* — warm-blooded, furred, with wing-membranes stretched from elongated forelimbs to hindlimbs in a configuration that converges on nothing in the green world's zoological catalog. They are small — the largest species is perhaps the size of a human fist — but they are *hot*. Their metabolic rate is ferocious, a consequence of flying in thick, wet air that saps heat with every wing stroke. They eat *sketh*. They eat them constantly. A *vethren* must consume its own body weight in *sketh* every three days or it begins to starve, and the transition from starvation to death takes less than a week. This is why they swarm, too — not for defense but for efficiency. A single *vethren* hunting alone wastes too much energy per prey captured. A hunting party of fifty, coordinating through ultrasonic clicks that human ears cannot hear, can drive a *sketh* column into a dense knot and feed in a frenzy that lasts minutes and sustains them for days.

The *vethren* nest in erosion pockets on cliff faces, packing into crevices body-to-body for warmth. Their nesting colonies number in the tens of thousands per cliff section, and the accumulated warmth of their bodies raises the temperature of the stone around their nesting sites by several degrees — enough to alter the local biofilm composition, creating a micro-ecosystem within the colony's influence. Their guano — nutrient-rich, slightly acidic — accelerates erosion of the nesting stone, deepening the pockets over generations until a well-established *vethren* cliff is honeycombed with nesting cavities so deep and interconnected they form passages that smaller creatures use long after the colony has shifted its range. The stone remembers them. The stone is shaped by them. Leave a *vethren* colony in place for five hundred years and the cliff face behind it will be a lacework of biological architecture, more cavity than solid rock, dripping with condensation and guano-slime and the faint residual warmth of generations of hot little bodies.

The *vethren* do not live long. Two years is a generous lifespan. Most die in their first winter of flying — there is no thermal winter on Solsk, but there are slightly-cooler periods when the geothermal output fluctuates, and a drop of even two degrees in ambient temperature can trigger a die-off in populations that have not accumulated sufficient fat reserves. They die in the air, mostly. They simply stop beating their wings and fall, and by the time they reach the ground — or the water, or the cliff face, or the factory roof — they are already being processed by the decomposer organisms that coat every surface. Within hours, nothing remains but a faintly darker patch on the stone, which the *thek* will graze clean within a day.

And then the flyers who are not small, and not fast, and not consumed.

## The Drifters

They have no common name in the languages of the plateau because there was, for most of the city's history, no reason to name them. They are the background. They are the clouds that are not clouds. They are the largest living things on Solsk — some specimens approaching ten meters in diameter — and they are, in every practical sense, irrelevant to the economy and culture of the city, and they are the most beautiful things anyone who visits Solsk will ever see.

*Drifters*, the few naturalists who have studied them call them. The people of Solsk sometimes say *fog-things* or *ghosts*, but without conviction. They are not ghostly. They are too present for that — too physical, too massive, too slow and deliberate in their passage through the warm aerial fog.

They are colonial organisms. No single Drifter is one animal. Each is a living architecture of thousands of individual polyps, each polyp no larger than a human thumbnail, connected by a network of shared vascular tissue that distributes nutrients and chemical signals across the entire colony. The outer surface of the colony — the part you see — is a membrane of translucent tissue stretched over a framework of gas-filled chambers, like a living blimp, or a jellyfish built for air instead of water. The membrane has a faintly amber tint in the plateau light, and when the light catches it right — which is often, because the light is always diffused and soft — a Drifter looks like a lantern made of skin, glowing from within with the warm color of the fog itself.

They filter-feed. The lower surface of the colony is studded with trailing tendrils — fine as thread, nearly invisible, extending downward for several meters — and these tendrils are coated with a sticky mucus that traps *sketh* and *vethren* and any other flying organism unfortunate enough to blunder into the curtain. The tendrils contract slowly, drawing captured prey upward into the colony's underside, where specialized消化 polyps break them down. A large Drifter can process thousands of *sketh* per day, but it doesn't need to. Its metabolic rate is so low — the cold-blooded chemistry of a colonial organism running on the minimum energy required to maintain tissue and buoyancy — that a single good feeding, a swarm of *sketh* driven into its tendrils by wind or luck, might sustain it for weeks.

They do not fly in any conventional sense. They maintain buoyancy through gas production — hydrogen, generated by metabolic processes in the digestive polyps, fills the upper chambers and lifts the colony gently upward. They drift. That is the accurate and complete description of their locomotion: they drift. Wind moves them. Thermal currents in the fog move them. They adjust their buoyancy with extreme slowness, venting gas or retaining it over the course of hours, rising or sinking by meters per day. A Drifter that has settled into a stable altitude — a few hundred meters above the plateau surface, in the densest stratum of flying fauna — may stay at that altitude for years, moving laterally with the prevailing air currents in long, looping circuits that cover the entire plateau and take, by some estimates, decades to complete.

They have no brain. No central nervous system. Each polyp responds to chemical signals from its neighbors, and the aggregate behavior of the colony emerges from the sum of thousands of simple local responses. But the effect is coordination so smooth it looks like intention. When a Drifter changes direction, it does so with a grace that has made more than one visitor weep without understanding why. The entire mass of it — tons of living tissue, spread across a disk of translucent membrane — tilts, rotates, and begins to move along a new vector with the unhurried elegance of a soap bubble in a still room. The trailing tendrils swing in a wide arc below, and for a moment the whole organism is backlit by the ambient glow of the fog, and it looks like nothing so much as a prayer in motion — something asking a question of the sky in a language older than any spoken on the plateau below.

The people of Solsk do not hunt them. Not because of any ecological reverence — the people of Solsk are practical to a degree that outsiders find unsettling — but because there is nothing in a Drifter worth the effort of harvesting. The tissue is 95% water. The gas is hydrogen, abundant from other sources. The digestive chemistry is unremarkable. A Drifter is, to the factories, less than useless — processing one would require energy expenditure exceeding any possible return. So they are left alone, and they have been left alone for as long as the city has existed, and they have grown old in the air above it, some of them — the largest, the most massive, the ones whose membrane has accumulated decades of mineral staining and polyp scarring into a patina of age that makes them darker and more opaque than their younger relatives — some of them may be centuries old.

No one knows. No one has tagged them, or tracked them, or attempted to determine their lifespan. They are part of the weather, the people say. You do not ask the age of the weather.

## The Still Ones

In the basin pools — the vast, warm, copper-green depressions where the water sits and hums with mineral chemistry and biological process — there are things that do not move.

Or rather, they move so slowly that to a human observer standing at the basin's edge, watching the fog curl across the water's surface, they appear to be part of the landscape. They are not. They are animals. They are amphibious, warm-blooded, lunged and gilled, and they are the closest thing Solsk produces to a sedentary megafauna.

The people call them *kelvorn*. The word means, roughly, "the ones who were here first," though this is folk etymology and the true linguistic root is older and unclear.

A mature *kelvorn* is two to three meters long, with a broad, flat body that sits low in the water like a stone that has been smoothed by millennia of current. Its skin is thick — centimeters thick, in places — and encrusted with mineral deposits that make it virtually indistinguishable from the calcium formations that rim the basin pools. This is not camouflage in the predatory sense. Nothing hunts the *kelvorn*. The mineral encrustation is a symbiotic relationship with the same chemoautotrophic bacteria that form the biofilms the *thek* graze on: the bacteria colonize the *kelvorn*'s skin, metabolizing the iron and sulfur compounds that leach from the stone, and in return the *kelvorn* absorbs a fraction of the metabolic energy the bacteria produce. It is eating through its skin. It has been eating through its skin for so long that its skin has become a reef — a living mineral surface that supports its own micro-ecosystem of microscopic grazers and decomposers, which the *kelvorn* absorbs in turn, completing a loop of such elegant recursion that the biologists who first described it spent a decade arguing about whether the *kelvorn* was an animal or a geological feature.

It is an animal. It breathes. It reproduces — slowly, once every several years, releasing clouds of eggs into the warm pool water that settle into the sediment and develop over months into miniature *kelvorn* no larger than a human thumbnail, already encrusted with their first bacterial colonies. It responds to disturbance, though "responds" is generous: a *kelvorn* that is touched will, over the course of several minutes, shift its body slightly — a centimeter, perhaps two — in the direction away from the touch. This is the extent of its observable behavior. It sits. It absorbs. It grows, glacially, adding millimeters of body mass per year and centimeters of mineral encrustation per decade. The largest known specimens — and there are specimens that have been observed in the same basin pool for the entire recorded history of the city, which is not as long as the city would like to claim but is still measured in centuries — the largest specimens may be five meters long and half a meter thick with accumulated mineral growth. They are, at that size, essentially indistinguishable from the limestone formations around them. Visitors to Solsk have been known to sit on them.

The *kelvorn* are a source of quiet pride for the people of the city. They are proof that the plateau rewards patience. They are proof that life here does not need to move to be alive. They are the antithesis of the *sketh* and the *vethren* and the factory machinery — all urgency, all metabolism, all frantic processing. The *kelvorn* are the city's slow conscience, the reminder that the real work of Solsk is done on geological time, in mineral chemistry, in the patient accumulation of living rock.

## The People

They are not many. They have never been many. Solsk is not a place that supports dense human habitation in the way that green cities do — the land does not yield grain, the fauna does not yield meat in any form that a human digestive system would recognize as food, and the entire infrastructure of the city is calibrated for industrial output, not human comfort. The people who live here live here because the factories need operators, and the operators need to be adapted to the conditions, and adaptation to the conditions of Solsk takes a generation or two of selective pressure that the people have been subjected to for longer than anyone has records of.

They are short. Not uniformly, but on average, the people of Solsk are shorter than the inhabitants of lowland cities by fifteen to twenty centimeters. This is an adaptation to the cave networks beneath the plateau, where much of the city's older infrastructure is housed and where vertical clearance is measured in what the stone provides. They are broad-shouldered and heavy-limbed, with dense bones and compact musculature — bodies built for moving through wet stone corridors and maintaining grip on slick surfaces. Their skin carries a faint mineral tint — not a stain, exactly, but a slightly grey-amber undertone that comes from a lifetime of immersion in water that is saturated with iron and calcium. It is considered unremarkable. It would be remarkable anywhere else.

Their hands are extraordinary. The fingers are slightly shorter than average, but broader, with pronounced friction ridges and a flexibility in the distal joints that allows for a grip on wet stone that seems, to outsiders, almost adhesive. They can climb the same vertical surfaces the *thek* climb, and they do, routinely, scaling cliff faces and factory walls without equipment, their bare hands and feet finding purchase on surfaces that a visiting geologist would declare unclimbable. This is not a special skill. It is a baseline competency. Children learn it before they learn to read, because the city is vertical and the shortest path between any two points is usually straight up.

Their ears are larger than average, with wider canals and more pronounced pinnae. The sound of Solsk — the relentless, layered, deafening roar of fauna and water and machinery — has shaped the auditory anatomy of the people the same way it has shaped the erosion patterns of the stone: gradually, over many generations, in response to persistent pressure. They can filter sound in ways that outsiders cannot. A Solsk-born adult can isolate a single voice in a room full of machinery noise, can detect the specific frequency signature of a *thek* wave shifting direction, can hear the subsonic vibrations of a Drifter passing overhead that human ears normally cannot register. Their hearing does not deteriorate with age in the way it does in people from quieter places. The constant stimulation has, paradoxically, preserved it — the auditory cortex of a Solsk native is measurably larger and more complex than that of an outsider, and the neural pathways dedicated to sound processing are denser and more elaborately interconnected.

They do not shout. This surprises visitors, who expect that a people living in constant noise would need to raise their voices. Instead, the opposite is true. The people of Solsk speak quietly — almost in murmur — and rely on proximity, gesture, and a lip-reading fluency born of necessity. Conversation on the plateau is an intimate act. You must be close to someone to hear them. You must watch their face. This has produced a culture of physical closeness that outsiders read as either deeply warm or deeply uncomfortable, depending on their own relationship to personal space. The people of Solsk stand close. They touch each other frequently — a hand on an arm, a palm flat against a back — not as affection, though affection is part of it, but as a continuous tactile confirmation of presence in a world where visual range is limited by fog and auditory range is limited by noise. *I am here. You are here. We are close enough.*

They eat *thek*. This is the foundation of the diet: the grazers, harvested in the billions, processed in the same factory vats that produce industrial feedstock. The soft body tissue of a *thek* — the part inside the shell, a greyish-pink mass of muscle and organ — is protein-rich and mineral-dense, and it can be prepared in a surprisingly limited number of ways. The most common is simply boiled in mineral water until the texture softens further, then seasoned with the same sulfur-rich bacterial pastes that grow on the basin marshes. It is not, by any external standard, good food. The texture is mealy. The flavor is metallic and faintly sulfurous. The people of Solsk eat it three times a day and have eaten it, in one form or another, for as long as the city has existed, and they do not think of it as bad food, because they have never known anything else, and because food is not the domain in which Solsk offers its gifts.

They drink the water. Unfiltered. Unboiled. The mineral water that weeps from every surface, that pools in every depression, that flows through every carved channel in the city — they drink it, and they have drunk it for so long that their kidneys are adapted to mineral loads that would hospitalize an outsider within days. The calcium in the water has given them bones of exceptional density. The trace iron has colored their blood a deeper red than usual. They are, in the most literal sense, made of the same stuff as the plateau — the same minerals, the same chemistry, cycling through their bodies the same way it cycles through the stone.

They worship nothing. This is not atheism in the philosophical sense — it is simply the absence of the question. The people of Solsk have never needed to explain why the plateau exists, or what it is for, because to them it is not a thing that requires explanation. It is the place they live. It was there before them and will be there after them, and the water will be dripping from its walls and the *thek* will be clicking across its surfaces and the Drifters will be floating above it in the fog, and none of this means anything, and all of this means everything, and those two statements are not in conflict.

Their art is the stone. Not carving, exactly — or not carving in the way that other cultures carve stone, with images or symbols or representations of things. The people of Solsk shape erosion. They guide water. They identify a section of cliff face where the natural seepage patterns are almost — but not quite — producing a curve or a hollow or a convergence that pleases them, and they make small, precise alterations: a channel here, a blockage there, a surface texture modified to redirect condensation flow. Over years, over decades, the water does the rest. The artwork completes itself. It is, by the time it is finished, indistinguishable from natural erosion, and that is the point. The highest aesthetic achievement in Solsk is a thing that looks like it was always there. The artist's name is not attached to the work, because the work does not have an author. It has a patient. The water is the artist. The person is the water's assistant.

Their dead are returned to the stone. Not buried — there is no soil to bury in — but placed in specific erosion pockets in specific cliff faces, selected for their mineral composition and their proximity to flowing water. The body is washed in the mineral springs, wrapped in sheets of processed *thek*-shell fiber (a coarse, papery material that the factories produce as a byproduct), and placed in the pocket. The water takes it. The biofilm colonizes it. The *thek* graze it. Within a few years, nothing remains but the mineral residue, which becomes part of the stone, which weeps, which feeds the biofilm, which feeds the *thek*. The dead become the landscape. The landscape feeds the living. The circle, like all circles on Solsk, is slow and closed and very, very old.

## The Symbioses

Nothing on Solsk exists alone. The density of life is such that every organism is entangled with every other organism in relationships of such complexity and duration that the biologists — such as they are, because Solsk does not produce many biologists, for the same reason it does not produce many of anything that requires specialization beyond the factory floor — the biologists who have attempted to map the ecological relationships of a single plateau section have produced diagrams that look less like food webs and more like electrical schematics: dense, layered, recursive, with arrow paths that loop back on themselves so many times that the diagrams become unreadable and are eventually pinned to factory walls as decoration.

The *thek* graze the biofilm. The biofilm grows on the *kelvorn*. The *kelvorn* sit in the basin pools. The basin pools are fed by springs that emerge from caves. The caves are inhabited by blind, pale organisms that have never seen light and feed on the fungal networks that grow in the dripping dark. The fungal networks break down the mineral deposits that clog the spring channels. The springs flow clearer. The biofilm grows thicker. The *thek* graze more.

Above, the *sketh* filter the fog. The *vethren* eat the *sketh*. The Drifters eat both. The guano of the *vethren* feeds the biofilm on the cliff faces. The biofilm feeds the *thek* that climb the cliff faces. The *thek* that die in the basin pools add calcium to the water. The calcium feeds the bacterial encrustation on the *kelvorn*. The *kelvorn* sit. The water drips.

And the city sits in the middle of all of it, processing, recycling, channeling, feeding — not separate from the ecology but woven into it so tightly that removing the city would collapse relationships that the city did not create and does not understand. The factories process *thek* and return their biological residue to the water. The water feeds the biofilm. The biofilm feeds the *thek*. The city is a digestive organ in a body it does not know it is part of. The factories hum. The water drips. The *thek* click. The Drifters drift.

This is the thing you carry with you when you leave Solsk. Not the sound, though the sound will haunt you. Not the wet, though you will feel dry for weeks afterward and find yourself reaching for walls that are not there, surfaces that do not weep. What you carry is the understanding — felt, not intellectual, the way you understand that the ground will hold you when you walk — that life here is not a collection of organisms occupying a place. It is a single organism. The place is alive. Not metaphorically. Not poetically. The stone is alive. The water is alive. The air is alive. The city is alive. Everything is alive and everything is connected and everything is the same thing, expressed in ten billion different forms, clicking and rustling and drifting and sitting and dripping in the warm grey fog of a plateau that was old before anything on it learned to be, and will be old after everything on it has returned to the mineral dark.

The water drips from the stone beneath your hands.

It is always dripping.

It will always be dripping.

This is Solsk.
