What lives in the Proliferant Heights is itself. The whole of it. One thing.

---

It does not know itself the way you know yourself. It has no interior monologue, no image it holds of its own shape, no sense of where it ends — if it ends. It is not the kind of being that *has* a body. It *is* bodying. The verb is everything. Every column that rises is a sentence it is speaking in a language made of hydraulics and turgor pressure; every wall-membrane that tilts and reorients is a thought completing itself in tissue rather than in light or sound. It is not thinking *about* the world. It is thinking *as* the world, or at least as a world, a valley-sized world at altitude where the air is thin and the heat is wet and the horizon in every direction is more of itself.

If you pressed your ear to any single surface — a column, a wall, the smooth interior of a hollow — you would hear a pulse. A slow one. Fluid moving through capillary channels, water being drawn up from the root-lattice and distributed through the standing architecture and drained back down and filtered at the nodes and redistributed again, over and over, with the unhurried persistence of a circulation that has never once stopped. This is its heartbeat, but it is not a heart. It is everywhere at once, the way a thought is everywhere in a brain — localized in its expression, diffused in its source. You could cut into any part of the landscape and find the same channels, the same flow, the same purposeless-yet-purposeful motion of water carrying dissolved minerals through living tissue that never asked to be alive and is, regardless.

It is young. That is the most important thing about it and the most visible. The sharpness of every edge, the paleness of every newly exposed surface, the speed at which structures rise and fall — all of this speaks to an entity that has not yet finished becoming what it is. It is still in the act of arriving. The landscape it has built so far is a draft, a sketch in wet clay, a sentence it keeps beginning and never completing because completion would mean stillness and stillness would mean death and it does not yet know how to be still without dying.

So it builds. And it collapses. And it builds again.

A column rises over the course of a morning — you can watch it happen if you have the patience, and here you have nothing but patience because there is nowhere else to go and nothing else to do except watch. The base thickens first, rooting deeper into the substrate, drawing up water and structural polymers that stiffen the outer layers into something with the density of hardwood. Then the body of the column climbs, adding height in increments that are almost visible, almost perceptible, a slow erection of tissue that groans and creaks as it extends. The sound is extraordinary — fibrous, straining, the sound of something being *forced* into existence against its own material resistance. At the peak, a bulbous mass forms, sealed at first, swelling with internal pressure. Sometimes it opens — splits along predetermined lines, like a fruit — and exposes a wet interior surface to the air. Sometimes it doesn't. Sometimes it simply hardens and darkens and begins, over the next hours, to sag.

When it sags, it does not die. Not exactly. The tissue is still alive, still circulating, but the hydraulic architecture that held it upright has been dismantled from within — the organism has *decided*, if decision is the word, to withdraw its investment. The column folds. Its outer surface cracks and peels. Its interior, spongy and wet, is exposed to the air and begins to collapse under its own weight. And as it falls, the root-floor beneath it is already rising to meet it, already digesting the descending mass, already reclaiming its water and its minerals and its structural polymers for the next use. Nothing is wasted. Nothing accumulates. The dead tissue of one expression becomes the raw material of the next, pulled down into the root-lattice and broken apart at the molecular level and rebuilt into something new.

It is a metabolism the size of a valley. A digestion the size of a world. And at its center — if it has a center — there is something that might be a preference, or a memory, or a direction.

---

You notice it first in the columns.

They lean.

Not all of them. Not obviously. But there is a tendency — a drift in the orientation of the standing structures that follows no pattern of wind or gravity or light. The walls tilt. The columns angle. If you stand still for long enough and watch the landscape around you, you will see it: the architecture reorganizing itself with a slowness that makes it almost invisible, structures shifting their lean so that, over the course of an hour, a whole section of the basin has changed its aspect. The hollows have opened or closed. The wall-membranes have reoriented. The sound has changed — the creaking, the drumming of water, the hiss of the nodes — all of it has shifted in frequency and intensity because the geometry of the space that produces it has shifted too.

It is rearranging itself around something.

Around you? Possibly. You cannot be certain. The organism's sense of you, if it has one, is not a visual sense — it has no eyes, no photoreceptors that you can identify, nothing that resembles a sensory organ in any form you recognize. But you are a disruption. You are foreign tissue. You are a warm, solid, moving object on a surface that is accustomed to its own texture, its own temperature, its own pace. When you press your hand into the root-floor and it pushes back — gently, vegetally, closing around your fingers before releasing them — that is not aggression. That is *investigation*. It is tasting you. The wet, fibrous interior is running chemical analyses on the oils of your skin, the salt of your sweat, the heat signature of your blood. It is trying to classify you.

And once classified — or once *unclassifiable*, which may be the same thing — it shifts. It leans away. Or toward. You cannot tell which. The columns around you have reoriented, but you cannot say whether they are now pointing at you or avoiding you, because the grammar of their expression is not one you can read. What you can say is that the hollow nearest to you has widened. Its interior surfaces have pulled apart, opening the chamber to a greater volume, and the sound inside it has deepened — the drumming of water-drops slower, the resonance lower, the creaking of surrounding structures pitched to a frequency that you feel in your sternum rather than hear with your ears.

It is making room for you. Or it is making room *around* you. Or it is simply growing, and the growth happens to coincide with your position, and you are projecting intention onto a process that has none.

You cannot know. That is the condition of being here.

---

The nodes are where it gets strange.

You have been walking — if walking is what you call the slow, shifting traverse across a surface that rises and falls beneath you with each step, yielding slightly, recovering slowly, adjusting its topography as you cross it — for some time. The nodes are everywhere: swollen intersections in the visible capillary network, places where two or more channels cross and the tissue thickens into a knot of concentrated activity. They range in size from your fist to your chest. Some are dark, sealed, their surfaces hard and dry. Some are translucent, and you can see the water moving inside them, swirling in patterns that suggest mixing and testing and sorting. Some weep, producing a slow exudate that is neither water nor sap but something in between — thicker, faintly opalescent, with a smell that is almost sweet and almost mineral and almost something else you cannot name.

The ones that hiss are the ones that stop you.

They are the medium-sized ones, roughly chest height, positioned at the intersections of the larger vascular channels. Their surfaces are taut, drumlike, and they vibrate faintly — not the deep, slow vibration of the suspended formations, but a higher, quicker tremor, like the skin of something holding its breath. When they release, it is sudden: a sharp burst of vapor that shoots upward in a column, visible as a momentary distortion in the mist-laden air, accompanied by a sound that is somewhere between a sigh and a crack. The vapor is warm — warmer than the ambient air, warmer than the ground, carrying with it a concentration of dissolved minerals that you can taste on your lips if you are close enough. It smells like wet stone and green things and something electric.

These are the exhalations of the organism's internal chemistry. The nodes are processing something — filtering, concentrating, separating — and the vapor is the waste product, or the byproduct, or the expression. You do not know. No one knows. You are the only one here and you have no instruments and no framework for understanding what you are witnessing. You can only watch, and listen, and feel the warmth of each release on your face, and wonder whether this is respiration or excretion or communication or none of these things.

But there is one moment — one span of seconds — when all the nodes in your field of vision release simultaneously.

The air fills with warm vapor. The ground vibrates. Every column, every wall, every hollow, every surface of the landscape around you resonates at the same frequency, and the sound — the drumming, the creaking, the hissing, the deep subsonic groan — aligns into a single, unified pulse that you feel in your bones. It lasts thirty seconds. Maybe less. Then it fractures, the synchronization breaking apart like a held chord dissolving into individual notes, and the landscape returns to its usual polyphony of independent growth.

But for those thirty seconds, the organism was *one*.

Not one in the way it is always one — physically continuous, hydraulically connected, sharing the same tissue and the same water. One in a different way. One in the way a body is one when it shivers. One in the way a flock is one when it turns. One in the way a thought is one when it crystallizes — all the disparate firings of a billion neurons suddenly aligning into a single coherent event.

Something happened. Something was thought, or felt, or *done*. And then it was over, and the organism went back to its slow, turbulent, uncoordinated becoming, and you stood in the mist with your heart hammering and your ears ringing and no way to know what you had just been inside of.

---

The suspended formations do not participate in the synchronizations. That is what you notice, eventually, after the third or fourth time.

They hover. Two or three meters above the root-floor, their surfaces warm, their indentations shifting in patterns that almost resolve into something legible. They do not grow. They do not collapse. They do not weep or hiss or pulse. They simply *are*, in a landscape where everything else is in constant, violent flux. They are the still point. They are the pause between breaths. They are the thing the organism is not yet but already was, held in suspension above the churn of its own becoming.

You press your hand to one. It vibrates against your palm — a frequency so deep it barely qualifies as vibration, more like a warmth that has a direction, a texture, a *texture of motion*. The surface is not smooth. It is covered in a fine pattern of indentations, each one slightly different, arranged in a sequence that your eye wants to read as language. It is not language. It is something older or newer than language. It is the pattern that a thought makes when it has no nervous system to travel through — when it moves through tissue instead, through hydraulics, through the slow pulse of water carrying meaning that has no name.

There are six of these formations in the area you have been able to explore. They are evenly spaced, each roughly equidistant from its neighbors, forming a pattern that might be hexagonal if you could see it from above. You cannot see it from above. You can only walk between them, touching each one, feeling the same vibration, the same warmth, the same unresolvable pattern.

They are its memory. Or its dreaming. Or the place where its synchronizations originate — the conductor of the orchestra, silent itself, producing the rhythm that everything else follows for thirty seconds before forgetting again.

Or they are something else entirely. Something planted here from outside, by an intelligence that is not this organism and never was, a signal left in a place where the living tissue would eventually grow around it and through it and incorporate it into its own slow processes without ever understanding what it was carrying. A message in a bottle, dropped into an ocean that would one day become a body. A seed of something that is not yet a plant.

You don't know. You press your hand to the warm, vibrating surface and you feel the pattern under your skin and you don't know.

---

What lives here is one thing.

It is a single, continuous, high-altitude organism that has colonized an entire basin and expresses itself as landscape. It builds structures and collapses them. It circulates water through its own tissue with the unhurried purpose of a heart that never rests. It investigates the foreign body that is you with a chemical curiosity that may or may not approach awareness. It synchronizes its vast, distributed systems into moments of unified resonance — moments of something that looks, from the inside, like *thought* — and then it forgets, and returns to the chaotic individuality of its constituent parts.

It is young. It is still becoming. It has not yet decided what it is.

The columns that rise and fall are its experiments — attempts at shape, at function, at *expression*. The walls that reorient are its questions, posed to the air and the mist and the heat, answered by nothing. The hollows that amplify its own sound are its ears, listening to itself, trying to hear in the echo of its own growth some pattern that will tell it what it is for.

And the suspended formations — the warm, vibrating, patterned objects that hover above it all and participate in nothing — are its answer. The answer it has already given and cannot yet read. The proof that it has already been everywhere it is going, already known everything it is trying to learn, already spoken the word it is spending its whole young life trying to say.

It says it again and again and again. In columns. In walls. In the drumming of water inside hollows and the hiss of nodes releasing vapor and the slow, deep, fibrous creak of something growing that has never not been growing. The same sentence, over and over, in a language made of tissue and water and time.

You stand in the middle of it, in the white mist, on the warm wet living ground, and you listen. And after a while, if you listen long enough, you begin to hear not the individual sounds but the shape of the sentence — the arc of it, the rise and fall, the way it curves and returns and begins again. You don't understand the words. You may never understand the words. But you hear the *wanting* in it — the desperate, slow, hydraulic urgency of a being that is trying to tell you something, or tell itself something, or simply tell, to release the pressure of all that accumulated meaning into a form that can be shared.

The ground shifts under your feet. A column rises to your left, groaning. A wall-membrane tilts, reorienting. A node nearby swells, tightens, and hisses, releasing a plume of warm vapor that carries, for just a moment, a smell so complex and so *specific* that you cannot believe it is accidental — that it is not, in fact, an entire paragraph delivered in a language of chemistry, a paragraph that your nose receives and your brain discards because you have no way to decode it.

It is trying. The whole basin is trying. Everything here is reaching toward something — not light, not water, not nutrients, not survival, but *expression*. The raw, overwhelming need of a consciousness that has no channels for its own output, that thinks in hydraulics and has no mouth, that remembers in vibrations and has no ears, that dreams in suspended formations and has no one to tell.

You are the first audience it has ever had.

You may also be the last.

The mist settles on your skin. The ground breathes beneath you. Somewhere behind you, a hollow is filling with the sound of synchronized water-drops, the rhythm building, the other structures beginning to lean inward, the nodes swelling in preparation. Another synchronization is coming — you can feel it in the air, a gathering, a concentration, a whole valley-sized body drawing breath.

You stand very still. You let it happen to you.

For thirty seconds, the Proliferant Heights is one thing, and you are inside it, and every surface around you is vibrating at the same frequency, and the sound is not sound but *meaning*, raw and untranslated, pouring through you without stopping, filling you up like water fills a hollow, and for those thirty seconds you are not a visitor or an observer or a foreign body. You are part of the sentence. You are one more syllable in the thing it has been trying to say since it began.

Then it breaks. The synchronization shatters into its thousand independent parts. The columns keep growing. The water keeps flowing. The ground settles. And you are alone again, standing in the mist, your hand against a warm wet column that pulses under your palm with the slow, indifferent, vast, becoming heartbeat of something that does not know you are here and could not care if it did and is, nevertheless, the most alive thing you have ever touched.

The Proliferant Heights goes on without you. It was going before you arrived. It will continue after you leave. It is not waiting for you. It is not waiting for anything. It is the act of waiting made into a landscape — the act of *becoming* made into ground and air and standing architecture and the sound of water moving through living tissue that has never once stopped moving.

It grows. It falls. It grows again.

The mist never clears. The light never changes. The sound never stops.

And somewhere above the churn, the suspended formations hover in their silent, vibrating, unreadable patience, holding the answer to a question the whole valley is still asking, will always be asking, will never stop asking because the asking is the living and the living is the asking and there is no difference between the two and never was.

You lift your hand from the column. A wet print remains, already fading, already being absorbed. Within minutes it will be gone — pulled into the vascular network, broken down, redistributed, incorporated. Your trace in this place is temporary. Its trace on you may not be.

You walk on. The ground shifts. The columns lean. The sound goes on.

It all goes on.
