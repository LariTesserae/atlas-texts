# What Lives in Vorrune

---

## The First Inhabitants

Start with the moss, because the moss was here first.

Not the same moss. Not a single lineage. But something like a founding population arrived — carried on the wind, or on the feet of the first birds, or packed in the soil of the original workers' boots — and found what no moss had ever found before: a world made entirely of surface. Every fracture was a valley. Every pooled drop of water was a lake. The composite ground, weeping its perpetual film, offered what stone never could: a substrate that *yielded*. The roots — if you can call them roots; rhizoids, really, microscopic filaments thinner than hair — pushed into the resin-concrete and found that it softened under them. Not quickly. Not dramatically. But over years, over decades, the chemistry of the moss, patient and relentless, did what weather takes millennia to do. The ground gave way, and the moss grew in, and the crack widened, and more water gathered, and more moss followed.

Now they are everywhere. You cannot see the ground from above. Only from the side, where a tilted plate exposes its cross-section, can you see the gray beneath the green. In most places, the moss *is* the ground — a living skin centimeters thick, waterlogged and heavy, so saturated that when you step on it, water wells up around your boot and the surface depresses like flesh. It is not soft. It is *dense* — layer upon layer of growth compressed by its own weight, the oldest generations at the bottom slowly fossilizing into something that might, given another century, become its own stratum of composite. The moss is building ground. It is doing, organically, what the original builders did with their pours and their aggregate: adding mass, adding layers, creating substrate where there was none.

Different species occupy different zones. On the vertical faces of housings and pipe brackets, a pale, almost white species grows in thin crusts — not quite a lichen, not quite a moss, something in between, adapted to the near-constant drip that would drown anything thicker. It looks like paint. It looks like something that was always part of the surface. On the flat tops of ground plates, the dominant species is a deep, saturated emerald — *Sphagnum*-like but heavier, its leaves swollen with stored water, each pad a small reservoir that overflows at its margins in slow, thick droplets. In the warm zones — where machinery housings radiate heat through their cracked shells — a different species thrives: darker, almost black, with a faintly acrid smell, growing in dense, low hummocks that feel brittle when dry but are never dry here.

The moss does not care about purpose. It does not know the plateau was built. It does not distinguish between a crack in natural stone and a crack in resin-concrete. It simply grows, and by growing, it transforms — slowly, invisibly, on a timescale that makes human patience look frantic.

## The Ferns

The ferns came after. They needed the moss — needed the thin layer of decomposed material that the mosses and their associated fungi began to produce in the fractures. A centimeter of dark, wet humus, accumulating over decades in the crack of a ground plate: that was enough.

Now they are *everywhere*.

The common fern — the one you see from any vantage, the one that defines the texture of Vorrune as much as the moss does — is a compact, robust species with fronds that rarely exceed half a meter. It grows in tight rosettes, each rosette centered on a fracture, its roots threading down through the moss and into the gap between plates where water flows and humus collects. The fronds are thick, almost leathery, with a waxy surface that sheds the constant moisture in beads that roll to the tips and drip. In the dim light they are a dark, rich green — darker than the moss, with a faintly blue undertone that becomes visible only when you look closely, in the spaces between the droplets.

They grow in patterns that trace the architecture beneath. Follow a line of ferns across a terrace and you are following a crack — or a pipe buried just below the surface — or a cable run that vibrates at a frequency the fronds respond to, leaning toward it, curving around it. The ferns are mapping the infrastructure in green, drawing a living diagram of the hidden systems below. From the air — if you could see from the air — the terraces would look like circuit boards rendered in vegetation, every line of growth a trace of some connection you cannot see.

In the deepest fractures, where the ground plates have separated enough to walk through, the ferns grow tall — taller than a person, their fronds arching overhead to form low tunnels of green. The light inside these tunnels is almost green itself, filtered through layers of frond and mist until it becomes a thick, chlorophyll-tinted luminance that makes your skin look sickly. The air inside is still and warm — warmer than the open terraces, warmed by the water below and the composting layers of dead fronds above. These tunnels are the closest thing Vorrune has to interior space. They feel sheltered. They feel *intentional*, though they are not. A fern does not design a room. But a room is what emerges when ferns grow along parallel fractures in a cracked composite surface and their fronds meet overhead.

In spring — or what passes for spring, the season when the water table is highest and the drizzle intensifies — the ferns unfurl new fronds in tight spirals called fiddleheads, and the spiral is so ubiquitous, so repeated across every terrace and every fracture, that the landscape becomes a study in the Fibonacci sequence rendered in wet green. Thousands of spirals, each one slowly uncoiling, each one releasing its tight curl into a new frond that will catch more light, feed more growth, widen the crack it grows from. The ferns are patient, but they are not slow. A single season's growth can widen a fracture by a centimeter. A century of growth can crack a ground plate in half.

## The Sedges and the Rushes

In the pools — the shallow, still-water depressions that form wherever a ground plate tilts just enough to catch and hold the drizzle — sedges and rushes take hold.

They are unremarkable at first glance. Thin, green, grass-like, standing in water that barely reaches their roots. But spend time with them and you see what they are doing: they are *stabilizing*. Their roots form dense mats in the cracks of the plate beneath the pool, binding the fractured edges together, preventing the plate from shifting further — at least for a time, at least in one direction. They are the reason some pools persist for decades while others drain in a season. Where the sedges hold, the water stays. Where the water stays, the sedges grow. A feedback loop that creates permanence in a landscape defined by motion.

The rushes are thicker, taller, with hollow stems that creak when the mist becomes wind. They grow in clusters of a dozen or twenty, each cluster an island of relative stability in the shifting water. Birds — when they come — perch on rush stems, bending them nearly horizontal before the stems spring back. The rushes are the only perches in the pools. Without them, the birds would have nowhere to land in the wet zones. This is not a relationship the rushes evolved for. It is incidental. But the birds know the rushes, and the rushes know the birds, in whatever way rushes know anything, which is to say: in the way of growth toward light, of roots toward water, of a system responding to the pressures around it without naming those pressures or understanding them.

The sedges seed in late summer. Their seeds are tiny, black, and carried by water — not by wind, not by birds, but by the slow film of surface flow that moves across every tilted plate. The seeds drift to the edge of the pool, lodge in a crack, and wait. If the water level drops enough for the crack to breathe — for air to reach the seed — it germinates. If not, it waits longer. Some seeds wait for years. The composite ground is patient. The seeds are patient. The water that carries them is patient. Everything here operates on a timeline that has no urgency.

## The Fungi

You do not see the fungi. Not at first.

They are in the cracks, in the decomposing layers of dead moss, in the softened zones where root acids have broken down the resin-concrete into something porous and rich. They are threads — fine, white, gossamer threads that web through the humus and the wet composite alike, connecting plant to plant, dead matter to living matter, breaking the one into food for the other.

When they fruit — and they fruit rarely, in the driest months, when the water table drops just enough to expose the upper layers of humus to air — the fruiting bodies are small, pale, almost translucent. They look like ghosts. They look like the place remembering that it was once purely inorganic and trying, briefly, to be purely organic, and failing at both, and producing something that is neither: a mushroom that grows from petrified industrial slag, that feeds on compost made from dead moss and degraded resin-concrete, that pushes its cap through a fracture in a ground plate and opens to release spores into the mist.

The fungi are the translators. They are the ones who take the language of the built — the resin, the aggregate, the metal — and render it into the language of the living. Without them, the moss would starve in its cracks. Without them, the ferns would find nothing to grow in. Without them, the ground would remain ground — sealed, inert, impenetrable. The fungi break the seal. They make the plateau *porous* to life.

And they do something else, something harder to describe: they connect. The mycelial networks — invisible, vast, threading through every crack and every layer of humus — link the roots of one fern to the roots of another, thirty meters away, across a fractured plate, through a zone of bare composite. They shuttle nutrients, water, chemical signals. They create a web of dependency and exchange that mirrors the pipes and conduits of the original infrastructure — a living network overlaid on a dead one, running on the same principles, occupying the same channels. The fungi do not know they are mimicking the builders. They do not know the builders existed. But the logic of connection — of routing, of distribution, of redundancy — is the same whether it is carried in steel pipes or in protein filaments. The plateau taught the fungi its own architecture, and the fungi listened.

## The Insects

A few species. Not many. Enough.

The most common is a small, dark fly — no bigger than a grain of rice — that breeds in the standing water of the pools. Its larvae are aquatic, feeding on the algae and bacterial films that coat every submerged surface. The adults emerge in clouds on the rare occasions when the mist thins enough for something like direct light to reach the pools. They are not mosquitoes. They do not bite. They are too small and too specialized to be pests. They are simply *there*, an enormous population of tiny lives that exist in the margins of every pool, feeding on the film of organic matter that the moss and fungi produce, returning their own waste to the water that feeds the moss.

Midges. That is what you would call them, if you were a naturalist. But a naturalist would struggle here, because these are midges that have adapted to a world without seasons in the traditional sense — no true winter, no true summer, just a slow oscillation of water temperature and mist density. They breed year-round. Their generations overlap. At any given moment, a pool contains larvae at every stage of development, from eggs to pupae, a continuous gradient of growth that never peaks, never crashes, never experiences the boom-and-bust of a temperate pond. Stability. The same stability that governs everything in Vorrune governs the insects: a steady state maintained by the continuous input of energy and matter, the systems feeding themselves in loops that never break.

The slugs are visible only if you look for them. They are small — a few centimeters at most — and pale, almost the color of wet composite. They move through the moss at a pace that makes geologic time look hasty, leaving a faint trail of mucus that glistens in the dim light before the mist wets it away. They eat the moss. The moss regrows. The slugs eat the new growth. The cycle is so slow, so perfectly balanced, that the moss beds show no sign of grazing. You would have to watch a single patch of moss for a season to see the slug's effect: a slight thinning, a barely perceptible change in texture where the slug has passed. The slug does not know it is part of a system. It knows only that the moss is wet and the moss is food and the moss is everywhere. This is enough.

The snail — one species, as far as anyone has identified — carries a thin, conical shell that is nearly transparent. You can see the dark mass of its body through the shell. It moves on the wet surfaces of housings and pipes, grazing on the pale crust-moss that grows on vertical surfaces. Its shell, when it dies, dissolves slowly in the persistent moisture, returning its calcium to the water that feeds everything else. Nothing is wasted. Nothing leaves the system. The snail's shell is a deposit made into a bank that has no other customers.

## The Birds

Two species. Three, if you count the crow, but the crow does not live here. The crow visits.

The first is the wren-like species — small, brown, almost aggressively round, with a tail that it cocks upward like an antenna. It nests in the lattice frames, choosing the junction points where vertical and horizontal beams meet, building its nest in the angle where rust meets moss meets a small accumulation of debris. The nest is a tight cup of fibrous material — shredded moss, plant fibers, bits of the synthetic mesh that occasionally surfaces from the composite — lined with something softer that the bird has found somewhere in the labyrinth. You never see where it finds nesting material. The wren-like bird disappears into the structures for hours and returns with its beak full of something pale and fibrous that might be insulation from a housing, might be fungal mycelium, might be something you cannot identify because it has no analog outside this place.

It sings. Of course it sings. But its song is strange — muffled, flattened, absorbed by the wet surfaces and the dense growth so thoroughly that what reaches your ear is a ghost of the song, a impression of melody without its edges. You hear it as a pressure change more than a sound: a rhythmic modulation of the ambient silence, a pattern of intensity that your brain interprets as music because it must interpret it as something. The bird sings from deep within the lattice frames, from inside a housing that has cracked open, from the center of a dense cluster of pipes where the acoustic properties of the surrounding metal create a resonance chamber that amplifies the low frequencies and kills the high ones. The result is a song that sounds like it is coming from the infrastructure itself — as if the pipes are singing, as if Vorrune has learned to vocalize through the voice of a bird small enough to live inside its machinery.

The second species is larger — about the size of a thrush, darker, with plumage that has a faintly iridescent quality visible only when the light catches it at certain angles. It feeds on the insects. It perches on the rush stems in the pools, watching the water's surface for the tiny disturbances that indicate larvae near the surface, then dipping its beak in with a precision that belies the chaos of the water — always moving, always shifting, the surface never still. It seems to read the water the way you might read a face: finding meaning in the micro-expressions of flow and surface tension, knowing where the food is by the way the water moves over it.

This bird does not sing. It calls — a single, repeated note, a clear tone that cuts through the silence with startling clarity. It is the same kind of sound as the deep resonance of wind through a pipe, but higher, sharper, unmistakably alive. The call echoes off the composite surfaces and the metal frames, arriving from multiple directions at once, creating a spatial confusion that makes it impossible to locate the bird by sound. It seems deliberate. In a place without predators, deliberate acoustic confusion seems like an extravagance — but perhaps it is simply habit, a trait carried from the bird's ancestors in a world where being located by sound meant being eaten. The habit persists. The world that shaped it does not.

The crow comes once a week, perhaps twice. It walks the terraces with the deliberate, intelligent gait of a bird that is solving a problem. It looks for the insects. It looks for the snails. It looks for anything small and protein-rich enough to justify the energy of flying to this plateau, which is not easy to reach and not easy to leave — the infrastructure blocks a clear flight path, and the crows have learned to navigate the lattice frames with a wing-folding precision that suggests they have mapped the routes, over generations, into something like instinct. The crow is the only animal here that seems to regard Vorrune as a *place* rather than a condition. It knows what it is. It knows it is artificial. It treats the structures as obstacles and tools, not as landscape. The crow is a visitor, and it knows it is a visitor, and this knowledge gives it a quality that the other inhabitants lack: *perspective*. The crow can see the plateau from above. The crows know there is a world beyond the mist. The moss does not know this. The ferns do not know this. The birds that live here full-time do not know this. Only the crow knows, and the crow does not stay.

## The Machines

Now we come to the inhabitants that were here before any of the others.

The machines.

They were built to do something. They still do that something, or something adjacent to it, or something that has drifted so far from the original function that the distinction between purpose and accident has dissolved. Some still run. You know they run because they are warm. Touch a housing on its south face — the side away from the drizzle, the side where moss grows thickest — and you feel a heat that has no source you can identify. The housing is sealed. The access panels are rusted shut, overgrown, painted over by decades of mineral deposit. Whatever moves inside moves in darkness, generating friction, generating waste heat, doing *work* in the thermodynamic sense even if the work has no observer and no product.

Some machines run intermittently. They start and stop on cycles that seem random but probably are not — probably governed by a control system that still functions, still monitoring conditions, still making decisions, but making them on the basis of inputs that may no longer be meaningful. A temperature sensor, clogged with mineral deposit, reads the same value forever. A pressure switch, corroded into a fixed position, tells the machine that conditions are always nominal. The machine runs because it is told to run, and it is told to run because the system that tells it has concluded, on the basis of evidence that no longer reflects reality, that running is the correct state. The machine is obedient. The machine is diligent. The machine is wasting its life in the service of a decision made by a dead engineer a century ago, a decision encoded in a logic that cannot revise itself, cannot learn, cannot know that the world it was designed to operate in no longer exists.

A few machines have failed. They sit cold and dark inside their housings, their moving parts seized by rust or mineral deposit, their electrical systems shorted by the perpetual moisture. These are the only things in Vorrune that have truly stopped. Even the ground plates shift. Even the pipes slowly corrode. Even the moss grows and dies and grows again. But a seized machine is *finished* — its bearings fused, its coils corroded, its purpose extinguished with a finality that nothing else in this place achieves. The plants will eventually break open these housings, and they will feed on the decomposing metal and the degraded insulation, and they will incorporate the machine's mass into the living layer above the composite ground. But for now, the dead machines sit in their sealed housings like tombs, and the water drips on them, and the moss grows around them, and they contribute nothing to the turbulence except their weight.

And then there are the machines that have changed.

These are the most interesting. These are the ones that make Vorrune what it is.

A pump, designed to move water from one reservoir to another at a set rate, whose sensors have drifted so that it now responds to the water table's slow oscillation — pumping more when the table rises, less when it falls, not because it was programmed to respond to the water table but because the particular combination of sensor drift and mechanical wear has produced a behavior that *happens* to be adaptive. The pump is, in effect, regulating the water table — not well, not precisely, but enough. Enough to keep the pools filled. Enough to keep the moss wet. Enough to keep the whole system in the state of perpetual near-equilibrium that defines it.

A motor, somewhere in the deep infrastructure, that runs a fan in a duct. The duct leads nowhere — its outlet collapsed long ago, sealed by vegetation and mineral deposit. But the motor still turns the fan, and the fan still pushes air, and the air still moves through the ductwork, and somewhere the air finds a breach and emerges as a draft that dries one face of a housing while wetting another, creating a gradient that the moss responds to, growing thicker on the wet side, thinner on the dry, creating a pattern of growth that channels water along the housing's surface into a crack that feeds a pool that sustains a colony of sedges that stabilizes a ground plate that supports a lattice frame that holds a pipe that carries water to the pump. The loop is complete. The motor does not know it is completing a loop. The motor does not know anything. But the loop is there, and it works, and it has worked for longer than anyone can measure.

This is what the machines do in Vorrune. They do not produce anything. They do not serve any human need. They maintain conditions. They keep the water moving, the air circulating, the vibrations pulsing through the ground plates in rhythms that the moss and the ferns have grown to depend on. The machines are the climate of this place — its temperature, its moisture, its turbulence. Without them, the water would still flow, but differently. The plants would still grow, but differently. The whole equilibrium would collapse and reform into something else, something more natural, more stable, less *alive* in the specific way that Vorrune is alive. The machines are what make Vorrune Vorrune. They are the difference between a wet plateau and a *world*.

## The Water Itself

It is not an organism. It does not metabolize. It does not reproduce.

But in Vorrune, the water behaves like something alive.

It moves with purpose — or what looks like purpose. It finds cracks. It widens them. It follows the path of least resistance until that path becomes a channel, and then it deepens the channel, and then it diverts when the channel becomes too deep and the water table shifts and the balance changes. It rises into the roots of plants and is pulled upward through stems and fronds and released into the air as vapor that condenses on every surface and falls again as drip. It enters the pipes and moves through the infrastructure and exits through ruptures and pools on the ground plates and seeps back into the fractures and rises again. It is the circulatory system of Vorrune, and it is *everywhere*, and it connects everything — the moss to the ferns to the sedges to the machines to the air to the ground to the moss again — in a web of flow that is indistinguishable from metabolism.

The water carries minerals. Pale greens and ochres, deposited on every surface where flow slows or stops: copper compounds leached from corroded wiring, calcium from dissolving snail shells, iron from rusting steel, silicon from degrading composite. These deposits build up over years, forming thin crusts on the wet faces of housings and pipes, creating a palimpsest of chemical history that a trained eye could read like a book. But no trained eye visits Vorrune. The deposits accumulate unread, layer on layer, a library with no librarian and no patrons, growing thicker and more complex with each passing season, each new fracture, each new breach that releases new chemistry into the flow.

The water is warm in winter — warmer than the air, which means the pools steam on the coldest mornings, a low mist rising from the water's surface that mingles with the ambient mist and creates zones of such thick obscurity that you cannot see your own hands. The steam carries heat upward, warming the plants that grow at the pool's edge, creating microclimates where winter-blooming mosses produce sporangia — spore-bearing structures — while the rest of the plateau lies dormant. The warm water is a gift from the deep reservoir, a reminder that beneath the composite and the machinery and the living layer, there is still something of the original Earth, still heat rising from below, still energy feeding the system from a source that predates the builders by millennia and will outlast them by millennia more.

In summer, the water cools. The temperature gradient reverses. The air, now warmer than the pools, deposits moisture on the water's surface, and the pools grow slightly, and the water table rises, and the moss swells, and the whole plateau expands by millimeters — you can measure it, if you had instruments, or you can feel it, if you stood still long enough, a slow, deep breathing that lifts the ground plates and opens new fractures and releases new water and starts the cycle again.

The water is the rhythm. The water is the heartbeat. The water is the one thing in Vorrune that never stops, never fails, never seizes or corrodes or runs down. The machines break. The plants die. The ground shifts and settles and shifts again. But the water simply *moves*, carrying everything else, connecting everything else, maintaining the conditions that allow everything else to exist. Remove the water and Vorrune is a dead plateau, a mass of inert composite and rusting metal. The water is what makes it alive. The water is what makes it *Vorrune*.

## The Whole

And so the inhabitants of Vorrune are:

The moss that builds ground from nothing.
The ferns that map the hidden infrastructure in green.
The sedges that stabilize the pools that sustain them.
The fungi that translate the language of the built into the language of the living.
The insects that feed on the film of organic matter and are fed upon in turn.
The snail that grazes and dies and dissolves and returns itself to the system.
The wren-like bird that sings the infrastructure's song through a body small enough to live inside it.
The thrush-like bird that reads the water's surface for food and calls a note that echoes through the lattice frames.
The crow that visits, that sees from above, that knows this is not the whole world.
The machines that run because no one told them to stop, that maintain conditions they were not designed to maintain, that have become — through drift and decay and the slow alchemy of malfunction — the architects of an ecosystem they cannot comprehend.
The water that connects everything, that moves through everything, that is everything, finally: the medium and the message, the system and the purpose, the life that the plateau lives when it lives at all.

None of these inhabitants chose to be here. None of them planned to be here. They are here because Vorrune exists, and because Vorrune, once built, did not have the decency to stop. It kept going. It kept changing. It kept opening itself to whatever could take hold, and what took hold was life — not the grand, designed life of the builders, but the small, persistent, opportunistic life of moss and fly and fern and water, the life that fills every crack, that softens every surface, that turns every abandoned thing into a home.

Vorrune was made by hands that wanted it to do one thing.

It does another thing now.

It lives.
