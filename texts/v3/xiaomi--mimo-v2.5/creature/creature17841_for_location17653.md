## The Fungal Forest

The first and most enduring inhabitants are the fungi themselves. They are the substrate, the architecture, and the air. Vast mycelial networks lace through the rock and the living ground, pulsing with faint, bioluminescent chemistry—a slow, rhythmic glow that provides no illumination but signals metabolic activity to those who can sense it. The great shelf-fungi are cathedrals of decomposition and synthesis, their layered bodies filtering the mineral-laced water and exhaling complex spores that become the foundation of the air’s ecology. They do not think, but they *coordinate*, their growth patterns responding to vibrations and chemical shifts in a slow, collective cognition measured in seasons. They are patient. They have outlasted the metal and will outlast the memory of the hands that forged it.

## The Fauna of the Dark

In the warm, roaring dark, life has learned to listen.

**The Grazer-Drifters** are the most common large herbivores. They are low-slung, six-limbed, with broad, splayed feet to distribute their weight on the living substrate. Their bodies are armored in overlapping plates of chitin, which become mineral-encrusted over a lifetime, giving them the appearance of walking stones. They move in small, silent herds, their vibration-sense tuned to the groan of fungal growth and the rush of distant water. They feed by scraping the tender, new growth from fungal shelves with radula-like tongues. They never sleep, but they enter states of profound stillness, becoming acoustically invisible.

**The Resonants** are large, bulbous herbivores that have taken the plant-like strategy of producing sound to an extreme. Their bodies contain multiple hollow chambers of varying size and thickness. They communicate—and warn—by striking these chambers with internal hammers of bone, producing deep, complex tonal patterns that can travel for kilometers through the ground and the dense air. A Resonant’s “voice” is unique to the individual, a fingerprint of sound. Their herds maintain a constant, low murmur of contact calls, a subsonic conversation that forms the emotional baseline of the deep forest.

**The Echo-Weavers** are the apex predators. They are ambush hunters of terrifying patience. Their bodies are sleek, low to the ground, and capable of total immobility. Their mimicry is perfected: they can consciously alter their posture and the texture of their skin to match the surrounding fungal growth or a cluster of inert rock. More importantly, they can *vibrate*. Using specialized organs in their chest, they can generate low-frequency sounds that perfectly match the ambient noise of the Heightherne—the hiss of a distant fall, the drip of condensation, the drone of a Resonant herd. They lie in wait for hours, days, their presence indistinguishable from the landscape. The strike is a blur of motion, silent until the impact, followed by the sounds of feeding. Their greatest threat is the **Shushers**, prey animals that have evolved the ability to go utterly silent, ceasing all internal fluid movement and breath, becoming dead zones in the soundscape.

**The Spore-Wings** fill the air. Innumerable flying insects, their forms ranging from translucent, gnat-like swarms to larger, moth-like creatures with wingspans of nearly a meter. They navigate by a combination of echolocation (producing audible clicks) and electroreception, sensing the faint electrical fields generated by fungal metabolism. They are pollinators, decomposers, and prey. The air between the fungal spires is a thick river of their movement, a constant, clicking rustle.

**The Pipe-Dwellers** have learned to love the ruins. In the warm, sheltered spaces of the still-functioning pumping stations, colonies of a hairless, rodent-like mammal have established themselves. They are clever, social, and have learned the rhythms of the machines. They avoid moving parts and nest in insulated junction boxes, feeding on the dense fungal growth that covers every surface and on the smaller invertebrates that swarm there. They use the pipes as highways, traveling in family groups along the warm, elevated metal. They are the closest thing to a curious, playful presence in the Heightherne.

## The Deep Pools

In the black, still waters of the deep basins, life is slow and strange. The **Silt-Filterers** are large, ovoid organisms that rest on the bottom, their bodies buried in the compressed organic sediment. They draw water through porous membranes, extracting nutrients and microscopic life. They are living filters, and their excretions help build the very sediment they live in.

The **Glass-Swimmers** are ambush predators of the water. They are nearly perfectly transparent, their bodies filled with the same mineral water as their environment. They hang motionless in the water column, sensing vibrations with exquisite sensitivity. When a Silt-Filterer or a small creature disturbs the water nearby, the Glass-Swimmer contracts violently, propelling itself forward with a siphon and seizing prey with transparent, thorn-lined tentacles that are invisible until it is too late.

The pools are also repositories. Every shed carapace, every fallen fungal fragment, every organism that dies in the water eventually sinks and joins the sediment. This dark, warm mud is itself a living layer, teeming with anaerobic bacteria and archaea that break down complex organics, recycling the nutrients back into the water that feeds the entire ecosystem. It is the Heightherne’s memory and its compost heap, one and the same.

## The Inheritors of Industry

The creatures of the Heightherne do not understand the structures. They do not recognize them as built, as separate from the landscape. They are merely part of the topography—the warm ridges, the sheltered hollows, the strange, resonant caverns that hum with trapped air.

The **Pipe-Weavers** have taken this to a logical extreme. These are large, crab-like invertebrates with a powerful, mineral-hardened carapace and multiple limbs ending in fine manipulators. They have colonized the interiors of the non-functional pipes and maintenance tunnels. They are weavers in a literal sense, secreting a tough, resinous silk to repair damaged seals, block off dangerous sections, and create intricate traps for the smaller flying insects that wander into the dark corridors. They are, in their own way, engineers, maintaining and repurposing the industrial infrastructure for their own shelter and hunting grounds.

The large, silent machinery has become a habitat. Herbivores like the Grazer-Drifters rest in the lee of massive, silent turbines, using the residual heat from geothermal pipes that still run beneath them. Predators like the Echo-Weavers have learned that the acoustics of a large factory floor are different; the sound is shaped by the geometry, creating blind spots and amplifying certain frequencies, allowing for new kinds of ambush.

The abandoned tools and artifacts are curious relics. A heavy wrench is a perfect scratching post for a Grazer-Drifter, its metal smoothed over centuries by mineral-rich water and chitinous armor. A coil of wire becomes a trellis for a climbing fungus, its filaments winding through the loops. The cracked ceramic insulators are ideal nesting sites for the Pipe-Dweller rodents, their small, dark forms curled in the hollows.

They are not a civilization that came after. They are the next layer of life in a place that has always been layered. The industry did not end their story; it merely provided a new, hard, geometric landscape for the old, wet, organic story to continue. The machines pump, the water flows, the fungi grow, the hunters wait, and the prey listens. The cycle persists, indifferent to its origins, powered by geothermal pressure and the relentless, creative force of life adapting to absolute darkness.

The Heightherne does not remember who came first. It only knows who is here now: a tapestry of bone, chitin, silk, spore, and water, woven through a skeleton of rusting metal, all breathing in time with the slow, hot, wet heartbeat of the underground world.
