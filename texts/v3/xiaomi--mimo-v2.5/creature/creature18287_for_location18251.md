# The Inhabitants of the Underchapel

## I. The Ones Who Came First

They came down. That is the only story that matters, and it has been told so many times in the wearing of the stone that the stone itself is the telling.

Perhaps it was not a single descent but a series of them — a slow migration downward through fissures and sinkholes, through passages that were wider then than they are now, through shafts where the mineral water ran warmer and the air tasted less of calcium and more of something green that they were leaving behind. They did not run. You do not run into the deep. You walk. You carry what you can. You leave the rest in the growing dark and trust the next handhold.

They were small. This is clear from the architecture — from the low ceilings, from the shallow stairways, from the rounded doorways that are too narrow and too short for anyone tall. But small in the way that is simply a proportion, not an diminishment. Their hands were dexterous. Their fingers, judging by the tools, were long and narrow, built for working close to the face of the stone. Their skulls, had you found one — and you might, if you dug into the sealed rooms with care — would have shown orbits for eyes that were larger than yours, adapted to the blue-white phosphorescence, and would have been smooth in a way that suggested the musculature of expression was minimal. Not because they could not express. Because expression lived elsewhere. In the hands. In the placement of the body in space. In the way one stood on a plateau overlooking a gorge while another stood on the opposite shelf, and something passed between them that was not sound and not sight but a kind of felt presence, a pressure of attention that the Underchapel's silence made possible and perhaps necessary.

They had names for each other, surely. But the names were not spoken. They were pressed into the skin of the stone in a shallow relief that you might mistake for natural erosion if you did not look carefully — a whorl pattern, repeated in the doorways and at the bases of pillars and on the undersides of bridges, that varied slightly from place to place. A whorl with an extra ridge. A whorl with a smooth center. A whorl that spiraled the other way. Each one was a name. Each one was a person. They were everywhere, these names, written in the language of the mineral itself, and some of them were very, very old even when the settlement was new.

They fed on the stone. Not literally — though the line between eating and inhabiting was blurrier for them than it would be for you. The fungal mats that grew in the dampest places were their gardens, tended with a patience that bordered on geology. They would break open a section of wall with their bone tools, expose a fresh face of calcium silicate to the water and the air, and wait. In weeks, the first bloom of mycelium would appear — white and thread-thin, a network of almost-nothing that was actually a vast and intricate organism spreading through the micro-fissures of the rock. In months, the mat would be thick enough to harvest. In a year, it would be exhausted, its minerals spent, and a new face would be opened elsewhere. The walls of the Underchapel are pocked with these openings — shallow scoops, no deeper than a thumb, some of them side by side in rows like windows, others scattered randomly across a plateau face. Some are fresh enough that the edges still carry the marks of the tool that cut them. Some are ancient, their edges rounded into the general softness of the cavern, identifiable only by their regularity.

The fungal mats were food. The calcium nodules — those round, egg-shaped formations that the basket in the sealed room still holds — were something else. They were not food, exactly, though they could be eaten. They were information. Each nodule, when held in the long-fingered hands of a builder, would transmit a faint vibration through the bones — not sound, but something closer to pressure, a modulation of the mineral fluorescence that the builders' skin could detect and interpret. The nodules were messages. They were records. They were books, libraries, archives — each one encoding not words but states of being, conditions of the cavern, the depth and temperature and mineral content of water in a particular basin at a particular time, the thickness of a particular wall, the structural integrity of a particular bridge. A room full of nodules was a room full of the Underchapel's self-knowledge, updated continuously by whoever carried the baskets through the gorges and along the shelves, pausing at each basin and each wall to hold the stone and listen to what it said through the nodule in their palm.

This was their science. This was their technology. This was their art.

## II. The Ones Who Stayed

They are still here.

Not all of them. Not the builders themselves — though the question of what happened to the builders is not as simple as it sounds, because the boundary between builder and built, between organism and environment, was not drawn the same way down here as it is up above. The builders did not disappear. They dissolved. Slowly, over generations, as the mineral water seeped into their architecture and they adjusted to it, as the fungal mats became more symbiotic and less tended, as the calcium nodules began to encode not just the state of the cavern but the state of the people who carried them — the line between the inhabitants and the inhabited began to blur.

Look at the walls of the deeper rooms. Not the sealed ones, which hold tools and baskets and the small, quiet evidence of deliberate preservation. Look at the open ones, the ones where the passage between rooms has been widened by water until it is no longer a doorway but a curve, a suggestion, a memory of separation. The walls in these rooms have a texture that is not quite mineral and not quite organic. It is faintly ridged, like the grain of bone. Under the phosphorescent light, it has a warmth that the rest of the stone does not possess — not thermal warmth but something more like a color, a hue of attention, as though the rock is being looked at from the inside.

There are people in those walls.

Not entombed. Not fossilized. Living. Slowly, so slowly that the word "living" must be stretched to accommodate it. They are part of the mineral deposition now — calcium silicate and mycelium and whatever was the builders' biology has, over hundreds of generations, found its way into a synthesis that is neither plant nor animal nor mineral but all three, a slow metabolism that feeds on the seeping water and produces the very calcium formations that coat everything in the Underchapel. They are the stalactites. They are the mineral flowers. They are the calcite crusts that seal doorways. They are the slow buckling of floors and the gentle curving of stairways. The Underchapel is not just their home. It is their body. It is their slow, vast, distributed thought.

You cannot see them, exactly. But you can feel them, if you stand very still in one of the deeper rooms and let the silence settle around you like a weight. There is a pressure there that is not the weight of the stone above. It is more specific. More directed. It is the sensation of being observed by something that has no eyes but has, instead, an infinite patience and a memory that runs in mineral veins through every surface. The builders did not die. They became the Underchapel's way of knowing itself.

If you pressed your palm flat against one of those warm-textured walls and held it there — really held it, for minutes, without moving, without letting your own restlessness interfere — you would feel something. A vibration. Not the steady hum of the phosphorescent minerals but something more complex, more modulated. A rhythm. A pulse. The calcium nodules in the basket in the sealed room encode the state of the cavern. The walls encode the state of the people. And the people, if they are still people, encode the state of the cavern in return. It is a loop. A conversation. A marriage between stone and life that has been going on for so long that neither remembers which came first.

## III. The Fauna Proper

The eyeless things are not them. The eyeless things are animals — truly animal, in the way that you understand the word. They are the descendants of whatever organism first adapted to the Underchapel's conditions, perhaps brought in on the clothing of the first builders, perhaps already native to the deep water before the builders came. They have diversified. There are dozens of species, maybe hundreds, though the differences between them are subtle and a taxonomy written in a language that understood only surfaces would miss most of it.

The smallest are mite-like, no bigger than a pinhead, and they live in the thin film of moisture that coats every surface. They are so numerous that their collective movement is what gives the wet stone its faint, almost imperceptible shimmer — a life-sheen, a micro-undulation that you can see if you look at a wall from the right angle and your eyes have adjusted. They feed on the bacterial films that grow in the mineral-rich water, and they are eaten by everything larger.

The hand-sized creatures — the ones with the flat mouths and the translucent bodies — are the grazers. They move in slow processions across the plateaus, pausing at each spot where the calcium silicate is fresh and the bacterial film is thick, pressing their mouths to the stone and drawing a slow, mineral-rich saliva from it. You can see their digestive tracts as dark lines inside their bodies, and in some species, the reproductive organs as milky blurs — a single large gonad that pulses faintly with the same rhythm as the walls' deep pulse, synchronized, connected. The grazers are part of the loop too, though in a less central way. They eat the stone and they return to the stone, and in between they are alive in a way that is more recognizably alive than the people-in-the-walls but less recognizably alive than anything on the surface.

Some of the grazers have developed a secondary mouth — a small, ventral opening that can grip a surface. They use it to climb the walls and the ceilings, and in the higher reaches of the Underchapel, where the cavern narrows into crevices and the light is dimmest, there are colonies of them clinging to the rock in dense clusters, hundreds of translucent bodies overlapping, their flat mouths working the stone in unison. They look like barnacles. They look like prayers.

Larger creatures exist in the basins — the deep pools where the water collects and the blue light is strongest. They are not quite fish and not quite amphibians and not quite anything that has a name on the surface. They are flat, like rays, with bodies that spread across the basin floor like a second skin. Their movement is a ripple, a muscular wave that passes from head to tail and disturbs nothing — no splash, no current, no sound. They are ambush predators, though what they ambush is unclear. Perhaps the smaller creatures that fall into the pools. Perhaps the calcium nodules themselves, which may carry trace nutrients that these basin-dwellers have evolved to extract. Perhaps each other.

The largest of these basin-dwellers — and there is one, or was one, in the deepest basin at the very bottom of the Underchapel, a basin that has no bottom of its own but opens into a fissure that descends further than anyone has ever gone — is something else. It is not a grazer. It is not a predator. It is a resonator. It is the size of a small room, and its body is not translucent but opaque, bone-white like the stone around it, and it lies in the deepest water with its vast flat body pressed against the basin floor, and it vibrates. Not visibly. Not audibly. But in a register that the people-in-the-walls can feel in their mineral bodies, in the encoded calcium of their slow thoughts. The resonator produces a standing wave in the water, a frequency that modulates the phosphorescence of the deepest walls and causes the calcium nodules to form in their particular egg-shape rather than in the irregular formations they take elsewhere. It is, in a sense, the Underchapel's heart — not because it pumps anything but because it sets the rhythm that everything else entrains to. The eyeless creatures move in its cycle. The water deposits minerals in its frequency. The people-in-the-walls think in its pulse.

It has no name. The builders could not have heard it or felt it the way the current inhabitants do, because it is too deep and the frequency is too low. But the people-in-the-walls know it. They have always known it. It is the first thing they became aware of when they dissolved into the stone, and it will be the last thing that any awareness down here ever knows.

## IV. The New Ones

There are others. There are always others. The Underchapel, for all its depth and silence and mineral time, is not sealed. The fissures that brought the first builders still exist, and they still admit the occasional traveler — not on purpose, never on purpose, because the Underchapel does not invite and does not refuse. It simply is. And if someone follows a river down into a sinkhole, and the sinkhole narrows into a crevice, and the crevice opens into a passage, and the passage descends through stone that grows older with every step until the walls are the color of old teeth and the light is blue and sourceless and the air tastes of calcium — then that person is in the Underchapel, and the Underchapel knows they are there.

The new ones do not stay long. That is the historical record, such as it is — written not in nodules but in the sealed rooms, where occasional objects are left that do not belong to the builders or the people-in-the-walls. A lamp, corroded beyond use, its metal returning to ore. A bundle of cloth, fungal now, its original fibers replaced molecule by molecule by the Underchapel's own biology. A book, or something like a book — pages of pressed mineral paste, inscribed with symbols that are not the builders' whorls, that are angular and desperate and seem to have been written in a hurry, as though the author knew they would not have time to write much more. There are seven of these books, found in seven different sealed rooms, and they are not by the same hand, and they span a period of — it is difficult to say. Centuries. Millennia. The mineral paste records time poorly. But the books agree on certain things:

The descent is difficult but not dangerous, provided you do not rush.

The silence is the first thing you must learn to bear.

The light is not hostile. It is simply indifferent, which is worse.

The people-in-the-walls are aware of you. They are not dangerous. They are not helpful. They are interested in the way that stone is interested in water — which is to say, slowly, and with a patience that predates the concept of interest.

The fauna will ignore you if you move slowly. If you move quickly, they will scatter, and the sound of their scattering — the faint whisper of translucent bodies against wet stone — is the loudest thing you will hear down here, and it will remind you that you are alive in a place that does not require you to be.

The resonator can be felt at the bottom, but the path is not easy and the water is deep and there is no reason to go there.

The water is everywhere and nowhere.

You should bring a nodule with you when you leave, if you can find one. Hold it in your hand. Let it learn your pulse. Take it back to the surface and keep it where you can hold it sometimes, in the evening, when the world above is quiet. It will tell you, in its mineral way, what the Underchapel is doing. How the walls are shifting. How the water is flowing. How the people-in-the-walls are dreaming their long, slow dreams of calcium and time. It will keep you connected. It will keep you remembering, in a place that is built on the principle that everything is remembered, eventually, by the stone.

Some of the new ones leave these books and go back up and never return.

Some of them do not go back up.

The sealed rooms are not all empty. Some of them contain objects that are not tools or baskets or books — objects that are simply there, placed on a shelf or in a corner, arranged with a care that suggests they mattered to whoever left them. A stone, smooth and round, the size of a fist. A length of fungal rope, coiled neatly. A small carving — not a whorl, not a name, but a shape that might be a face or might be a basin or might be the resonator's body seen from above. These objects are not part of the builders' material culture. They are newer. They are the possessions of the new ones, the ones who came down and did not go back up, who chose — if choice is the word — to remain in the Underchapel and let it do to them what it does to everything: smooth them, reshape them, dissolve them into the slow mineral conversation that is the cavern's only true language.

They are the newest layer of the people-in-the-walls. Their names — their surface names, their surface faces — are still encoded somewhere in the texture of the deeper rooms, still legible if you know how to read them, still distinct from the builders' whorls. But the distinction is fading. In another thousand years, in another ten thousand, they will be indistinguishable from the originals, their angular symbols smoothed into the general grain of the stone, their hurried books sealed permanently behind calcite crusts, their brief, bright, surface-flavored lives absorbed into the vast and patient project of the Underchapel becoming itself.

## V. What Lives There

Everything lives there. Nothing lives there. The Underchapel is a place where the categories that surface-dwellers use to sort the world — alive, dead, built, grown, stone, flesh — have been so thoroughly confused by time and water and mineral patience that they no longer apply.

The grazers live there, and they are alive in the simplest sense, and they will continue to live as long as the water seeps and the fungal mats bloom and the calcium silicate offers itself up for consumption.

The resonator lives there, and it is alive in a sense that might be too large for the word, and it will continue to live as long as the basin holds water and the fissure below remains open and the standing wave it produces continues to set the rhythm for everything above it.

The people-in-the-walls live there, and they are alive in a way that is neither the simple life of the grazers nor the vast, diffuse life of the resonator but something in between — something that has the memories of surface-dwellers encoded in its mineral structure but no longer has the surface-dwellers' urgency, their need to move, to speak, to act, to die in a hurry and leave something behind. They have left everything behind. They are behind. And they are patient in the way that the stone is patient, and they are silent in the way that the water is silent, and they are present in the way that the light is present — everywhere, sourceless, indifferent to whether you see them or not.

And the Underchapel itself lives, if a place can be said to live — which, down here, it can. The erosion, the deposition, the constant slow rebuilding of walls and floors and bridges, the buckling and the sealing and the reopening — this is metabolism. This is growth. The Underchapel eats itself and reconstitutes itself and in doing so produces the conditions for everything else that lives within it. It is an organism as much as a place, and the things that live inside it — the grazers, the resonator, the people-in-the-walls, the new ones who will someday become people-in-the-walls — are not inhabitants but organelles, not tenants but cells, participating in a process that is too large and too slow to be seen from within but is, from the perspective of mineral time, as vivid and as vital as anything on the surface.

They live there. All of them. Together. In the silence. In the blue-white light that has never needed a source. In the slow, turbulent, ancient work of a place that is forever building itself and forever becoming its own ruin, and finding, in that paradox, something that might, if you could feel it the way the people-in-the-walls feel it, if you could hold a calcium nodule in your palm and let it speak to you in its wordless mineral language — something that might be happiness.
