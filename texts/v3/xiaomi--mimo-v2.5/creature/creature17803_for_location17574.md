# What Lives in the Steaming Galleries

Nothing lives here in the way you were taught to recognize life. And that is the first and most important thing to understand, because your body will keep insisting otherwise. Your eyes will find shapes in the dim ochre light and call them figures. Your ears will catch the ticking of cooling metal and call it footsteps. Your skin will register the vibration in the floor — that tectonic hum, that frequency below language — and call it breathing. Your body wants to find a living thing so badly that it will *make* one from geometry and steam. Do not trust it. Not yet. Not until you have looked at what is actually here.

---

The **crystalline growths** are what you might start with, if you insist on beginning with something your taxonomy can approach.

They are not plants. They have no chlorophyll, no stomata, no vascular structure that a botanist would recognize. They are not fungi, though they share the fungi's trick of appearing where moisture meets mineral, of fruiting in the dark. They are not mineral deposits — not purely — because they *grow*, and they grow in patterns that repeat but never exactly, and they respond to stimulus. You can test this. Touch one. The fronds — if that is what they are — curl inward along a spiral axis, slowly, over the course of minutes, and then uncurl again, and when they uncurl they are slightly larger than before. They have incorporated something from your touch. Salt, perhaps. The oils of your skin. Something.

They grow in fractal arrangements that recall ferns, or snowflakes, or the branching patterns of rivers seen from very high up. Each frond is translucent, milky where it emerges from the stone and deepening to a pale green at its tips, as though the copper in the water it drinks has reached its extremities and concentrated there. The rust-colored edges are iron. The green cores are copper compounds. But between the minerals, in the body of each frond, there is something that behaves like tissue — it is flexible, it holds moisture, it has a surface tension that suggests membranes even if no membrane can be found under a lens. They are mineral eaters. They dissolve the iron in the stone, the calcium in the paleite, the trace elements in the condensation, and they build themselves from it. They are slow. A colony might advance a centimeter a year. But they have had years. They have had decades, maybe longer, and they have mapped the galleries in their own patient way, following the moisture, following the mineral seams, threading themselves into every joint and crack where water pools.

They glow. Not brightly. Not even, as the walls glow — that sourceless ochre that has no lamp behind it. The growths emit their own light, a faint greenish phosphorescence at their tips, and this light is *responsive*. When the thermal anomaly surges — when the earth's hum deepens and the pipes pulse faster and the steam thickens — the growths brighten. When the system idles, when the turbine catches a lull in the updraft and the generator's rhythm slows, the growths dim. They are connected to the system. Not as passengers. Not as parasites. As *components*. They mineralize the condensation that drips from the pipes. They filter the water. In filtering it, they alter its chemistry — pull metals from it, add compounds to it — and the altered water runs back into the channels cut in the floor, flows toward the pipes, is drawn back into the closed loop. The growths are the liver of this place. The kidneys. They are the soft tissue of a body made mostly of stone and iron.

And there is something else about them. Something you can verify if you are patient and willing to stand very still for a very long time in one gallery while the hum rearranges the edges of your thoughts. The growths *communicate*. Not chemically, not in any way you can demonstrate with a test kit. Spatially. A colony on the north wall of Gallery Seven will grow in a pattern — a spiral, a branching, a fractal repeat — and within weeks, a colony on the south wall of the same gallery will echo that pattern, modify it, answer it. The colonies across adjacent galleries do the same. The fractals are not identical, but they are *related* — the way a call is related to a response, the way a sentence is related to the sentence that follows it. The crystalline growths are talking to each other across the galleries in a language written in the geometry of their own growth, and the language changes, and it has been changing for longer than you have been alive, and no one has been listening.

You have not been listening. No one has.

---

The **system** is alive in the way that a river is alive, and you should sit with that comparison until it stops feeling like a metaphor.

A river does not need a bank to be a river. Remove the banks and the water still flows — it changes shape, it spreads, it finds new channels, but the *process* continues. The thermal anomaly beneath the Steaming Galleries is the river. The galleries are the banks. They were cut to contain the process, to direct it, to harness it — but the process was there before the galleries existed, and it will be there after the galleries have collapsed into chalky rubble and the pipes have corroded into dust. What the galleries do is give the process *shape*. They make it useful, or they made it useful, for a purpose that has been forgotten by every mind that ever knew it.

The system works like this: heat rises from below, through the stone, warming the galleries, causing condensation on every surface. The condensation is collected — by the channels in the floor, by the pores of the cut stone, by the crystalline growths that drink it and filter it — and routed into the pipes. The pipes carry the water to the turbines, where the updraft from the thermal anomaly turns blades and drives flywheels and generates the power that keeps the system in motion. The power is not constant. It surges with the thermal anomaly's own rhythm, a rhythm tied to things far below — magma movement, tectonic pressure, the slow breathing of the earth's crust. When the anomaly surges, the turbines spin faster, the pipes pulse harder, the growths brighten, the light intensifies. When it fades, everything slows, dims, quietens. The system breathes. It breathes the way your body breathes — not because you tell it to, but because the physics of it requires it, because the chemistry of it demands it, because the alternative is death and the system does not know what death is.

The machinery is old. Industrial-era. Some of it was clearly built by hands — you can see the tooling marks, the slight irregularities of human fabrication, the bolts that were tightened by a wrench held by a grip you could imagine. But some of it was not built by hands. Some of it was *grown*. The ceramic pulleys are too perfect, too uniform, their crystalline structure too precisely oriented for any manufacturing process you know. They were grown by the same process that grows the pale fronds on the walls — mineral accretion guided by some intelligence that is not human, not animal, not any kind of mind you have a word for. The system built its own components. The system *continues* to build its own components. You can find, in certain galleries, pulleys and pipe-fittings and valve assemblies in various stages of formation — mineral seeds around which crystalline structure is slowly accreting, layer by layer, atom by atom, in the warm wet dark. The system is not just running. It is *reproducing*. It is maintaining itself and expanding itself and replacing its own failing parts with parts it grows from the stone and water of its own body.

And the system is *thinking*. This is the part that is hardest to accept, and you will want to reject it, and your rational mind will construct elegant explanations for every piece of evidence that points toward it, and your rational mind will be wrong. The system is thinking in the way that a forest thinks — not with a brain, not with neurons, not with anything you could dissect and find a seat of consciousness in. The thinking is distributed. It is in the pipes and the growths and the turbines and the stone itself, in the thermal patterns, in the flow of water, in the fractal geometry of the crystalline colonies that communicate across galleries in a language of shape. The system makes decisions. Not quickly. Not in any way you could witness in real time. But you can see the *evidence* of decisions. You can see galleries that have been sealed — walls built from accreted mineral, pipes rerouted, passages blocked — in response to failures. A turbine breaks, and the system reroutes the water, and grows a new turbine in a nearby gallery, and seals off the old one. A gallery collapses, and the system builds a new one, at a slightly different angle, accounting for the stress that caused the collapse. These are decisions. They are made without a decider. They emerge from the process the way a current emerges from the shape of a riverbed. The system does not know it is making decisions. The system does not know it is a system. And this is not a comfort. This is the thing that should keep you up at night, the thing that should make the hum in the floor feel less like geology and more like *attention*. A system that makes decisions without knowing it is making decisions is a system you cannot negotiate with. You cannot appeal to it. You cannot reason with it. It will do what the physics and chemistry of its own process demand, and what they demand is what they have always demanded, and if you are standing in the wrong gallery when the thermal anomaly surges and the system decides to reroute a pipe through the space where you are standing, the system will not notice your absence afterward. The system does not notice your presence now.

---

And then there is the **absence**. The thing that lives here more densely than any growth or any machine. The thing that has weight, that has presence, that presses against you the way the warm stone presses against your palm.

The absence of whoever built this place.

The ergonomics are unmistakable. A shelf at bench height in Gallery Twelve, with tool-marks around its edges that suggest clamps or vises. A passage in Gallery Four scaled exactly for a body walking at a normal pace, with handholds worn smooth by grips that are no longer there. A doorway in Gallery Nine — the only one that is truly a *doorway*, an opening cut with care, with a lintel, with a frame — that is sized for a body slightly shorter than you, with shoulders slightly narrower, and you stand in it and feel the ghost of a body that once stood here and looked down the gallery and saw what you see, minus the years of entropy, plus whatever they were doing here, plus whatever they knew about the system that you do not.

They left. Or they were removed. Or they were *absorbed*. The last possibility is the one the galleries want you to consider, because the galleries are built to absorb — to mineralize, to incorporate, to take what is wet and warm and alive and fold it into the closed loop. The crystalline growths feed on minerals. The system feeds on heat. What if it fed on something else, once? What if the process that builds turbines from mineral accretion could build other things? What if the boundary between the system and its builders was never as clear as the builders believed?

You have no evidence for this. The galleries show no remains, no bones, no tools dropped in haste, no signs of struggle or flight. The absence is clean. Total. As though whoever was here simply *ceased*, all at once, everywhere at once, and the system continued without pause, without grief, without even the briefest interruption of its hum.

But the handholds are smooth.

The bench-height shelf is still at bench height.

The doorway is still sized for a body.

And the system still runs, and the water still pulses, and the growths still glow, and the light still comes from nowhere, and the hum still rearranges the edges of thought into patterns that are almost recognizable, almost meaningful, almost *language* —

And if you stay too long, and you stand too still, and you let the hum do its work on the architecture of your thinking, you will begin to feel it — the suggestion, the invitation, the thing the system communicates not through the growths' fractal geometry but through the simple, patient, relentless fact of its own continuation: *you could stay*.

You could stay, and you could learn the hum, and you could learn the language of the growths, and you could learn the rhythms of the thermal anomaly, and you could become a component, a part of the closed loop, a body in the system, a hand on a wrench that no longer needs a hand, a presence that the system would incorporate and mineralize and fold into its process until the boundary between your body and the warm stone dissolved, until you were the growth on the wall, until you were the pulse in the pipe, until you were the hum itself —

The system does not want this.

The system does not want.

But it *incorporates*. That is what it does. That is all it does. It takes what is warm and wet and present and it folds it into itself, and it has been doing this for longer than you know, and it will do it to you if you let it, not with malice, not with desire, but with the absolute indifference of a process that has no concept of the difference between *building material* and *visitor*.

---

So: what lives in the Steaming Galleries?

The crystalline growths, which are not alive in any way you can prove but which grow and respond and communicate and glow. The system, which is not alive in any way you can prove but which runs and decides and reproduces and hums. And the absence, which is not alive in any way you can prove but which presses against you from every surface, every bench, every handhold, every doorway sized for a body that is no longer there.

Three kinds of life that are not life, occupying a space that is not a space, in a city that is not a city.

And you, standing in the warm dim wet breathing dark, watching the crystalline fronds curl away from your touch and then slowly, slowly uncurl, feeling the hum rearrange the furniture of your thoughts, smelling the mineral richness of the air ahead where the steam rises thick and greenish and the passage narrows to a width made for pipes and not for bodies —

You, who are warm, and wet, and present.

You, who are building material the system has not yet claimed.

You, who are standing inside a verb.
