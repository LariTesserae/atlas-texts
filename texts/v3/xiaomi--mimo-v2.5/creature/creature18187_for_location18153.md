# The Inhabitants

There are not many of them. That is the first thing you would notice, and possibly the last thing you would understand.

Perhaps two thousand people live in the Flats of Lethara — spread across a plain that could support many times that number, scattered among the growing structures in groupings that seem too small to be settlements and too deliberate to be accidents. They live alone, or in pairs, or in groups of five or six. Occasionally a cluster of a dozen structures will hold something closer to a village — a dozen people, maybe fifteen — but this is rare, and seems to happen only when the architecture insists on it, pressing dwellings close together until the root-mats between them merge and the residents have no choice but to share thresholds and shallow water and the early-morning sound of each other's breathing.

The people of Lethara are not small, but they do not take up much space. This is not a contradiction you notice immediately. It takes time to understand what it means — that a person can be of normal height, of ordinary weight, and still seem to *occupy less room than expected*, to move through a space without claiming it. They have learned this from the architecture. The buildings teach it, the way a narrow hallway teaches a large person to walk differently. You learn to move as though the room is doing you a favor by existing.

## What They Look Like

Their skin is not wet, but it is never entirely dry. There is a thinness to them, a translucency at the edges — at the wrists, the temples, the backs of the hands — where you can almost see the plumbing beneath. This is not illness. This is adaptation, or something more interesting than adaptation, which is that they have lived in this moisture so long and so close to these organisms that the boundary between inhabiting a place and being made of it has become difficult to locate. Their bodies hold water differently than the bodies of people who live in dry air. They perspire without effort, cool themselves without thinking about it, and in the evenings, when the geothermal surges pass through and the air goes briefly scalding, they stand in the steam columns with their arms spread and their faces upturned, and they do not burn. They absorb. The moisture enters them through their skin, and their skin drinks it the way the architecture does — without ceremony, without complaint, as though it is simply what bodies are for.

Their coloring is the coloring of the Flats. Warm browns and deep clays and the faintest suggestion of green beneath, especially along the forearms and the soles of the feet, where the body meets the root-mat most intimately. Their hair — those who have it, and not all of them do — grows in dense, heavy strands that hold water and carry the faint smell of the architecture: vegetable, mineral, warm, the smell of something alive and ancient and unconcerned with your opinion of it.

Their eyes are notable. Not for their color, which varies — amber, brown, the occasional startling grey — but for their *stillness*. People from outside, if any still arrived, would find it unsettling. The inhabitants of Lethara can look at a wall for a very long time without appearing to lose themselves in thought, because they are not losing themselves. They are *listening*. The buildings speak in slow structural language — shifts of pressure, alterations of warmth, the faintest change in the texture of a surface beneath a hand — and the inhabitants have learned to parse these signals with a fluency that makes the word *intuition* feel embarrassingly inadequate. A person standing still before a wall in Lethara is conducting a conversation. You simply cannot see one half of it.

## How They Move

They walk slowly. Not from age, not from caution, but from a practice so deep it has become physiology. Fast movement in Lethara is noticed — by the architecture, by the root-mat, by everything alive — and the inhabitants long ago discovered that a measured pace produces a more cooperative environment. Walking slowly on the root-mat sends small, even vibrations through the living ground: *I am here. I am not hurried. I am not afraid.* The roots part gently for a slow footfall. They stiffen beneath a quick one. The insects scatter. The water pools do not ripple as wildly. And the buildings — the buildings *open*.

You can watch an inhabitant move through the settlement and read the quality of the negotiation by the speed of the architecture's response. A person walking at their natural pace — unhurried, deliberate, each footfall placed with the care of someone laying down words in a language that matters — will find doorways already widening as they approach, platforms extending to meet their step, the fibrous walls pulling back just enough to create a sense of generous space. This is not the buildings obeying. This is the buildings *agreeing*. The distinction is important, and the inhabitants guard it fiercely, though they would not phrase it that way. They would say: *the room wanted to.*

Running is possible but rude, like shouting in a library, except the library will close its doors on you if you do it too often. Children learn this young. A child who runs through a corridor will find the next corridor narrower, and the one after that a dead end, and the child will stand there, damp and breathless, facing a wall that was not there a moment ago, and the wall will be warm, and patient, and utterly uninterested in the child's opinion about walls. The child will learn to walk. They always do.

There is a way of moving that you see only in the oldest inhabitants — people who have lived in the Flats for sixty, seventy, eighty years. They do not walk so much as *conduct*. Each step is part of a continuous negotiation with the ground, the air, the walls, the root-mat, and they move through the settlement as though they are part of its circulatory system, which, in a sense, they are. Their feet know which roots are load-bearing and which are decorative. Their hands trail along walls not for balance but for information — reading the pulse, the temperature, the emotional temperature, if that phrase means anything here, of the structure they are passing through. These elders leave a wake behind them in the architecture: doorways stand open a little longer, rooms they have passed through remain warm and responsive for hours, as though the building is still savoring the conversation.

## What They Do

They do not farm. The root-mat provides — not generously, not in abundance, but with the reliable sufficiency of something that has been providing for a very long time. Certain roots swell with starchy nodules at their junctions, and these are harvested by hand, pulled from the mat with a twisting motion that the inhabitants perform with their eyes half-closed, feeling for the right moment, the right node, the right amount of tension that tells you *this one is ready and the root-mat will not mind.* The architecture itself produces edible growths — soft, fibrous masses that appear on interior walls, pale and faintly sweet, that the inhabitants cut away and eat without preparation. They appear and reappear on a cycle that no one has ever charted precisely, because no one has ever needed to. They are simply *there* when you are hungry, or not there when you are not, and the relationship is so old that to ask the mechanism behind it would feel like asking why your own heart beats.

They drink the water that seeps perpetually from the clay. It is warm and mineral-heavy and faintly metallic, and outsiders would find it unpalatable, but the inhabitants drink it the way anyone drinks the water of their home — without tasting it, without noticing it, as an act so constant it has become invisible.

There is no visible labor in the way that dry-world civilizations understand labor. No fields being plowed, no materials being shaped, no structures being built. And yet the inhabitants are *busy*. They are in constant conversation with the architecture, and this is not a metaphor, and it is not passive. A person crouched at the base of a living column, hands pressed flat against the fibrous surface, eyes closed, mouth slightly open — that person is working. They are telling the structure about a preference: a window should face east, not west; a platform should extend two meters, not one; a room should be three degrees warmer tonight because a child is ill. The structure listens. The structure adjusts. Sometimes slowly, sometimes with the startling speed of a thought finding its shape. Sometimes the structure disagrees, and the conversation continues for hours or days, the person returning each morning with their hands on the wall and their patient, still eyes fixed on nothing visible, until a compromise is reached that neither the person nor the building could have predicted.

This is governance. This is construction. This is the daily labor of Lethara, and it looks like nothing at all.

## How They Speak

They speak aloud, in a language that is slow and liquid and full of long vowels that carry well through the humid air. But this is only one mode of communication, and perhaps not the primary one.

The inhabitants have developed — or perhaps been given, or perhaps simply *grown into* — a way of communicating through pressure and touch that is so refined it constitutes a parallel language. A hand on a forearm. A shoulder pressed briefly against a wall. The specific way two fingers tap a root-mat surface: once, twice, pause, once. This is not sign language. It is not coded gesture. It is something more fundamental — a vocabulary of contact that works between person and person, person and architecture, person and the living ground itself, and it carries meaning that the spoken language cannot hold.

When two inhabitants greet each other on a path between structures, they do not always speak. They may simply stand facing each other for a moment — three seconds, five, sometimes a full minute — in a stillness that looks like silence and is actually an exchange. They are reading each other's surface. The warmth of the skin, the rhythm of the pulse, the faintest shift in moisture at the temples. They are asking, without words, *how are you?* And the answer is not verbal. It is thermal, and textural, and as specific as a fingerprint. The inhabitants say that you can know a person's entire day by touching the back of their hand. They are not exaggerating much.

With the architecture, the communication is slower and deeper. An inhabitant might spend an entire morning with both palms pressed flat against a wall, breathing slowly, feeling the capillary networks rearrange themselves in response to the pressure and warmth of human touch. What they are saying to the building — what the building is saying to them — resists translation into spoken language. It is something about *need* and *shape* and *the quality of emptiness that a room should hold when someone is sleeping in it.* The buildings understand this. They have always understood this. It is, perhaps, the only language they speak fluently.

## What They Remember

They remember the dry world. This is important. They are not a people who have forgotten origin — they carry it, carefully, the way you carry a word in a foreign language that you are terrified of pronouncing incorrectly. They remember that there was a time before the Flats, a place where the ground was hard and the air was thin and buildings were made of dead things — stone, metal, the processed skeletons of trees — and they remember it with a mixture of grief and relief that has never fully resolved and never will.

The older generation tells stories. Not often, and not in the way that storytellers in other places perform. The stories come out sideways — in a pause before sleep, in the way an elder rests their hand on a wall and says *this room is wider than the one I grew up in, the one in the dry world, the one with corners,* and the word *corners* carries an entire architecture of loss. The young people listen, and what they absorb is not the facts of the dry world but the *feeling* of it — the hardness, the brittleness, the terrifying permanence of structures that could not heal, could not rearrange, could not grieve, could not open their doors wider when you came home tired and close them gently when you needed to be alone.

They feel pity for the dry world. This is genuine and uncomplicated and slightly embarrassing to them, the way any unexamined assumption is embarrassing when noticed. They pity the people who live in buildings that cannot hear them, who walk on ground that does not know their weight, who drink water from channels that do not care whether they are thirsty. This pity is not arrogance. It is closer to the pity you feel for someone who is sleeping on a stone floor when there is a bed in the next room. *Why are you doing this? Don't you know the wall will hold you if you ask?*

## How They Die

The inhabitants of Lethara die as all people die — in their time, in their way, with whatever dignity or indignity the moment allows. But the *manner* of it, the shape of the leaving, is specific to this place.

When an inhabitant knows that death is approaching — and they almost always know, because their bodies have become so attuned to the rhythms of the Flats that the body's own winding-down is read with the same fluency they bring to the architecture — they leave their dwelling. Not always immediately. Sometimes they spend days or weeks in quiet negotiation with their structure, standing in the center of the room with their hands on the walls, feeling the building's resistance to what is coming, feeling it search for alternatives in the cellulose, in the capillary networks, in whatever deep rootlight energy feeds these impossible organisms. The building tries. It always tries. It offers warmth, it offers a new configuration, it offers a platform that extends toward the sun as though height alone might be enough to forestall what is coming. The inhabitant accepts these offerings with gratitude and does not let them change the outcome.

When the time comes, they walk — slowly, always slowly — to a place where the root-mat is thickest. A junction. A confluence where multiple root systems overlap and the white fibers are so dense that the ground beneath is invisible, and the clay is a rumor, and the living mat hums with the combined pulse of dozens of structures, all breathing together. The inhabitants call these places *mouths*, and they are the closest thing Lethara has to sacred ground, though the inhabitants would not use that word. They would say: *this is where the ground listens best.*

They lie down. The root-mat accepts them. Over hours or days, the fine white fibers rise around and over the body with the slow, courteous insistence of someone making a bed for a guest who has stayed too long and is finally, finally ready to sleep. The water film covers them. The clay beneath warms. And the architecture — every structure within reach — goes quiet. The deep hum drops to a frequency below hearing. The birds stop. Even the insects still their chittering. For a few minutes or a few hours, the Flats of Lethara are silent, and the silence is so unusual that the youngest children, the ones who have never heard it, will look up from whatever they are doing with wide, startled eyes, and an older person will place a hand on their head and say: *shh. Someone is going home.*

Afterward, the root-mat closes. The body is taken in — not consumed, not recycled in any way that the word implies, but *incorporated*, woven into the living network so thoroughly and so completely that within a season there is no place where the person ends and the root-mat begins. The architecture remembers. A room that was loved will remain warm long after its occupant has gone, and the warmth will not fade for years, and the structure will resist being reconfigured in that room for a long time afterward, holding the shape of the life it contained the way a hand holds the memory of what it once grasped.

The inhabitants visit these rooms. They stand in them with their hands on the walls. They are reading the grief — or the structural equivalent of grief — that the building holds, and they are adding their own warmth to it, and the room holds them both, the living and the memory of the dead, and the cellulose fibers rearrange themselves in tiny increments that no one watches and everyone feels.

## What They Are Becoming

It would be wrong to say that the inhabitants of Lethara are unchanged by this place. It would be wrong to say that they are the same people who arrived centuries ago — or their descendants are, but what does that mean when the word *descendant* implies a continuity of form that the Flats have been slowly, politely, over generations, dissolving.

The children are different from the elders, and not only in age. Their skin is more translucent. Their sensitivity to the architecture is more acute — a child of five in Lethara can read the emotional state of a building by pressing their cheek against a wall, a skill that takes decades to acquire for an adult who arrived from outside. The young people move through the settlement as though they are part of it, not because they have learned to, but because some membrane between *person* and *place* has thinned to the point where the distinction is theoretical.

There is a word in their language — *rootbound*, which outsiders might translate as *belonging*, though it carries a connotation that belonging does not quite capture. To be rootbound is to be held. Not trapped. Not imprisoned. *Held* — the way a hand holds water, gently, without squeezing, allowing the water to be water while keeping it from dispersing. The rootbound person is part of the settlement's living system in a way that goes beyond habitation. They are *metabolized* by the place. They contribute their body heat, their movement, their breath, their emotional life — whatever that is, whatever the architecture reads when a person cries or laughs or stands in a doorway feeling uncertain — to the settlement's total metabolic process. They are, in a sense that biology would struggle to formalize and the inhabitants would struggle to deny, *organs.*

The oldest among them know this. They have felt it happening — the slow integration, the gradual softening of the boundary between self and structure. They do not resist it. They do not celebrate it. They simply note, in the quiet way of people who have been noting things for a very long time, that the wall responds to their touch faster now than it did when they were young. That the root-mat parts for their feet before they have decided to step. That they can feel the geothermal surges coming a full hour before they arrive — not as prediction but as *sensation*, a warming in the bones that is identical to the warming in the clay beneath them, as though the distinction between *my bones* and *this ground* has become a courtesy rather than a fact.

They are not becoming the architecture. That is too simple, too horror-story, too much like the narratives of the dry world, where transformation is always a violation. They are becoming *comensurate* with it. They are becoming the right size and shape and temperature to live in a place where the buildings think and the ground breathes and the water rises without cause. They are becoming, after centuries of negotiation, *easy to hold.*

And the architecture holds them. Gently, perpetually, without condition, the way the root-mat holds everything — the insects, the water, the ancient clay, the slow pulse of rootlight rising from below. The inhabitants of Lethara are held the way all living things in this place are held: in a warmth that does not explain itself, in a moisture that never dries, in a turbulence that rearranges everything and destroys nothing, in a silence that falls like a blanket over the mouth of the living ground when someone goes home at last.

It is not a paradise. It is not a horror. It is a *place* — the most literal, the most material, the most undeniable kind of place — and it is full of people who have been held long enough to understand that being held and being free are not opposites, that a wall that closes on you is not a wall that hates you, that a root that curls toward the vibration of your weight is not a root that traps you, and that the deepest kind of home is the one that knows what you need before you do, and offers it, and waits.
