# What Lives in The Weft

---

## I. The Growers

There is a woman named Tessick who has not touched stone with her bare hands in eleven years. Not because she avoids it. Because the settlement has shifted, over those years, so that every path she walks is root-mat and living bark and the dense spongy give of compressed peat. The last stone surface she remembers was a stretch of exposed basin-floor near the eastern lip where a landslide had scoured the vegetation bare, and she'd stepped on it reflexively while carrying a bundle of drying rope-fiber, and the shock of it — cold, hard, *unyielding* — had stopped her mid-stride. She'd stood there for a moment, feeling the stone through the calluses of her feet, and thought: *that is what the outsiders live on. That is what they build with.* And she'd walked on, back into the softness, back into the breathing ground.

Tessick is forty-three, which is old for Sowren, though not exceptionally so. The damp works into joints early. Her hands are permanently slightly swollen, the knuckles widened, the fingers curved inward from a lifetime of gripping curved surfaces — branch-handles, vine-loops, the rounded edges of bark-doorways. She moves the way all Sowren elders move: deliberately, economically, with a kind of rolling balance that keeps her center of gravity low and adaptive, ready to shift weight at any moment because the surface beneath her feet is never quite where it was yesterday.

She is a Master Grower, which is not a title anyone confers. It is something that accumulates, the way moss accumulates, until one day people begin to defer to you without quite knowing when the shift happened. Tessick knows, perhaps, sixty species of woody vine by touch alone — the grain, the flexibility, the smell of healthy versus stressed tissue. She knows which species will accept which training, which will rebel and grow straight no matter what you do, which will die if you prune them at the wrong time of year, which will survive anything, which will *listen.*

That is the word the growers use. *Listen.* Not in the mystical sense, though outsiders sometimes romanticize it that way. In the practical sense. The plants in Sowren respond to stimuli — sound, chemical, mechanical — and a skilled grower reads those responses the way a sailor reads wind. Tap a wall-vine at a certain rhythm, three quick and two slow, and within a season the tissue at that point will thicken and split, sending out two new branches that can be trained into an arch. Apply a paste of fermented beetle-husks and certain mineral clay to the base of a platform-tree, and the bark will harden faster, creating a denser outer shell. It is not magic. It is observation refined over centuries, passed from hand to hand, mouth to mouth, because nothing in Sowren stays still long enough to be written down.

Tessick's apprentice is a boy named Orru, sixteen, who has hands that are still soft enough to feel the subtle difference between a vine that is growing and one that is merely alive. This is considered a gift. In a few years the calluses will close in, and he will have to learn to read the plants through other senses — smell, sound, the vibration of tapping a fingertip against bark. But for now he feels things Tessick can no longer feel, and she uses him ruthlessly. *Touch this. Now this. Tell me what you feel.* He is sometimes wrong, and she lets him be wrong, because being wrong about a vine's readiness is how you learn to be right about it.

They are not related. In Sowren, kinship is measured by what you have built together. Tessick raised Orru from age four, when his mother died in a root-collapse that also killed two others. The collapsed section was rebuilt within a week — a new species of fast-growing liana was trained into the void, and within a month the passage was stronger than before. Orru does not remember the collapse. He remembers the new growth.

## II. The Smoke-Keeper

Fire lives in Sowren the way a wild animal lives in a house it has not been invited into: tolerated, watched constantly, never trusted. There are three hearth-platforms in the settlement, stone-lined depressions set into the upper surfaces of the great platform-trees, each one tended by a Smoke-Keeper — a role that combines elements of cook, engineer, priest, and firefighter.

The Smoke-Keeper of the central tree is a man named Delune. He is thin, precise, and possessed of an anxiety that the other Sowren people find both excessive and indispensable. Delune sleeps less than anyone else in the settlement. He claims this is because fire demands vigilance, but the truth is closer to the reverse: his vigilance has made him unable to sleep. He has memorized the moisture content of every significant structure within thirty meters of his hearth-platform, and he monitors it constantly, pressing his thumb against bark and reading the dampness the way a physician reads a pulse.

The hearth is a shallow dish of fitted stone, perhaps half a meter across, set into a carved depression in the platform's surface and surrounded by a ring of wet-moss packing that must be re-saturated every few hours. The fire itself is small — never more than a fistful of dry material at any one time. Delune feeds it in measured increments, maintaining a steady bed of coals that can be brought to flame for cooking or smoking and reduced again to embers for storage. The wood he burns is a specific species — dense, slow-burning, low-smoke — that grows only on the upper slopes and must be carried up by the basket-load. He hoards it. He knows exactly how many days of fuel he has at any given time, and he adjusts the settlement's cooking schedule accordingly. When fuel runs low, the meals shift from cooked to raw, and nobody complains because nobody wants to be the reason Delune has to burn something that isn't right.

The smoke from his hearth filters up through a natural chimney formed by the hollow core of the platform-tree's trunk, rising through successive levels and dissipating into the canopy above. This is not an accident. The original Smoke-Keeper, three centuries ago, chose this tree precisely because of its chimney-like hollow, and the knowledge of which trees have this feature has been part of the Smoke-Keeper's training ever since. A hearth without a chimney is a death sentence for the settlement.

Delune's hands are scarred in a pattern that every Smoke-Keeper bears: the inside of the left wrist, where the torch-flare catches you when you lean too close to light a reluctant coal. He has four such scars, each from a different year, each a lesson. The first was when he was twelve, apprenticed to the previous keeper. The second was during the great drought of his twenty-second year, when the fuel ran so low he had to burn green wood and the sparks flew. The third was an accident that he will not discuss. The fourth was last winter, and it healed poorly, and the scar tissue is raised and angry, and he rubs it when he is thinking.

The smoke-smell that permeates Sowren — that sharp, savory undercurrent beneath the overwhelming green — is Delune's signature on the settlement. His hearth produces most of the smoke that drifts through the central tunnels. He smokes the fish. He smokes the fruit-leather. He dries the medicinal herbs. And in the evenings, when the temperature drops and the mist thickens and the damp settles into everything, he keeps a small fire going on his platform for anyone who needs to warm their hands, or their joints, or their spirits. People come to him for this. They sit around his hearth-platform in the gathering dark, wrapped in damp blankets, and they let the smoke and warmth penetrate them, and they talk, and the fire crackles, and Delune watches it with the focus of a man guarding the most dangerous and necessary thing in his world.

## III. The Weavers

Not all of Sowren is grown. Some of it must be woven.

The Weavers are a small group — eight or nine at any given time — who work with plant fiber to create the flexible connections that hold the settlement together. Hammocks. Fishing nets. The tension-lines that stabilize bridges between platform-trees. The wrapping that protects stored seeds from moisture. The satchels and pouches that everyone carries. The slings that mothers strap their infants to their backs with. The clothes, such as they are — lightweight, open-woven, designed to dry quickly in an environment where nothing dries quickly enough.

The Weavers work on the second level of the eastern platform-tree, in a space that has been deliberately grown open — a wide, airy shelf of dead wood surrounded by living branches, with a floor of tightly woven mat that drains efficiently when it rains, which is always. Their workspace smells of drying fiber, a sweet, vegetal scent that is distinct from the living green smell of the tunnels. It smells, the Weavers say, like *finish* — like something that has been taken from the wild and made into a thing with a purpose.

The eldest Weaver is a woman named Sula, who is sixty-one and nearly blind. She does not need to see. Her fingers read the fibers the way Orru's read the vines — feeling for inconsistencies, for weakness, for the telltale softness of a strand that will rot within a season. She teaches the young Weavers by touch, guiding their hands along a half-finished weave and making them identify, without looking, where the pattern breaks. *There. Feel it? That strand is wrong. Pull it out. Start again.*

Sula weaves with a speed that is almost invisible. Her hands move in patterns so practiced that they seem to operate independently of thought, the fingers flying, the fiber blurring. She can produce a hammock strong enough to hold a grown man in four hours. She has woven, she estimates, over three thousand hammocks in her lifetime. Each one lasts five to eight years before the fiber degrades. She does not mourn the ones that rot. Rot is just the fiber returning to the conversation.

The Weavers are the only people in Sowren who regularly use metal tools. Each of them carries a small copper awl, kept wrapped in oiled cloth when not in use, used to punch holes in tough fiber for threading. These awls are family possessions, passed down, re-sharpened on river stones until they are little more than stubs. Sula's awl was made by her grandmother, and the handle is so worn it has taken the shape of her hand exactly, and when she holds it, it feels like holding her own finger.

## IV. The Watchers

Every settlement needs someone who looks outward. In Sowren, this role falls to the Watchers — a loosely organized group who maintain the paths that connect the basin to the outside world and who serve as the first point of contact for anyone approaching the settlement.

The chief Watcher is a man called Pend. He is younger than Tessick but older than he looks, his face weathered to a permanent squint by years of scanning the approaches, his body lean and coiled with the readiness of someone who spends a great deal of time climbing root-tangled slopes in wet conditions. He carries a staff of hardwood — one of the few pieces of dead wood in Sowren that is kept polished and maintained as a tool rather than allowed to decompose — and a copper knife that he has never used on a person, though he has used it on snakes.

Pend knows every approach to the basin. There are four: the northern root-ladder, which is the most reliable but steepest; the eastern scree, which is passable in dry weather but treacherous when wet, which is most of the time; the western tunnel, a natural cave-system that has been widened and maintained over generations, used for carrying heavy loads; and the southern slope, which is technically possible but so overgrown that it functions more as a theoretical route than a practical one. Pend uses all four, rotating his patrols, checking for landslides, clearing fallen debris, testing the integrity of root-bridges.

He also watches for people. Outsiders. Traders, mostly, who make the grueling climb with goods to exchange — metal tools, salt, dried grain, cloth woven from non-local fibers. The traders never stay long. The settlement unnerves them. The sounds. The movement. The pervasive damp that seems to get into their clothes and their lungs within hours. Pend is kind to them but firm: this far, no further. The tunnels are not safe for people who don't know them. One wrong turn and you could find yourself in a passage that dead-ends in a wall of fungal growth, or worse, in a space where the floor is rotten and your weight will take you through it into the muck below.

Pend has a daughter named Wynn, who is twenty and has inherited his watchfulness but not his caution. She goes further than he does on her patrols, mapping the upper slopes, learning the terrain beyond the approaches. She has, on three occasions, followed trade routes down the mountain to the valley settlements below, returning with information, goods, and the strange hollow feeling of walking on flat, dry stone for days on end. She describes it to the other young people of Sowren as a kind of sensory deprivation — *everything is so quiet. So still. The ground doesn't move. The walls don't breathe. I felt like I was walking on a corpse.*

She is not entirely joking.

## V. The Child Who Counts

There is a child in Sowren — one of perhaps thirty children under the age of ten — who has developed a private habit that the adults find both charming and slightly troubling. Her name is Lir, and she is seven, and she counts things.

She counts the moths on the railing of her family's platform in the morning — thirty-seven yesterday, forty-two today, twenty-eight when it was colder. She counts the drips that fall from a particular leaf outside her hammock during the time it takes her to fall asleep, which averages one hundred and forty. She counts the ant-columns that pass a certain junction in the tunnel nearest her home — three between dawn and midmorning, five on warm days, sometimes seven during hatches.

The adults smile at this because children count things. It is what children do. They count their fingers, their toes, the fruits in a cluster, the beads on a string. But Lir's counting has a quality that is not quite play. She keeps no record — there is no way to keep a record; anything she wrote would rot — but she seems to hold the numbers in her head, and she notices when they change. She has told Tessick, who listens to children because children see things adults have learned to overlook, that the moths have been fewer this month than last month, and that the ant-columns have shifted their routes, and that the dripping leaf has moved — not fallen, not died, *moved* — three centimeters to the left since the beginning of the wet season.

Tessick finds this interesting. She asks Lir to keep counting.

Lir is not the first child in Sowren to develop this habit. There is an old word for it in the settlement's spoken tradition — *rekkal*, which means something like "the one who watches the pattern" — and every few generations, a child emerges who counts compulsively, who notices the rate of change in the living settlement and reports it with a precision that exceeds what any adult, busy with the demands of growing and building and surviving, has time to observe.

The last *rekkal* was a man named Gend, now dead twenty years. He predicted, in his later life, the collapse of the western platform-tree's third branch — a collapse that occurred, exactly as he described it, four years after his death. The adults had not believed him at the time. They had been polite about it. The branch was healthy, they said, the bark was sound, the roots were deep. Gend had shaken his head. *The roots are deep,* he said, *but they are moving. I can feel it in the tunnels below. The vibrations are different.*

The vibrations were different. Nobody had been listening.

Lir does not know about Gend. She does not know she is a *rekkal.* She knows only that the numbers change, and that the changes mean something, and that the something is important, even if she cannot yet say what it is. She counts, and the settlement grows around her, and the mist falls, and the water drips, and the moths land on the railing, and she watches them with dark, still, counting eyes.

## VI. What the Place Does

There is a question that outsiders sometimes ask, when they have been in Sowren long enough for the initial overwhelm to subside and the mind to begin its work of comprehension: *How long can this last?*

The settlement is built of living material in a state of constant growth and decay. The basin fills with water that has nowhere to go. The fauna is excessive and uncontrollable. The people have no metal, no written language, no fixed architecture, no way to stop the slow rotation of the seasons and the relentless accumulation of biomass. Everything here is temporary. Everything here is always being consumed by something else. The tunnels grow and collapse and grow again. The platform-trees shift their branches over decades. The peat-muck deepens by a fraction of a centimeter each year, raising the basin floor imperceptibly, slowly, steadily, slowly.

A geologist — if one could be brought to the basin, which is unlikely — would see the deep time of the place. The basin was not always this deep. A thousand years ago it was shallower. Two thousand years ago it was a bare shelf of stone with a few pioneer species clinging to its cracks. The roots did the rest. The roots and the water and the patient, inexorable labor of ten thousand species pulling mineral from stone and turning it into peat, into soil, into the deep soft foundation on which everything now grows. The basin is filling in. In another few thousand years, it will be a hill of organic matter, high and dry, and the water will find somewhere else to weep, and the species will move on, and the place where Sowren once stood will be indistinguishable from any other high meadow.

The people do not think about this. They do not have the framework for it. They know that the muck is deeper than it was in their grandparents' time. They know that passages which were once at ground level are now slightly below it. They know that the oldest platform-trees have roots that reach further and further into the basin floor, and that new platform-trees — younger, thinner, still growing — have taken root in places where, within living memory, there was bare stone. This is not a crisis. This is *the way things are.* Things grow. Things change. You grow with them, or you are buried, and either way, something new rises from what you leave behind.

The settlement will endure, in one form or another, for as long as the water falls and the roots grip and the people keep their hands soft enough to feel what the plants are doing. And when those conditions end — when the basin fills, when the climate shifts, when the last platform-tree's roots finally lose their purchase on the disappearing stone — the people will have moved on, or become something else, or simply become part of the peat-muck that holds the memory of their passage in layers of compressed leaf and bark and bone.

This is not tragedy. This is not triumph. This is the Weft — the interleaving of life and place, the constant weaving and unweaving of the fabric that holds everything together for as long as it holds, and no longer, and that is enough, and more than enough, because what else is there?

## VII. A Life

Tessick stands on the third platform of the central tree in the late afternoon, when the mist has thickened again and the light has gone grey-green and soft. She is holding a cutting — a fresh length of vine, perhaps a meter long, severed from its parent plant an hour ago — and she is studying it. The cut end weeps sap. The vine is still alive, still responding, still processing the severance as a stimulus rather than a death.

She will carry this cutting to the southern tunnel, where a section of wall has begun to thin — the bark-shell drying, the inner tissue losing vigor — and she will make a new incision in the existing structure and insert the cutting's base into the wound, binding it with fiber-cord, packing the joint with the fermented clay paste that encourages root initiation. If she has read the species correctly, and if the humidity stays within its usual range, and if the boring beetles do not find the new growth before it establishes, the cutting will take. It will root into the tunnel wall. It will send out new branches that she or Orru will train into the existing framework. The thinning section will be reinforced. The tunnel will hold for another decade, maybe two, maybe five, until the next grower reads the next sign and makes the next cut and keeps the conversation going.

She will do this tomorrow. Tonight she is tired, and the damp is in her knees, and she wants to sit near Delune's hearth and warm her hands and watch the fire do its dangerous, beautiful work. The smoke will rise through the hollow trunk, and it will pass the second level where Sula sits weaving in the last of the light, and it will pass the first level where Orru is practicing his tapping on a section of new growth, and it will reach the canopy and dissipate into the mist that is Sowren, that is always Sowren, the grey cool breathing mist that holds the settlement the way a hand holds water — loosely, temporarily, but with a grip that, for now, is everything.

Tessick tucks the cutting under her arm. She begins the walk to the hearth, bare feet finding the familiar grooves, the bark warm and damp beneath her sole, the tunnel alive around her — dripping, humming, creaking with growth, murmuring with the calls of small bright creatures hidden in the walls, smelling of chlorophyll and mineral and the deep sweet undertone of things growing and things dying and things growing from the dying, the endless green conversation that is Sowren, that is the Weft, that is the only world she has ever known, and that she would not trade for all the dry, still, silent stone in the world below.
