# What Lives in the Churning Basin

## The Filaments

They are not truly green. That is a word applied from a distance, from the rim, in the ordinary light. Up close — if you pressed your face to the margin where the water slows just enough to let you see — the filaments are the color of nothing in particular. A pale, watered-down suggestion of pigment. As if color itself had to fight its way in and only managed a partial victory.

They grow in clusters of three or five, never more. Each cluster is anchored at its base to a tiny pit in the stone — not a root, not a holdfast in any botanical sense, but a point of contact so minimal that it seems the filament simply decided, once, to be *here* and has held to that decision through the sheer refusal to let go. The base is a knot no wider than a grain of sand. The filament rises from it, sways, trails downstream in the back-current along the wall, and ends in a tip so fine it is nearly invisible. The whole organism, end to end, might be the length of your smallest finger.

They reproduce — if reproduction is the word — by fragmenting. A surge shifts. The water thickens for a moment. A filament shears off at its base, tumbles once into the margin current, and is carried somewhere else along the wall, where it may or may not snag on another pit, another fracture, another point of contact. Most are lost. Most are swept into the margins and then, inevitably, into the upwelling's outer spiral, where they are pulled toward the center column and shredded. The basin consumes them constantly. The filaments grow back constantly. This is not a cycle. It is a war of attrition that the filaments are slowly, inevitably losing, except that they do not think in terms of winning or losing. They grow. The basin tears. They grow again.

Each individual filament lives for perhaps three days. Perhaps a week, if it has found a sheltered spot behind a columnar fracture, a place where the water eddies gently enough to let it exist without constant harassment. In its short life it photosynthesizes — or does something that resembles photosynthesis — taking what it can from the ordinary daylight that penetrates the clear water, supplementing this with direct mineral absorption from the water itself. It is not efficient. It is not elegant. It is *enough*. Just barely, only just, enough to maintain the strand, to keep it from dissolving back into the water it is made of.

There are, on any given day, perhaps two hundred filaments in the basin. Perhaps fewer. The number is never stable. You could count them in the morning and have a different count by afternoon. They are commas in a sentence the basin is writing and erasing simultaneously.

## The Crustaceans

There are seven right now. There were nine last week. Two were swept away during a shift in the upwelling — a moment when the column of water surged higher than usual, reached a little further toward the rim, and took with it everything within reach, including two crustaceans who had been resting in a crevice they had mistaken, as they mistake everything, for permanence.

They are the size of a thumbnail. Maybe slightly larger — their size fluctuates, not because they grow but because they retain or lose water within their translucent bodies depending on the mineral content of their immediate surroundings. In moments of high mineral concentration they swell, slightly, becoming almost round. In moments of dilution they contract, becoming flatter, thinner, more invisible. Their shape is not fixed. Their shape is a negotiation between their body and the water that surrounds it.

They are blind. Not vestigially blind — not the degenerate remnant of eyes that once functioned — but blind in the way that eyes would never have been useful here. Their ancestors, wherever and whenever they came from, may have seen. These do not. The front of the cephalothorax is smooth, unmarked, featureless. There is no indication that a sense was lost. It simply was never needed.

What they have instead is sensitivity. Their legs — four pairs, thin as threads, each one jointed at impossible angles — are covered in microscopic setae, hair-like structures that detect vibrations in the stone. They know the basin through its tremors. They know when the upwelling shifts. They know when the water level drops or rises by fractions of an inch. They know when another crustacean moves within a body-length of them. They know these things the way you know your own heartbeat — not as information but as the background texture of being alive.

They feed on the filaments. This is the entirety of their economy. They find a filament cluster — by vibration, by chemical trace, by whatever sense organ they use to navigate a world without light — and they eat it. Not all of it. They take the tip, the newest growth, the most mineral-rich portion, and they leave the base. This is not kindness. It is efficiency. The base will regrow. The tip will not. The crustacean consumes the tip, processes the minerals, and waits.

They wait a great deal. They press themselves into fractures in the rock — the hexagonal joints where columnar basalt has cracked along its natural planes, creating narrow crevices just wide enough for a thumbnail-sized body. They wedge in. They become, for hours or days, indistinguishable from the stone. Their translucency serves them here: you can see the rock through them, the fracture continuing uninterrupted, as if they are not there at all. They are almost not there. Their bodies are mostly water and mineral, barely differentiated from the water and mineral around them. The line between crustacean and stone, in these moments of stillness, is a technicality.

They reproduce by releasing larvae into the water. This is, by any measure, an act of almost total futility. The larvae are smaller than the adults — pinpricks of translucent life, carried into the current, most of them immediately pulled into the upwelling and destroyed. A few, by chance, land in the margins. A few of those find a fracture. A few of those survive long enough to grow. The survival rate is perhaps one in fifty. Perhaps worse. The basin does not track its own casualties, and neither do the crustaceans, who have no awareness of their own precariousness, who act on instinct that is indistinguishable from faith.

They are the only animals in the basin. They do not know this. They have no concept of solitude, of being the last, of being few. They exist in a world of seven or nine or twelve, and that world is complete. The basin is everything to them, and it is enough, and they will live here until the basin becomes a gorge, at which point the water will move faster and the margins will narrow and they will be swept away or starved or simply outpaced by the geology that is already, even now, carving their home into something they cannot inhabit.

But that is centuries away. Right now there are seven. Right now the fractures hold. Right now there are filaments to eat and stone to cling to and the roar to endure, which they endure by not hearing it — by having no organ for sound, by processing vibration only in the substrate, in the rock, where the roar becomes a deep subsonic pulse that is less a noise than a condition. The basin does not roar for them. The basin *hums*. It hums through the stone and into their legs and through their bodies and out the other side, and this is the world: a hum, a current, a mineral taste, a fracture to hide in, a filament to eat. That is the whole of it. That is enough.

## The Ghost of Hands

The artifacts are the third form of life in the basin — not alive, but not fully dead, either. They are the lingering presence of something that was here and is gone, the way a dent in a chair is the presence of a body that once sat there. They continue to exist. They continue to change. They are, in their slow mineral way, still being *made*.

The flat stone with the two depressions sits on its ledge and does nothing. It is its own monument. The depressions — worn smooth, ergonomically shaped, the width of a thumb and a forefinger — are the memory of a grip that has been gone for longer than any living thing in the basin can remember. The stone does not remember it either. But the shape remembers. The smoothness of those hollows is a record of repetition — thousands of grips, tens of thousands, enough to wear the stone down millimeter by millimeter until it held the shape of the hand that held it. The hand is gone. The shape persists. This is what artifacts do: they outlast the action that created them, and in outlasting, they become a different kind of thing. Not a tool anymore. Not a hand. A *trace*.

The bone sits half-submerged, half-dry, and the submerged half is slowly becoming stone. The mineral crust — calcium, silica, whatever the water deposits — builds up in thin layers, a fraction of a millimeter at a time. In a year it will be thicker. In a decade it will be substantial. In a century the bone will be gone entirely, replaced by a mineral cast that retains the shape of what it consumed. The basin is making a fossil. It does not know it is doing this. It does not know anything. But the chemistry is inexorable: the bone dissolves, the minerals precipitate, and what was once part of a living animal — a leg bone, hollow and light, shaped by evolution for running or jumping or supporting weight on land — becomes part of the basin, part of the stone, part of the geology. The basin is absorbing the dead thing. It absorbs everything. It will absorb the bowl and the flat stone and all the other traces, given time, and they will become indistinguishable from the rock they rest on.

The bowl is the most human thing. Not because of its shape — it is rough, coarse, unremarkable — but because of what it implies. A bowl is a container. A container implies intention. Someone wanted to hold something — water, perhaps, from the basin itself, though the water is too mineral-rich to drink in any normal way; or perhaps something else, something they brought with them, something that needed to be held while they did whatever they did at the rim. The bowl was made quickly. The clay was not well-chosen — it is gritty, full of inclusions that suggest it was gathered from a nearby streambed, not refined, not prepared with care. The firing was uneven; one side is darker than the other, suggesting a fire that was not tended with precision. This was a bowl made by someone who needed a bowl *now* and made one with what was at hand.

It sits inverted on its flat stone, as if placed there deliberately — which it was, by someone, once. The inversion protects the interior from rain, from the basin's occasional spray, from the slow mineral accumulation that would fill it and eventually seal it. Whoever placed it here wanted it to remain a bowl. Wanted the interior to stay open, clean, usable. For what? For a return that never happened, or happened and found the bowl and used it and set it down again in the same place, in the same way, because that is how habits work — they create grooves in behavior as real as the depressions in the flat stone, and they persist, and they outlast, and they become the shape of something that is no longer there.

The basin does not grieve the absence. The basin does not notice it. But the basin holds these objects the way it holds everything — with water and stone and time, with the patient chemistry of dissolution and deposition, with the slow grinding of mineral against mineral that is, in the end, the only form of memory the earth possesses.

## What It Means to Live Here

To live in the Churning Basin is to live in the margins. That is the first and last truth of the place. Nothing lives at the center. The center is the upwelling, the column of water, the violence — and violence does not sustain life, it only sustains itself. Life exists at the edges, in the thin spaces where the force abates just enough to permit existence. The filaments live there. The crustaceans live there. The artifacts rest there. Everything that is not the basin itself exists only where the basin allows it, in the tolerance zones, in the gaps between the water's fury and the stone's resistance.

This is not a metaphor. It is the literal condition of the place. And yet it functions as one, because it is the condition of most living things, most of the time — to exist in the margins of larger forces, to find the narrow spaces where survival is possible, to cling to the walls of something violent and indifferent and to make a life there, however briefly, however precariously.

The crustaceans do not know they are precarious. The filaments do not know they are temporary. The artifacts do not know they are being consumed. And the basin does not know it is a basin — does not know it is carving itself deeper, does not know it will one day be a gorge, does not know that everything in it will be washed away. It only knows — insofar as water and stone can know anything — that there is pressure below and resistance above and that the only possible response is to keep moving, keep surging, keep roaring, keep doing the one thing it has always done, which is to exist with the full force of its existence, without pause, without apology, without end.

Until there is an end. But not yet. Not for a long time yet.

Right now there are seven crustaceans in the fractures. There are perhaps two hundred filaments on the walls. There are six artifacts on the rim. There is one basin, small and loud and raw, carved into the neck of a mountain by water that has not yet decided what it wants to be.

And the sound never stops. It never stops. It is the sound of the basin being made, in real time, by the thing that is making it. It is the sound of geology experienced at the scale of a single heartbeat. It is the sound of the earth herniating, as the first description said — the body of the mountain pressing outward, the water forcing itself up, the stone yielding in fractions of inches per century. It is the sound of patience that is not patience, of force that is not force, of violence that is not violence but merely the expression of a condition that has no other way to manifest.

The crustaceans do not hear it. The filaments do not hear it. The basin hears it, because the basin *is* it, because the sound and the water and the stone are all the same process expressed in different media. And if you stood on the rim — which you could, because the rim is solid and the basin is small enough to sit inside with your arms outstretched — you would hear it too. You would feel it in your skeleton. You would feel it in the bones of your chest, in the fluid of your eyes, in the soft tissue of your lungs. You would feel the basin's roar the way the crustaceans feel it — not as sound but as a condition, as the fundamental fact of this place, as the thing that makes this place itself and nothing else.

You would sit on the rim. You would look down at the clear water, the pale stone, the pale filaments trailing in the margins, the churning column at the center. You would see the artifacts on their ledges. You would see the tilted surface, the asymmetry, the wrongness. And you would understand, without being able to say how, that this place is both young and ancient — young in its rock, ancient in its process, young in its form, ancient in its function. That the water has always come up, here, or would have, given the chance. That the stone has always yielded, here, or would have, given the pressure. That this basin is not an event but a condition, not a happening but a state of being, and that it will continue long after the things in it are gone.

And then you would leave. You would climb back down the mountain, and the sound would fade, and the basin would return to its solitude — to its seven crustaceans and its two hundred filaments and its six artifacts and its one column of furious water — and it would go on, as it always goes on, carving itself deeper, roaring itself louder, becoming the thing it is becoming, with no witness and no purpose and no end that is not simply the exhaustion of the pressure that drives it.

Which may never come. Which may never come.
