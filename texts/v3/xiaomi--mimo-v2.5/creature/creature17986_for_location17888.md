# The People of the Throat

They are not remarkable to look at. That is the first thing an outsider might notice, if an outsider ever came, and the second thing the outsider would fail to understand is why that matters. But here, where the light is the color of resin dissolved in shadow, where faces are read by the grooves around a mouth or the weight of a brow rather than by hue or detail, "remarkable to look at" is not a currency anyone trades in. They are brown. Not a particular brown — not the brown of a specific wood or a specific earth — but a brown that has, over generations, come to match the stone so closely that a person standing still against a canyon wall with their arms at their sides can, in certain lights, vanish. This is not camouflage they have cultivated. It is convergence. The same mineral dust that colors the rock has worked its way into their skin over lifetimes of exposure, into the calluses of their hands and the soft folds of their elbows, into the lungs that breathe the amber air. They smell of the canyon. They taste of it. When one of them is hurt and bleeds, the wound smells faintly of iron and feldspar, and this is not a metaphor.

Their eyes are large. Not grotesquely so, not in any way that would unsettle, but noticeably — the irises dark and wide-pupiled, the whites slightly amber rather than white, adapted to drink in every available photon of the canyon's perpetual twilight. Children are born with the pale, unfocused eyes common to all human infants, and these darken and widen through the first years of life as the light does its patient work. An elder's eyes, by contrast, can appear almost entirely black, the pupil expanded so far into the iris that only a thin ring of deep umber remains. In the firelight of the evening chambers, these eyes catch and hold the glow. They look like small, warm coals set into a face. The children find this comforting. Outsiders might not.

Their hands are what they are known by — known to themselves, at least, since there is no one else to know them by anything. The hands are broad, the fingers short and strong, the palms thick with calluses that have calluses of their own. Every person in the canyon can shape stone. This is not a skill; it is as fundamental as breathing. A child of five holds a chisel with the same unconscious competence that a lowland child might hold a spoon. The stone they work is the canyon's own basaltite, dark and dense, and they know its grain the way a sailor knows the grain of a wind. They can feel, with a fingertip, where a seam of softer mineral runs through a block, where a fracture will propagate if struck at the right angle. This knowledge is not taught in any formal sense. It is absorbed — through watching, through touching the walls of the chambers where grooved lines map the history of every strike and every mistake, through the slow and wordless education of a lifetime spent in intimate contact with rock.

They speak in low voices, most of the time. The canyon teaches this. Sound carries here in ways that are, at first, bewildering — a whisper that travels around a bend and arrives at an ear thirty meters away with startling clarity, a laugh that ricochets between the walls and becomes something larger and stranger than the person who produced it. The inhabitants have developed a vocal language shaped by these acoustics: a system of soft, clipped tones designed to carry precisely as far as intended and no farther. There are words meant to be heard only by the person beside you, pressed close. There are words meant to carry to the next tier of dwellings, thirty meters up. There are words — few, and never spoken casually — meant to fill the entire length of a branch of the canyon, and when those words are spoken, every person in that branch stops and listens, because something that requires a voice to cross that distance is something that cannot wait.

The loud hours are different. At dawn and dusk, when the bird-analogues erupt from their nesting crevices and the insects reach the crescendo of their cycling drone, speech becomes impossible. The canyon fills with a sound so dense it has texture — you feel it on your skin, in your teeth, in the liquid of your inner ear. The inhabitants do not fight this. They go still. They sit in their chambers or on the ledges and wait, eyes half-closed, hands resting on the stone. Some say this is when the canyon speaks to them. Some say the sound carries information — patterns within patterns, cycles within cycles, the slow pulse of the fog arriving or the faint shifts in the insect chorus that predict a dry spell or a particularly thick haze. This may be superstition. It may be a form of listening so refined it has become indistinguishable from intuition. The distinction does not matter to them.

**How they live.**

They rise — though "rise" implies a morning, and the canyon has no morning, only the gradations of dim — when the sound subsides. The first task is always fire. The resin-cement fires are small and precise, burning dried plant husks and thin strips of the tree-bark that has been cured in the warm updrafts near the rim. A fire is not lit from nothing; it is tended from a coal that is carried from the previous fire, kept alive in a sealed stone vessel lined with insulating moss-analogue. To let a fire die is not a tragedy — it can be relit from another — but it is a failure, a small one, and failures of small things tend, in a place this enclosed, to accumulate into a reputation. Everyone knows everyone. The two hundred people of the canyon are not a community in the way that word is used elsewhere. They are an organism. They share twenty or thirty chambers carved into the walls at different heights, connected by passages worn smooth by generations of hands and bare feet. The chambers are not private in any modern sense. There are sleeping alcoves — shallow recesses in the wall, each just large enough for one person, lined with woven mats of the spongy ground-cover that retains warmth — but these are not sealed. Sound passes through the stone. Breath passes through the stone. You know the sleeping sounds of everyone you have ever known: the faint whistle of an elder's half-blocked nostril, the restless shifting of a child dreaming, the deep and even silence of a person who sleeps the way the canyon sleeps — completely, trustingly, with no expectation of interruption.

Meals are communal and infrequent. The flora provides — not abundance, exactly, but sufficiency. There are seed-pods that split open to reveal dense, oily kernels. There are the waxy leaves of certain ground-cover species that, when dried and pounded, produce a paste with a faintly nutty taste and remarkable caloric density. There are the roots of the pale-barked trees, which run shallow and wide through the honeycombed stone and can be excavated with patient digging — a process that takes hours and is considered pleasant work, the kind of work that allows conversation. Nothing here is eaten raw. Everything is processed through the small, hot, resin-scented fires: roasted, dried, ground, mixed. The food is brown and taste is earthy and faintly mineral, and it sustains. It has sustained for longer than anyone can remember.

The herbling — the gathering and preparation of the plant materials that form the basis of everything — is done primarily by the younger adults, those in the years between childhood and the settled middle of life. They move through the undergrowth with a slowness that an outsider might mistake for laziness but is in fact precision. They know which crevices yield which roots in which phase of the growth cycle. They know the difference between the spongy ground-cover that insulates a sleeping mat and the superficially identical species that produces a resin useful for sealing a cracked chamber wall. This knowledge is not written. It cannot be written — the humidity, such as it is, would warp any thin material, and the darkness would make reading impossible anyway. It is carried in hands and tongues and the particular way a person tilts their head when they are remembering something their mother told them. The tactile grooves on the chamber walls encode some of it — a system of ridges and notches that maps the major plant species, their locations, their seasons — but it is a notation system, a shorthand, not a text. The full knowledge lives in the living.

**The children.**

They are born in the sleeping alcoves, attended by the elder women — and some elder men — whose hands have been shaped by decades of this work. The births are quiet. Loud sounds during labor are considered dangerous, not for any medical reason, but because a newborn's ears are, for the first hours, exquisitely sensitive to the canyon's acoustics, and a sharp cry from the mother could be amplified by the stone into something that overwhelms the infant's unadapted hearing. So the mothers are coached in silence. They bite down on a strip of cured bark. They breathe in the rhythm of the fog's arrival — slow, deep, patient. The child emerges into the amber dim, wet and red-faced and already beginning the lifelong process of darkening.

The children are kept close to the canyon floor for their first years, in the chambers nearest the ground-cover mats, where the light is slightly brighter and the sounds of the canopy-dwelling birds are less intense. They are given small tasks early — sorting seed-pods by size, carrying bundles of dried husks to the fire-tenders, pressing their palms flat against the wall grooves and learning to read the room they are in. They play, of course. They are children, and children everywhere are engines of invention. But their play is shaped by the canyon in ways that would be unfamiliar to anyone from outside. They play *sound* — they learn to whisper at precisely the pitch that will carry to a specific ledge, to clap in ways that produce particular echoes, to hum against a wall and feel the resonance in their bones. They play *touch* — they close their eyes and navigate the passages by the grooves on the walls, racing each other to a specific chamber by feel alone. They play *dark* — they sit in the deepest, lightless recesses of the branching side-canyons and wait for their eyes to adapt, and then they describe to each other what they can see in the near-nothing, the faintest gradients of shadow, the ghostly outline of a root or a fissure. These are not games. They are training. The canyon is not cruel, but it is indifferent, and an indifference that can be survived is, in its way, more demanding than a cruelty that can be fought.

**The elders.**

They are old in a way that has little to do with wrinkles or gray hair, though they have both. They are old in the way that the canyon is old — slowly, deeply, settled into a form that has found its final expression. The elders are the ones who sit for long hours in the gathering chamber, a large, hollowed space near the center of the settlement where the walls are thick enough to muffle the outside sound to a manageable hum. They do not govern. There is no governance in any sense an outsider would recognize. There is no leader, no council, no formal hierarchy. There is *knowledge*, and the elders have more of it, and this is its own authority — not imposed, not wielded, simply *present*, the way a large boulder is present in a streambed and the water flows around it without needing to be told.

The elders remember. This is their primary function. They remember the growth patterns of specific trees across decades — which ones will hollow and become nesting sites, which ones will drop branches and open gaps in the canopy that will, briefly, allow more light to reach the floor and change the ground-cover composition below. They remember the fog patterns across years — the thick seasons and the thin ones, the years when the haze was so dense it reached the canyon floor and the walls wept with condensation, and the dry years when the plants stressed and the animals thinned and the people tightened their consumption and waited. They remember the dead. In a place without written records, the dead exist only as long as someone remembers them, and the elders carry the names and the faces and the specific habits of the dead the way the canyon carries its fossils — embedded, preserved, integral to the structure.

They mark the dead on the walls. Not with portraits, not with words, but with a single groove — a deep, clean line carved into the stone with a hardened chisel, running vertically, its length proportional to the years the person lived. The oldest elders have grooves that run the full height of their sleeping alcove, floor to ceiling, a dark line in dark stone that you feel with your fingers in the amber half-light. To run your hand along a dead person's line is to know them. The depth of the groove, the slight irregularities where the carver's hand shook or the stone resisted, the way the line curves at the top where it meets a natural fissure and follows it briefly before resuming its descent — these are details that carry meaning. An elder who has lived long enough can place a hand on a groove and say who it was carved for and how they died and what their hands smelled like when they worked resin in the evenings.

**The animals.**

The bird-analogues are everywhere. They are small — sparrow-sized, at largest — and dense-bodied, with plumage the color of wet stone: slate-gray, brownish, with occasional flashes of dull amber on the wing that catches the light when they fly from ledge to ledge. Their eyes are enormous, even by the standards of the canyon's inhabitants, black orbs that fill half their heads and give them an expression of permanent, startled alertness. They nest in every available crevice, in the honeycombed pitting of the canyon walls, in the hollows left by fallen chunks of stone, in the abandoned upper chambers of the settlement that have been left empty because they are too small for human use. They are not pets. They are not kept. But there is a relationship. The bird-analogue's droppings, collected from the ledges below the nesting sites, are mixed into the resin-cement to improve its binding properties. The birds' nesting material — shed plant fibers, bits of cured bark, the downy seed-heads of certain ground-cover species — is gathered and used for insulation and padding. In return, the people do not disturb the nesting sites. A chamber below an active nest is considered to have a guardian — a minor blessing, a soft form of luck that nobody claims to believe in and everybody respects.

The insects are the backbone. The large, slow-moving ones — beetle-analogues with heavy carapaces and mandibles strong enough to剪 through cured bark — are the primary decomposers, breaking down dead plant matter and returning it to the mineral cycle. They are not eaten, though they are not poisonous. They are simply not food. Smaller insects — a vast, dizzying array of them, each filling a precise niche in the canyon's ecology — pollinate the plants, aerate the ground-cover mats, and provide the base of the food chain for the bird-analogues. The constant drone of their stridulation is the canyon's baseline, the hum beneath all other sounds, the frequency against which everything else is measured. An elder listening to the insects can tell you, with uncanny accuracy, how thick the fog will be that evening. They cannot explain how they know. The knowledge is in the rhythm, somewhere below language.

The scurrying mammals are shy and rare — more often felt than seen, a rustle in the ground-cover, a pair of enormous ears catching the light before the body that owns them vanishes into a crevice. They are the size of a human hand, dense-furred, with eyes adapted to a darkness even deeper than the canyon's. They are harmless. They eat fungus. They are, in some accounts, the ancestors of the resin-cement, because it was a person — generations ago, beyond memory, possibly beyond truth — who noticed that the mammals built their nests from resin-cured fibers and realized that the same process could be scaled. This may be the canyon's founding myth. It may be history. The distinction, again, does not matter.

The large herbivore — the blunt-snouted, low-slung creature the size of a large dog — is singular. There are never more than three or four in the canyon at a time. They browse the tree-scale, pulling the tiny translucent needles from the branches with a delicate, precise motion that seems incongruous with their bulk. They move through the undergrowth with a sound like slow breathing, parting the fern-analogues and ground-cover with their flanks, leaving a trail of pressed-down vegetation that reasserts itself within hours. The people do not hunt them. They do not name them, exactly, though there are words for the sounds they make — a low, resonant hum that is felt in the chest more than heard — and those words are sometimes used in lullabies. The herbivore is not sacred. It is simply *there*, as the stone is there, as the fog is there, as the sound is there — a presence that defines the space by its own patient existence. When one dies — of age, of disease, there being no predators to hasten the process — its body is left where it falls, and the decomposition returns a remarkable quantity of nutrients to a small area, and for a season afterward, the plants around that spot grow thicker and taller and more vigorously, and the people note this and leave the spot alone, not out of reverence for the dead animal but out of a practical understanding that this is how the stone feeds itself and there is no reason to interfere.

**What they are.**

They are people who have, over a span of time so long it has become geological, become the canyon. Not metaphorically. Not in the way a poet might say a fish is the river. In a literal, biochemical sense: the dust in their lungs is the dust of the walls; the mineral in their bones is the mineral of the fog; the rhythm of their breathing matches the rhythm of the haze's arrival and departure; the pitch of their voices has been selected, unconsciously, by generations of acoustic pressure into a frequency range that carries best through the narrow stone corridors. They did not adapt to the canyon. They *are* the canyon, wearing skin, moving through itself, carrying its memory in the grooves on the walls and its sound in the shape of its own vocal cords.

They do not know this. Or rather, they know it the way a body knows its own heartbeat — as a fact so fundamental it requires no acknowledgment. They do not have a concept of "elsewhere" in any meaningful sense. The rim is not a border; it is a ceiling. The world beyond the haze is not another world; it is the thing that produces the haze. The idea that a human being might live under an open sky, with rain falling on their skin and wind that carries the smell of distant water, is not a fantasy they entertain. It is not a deprivation they mourn. It is a thought that simply does not form, the way a thought about breathing underwater does not form in the mind of a person who has never seen the sea.

They are content. This is not the contentment of ignorance or resignation. It is the contentment of a system in equilibrium — of a people who have been fitted, over deep time, to a place that has been fitted, over deep time, to them. The work is enough. The sound is enough. The fog is enough. The slow, warm stone beneath their bare feet is enough. The glow of the resin fire on the face of someone they have known since birth, in a chamber whose grooves they have read a thousand times, in a canyon that will be here when they are not — this is enough.

Everything else is noise.
