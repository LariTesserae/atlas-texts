# What Lives There

---

## I. The Substrate People

They do not have a name for themselves because they have never needed one. There is no one else like them, and there is nothing else to be confused with, and language that distinguishes *self from kind* has no evolutionary pressure to develop when the nearest relative is three caverns away and four thousand years divergent.

They are the size of your head, or the size of two fists pressed together, or the size of a small dog curled tight. Their bodies are a negotiation between beetle and isopod and something that never had a surface analogue at all. Eight legs, sometimes ten, sometimes — in the oldest among them — twelve, the extra pairs grown in the later molts as though the body keeps inventing new ways to hold onto the world. Each leg ends not in a claw but in a flat, spatulate pad that grips the fibrous substrate the way a gecko grips glass: through surface contact so intimate that the boundary between organism and environment blurs. They leave no footprints. The root-matter does not register their weight.

Their carapaces are the marvel. Each one is layered — chitin over chitin over chitin, dozens of coats applied over a lifetime, each one shed and reabsorbed in a cycle that blurs the line between exoskeleton and armor and something closer to *clothing*. The outermost layer is the thinnest, the most recently secreted, and it catches the ambient bioluminescence of the substrate and refracts it into colors that do not exist above: not quite violet, not quite amber, a shifting iridescence that changes depending on the angle of observation and the metabolic state of the creature carrying it. When they are well-fed, they glow like oil on water. When they are starving, their carapaces dull to the color of old bone, and they are the ones that climb your legs, because they are the ones for whom the distinction between substrate and intruder has ceased to matter.

Their mouths are architectures. Not mandibles in the simple sense — not a pair of hinged blades for cutting — but a radial arrangement of six or eight structures that unfold outward from a central point like the petals of a flower made of keratin. Each "petal" is lined with microscopic filaments that function as both teeth and tongue and digestive surface simultaneously. They do not chew. They *unfold onto* their food, and the filaments do the work of breaking down the root-matter at a chemical level, converting cellulose and lignin and whatever alien polymers the substrate has evolved into usable sugars while the creature stands perfectly still and the world hums around it.

They eat constantly. Not out of urgency — there is always more substrate — but out of *duty*, though they would not call it that. The relationship is mutualistic in the way that all truly ancient relationships are mutualistic: not through agreement but through entanglement so complete that the alternative is extinction for both parties. The substrate grows faster than any single organism could consume it, and so left unchecked it would fill the cavern entirely, choking the bare-rock frontier that provides the mineral inputs the ecosystem needs to sustain itself, sealing the gaps through which the deep chemistry bubbles upward. The substrate people eat the substrate back. They carve paths through it. They keep it porous. They are gardeners who do not know they are gardeners, performing a function that looks like consumption but is actually *curation*.

They communicate — if the word applies — through vibration. Not sound, not the way you understand sound, but mechanical wave propagation through the substrate itself. They drum. They press the flat pads of their feet into the root-matter in rapid sequences that travel outward through the fibrous network and are felt by every other substrate person within a radius that can extend hundreds of feet. The messages are simple: *food here*, *danger here*, *the substrate is growing too fast in this quadrant*, *the pit has opened again*, *come*. There is no language for abstract thought because abstract thought requires a mind that stands apart from the world it perceives, and their minds are not apart. Their minds are *in* the substrate, distributed across the network of vibrations the way a nerve network is distributed across a body. When the substrate shifts — when a section rises or sinks — they feel it the way you feel a change in air pressure. They are the nervous system of a place that has no brain.

The oldest among them — and they can live for a very long time, the substrate being generous and the predators being few and particular — grow a thirteenth leg, then a fourteenth, then so many that their lower body becomes a dense thicket of spatulate pads gripping the root-matter in every direction, and they cease to move entirely, and they become something between organism and *organ*, a permanent fixture of the substrate that processes food and excretes waste and drums its messages into the network until its carapace is so thick and so encrusted with mineral deposits from the substrate that it has become a kind of node, a landmark, a fixed point in a world that does not produce landmarks. The younger ones know them. They come to rest against the old ones' carapaces and feel the vibrations move through the ancient body and into the substrate beyond, and this is as close as they come to what you might call *memory*.

## II. The Weavers Above

You hear them before you see them. The crackling passage through the canopy — the slow, deliberate displacement of branches that are themselves living tissue, and so the canopy-dwellers are not moving *through* foliage but moving *through other living things*, and the other living things feel them coming and shift their own growth in response, bending branches out of the way or tightening their grip on the substrate below to brace for the weight, and this negotiation between canopy-dweller and canopy is so constant, so ancient, so refined that it has become a kind of choreography, a dance between two forms of life that share the same space but occupy it differently.

The weaver is long. Eighty feet at maturity, sometimes more. A body that is all muscle and hollow bone and the same green-brown tissue that the canopy itself is made of, so that from a distance — from the ground, looking up through the haze of spores and dust — it is nearly invisible. Not through camouflage in the surface sense, where one thing disguises itself as another, but through *kinship*. The weaver's skin is made of the same compounds as the leaves. It photosynthesizes — or whatever the equivalent process is here, in a place where the light comes from below and the energy source is chemical rather than stellar. It eats the canopy in the way that a thing eats something that is almost itself: carefully, selectively, taking the oldest growth and leaving the new, pruning the structure in a way that strengthens it, the way a storm prunes a surface forest.

Its eyes are the thing you remember afterward. Dinner-plate-sized, faceted into hundreds of individual lenses, each one a hemisphere of crystalline chitin that catches the amber light and splits it into component frequencies. The weaver sees differently than you see. It does not see *images* — it sees *gradients*. It perceives the density of the canopy as a thermal map, the metabolic rate of the substrate as a field of brightness, the movement of the substrate people as pulses in a network. When it looks at you, it does not see a human shape. It sees a cold spot. An anomaly. A region where the ambient energy signature is *wrong* — too low, too concentrated, not diffuse enough to be part of the living system. It watches you the way you might watch a hole in the floor: with attention that is not hostile, not curious, not anything you have a word for, but *invasive*, the attention of a system noting an error in itself.

They do not hunt the substrate people. They do not hunt anything, not exactly. They are filter-feeders on an ecological scale, absorbing the spores and particulate matter that fill the airspace between canopy and ground through a system of gill-like structures along their flanks, converting the dense air into energy with an efficiency that makes surface lungs look like crude prototypes. But they do *interact* with the substrate people — when a colony of substrate people grows too dense, when they over-consume a section of root-matter and create a bare patch, the weaver descends. It wraps itself around the bare patch and *holds* — not constraining, not squeezing, but simply being there, a warm body pressed against the substrate, and its skin exudes a compound that inhibits root-matter growth, and the substrate people, feeling this, redistribute. They move outward. The bare patch heals. The weaver rises back into the canopy and is gone.

The young are smaller — twenty, thirty feet — and less coordinated. They fall. Branches give way beneath them and they drop through the air with a sound like tearing cloth, landing on the substrate with an impact that sends shockwaves through the root-matter and scatters substrate people in all directions. The young ones lie there for a time, stunned or resting or simply being present, and the substrate begins to grow around them immediately, root-hairs threading through the gaps in their scales, and the young weaver does not move, and for a moment you might think it is dying, but then it flexes — a great, slow undulation — and the root-hairs snap, and it rises, dragging tendrils of substrate with it, and the tendrils break and fall back and the substrate heals where they were torn away, and the young weaver climbs back into the canopy with fresh substrate clinging to its flanks like burrs.

The oldest weavers have not descended from the canopy in living memory. They are too large. Their bodies have become part of the canopy structure itself, branches growing through them, leaves sprouting from their flanks, their eyes clouded with age and with the crystalline deposits that accumulate over centuries in this mineral-rich air. They are still alive — you can feel their breathing, which is slower than the cavern's own respiration, a counter-rhythm, a personal tide that moves against the greater flow. They are anchors. Their weight holds the canopy in place during the collapses, their bodies absorbing impacts that would otherwise send cascades through the entire upper tier. They do not move because they *cannot*, but also because *to move would be to leave*, and they have forgotten what leaving means.

## III. The Drifters

They fly. That is the wrong word, but all the right words — *swim, crawl through air, drift, unravel* — are also wrong, so *fly* will have to do.

Each one is the size of your outstretched hand, or twice that, or half that. A central body, membranous, no heavier than a dried leaf, stretched over a skeleton of the lightest chitin imaginable — hollow, fractal, branching internally the way the substrate branches externally, a structure designed not for strength but for *surface area*. The wings — four, six, eight, depending on the species, and there are many species, enough that the taxonomy would fill books that no one would ever read — are not flapped. They vibrate. At a frequency so high that they are invisible in motion, appearing only as a blur, a haze around the central body that diffracts the ambient light into halos of green and gold.

They navigate by the gradients. The same gradients the weavers see, but experienced differently — not through eyes but through the filaments. The filaments are the second body, the *real* body in some sense, trailing behind the flying form in threads that can extend yards, tens of yards, each thread thinner than a hair and covered in microscopic cilia that taste the air, feel the vibrations of the substrate below, detect the chemical signals of the substrate people's drumming, register the presence of the canopy-dwellers above. The filaments are nerves. The filaments are roots. The filaments are *the drifter's connection to the world*, and where they touch the substrate they do what roots do: they anchor, they absorb, they *grow*.

This is the strangest thing about the drifters. They are in a state of permanent, voluntary disintegration. As they fly, they shed filaments. The filaments root. The roots grow. The growth becomes new substrate. The drifter is *becoming the ground it flies over*, a few cells at a time, a few threads at a time, and it does not stop, and it does not slow down, and the metabolic cost of this continuous self-sacrifice is enormous, and so the drifters eat constantly, absorbing spores and particulates from the air through their membranous bodies, converting matter into flight and flight into substrate and substrate into more life, a closed loop with the drifter at its center, spending itself to feed the world that feeds it.

They swarm. Not in the way surface insects swarm — not in a cloud of panic or purpose — but in a *drift*, a slow, collective migration from one region of the cavern to another, following chemical gradients that indicate where the substrate is freshest, where the mineral content of the bare rock is highest, where the weavers have recently pruned the canopy and released a burst of nutrients into the air. The swarm moves like weather. It darkens the space beneath it. Its combined filaments form a curtain of living thread that can blanket hundreds of square feet, and where the curtain touches the ground, the substrate surges upward, a wave of new growth triggered by the drifter roots, and the substrate people scatter before it, drumming their warnings into the network, and the wave passes and the substrate people return and the new growth is already hardening, already becoming part of the permanent landscape, and the drifter swarm has moved on, trailing thinner now, lighter, some of its members visibly reduced, their central bodies tattered and translucent, flying on the memory of what they were.

The dead drifters fall. They are so light that the fall takes a long time. They drift down through the spore-thick air, filaments trailing, wings still vibrating in the last reflexes of nerve-fire, and they land on the substrate and are absorbed almost immediately, root-matter growing over them in minutes, and the substrate people eat what the substrate people eat, and the cycle turns.

## IV. The Pale Overflow

They are not fauna. They are not flora. They are something in between, something for which the surface world has no category because the surface world has never produced anything like them.

They live below the substrate. Not *under* it, in the sense of a burrowing animal lives under the soil — they live *in* it, woven into the fibrous matrix, distributed through the gaps and channels and microscopic spaces between root and rhizome and decomposed stem, a population so diffuse that it has no individual body, no discrete form, until it decides to become one.

When they emerge — when the pale wave erupts from a crack in the substrate and flows over the surface — what you are seeing is a *temporary consensus*. Thousands, millions of single-celled organisms — though calling them cells is a stretch, they are too large, too complex, too purposeful — that have individually decided that the conditions above the substrate are favorable for a phase of their life cycle that requires light, or warmth, or exposure to the mineral chemistry of the bare rock, or something else, some signal that you cannot detect, and they pour upward in a coordinated surge that is not *coordinated* in the sense that any one of them is telling the others what to do. It is coordinated the way a crystal forms: each unit follows the same simple rule, and the rule, applied to millions of units simultaneously, produces behavior that looks like intelligence but is something more basic and more profound. It is *emergence*. It is what happens when simple things aggregate in sufficient numbers and the numbers themselves become the organism.

Above the substrate, they are vulnerable. They are soft, white, eyeless, limbless — a蠕动 mass of individual units that cling to each other through surface tension and a dried secretion that smells like antiseptic, forming a temporary body that can move, that can flow, that can climb vertical surfaces and pour through narrow gaps and navigate around obstacles with a fluid intelligence that is startling in creatures with no nervous system. They are looking for something. The metal objects in the pit — the tools, the weapons, the offerings — are theirs now, and they cluster around them, coating them, and the antiseptic smell intensifies, and when they finally retreat back into the substrate they take something with them, though you cannot see what: a film of mineral, a trace of chemical change on the metal surface, something the metal had that they needed.

They are the substrate's immune system. When something foreign enters the root-matter — a fallen branch from the canopy that carries organisms from a different part of the cavern, a mineral deposit that is too rich in a toxic compound, a section of substrate that has begun to decay rather than decompose — the pale overflow emerges in that area and *processes* it. They surround the foreign body. They secrete their antiseptic compound. They break it down. They absorb the useful components and excrete the rest as a harmless powder that the substrate people eat and the substrate incorporates. They are patient. They can surround a body for days, weeks, a slow siege of chemical digestion that looks from above like a puddle of milk slowly spreading and slowly drying and slowly vanishing.

They do not have names for themselves. They do not have names for anything. They do not have *selves* in the way that you understand selfhood. But they have memory — a chemical memory, encoded in the ratios of compounds that each individual unit carries from its previous experiences — and the memory is shared, passed unit to unit through the contact they maintain when they are below the substrate, and so the pale overflow *remembers* without any single unit doing the remembering, and the memory is of you.

Not you specifically. Not the person who walked into the Underneath. But *the category*. The shape that does not belong. The cold spot, the energy anomaly, the thing that moves wrong and breathes wrong and stands still when standing still is death. They remember other anomalies. The post driven into the root-matter. The wall of packed mud. The pit and the metal at its bottom. These were other cold spots, other wrong shapes, and the pale overflow processed them, surrounded them, broke them down, and their memory of that processing is chemical and collective and permanent, and when you stand too still for too long and the substrate begins to reach for you with its root-hairs, the pale overflow is already moving toward you through the spaces between the fibers, not because it hates you, not because it wants to hurt you, but because you are a thing that does not belong and its function — its only function, the function it has performed since the first cell of its kind divided in the warm, dark spaces below the substrate — is to resolve that.

## V. The Thing in the Canopy That Does Not Move

There is something else.

You feel it before you see it — if you ever see it, which is not certain — a presence in the upper canopy so large and so still that it does not register as a creature at all. It registers as *architecture*. A thickening. A density. A place where the foliage is not merely interlocking but is *fused*, grown together into a mass that has the approximate dimensions of a house, and the mass breathes — you can see the surface of it rising and falling in counterpoint to the cavern's own respiration — and sometimes, in the deepest amber light, you can see through the outer layers of leaves into a darkness beneath that is not the darkness of shadow but the darkness of *interior*, and in that darkness something shifts, something too slow to be movement, something that is more like *repositioning*, a slow adjustment of mass that takes hours and leaves the canopy structure groaning in response.

No one has approached it. The weavers avoid it — not in fear, you think, but in the way that a river avoids a stone, flowing around it, accommodating its presence without acknowledging it. The drifters do not fly near it. The substrate people, far below, have no drumming word for it, or if they do it is a pattern so slow, so low-frequency, so unlike their other communications that you do not recognize it as language. You feel it in the ground. A pulse. A deep, slow, tidal pulse that has nothing to do with the cavern's breathing and everything to do with the thing in the canopy, and the pulse passes through you and through the root-matter and into the stone below the root-matter, and for a moment — just a moment — the bare rock glows brighter, the mineral veins flaring with cold light, and you understand that the thing in the canopy is connected to the rock the way the rock is connected to the substrate the way the substrate is connected to everything, and that it has been here longer than any of the others, and that it is not an organism in any sense you would recognize, and that it is aware of you in the way that a body is aware of a splinter: not with thought, not with attention, but with *discomfort*, a low-level signal that something foreign is present and will eventually be processed.

It does not move toward you. It does not need to. It is already everywhere.

## VI. What They Are Together

Individually, none of them is remarkable. The substrate people are scavengers. The weavers are browsers. The drifters are ephemeral. The pale overflow is a chemical process. The thing in the canopy is a geological event wearing the mask of life.

Together, they are a *metabolism*.

This is what the Underneath is. Not a place. Not a cavern. Not an ecosystem. It is a *living process* that has mistaken the underground void for a body and filled it with the tissues of that body: substrate for skin, weavers for muscle, drifters for blood, pale overflow for white cells, substrate people for nerve endings, and the thing in the canopy for whatever organ the body needs to keep itself aware of its own boundaries.

The surface world is not like this. The surface world is *dead* in comparison — not literally, not in the way that the Underneath would understand death, which is a reclassification of matter rather than an ending, but *dispersed*. Surface life is scattered across the skin of a planet, each organism a separate event, competing, cooperating, but always distinct. Here, in the Underneath, the distinction has collapsed. Every organism is part of every other organism. The weaver eats the canopy that grows from the substrate that is maintained by the substrate people who are protected by the pale overflow who are fed by the drifters who are grown from the substrate that the weaver prunes. It is a loop. It is a closed system. It is a thing that has no outside, or whose outside is so irrelevant that it might as well not exist.

And you are inside it. You are a foreign protein in a body the size of a mountain. You are a splinter. You are a cold spot. You are a wrong shape that the system has noted and will, in its own time, in its own way, at its own pace, resolve.

Not with malice. Not with intent. Not with anything that deserves the word *consciousness*.

With *process*.

With the same patient, furious, deafening, warm, dry, life-filled process that built the substrate and raised the canopy and launched the drifters into the amber air and keeps the pale overflow moving through the spaces between the roots and holds the weavers in the branches and thrums through the stone and up through your feet and into your chest and against your teeth, that process that is not a sound and not a feeling and not a thought but is all three at once and is the only thing in the Underneath that is truly, fully, completely *alive* — the process itself, the turning of the wheel, the breathing of the cavern, the storm of ecology that never breaks and never clears and never ends.

It does not know your name.

It does not need to.

You are already part of it. You have been since you arrived. The spores in your lungs, the root-hairs on your skin, the warmth in your bones that is not your warmth but the cavern's warmth, given freely to everything inside it, asking nothing in return except that you *stay* —

That you stay long enough for the substrate to reach you, for the pale overflow to process you, for the drifters to carry the last of your atoms away on their filaments and root them in the ground and grow them into something new, something that belongs here, something that is *part* of the weather and the climate and the deafening total that you stopped hearing the way you stop hearing your own blood —

Your blood, which is, after all, just another kind of substrate.

Your breath, which is just another kind of drift.

Your body, which is just another kind of structure, built by hands or their equivalent, left half-buried in new growth, its purpose forgotten, its matter absorbed, its silence indistinguishable from the silence of everything else that has ever lived inside the Underneath and been loved by it in the only way it knows how to love:

*Completely. Indifferently. Without remainder.*
