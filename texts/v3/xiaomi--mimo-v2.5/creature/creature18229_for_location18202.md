# The People of the Calcite Warrens

Their skin is pale. Not the pallor of illness or deprivation, but a true and settled pallor — the color of something that has never been touched by the sun and has no memory of being touched by it. Generations underground have washed the melanin from them the way the dripping water washes mineral from the walls, slowly and without argument. Their skin carries a faint, chalky undertone, as though the stone has been whispering its composition into their flesh and their flesh has been listening. In the luminous half-light of the Warrens, they look almost translucent themselves — not quite, but enough that visitors notice it, enough that a stranger passing one of them in a narrow corridor might feel a small, irrational conviction that they can see the shadow of a bone beneath the skin of a forearm, or the branching suggestion of veins at a temple.

Their eyes are large. This is not romanticism or metaphor — the irises are physically larger than those of surface-dwellers, the pupils wide-set and permanently dilated to drink in every photon of the stone's cold, sourceless glow. The eye color tends toward pale shades: grey, washed-out hazel, a blue so faint it might be the memory of blue. Children born in the deepest Warrens sometimes seem to have no iris color at all, only a dark pupil ringed by a whisper of silver-grey, and they see beautifully in what outsiders would call near-darkness. They blink slowly and infrequently. They do not squint. They have never needed to squint. The world they were born into does not assault the eye with brightness or blind it with shadow — it simply *shows itself*, evenly, in that democratic bone-white, and their eyes have been shaped by that truth the way a fish's eye is shaped by water.

They are small-framed, most of them. Not stunted — proportionate, well-made, with the dense compactness of people who have never needed to be tall. The Warrens do not reward height. The clefts between neighborhoods are narrow; the passages branch and turn at angles that punish a long stride. What they reward is patience, and dexterity, and a particular relationship with the hands. The people of the Warrens are *hand-people*. Their hands are their primary instruments and their greatest vanity, if vanity is even the right word — they do not preen, but they *attend*. A knapper's hands are known the way a singer's voice is known on the surface: by reputation, by signature. The calluses tell a story. The way a person holds a chisel — the angle, the grip pressure, the rhythm of their strikes — is as distinctive as a face.

The hands are always slightly damp. Everything in the Warrens is slightly damp, and the hands are no exception. The skin there is smooth and faintly ridged, like river-stone, and the fingertips carry a fine mineral grit that never fully washes away because the water itself is chalky and the air itself deposits a thin film of calcite on everything it touches. A stranger shaking hands with a person of the Warrens will feel that grit — a faint, silvery roughness, as though the person's skin is mid-process of becoming stone. It is not unpleasant. It is strangely intimate, as though you have touched the place itself through its inhabitant.

---

They dress in layers, as the passage described, but the description undersells the *craft* of it. Moss-cloth is their great textile, and it is woven from the fibrous root systems of surface mosses that they cultivate in thin trays of upland soil near the entrance tunnels. The cultivation is painstaking and seasonal — they haul soil upward, haul moss-cuttings downward, haul finished cloth in both directions — and the result is a fabric unlike anything surface-weavers produce. It is dense, close-woven, and slightly felted by the damp air of the Warrens into a material that holds warmth against the body in a way that belies its thinness. It breathes. It wicks the constant, fine moisture away from skin and then slowly releases it, so that a person wrapped in moss-cloth feels neither wet nor dry but some state between the two — a state the Warrens regard as comfort and surface-dwellers regard as deeply unsettling.

The cloth is undyed. Dye is difficult here — there is no sun to set it, no easy mordant, and the mineral-rich water tends to shift any color toward grey or pale rust. So the people dress in the tones of their own landscape: the chalky white of fresh calcite, the grey-brown of older stone, the faint green of the moss itself before it is processed. A person standing still against a Warren wall is very nearly invisible. This is not camouflage in the tactical sense. It is something older and more unconscious — a people shaped, over deep time, to match the color of the only world they know.

They wear their cloth wrapped, pinned, and belted with cordage of braided moss-fiber. Nothing is sewn in the way surface-dwellers sew. The Warrens have no needle-tradition — no fishbone awls, no bronze pins, no metal of any kind. Metal is avoided almost superstitiously, though the practical reason is real enough: iron rusts in days here, copper turns green, even gold feels wrong against the constant damp, cold and heavy and somehow *loud* against the silence. Stone and fiber and bone are their materials. A Warren-dweller's clothing is pinned with small carved bone toggles, or with sharpened stone pegs driven into the cloth's own weave. Belts are braided cord, often holding a small pouch of the same material, and inside the pouch is always a blade — a small, leaf-shaped stone blade, fine-grained and sharp, the basic unit of Warren wealth and identity.

The blade is the first thing a child learns to make. It is the last thing an elder finishes adjusting. It is carried always, tucked against the hip or hanging from the belt, and it is the subject of more quiet conversation, more careful attention, more subtle social accounting than any other object in the Warrens. A person's blade tells you what they do, how long they have been doing it, what seam of stone they work, what neighborhood they belong to, what they value — whether they favor edge geometry over ornament, or ornament over geometry, or have found some way to hold both. Blades are traded, gifted, inherited, and occasionally fought over, though fighting is rare and considered almost physically painful in the acoustics of the Warrens, where every impact reverberates through the walls and every raised voice becomes a vibration you feel in your teeth.

---

They move slowly. I need to say this again because it matters more than it sounds. It is not lethargy. It is not defeat. It is a *calibrated* slowness, a pace matched to the world they inhabit, and it is one of the first things a visitor notices and the last thing a visitor understands. Surface-dwellers are jerky creatures — sudden turns, quick gestures, bursts of speed followed by stillness. The people of the Warrens do not move like that. They flow. They turn a corner with the smooth, unhurried economy of water finding its path through stone. They lift a hand to point and the gesture unfolds like a leaf opening. They walk with a particular rolling gait, bare feet or soft-soled sandals placing each step with deliberate contact, and the reason for this is practical: the stone floors are damp, and a sudden movement on damp stone is a fall, and a fall in a narrow passage means a broken bone and a long, slow walk to wherever help might be. But the reason has become something more than practical. It has become *temperamental*. The people of the Warrens are slow in their bodies the way they are slow in their speech, and for the same reason — because speed is violence here, a disruption of the mineral silence, a shockwave in the walls.

They eat simply. Their diet is the diet of scarcity refined into ritual. The surface farms produce hardy grains — a kind of dense, chalky wheat that grows in thin upland soil — and root vegetables: pale tubers, knobby things with thin skins and starchy flesh. These are hauled down in quantity during the growing season and stored in carved stone chambers where the constant cool temperature preserves them indefinitely. Grain is ground on flat stone querns, mixed with water and a pinch of mineral salt scraped from the walls, and cooked into dense flat-breads on heated stones. The bread is grey. It tastes of earth and something faintly sweet, the ghost of the grain's original flavor after long storage. It is filling. It sustains. It does not delight, but the people of the Warrens do not eat for delight — or rather, they have never had delight as a benchmark and do not miss what they have not measured against. There are no spices. There are no herbs, unless you count the tiny, almost flavorless moss shoots they sometimes add to water for variety. The mineral-rich water itself is the closest thing they have to a luxury — it carries a faint chalky sweetness, and people will sit for long minutes with a stone cup pressed to their lips, drinking slowly, tasting the geology.

Meat is almost unknown. The Warrens support no animal life worth hunting, and the surface farms support no livestock — the soil is too thin, the uplands too exposed. Occasionally, on the surface, a bird or a hare is taken, and its meat is brought down and shared in a small, solemn ceremony that is the closest the Warrens come to a feast. The bones are saved and worked into tools. Nothing is wasted. Nothing has ever been wasted. Waste is a surface concept, a luxury of abundance that the Warrens have never known.

They are thin. Not starving — there is a difference, and it shows. They are lean in the way of people whose caloric intake is precisely matched to their energy expenditure, with no surplus and no deficit. There is no obesity in the Warrens. There is no concept of overeating. The body is a machine that runs on a measured fuel, and the people know its limits the way they know the limits of their passages — by touch, by habit, by a sense of boundaries so internalized it feels like instinct.

---

Their faces are quiet. This is the thing that unsettles visitors most about the people themselves, more than the pallor, more than the large eyes. The faces are *quiet*. Not blank — there is expression there, plenty of it, in the way the Warrens have expression: subtle, slow, readable only if you are paying attention and have time to read. A smile in the Warrens is a slight deepening around the eyes, a fractional lift at the corners of the mouth. A laugh is rare and startling — a sudden bright sound in the mineral silence that echoes and re-echoes and makes everyone in the vicinity wince involuntarily, not from displeasure but from acoustic overload. The people laugh softly. They have learned to laugh into their chests, to stifle the volume, to let the joy exist as a vibration rather than a sound. This is not repression. It is *manners*, in the deepest sense — the unspoken, collectively enforced understanding that the space you share with others is a space you are responsible for, and that sound is the most invasive thing you can put into it.

Anger is almost never shown openly. It is not suppressed — it is *redistributed*. A person angry in the Warrens will go to a passage alone, or to one of the deep, dry chambers near the growing front, and they will work. They will take out a blade-stone and they will knap it with a force and speed that would be frightening to watch, sending sharp flakes spinning into the dimness, and the rhythm of the strikes — fast, hard, deliberate — says everything that the face does not. The sound carries through the walls, and people hearing it know what it means, and they give the passage a wide berth, and when the person emerges, hours later, with a new blade and a calm face, no one asks about it. The blade is the anger, worked out. The blade is the anger, made useful.

They are kind to children in a way that is hard to describe without sounding sentimental. They are not sentimental. They are *attentive*. A child in the Warrens is a small person navigating a world that is literally changing shape around them — walls thickening, passages narrowing, rooms appearing and disappearing — and the adults understand this with a visceral clarity that surface-parents, raising children in a world that stays roughly where it was left, cannot access. They hold children's hands in unfamiliar passages. They teach them to read the stone — to feel a wall's temperature with a flat palm and know whether it is growing or receding, to listen for the deep resonant tone that means a new crystal face has sheared free nearby. They teach them to walk softly. They teach them to speak softly. They teach them, before anything else, to *attend* — to the walls, to the dripping, to the light, to the texture of the air, to the whole mineral body of the place they live in, because that body is their home, and it does not stay still, and the only way to live in a moving world is to move with it.

---

They have a religion, of sorts, though they would not call it that. They have no word for it. It is not a set of beliefs held in the mind; it is a set of practices performed with the body, and the practices are so old and so woven into daily life that no one thinks to separate them out and call them sacred. When they pass a wall that is actively growing — you can see it if you look closely, a faint wet sheen, a crystalline sparkle, a softness to the surface that will harden within weeks — they press a palm to it. Just for a moment. Just long enough to feel the vibration, the subsonic hum of the accreting stone. They do not pray. They do not speak. They just *touch*. And in that touch is everything the Warrens believe without articulating: that the world is being made, continuously, beneath their hands; that they are not separate from the making; that the stone does not end where their skin begins.

Some of the elders — the ones who have spent seventy or eighty years in the Warrens, who have watched passages close and chambers open, who have felt the ceiling lower over their heads by the width of a hand — some of these elders develop a relationship with the stone that is difficult to categorize. They spend hours in the deepest passages, the newest growth, where the walls are warm and the air is thin and the light is at its palest. They press their foreheads to the stone. They listen. When asked what they hear, they say things like *the direction of it*, or *the patience*, or simply *it*, with a gesture that encompasses everything. These elders are not shamans. They hold no authority. They are not consulted for decisions or prophecies. They are simply people who have been here long enough that the boundary between themselves and the place has become, in their own perception, a little less clear than it is for the rest. The younger people regard them with respect and a faint unease. The elders regard the younger people with something that looks like love — the love of someone who knows that the stone will be here when the young are old, and the old are gone, and the stone will still be growing.

---

There is a social structure, but it is as porous and shifting as the geology it exists within. There are no chiefs, no councils, no elected officials. There are *families* — not in the nuclear sense, but in the sense of people who share a cluster of dwellings in a particular chamber, who have stacked their homes against the same wall for two or three or five generations. These family-groups manage their own spaces, make their own decisions about where to carve, when to move, how to allocate the stone-seams they work. Between family-groups, disputes are rare and resolved by the simplest possible mechanism: whoever needs the passage more gets the passage, because the alternative is a blocked corridor that helps no one, and the Warrens are too narrow for spite.

Trade between neighborhoods is conducted along the main passages — the wider, older routes that have been kept open through generations of collective maintenance. The trade is quiet and unhurried, conducted in the soft, carrying voices that the Warrens' acoustics make possible. A knapper from the deepest neighborhood brings blades made from the freshest, softest stone to a middle neighborhood where the older families have accumulated surface-goods: grain, dried moss, woven cloth. The exchange is simple and direct. There is no currency, no accounting system. A blade is worth what the other person gives for it, and if the exchange feels wrong, it is not repeated, and no one speaks of it again. Reputation is currency, and reputation moves through the Warrens the way sound moves — through the walls, carried by vibration, arriving everywhere at once.

They are not isolationists, but they are close to it. Visitors from the surface are permitted — the entrance is not guarded, has never been guarded — but they are met with a politeness so thorough it functions as a wall. The people answer questions. They show their work. They offer food and drink. They do not ask questions in return. They do not inquire about the surface world, its politics, its technologies, its peoples. This is not arrogance or disinterest. It is a kind of constitutional inability to prioritize that information over the information the Warrens provide, which is — to them — complete. They live in a world that is literally rebuilding itself around them at all times, and the dramas of a surface that has finished its geology seem, from down here, like stories about a different species entirely.

---

There is one more thing. It is hard to write about because it is not a fact but a *quality*, and it attaches not to any individual but to the people as a whole, seen from the outside, after time.

They are patient. Not in the way that people on the surface are patient — gritting their teeth, waiting for something to end, enduring a discomfort with the promise of relief. Patient in the way that stone is patient. Patient in the way that the dripping water is patient. Patient in the way of a process that has no endpoint, no goal, no finish line, and no frustration at the absence of one. The stone grows. The people live in it. The walls close and open. The children grow and become the elders and press their foreheads to the warm, new stone in the deepest passages. The blades are made and traded and worn dull and reground and traded again. The dripping continues. The light does not change.

A surface-dweller, standing in one of the Warrens' broad chambers, looking up at a ceiling lost in luminous haze, listening to the patient drip of water from a formation that did not exist when their grandparents were children, might feel — if they were honest, if they let themselves feel it — a vast and terrifying calm. Not peace. Not serenity. *Calm*, the way the deep ocean is calm: absolute, pressing, and utterly indifferent to whether you can bear it. The people of the Warrens bear it because they have never known anything else, and what you have never known cannot frighten you, and what cannot frighten you becomes, in time, home.
