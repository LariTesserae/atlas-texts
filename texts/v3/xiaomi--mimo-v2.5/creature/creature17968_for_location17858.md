# What Lives in the Grown Quarters

---

The first things you notice — because they are everywhere and cannot be avoided — are the mites.

Not true mites, nothing with that specific ancestry, but creatures that have arrived at the same solution from a different starting point: small, soft-bodied, six-legged, each no larger than a lentil. They are pale, the color of new bone, and they move through the compressed moss of the street-floors in slow processions that, at first glance, look like veins of mineral in the substrate — lighter than their surroundings, barely distinguishable from the pale fungal threads woven through the floor-matter. But look closer and the procession separates into individuals. Each one carries a fragment: a curled shred of dead cell-wall, a flake of shed photosynthetic skin, a pellet of processed substrate excreted from some higher tier. They carry these not in mandibles — their mouthparts are too reduced for that — but pressed against their ventral surfaces with specialized forelimbs that curve inward like scoops. They move in lines because the lines follow grooves in the floor-matter where condensation has carved the softest channels, and the grooves are the path of least resistance, and mites do not waste energy on resistance.

They have no eyes. Or rather, they have vestigial light-sensitive patches that tell them nothing useful in the dim of the lower passages — only whether a passage opens upward toward the canopy's thin green light or closes overhead into the dark of a chamber ceiling. This is enough. Directionality is all they need. They navigate the settlement the way a river navigates terrain: by following gradient, by pooling in depressions, by splitting around obstacles and reconverging on the far side. If you sit still on the floor of a lower passage and watch, you can see the traffic pattern of an entire colony hour by hour — the slow pulse of foragers heading outward toward the dead-tissue margins of a chamber, the steady return of carriers heading inward toward the deep fungal networks where their cargo will be broken down and absorbed into the living system. They are the circulatory cells of the settlement, and they do not know they are anything else.

Their lifecycle is simple and entirely contained within the walls. Females lay eggs in small cavities they excavate — not by chewing, but by secreting a softening enzyme that loosens the substrate enough for their forelimbs to scoop it into a bowl-shaped depression. A dozen eggs per clutch. They hatch into larvae that look like smaller, even paler versions of the adults, and the larvae immediately begin to eat: not the living tissue of the architecture, but the fungal threads that permeate it, feeding on the same detritus the adults carry. The larvae never leave the chamber where they were born. They live their entire lives in a space the size of a cupped hand, surrounded by food that arrives on the bodies of adults who pass overhead carrying the dead remnants of the city above them. When the larvae mature, they climb — a journey of meters, for them an odyssey — and join the traffic of the passages, and the cycle continues without interruption.

They die often and in great numbers, and their bodies are eaten by the same fungal networks they fed on in life, and the architecture absorbs them, and they become part of the walls. There is no graveyard in the Grown Quarters. There is no waste.

---

Higher up, in the mid-tier passages where the architecture is younger and the walls still carry their pale pre-photosynthetic color, you find a different order of animal entirely.

These are the weavers — another provisional name, chosen because of what they do, not because of any kinship with Earth's spiders, though the convergence is striking. They are arachnid-analogues, eight-limbed, each limb tipped with a.spinneret-like organ that extrudes a fine filament of chitin-protein composite. The filaments are not silk — they are thicker, coarser, almost like fishing line, and they are not sticky. They serve a structural purpose.

The weavers are small — body mass comparable to a thumbnail — but what they lack in size they make up for in numbers and in the specificity of their architectural labor. They live in loose colonies of several hundred, nested in the spaces between the growing tissue layers of mid-tier walls, and their primary activity is the reinforcement of passages. When a new wall grows upward, its tissue is soft and prone to sagging under the weight of condensation, and the weavers colonize it almost immediately, spinning their filaments across the surface in dense, criss-crossing networks that hold the young tissue in place while it lignifies — while it hardens and becomes structural. Over time, the living tissue grows around and through the filament networks, incorporating them, and the wall becomes stronger for it. The weavers have, in effect, taught the walls to use rebar.

They work in a coordination that is not coordinated. No individual weaver knows what any other is doing. Each one responds to a simple local stimulus: the degree of give in the wall surface beneath its legs. Where the tissue is soft, it spins. Where it has hardened, it stops. The result, viewed from outside, looks like a deliberate engineering project — filament networks denser at stress points, sparser where the wall is already self-supporting. But there is no engineer. There is only a few hundred small animals responding to the same physical conditions, and the mathematics of their aggregate behavior producing a structure that no individual intended.

At night, in the bioluminescent glow, you can sometimes see the weavers at work: small dark shapes moving across the luminous walls, leaving faint trails in the fungal glow where the filaments have disrupted the surface hyphae. They move in slow, deliberate arcs, and they are silent, and they have been doing this work for longer than the oldest structures have stood.

The weavers die, too. Their bodies are small enough to be absorbed by the fungal networks without leaving a trace. But their filaments persist — woven into the walls like the ghost of a skeleton, long after the weaver that spun them has been recycled into the architecture. Some of the oldest walls in the lower quarter are more filament than tissue, the original structural organism long dead and replaced by layer upon layer of secondary growth, held together by the accumulated labor of thousands of generations of weavers. You cannot see the filaments with the naked eye in the dim light, but you can feel them: if you press your fingers to the wall and slide them along the surface, there is a texture — a faint grain, like very fine cloth — that is not plant, not fungus, not mineral. It is animal. It is the invisible scaffolding of a city built by creatures that do not know they have built anything.

---

The cat-sized things — the six-limbed ones, the dark chitin-green ones — are called, by the few humans who have visited this place and returned, the *keelbacks*, for the ridge of thickened tissue that runs along their dorsal surface from head to tail-tip. This ridge is not bone. It is a dense concentration of chitin and structural protein that serves a dual purpose: armor against the rare and slow predation that exists here, and — more importantly — a tool for manipulating the architecture.

Keelbacks are the only animals in the Grown Quarters that regularly damage the living walls, and they do it on purpose.

They eat the structural tissue. Not the moss-outer layer, not the fungal networks, but the pale, dense, cambium-arranged fibers beneath — the tissue that gives the walls their load-bearing capacity. They eat it by pressing their dorsal ridge against a wall surface and rocking, side to side, in a slow grinding motion that shears the tissue loose from the layers above and below. A single keelback can remove a strip of structural fiber the length of your hand in the course of an afternoon. They eat slowly. Everything here eats slowly. They press against the wall and grind and pause and grind and pause, and the wall yields its tissue to them in thin curls of pale fiber that fall to the spongy floor and are quickly colonized by the decomposer mites.

The damage they cause is not trivial. In the passages where keelbacks are common — the mid-tier corridors where structural tissue is thickest and most accessible — the walls are scarred with concave depressions where generations of keelbacks have fed, and the architecture compensates by growing thicker in these areas, bulging outward, producing buttressing tissue that has the look of an immune response: swollen, dense, overbuilt. The keelbacks and the architecture are in a slow arms race. The walls grow tougher; the keelbacks' ridges grow harder. The walls grow thicker; the keelbacks' feeding motions grow more sustained. Neither side is winning. Neither side is trying to win. They are simply responding to each other, adjustment by adjustment, over a timescale that makes the concept of strategy meaningless.

Keelbacks are solitary except during their reproductive period, which occurs once per year in the deep cold when the mist thickens to near-solid and the bioluminescence in the walls brightens — whether in response to the cold or to some other signal, no one knows. During this period, keelbacks emerge from their crevice-dens in numbers, moving through the passages with something that looks, from a distance, like purpose. They converge on the upper tiers, where the youngest structures sway in the wind, and they mate there, among the translucent new walls, in slow deliberate encounters that last hours. The female lays a single clutch — two or three eggs, no more — in a cavity she grinds into the base of a young column, and then she leaves, and she does not return, and the eggs hatch weeks later into keelback nymphs that are already equipped with working ridges and an instinct for grinding. The nymphs feed on the walls of their birth-column and grow slowly and disperse, and within a year they are indistinguishable from the adults, and the cycle has completed, and nothing has been decided.

Keelbacks are aware of humans. This is the unsettling part. The decomposer mites are not — they have no sensory apparatus to register you as anything other than a large, warm, immobile obstruction to be bypassed. The weavers might register you as a vibration, a disruption in the wall surface they are reinforcing, and would pause in their work until you moved on. But the keelbacks see you. Their eyes are small and dark and wet-stone-bright, and they turn to track your movement as you pass, and they do not flee, and they do not approach. They simply observe, with the patience of something that has never been surprised, and then they resume their grinding, and you are left with the distinct and unprovable feeling that you were catalogued — measured, filed, integrated into some mental model of the passage that the keelback will update if you pass this way again.

They are not intelligent. Their brain-mass is too small, their behavioral repertoire too narrow. But they are *attentive*, and in a place this quiet and this still, attentiveness can be mistaken for something deeper than it is.

---

In the water — in the cistern-cups, in the thin films that run down the wall-surfaces, in the channels within the walls where condensation collects and drains — there are organisms that have no analogues outside of this place.

They are called, with some justification, the *glass-feeder*s. They are sessile — rooted, in effect — attached to the inner surfaces of the water-collection cups and the drainage channels by a basal disc of adhesive tissue that bonds to the plant-surface as firmly as paint to a wall. Their bodies are small, no larger than a coin, and almost perfectly transparent: a sac of fluid surrounded by a membrane so thin you can see the water moving inside it, see the faint green of a few photosynthetic cells clustered near the membrane's outer surface, see the dark dot of whatever organ serves as a digestive center. They look like drops of water that have decided to stay.

They feed on the microbial film that coats every wet surface in the settlement — the bacteria and algal-analogues that grow in the thin boundary layer where water meets living wall. The glass-feeders extend a fringe of cilia from their basal surface — downward, into the film — and the cilia beat in slow coordinated waves that draw the film across the glass-feeder's underside, where the digestive cells absorb what they can and excrete the rest. They are, in essence, small pumps, pulling the microbial ecosystem across their surfaces and extracting what they need. Their waste product is a slightly different chemistry than what they take in — enriched in certain nitrogen compounds, depleted in others — and this altered chemistry flows onward through the drainage channels, and the walls respond to it, growing differently in areas where the glass-feeders are dense. The glass-feeders are not shaping the architecture the way the weavers are, through physical reinforcement, but through chemistry — a slow, silent conversation conducted in the language of nutrient gradients, each participant altering the conditions the other experiences.

Glass-feeders reproduce by budding. A small pouch forms on the parent's membrane surface, swells with fluid, develops its own basal disc and its own membrane, and eventually pinches off to settle on the nearest wet surface. The parent does not notice. The offspring does not need to learn anything. Within hours it is pumping, feeding, altering the water chemistry of its channel. Within weeks it is producing buds of its own. The glass-feeder populations in the oldest drainage channels are dense enough that the inner surfaces of the conduits are carpeted with them — layer upon layer of transparent tissue that turns the channels from smooth tubes into complex, textured passages where the water flow is disrupted and redirected by thousands of tiny, pumping bodies. The architecture of the drainage system is not entirely the product of plant-growth. It has been modified, at the microscopic scale, by animals that do not know they are modifying anything, and the modification is functional, and it persists, and it changes the way water moves through the settlement in ways that benefit both the walls and the glass-feeders, and neither party is aware of the arrangement.

At night, the glass-feeders are faintly luminous — not with their own light, but because they are transparent, and the bioluminescence of the fungal networks behind them passes through their bodies like light through a lens, so that each glass-feeder appears to glow from within, a small bright point in the dark water of a cistern-cup, and if you look at a cup full of them, you see a constellation: dozens of points of cold blue-green light, suspended in water, pulsing faintly with the rhythms of the fungal network beneath them.

---

There is one more thing that lives in the Grown Quarters, and it is the hardest to talk about because it resists every category.

In the deepest passages, in the oldest part of the settlement, where the walls are centuries thick and the air is warm with the heat of continuous decomposition, there are structures that do not correspond to any plant, animal, or fungus that has been described. They are dark — darker than the surrounding walls, darker than the shadows — and they are soft, almost spongy, and they grow in irregular masses that protrude from the wall surfaces into the passages like tumors, or like fists, or like something trying to reach across a gap and failing. They are not part of the architecture. The architecture grows around them, through them, incorporating them and being incorporated by them in a way that makes separation impossible. They are darker because they contain no photosynthetic tissue. They are soft because their cell-walls are thinner, less structured, more like animal tissue than plant tissue. And they are warm — noticeably, perceptibly warm, even against the generally warm walls of the lower quarter. They generate their own heat, more than the surrounding biomass, and the warmth is localized, concentrated, so that if you place your hand on one of these masses, it feels like pressing your palm against another living body.

They have no observable behavior. They do not move, do not respond to light or touch in any visible way, do not grow at any rate you can measure during a human visit. But they are alive — their tissues are dense with metabolic activity, with chemical reactions proceeding at a pace that is slow by mammalian standards but fast for a cold, dark, high-altitude ecosystem. They consume the surrounding tissue and replace it with themselves. The architecture accommodates them. The decomposer mites avoid them. The keelbacks do not feed on them. The glass-feeders in the nearby drainage channels alter their pumping rhythm when the water passes through a section of wall that contains one of these masses — slower, more deliberate, as if the water is being asked to wait.

The humans who have seen these structures call them *hearts*, which is not a scientific name but an emotional one, and it tells you more about the humans than about the structures. No one knows what they are. No one has sampled one successfully — the tissue is dense and resistant to the simple tools available at this altitude, and more than one attempt has resulted in the tool being swallowed by the mass, closing around it like a slow mouth and not letting go. The mass does not digest the tool. It simply holds it, incorporates it, grows over it, and within a season the tool is invisible, buried in layers of dark, warm tissue that has already forgotten it was ever foreign.

What these masses are — whether they are a life form, a phase of the architecture, a disease, a reproductive organ of something larger, or something that has no analogue anywhere else — is unknown. They are the one element of the Grown Quarters that does not resolve into function when you study it long enough. Everything else — the mites, the weavers, the keelbacks, the glass-feeders, the architecture itself — can be understood as a component of a system, a participant in the economy of growth and decay that makes the settlement possible. But the hearts do not contribute in any visible way. They do not reinforce. They do not decompose. They do not filter water or spin filaments or grind walls. They simply exist, warmly, in the dark, surrounded by a system that has made room for them without understanding them, and they pulse with a metabolic activity that is directed at nothing you can name.

If you stay long enough in the deepest passages — if you sit in the dark with your back against a wall that breathes and your hand resting on a heart that is warmer than you are — you may begin to feel something that the place does not intend but cannot prevent: the sense that you are sitting inside something that is aware of itself in a way that has nothing to do with thought. Not conscious. Not sentient. But *coherent* — organized to a degree that exceeds what any of its individual components can account for, maintained by processes that no single participant controls or comprehends, persisting in a cold and dark and high place where persistence is not guaranteed and not owed.

The hearts are the closest thing the Grown Quarters has to a question it cannot answer about itself.

---

Nothing here is waiting for you. Nothing here will notice your absence. The mites will continue their processions along the grooved floors. The weavers will continue to spin their invisible reinforcements into the young walls. The keelbacks will grind their slow furrows in the structural tissue and watch you with wet-stone eyes and resume their work. The glass-feeders will pump their clear bodies against the surfaces of the water-cups and glow, faintly, in the dark.

And in the deepest passages, the hearts will be warm, and dark, and full of chemistry that means something to itself and to nothing else, and the architecture will grow around them as it has always grown around them, and the water will flow, and the walls will breathe, and the settlement will add another millimeter of height to its newest structures, and another layer of age to its oldest, and the whole slow organism will continue its revision of itself in the silence that is not absence but presence of a kind that does not require witness.

You were here. The Grown Quarters did not notice. This is not a failure of the place. It is its most complete expression.
