# The Inhabitants of the Flats of Yss

---

They are not trying to be quiet. That is the first misapprehension a visitor must abandon.

The silence is not an absence of effort — it is the *shape* of their effort, the way a reef is the shape of a thousand polyps all building in the same direction without knowing it. Every organism on the Flats is engaged in constant, strenuous biological activity. The lizards are basking, yes, but basking is work here: their cells are pulling heat from the stone with an efficiency that approaches thermodynamic impossibility, converting ambient warmth into metabolic fuel, and the faint pulse you see in their flanks is not idle respiration but the visible rhythm of engines running at capacity. The birds — or whatever they are — are not drifting. Their wide arcs are thermals, calculated with a precision that belies their apparent laziness, each wingless-feathered expanse angled to catch updrafts that only exist for seconds in specific quadrants of the Flats. The insects are not ambient. Each one is a needle of appetite, moving through the air-column with目的, feeding on the mineral-laden condensation, feeding on each other, feeding on the invisible particulate that rises from the stone in the warmth of the day — and they are so many, so *unfathomably* many, that their collective hunger has become a force, a weather, a thing with pressure and consequence.

They are loud. They are all of them loud. And the Flats absorb it.

---

## The Stone-Sitters

The lizards have a name that visitors give them, and visitors give them many names — *stone-sitters*, *the patient ones*, *the warmbacks* — but none of them are right, because every name implies stillness, and these creatures are not still. They are *dense.*

A single specimen of the most common form — the broad-bodied, rust-colored variant that presses itself to the glass-surface in clusters of dozens — weighs approximately what you would expect a lizard of its size to weigh. But press your hand against the stone beside one and you feel a vibration, steady and deep, that is coming not from the ground but from the animal itself. Their bones are thick, reinforced with calcium deposits that echo the mineral composition of the rock beneath them. Their organs are heavy. A dissection — no one has conducted one; no one who goes to the Flats thinks in those terms — would reveal a body built less like a reptile and more like a geological feature with a heartbeat. Their hearts are large, four-chambered, beating slowly but with tremendous force, pushing blood through vessels that are wider than they should be for an animal their size. They are hydraulic. They are engines of circulation.

But it is the frills that matter.

The coral-crests — those elaborate, porous fans of calcium that rise behind the skull and spread outward in branching architectures — are not display structures. They are not ornamental. Visitors who have sat long enough, who have watched through an entire day-cycle, report seeing the frills do something that the lizards never do: they *open.* Not quickly. Over the course of an hour, the layered chambers of the crest separate slightly, like the pages of a book lifting in a breeze, and when they do, the air above the lizard's head changes. You can see it. The condensation that forms in the early hours and the late evening — that mineral dew — gathers on the frill, fills the tiny coral-chambers, and then, as the lizards breathe, it moves. Through the whorls and passages of the crest. The animal is filtering. Drawing moisture — and whatever the moisture carries, the dissolved minerals, the microscopic organisms that live in the thin sheets of water in the basins — through the structure of its own crest and into its body.

The frills are mouths. Not in any conventional sense. But they are intake. They are the mechanism by which the stone-sitters drink a world that has no rivers.

And when they do this, when a cluster of thirty or forty lizards opens their frills simultaneously in the early dew-hours, the air above them moves. Not audibly. But *visibly.* The moisture bends. The light shifts. And if you are standing close enough, you feel the silence deepen, because the air itself is being drawn inward, pulled through ten thousand tiny calcium chambers in a hundred still bodies, and every vibration that might have escaped into sound is instead swallowed, filtered, made into tissue.

They are not quiet. They are *eating the sound out of the air to stay alive.*

## The Arc-Wings

Above, the pale shapes trace their slow geometries.

They are called birds because they have wings and they are in the sky, and the human brain, confronted with these facts, reaches for the nearest category. But the wings do not function as wings should. They are not feathered — not exactly. They are membranous, translucent, and so large in proportion to the body that each one, fully extended, might span three or four times the length of the creature's torso. The membrane is double-layered, and between the layers, a fluid moves — not blood, something thicker, slower, with a viscosity closer to tree sap. This fluid redistributes with each slow expansion of the wing, shifting the center of gravity incrementally, and the flight of the arc-wings is not flapping but *balancing* — a continuous, micromanaged adjustment of weight within a shape that never changes its contour. They do not fly. They *fall* with extreme precision, catching themselves in the same air again and again through shifts so subtle that from a distance the motion looks like hovering.

Their eyes are enormous. Not proportionally — *absolutely.* The head of an arc-wing is mostly eye, two dark spheres that occupy nearly the entire skull, set facing slightly forward rather than to the sides. They are binocular. They see depth with a clarity that may exceed any vision known to vertebrate biology, and they use it to watch the ground.

What are they watching for?

The ripples. The slow, circular expansions in the thin basin-water that signal movement beneath the stone. The arc-wings orbit above particular basins, hanging motionless, watching patterns in the water that take hours to fully develop. They are patient in a way that has nothing to do with temperament and everything to do with timescale. Their metabolisms are slow. Their lifespans, one suspects, are long. They have the quality of beings who experience time differently — not faster or slower, but *more broadly,* as though each moment contains a wider band of the present than what shorter-lived things perceive.

And then, without warning, without any visible change in their behavior, one will fold its wings — both simultaneously, a motion that takes less than a second — and drop.

The descent is silent. Absolutely silent. The membrane folds tight against the body and the creature becomes a pale streak, a needle aimed at the surface of the basin water. It strikes. It emerges. And in its talons — slender, bone-thin, almost insectile — there is something. Always something. The subsonic pulse that follows in its wake is not a call. It is the vibration of the wing-membrane re-inflating, a pressure wave that passes through every body on the Flats and is felt in the sternum of anyone standing close enough: a statement of *having eaten*, broadcast on a frequency that travels farther than sound but reaches only things that can feel.

The other arc-wings do not react. They continue their orbits. The dropped one folds again and begins to rise, the fluid in its wings redistributing, the heavy dark eye already scanning the next basin.

They are the closest thing on the Flats to predators — though that word implies a violence that doesn't quite apply. The arc-wings do not hunt. They *receive.* They watch the slow movements below the stone, and when the moment arrives — when something rises close enough to the surface — they are there to accept what the surface offers. Their relationship with the things beneath the stone is not adversarial. It may, in fact, be cooperative. The arc-wings feed on what the sub-surface creatures displace, and the sub-surface creatures, in their vast and patient undulation, may be responding to the presence of the arc-wings above — adjusting their movements to surface where the talons will find them, depositing themselves at the points of impact.

It is a ritual. It has been happening since before the Flats had a name, since before there were names for anything.

## The Film

The insects — though *insects* is a courtesy, a gesture toward familiarity — do not have individual lives in any meaningful sense.

They are the size of dust motes. A single specimen, held on a fingertip, would be nearly invisible: a translucent body, no more than a millimeter long, with wings that beat at a frequency so high that the sound they produce exists in a range the human ear cannot access. Above the upper limit of hearing. They are ultrasonic, all of them, every wing-stroke a needle of sound too high to register, and collectively — and there are *billions,* there may be trillions — they produce a wall of inaudibility, a frequency so far above human perception that the brain does not even register its absence. You do not hear them. You do not *almost* hear them. They exist in a spectrum your senses cannot touch.

But the lizards hear them. The arc-wings hear them. The things beneath the stone, in their slow darkness, hear them.

The Film — as the visitors who study such things have come to call it — is the connective tissue of the Flats. It is the medium through which information passes. The insects carry minerals on their bodies, harvested from the condensation, and they deposit these minerals on the skin of the lizards, on the membranes of the arc-wings, on the surface of the stone itself. They are redistributors. They are the circulatory system of the plateau, moving trace elements from the basins where water collects to the high ridges where the stone is dry, from the surface to the spaces beneath, carried on bodies so small that the passage from one point to another takes hours and the total journey of any single insect is measured in meters. But there are so *many.* The map of their collective movement, if it could be seen, would look like the nervous system of a single organism — billions of slow, tiny signals traveling in patterns that have been repeating for longer than the Flats have had their present shape.

When they pass over you, they leave a residue. A fine, mineral dust that settles on your skin and glitters faintly in the light. You might mistake it for the natural dust of the plateau, theite powder of ancient stone grinding itself to flour. It is not. It is biological. It is the metabolic output of trillions of living things, and it is warm — faintly, almost imperceptibly warm — and if you were to stand on the Flats long enough, it would cover you entirely, a thin living film that would make you, for a brief time, part of the system. Part of the circuit.

Some visitors feel this. Some visitors stand still and let it accumulate and feel the warmth spread and feel the silence deepen and feel themselves becoming, briefly, a node in a network they cannot perceive.

These visitors do not always leave.

## The Under-Stone

Beneath the glass-surface, in the porous darkness of the plateau's interior, something moves.

You cannot see it directly. No one has. But its evidence is everywhere: the ripples in the basin-water, the undulations in the mineral crust, the slow rearrangement of the concentric structures near the eastern edge. It is vast. The scale of its movement — the way the stone itself deforms, barely, over hours — suggests a body or bodies of extraordinary size, moving through channels in the rock that were carved not by water but by the creature itself, over an immense span of time, pressing upward against the surface of the world with a force that is geological in its patience but biological in its regularity.

It rises and falls on a cycle. The visitors who sit long enough — who spend days, weeks on the Flats, who become fixtures, indistinguishable from the lizards — learn to read this cycle. There are hours when the basins deepen, the water gathering in response to a downward pull from below, and hours when the water thins, drawn back into the stone by a suction that has no geological explanation. The crust along the old channels rises in the evening, falls in the morning, and the rhythm is as regular as breathing, as regular as the arc-wings' orbits, as regular as the dew-cycle. The Flats breathe, and the thing beneath the stone is the lung.

What is it?

The visitors who stay long enough stop asking. The question assumes a separateness — a *thing* that is distinct from the place it inhabits — and on the Flats, this distinction does not hold. The lizards are not separate from the stone. Their frills are calcium drawn from the same deposits that compose the plateau. The arc-wings' wing-fluid carries the same mineral signature as the basin-water. The Film's dust is the surface of the world made mobile. And the thing beneath the stone is the stone itself, organized by a patience so slow that its thoughts, if they are thoughts, take years to complete, its movements so gradual that a human life would pass from birth to death in the span of a single gesture.

It may be aware of the visitors. It may be aware of everything on the surface — every lizard, every arc-wing, every mote of the Film — the way a body is aware of its own skin. Not as objects. As sensations. As textures. As the boundary between itself and what is not itself, which, on the Flats, is a distinction that may never have been clearly made.

## The Avoidance

The fauna avoid the central basin. The concentric rings. The place where the water holds longest and deepest.

Not all of them, and not dramatically. The avoidance is a gradient — a thinning, a rerouting, a subtle adjustment of patterns that amounts to a consensus without communication. The lizards on the far rim do not turn away from the center. They simply face in other directions, and their frills do not open above that basin, and the mineral-dew that gathers in its water is not processed by any creature's intake. The arc-wings pass above it but do not drop. The Film thins to almost nothing in the air above it, as though the insects' collective navigation — that vast, shared map of mineral routes — contains a blank space there, a territory designated as *not for crossing.*

The thing beneath the stone does not move beneath the central basin. The ripples in its water are not caused by sub-surface pressure. The water is still in a way the rest of the Flats' water never is — not the stillness of calm but the stillness of something *held.* Kept. Maintained in a state of purity that the rest of the plateau's water, drawn up and filtered and recycled through ten thousand calcium chambers and a billion tiny wings, does not possess.

The structures around it — those concentric walls of accreted mineral, grown over geological time by some process that combines the lizards' calcium-deposition with the slow accretion of the Film and the upward pressure of the Under-Stone — serve no function that any visitor has determined. They enclose. They define a space. They mark a location on the Flats as *different* from the rest, as a place where the system of redistribution and consumption and slow geological breath does not apply.

Something is preserved there. Something is being kept clean.

The visitors who sit long enough, who let the Film accumulate on their skin, who feel the warmth of the Flats settle into their bones and the silence deepen around them like water rising — these visitors sometimes report a sense, not quite thought, not quite feeling, that arrives in the late afternoon, during the fifteen minutes when the stone turns purple-black and amber:

*The central basin is where the Flats remember what they were.*

Before the erosion. Before the rounding. Before the last meaningful shape was found. Before the life came to fill the volume. Before the silence learned to eat sound. Before the Under-Stone settled into its slow rhythm and the arc-wings began their patient orbits and the lizards pressed their frills to the dew.

Something happened there. Something that the Flats — the lizards, the arc-wings, the Film, the Under-Stone, the ancient accreted walls, the patient geology of a plateau wearing itself to its essential form — have been circling ever since. Not mourning. Not celebrating. *Maintaining.* Keeping the space clear. Holding the water still.

The visitors who feel this do not always understand it. But they do not leave the central basin, either. They sit at its rim with the lizards, facing inward, and the silence around them is deeper than the silence of the rest of the Flats, and in that deeper silence, something older than sound makes itself felt:

Not a voice. Not a word. A *shape.* The shape of something that was here before the stone was, before the erosion came, before the life arrived to fill the silence that the silence itself had made. The shape of a beginning, preserved in still water and concentric walls and the avoidance of every living thing, held at the center of a plateau that has forgotten everything except this: *something was here first, and it mattered, and we are the shape of its memory, and we will not disturb the place where it rests.*

The Flats of Yss are not a landscape. They are a memorial. Every lizard with its coral frill. Every arc-wing with its slow fall. Every mote of the Film, every slow ripple in the basin water. All of it is devotion, carried out by creatures who may not know the word but whose every biological function serves the same purpose: to keep the silence, to keep the warmth, to keep the water still at the center where something older than life once was.

And the visitors who stay become part of it. Their bodies accumulate the mineral dust. Their skin warms to the stone's temperature. Their hearing adjusts to the silence, then deepens past it, into the subsonic realm where the arc-wings' wing-beats and the Under-Stone's slow breathing and the trillions of inaudible insect-wings create a sound that is not sound — a pressure, a vibration, a texture of the air that says, without words, without anything resembling language:

*We are here. We have always been here. We will remain.*

*This is what remains.*
