## Preparing to Visit the Still Ward

Prepare less as though you are visiting a city and more as though you are entering a shared instrument—one whose stone, water, gardens, and inhabitants can all be disturbed by careless movement.

### Before descending

Arrange permission in advance if possible. The entrance may appear unguarded, but the seam, junipers, upper steps, and nearby condensation routes are almost certainly tended. Do not widen the crack, cut vegetation, drive stakes into the limestone, or leave markers on the plain. Wait near the juniper screen and announce yourself with a quiet voice rather than shouting into the opening.

Travel in a very small party. One or two visitors will be easier for the Ward to house, warm, and supply. Large groups would strain its paths, water stores, and silence.

Leave behind anything that strikes, rings, buzzes, or emits powerful vibration:

- Firearms and explosive equipment  
- Metal-shod boots, loose buckles, dangling cookware, and hard-wheeled cases  
- Hammers, climbing pitons, bells, whistles, and spring-loaded tools  
- Drones, fans, loud pumps, alarms, and vibrating motors  
- Speakers or devices with audible notifications  
- Strobing or high-powered lamps  
- Open flames and smoky fuel  

Wrap necessary objects individually in felt. Secure every clasp so nothing can fall onto stone. Set all electronics to silent—not merely vibrate.

### Clothing and personal equipment

The Ward is cold, wet, intensely bright, and physically cramped. Wear layered clothing that remains warm when damp:

- A wool or synthetic base layer  
- A quiet insulating layer  
- A soft, non-rustling outer garment  
- A waterproof shoulder cape rather than a stiff, crackling shell  
- A close-fitting cap and thin gloves with good dexterity  
- Soft-soled shoes with excellent grip  

Avoid hard heels and rigid boots. The worn limestone will be slick with condensation, moss, and mineral glaze. Bring spare socks in a sealed cloth or padded bag.

Despite being underground, eye protection may be useful. The reflected daylight is diffuse but relentless, and travelers coming from darker environments may find the white masonry painful. Use lightly tinted, non-mirrored lenses rather than dark goggles; you will still need to read shallow steps and subtle hand signals.

Carry a small, hooded lamp with a warm, low setting for use after the roof light fades. Ask before using it. Never shine it into homes, nesting holes, cisterns, or residents’ faces.

A compact sitting pad, wrapped water bottle, and silent notebook are preferable to elaborate expedition gear. Pens that click should be replaced with pencils or capped markers. Even then, write gently: tapping or scraping on a wall, railing, or tabletop may be considered both rude and dangerous.

### Water, food, and fuel

Do not expect the Ward to provision you generously. Its apparent lushness is the result of collecting tiny yields from many carefully balanced places. A visitor’s meal may represent harvests from dozens of beds.

Bring your own compact food, favoring items that:

- Require no cooking  
- Produce few crumbs  
- Have little odor  
- Come in quiet, reusable wrapping  
- Do not need washing in local water  

Do not bring seeds, fresh soil, live cultures, unsealed fruit, or plants. The Ward’s ecology is enclosed and highly specialized. Spores, mites, molds, or ordinary surface insects could be disastrous.

Bring enough drinking water to avoid burdening the cisterns, but not so much that you cannot move safely through narrow stairs. If offered local water, let your host draw it. Never dip your own vessel into a basin. Every cup and channel may have a designated place in a larger sequence, and what looks like runoff may be someone’s drinking supply, pantry coolant, medicinal bed, or frog habitat.

Fuel is more precious than hospitality. Do not request a private fire. Expect to sleep clothed around a shared hearth, and bring a warm bedroll that does not hiss or crackle when moved.

### Health precautions

The cold and damp will worsen asthma, arthritis, poor circulation, and some respiratory conditions. Travelers should be able to climb irregular stairs while crouching and carrying their own gear. The architecture is not spacious enough for ordinary stretchers, and evacuation through the entrance may be slow.

Recommended preparations include:

- Up-to-date routine vaccinations  
- Personal medication in moistureproof, padded containers  
- Antifungal foot care  
- A compact first-aid kit without clattering metal cases  
- A soft respirator for mold sensitivity, used discreetly  
- Treatment for minor hypothermia and sprains  
- A means of testing unfamiliar water if you must drink independently  

Assume the Ward has unfamiliar microbes and that you carry equally unfamiliar ones. Wash before entry, wear clean outer clothing, and accept any local quarantine or cleansing custom. Do not touch frogs, snails, moss beds, insects, nests, or water channels. The tiny eyeless frogs in particular should be treated as protected indicators of water health, not curiosities.

If you are ill, postpone the visit. A respiratory infection in such close quarters could move through the whole settlement.

### Learning to be quiet

Silence here is infrastructure.

Before entering, practice walking without heel strikes, setting objects down rather than dropping them, and speaking at arm’s length. Learn a few basic gestures for greeting, thanks, apology, permission, danger, and “Where may I step?” If you have a local guide, follow their pace exactly.

Inside the Ward:

- Never call to someone across a room or alley.  
- Do not clap, whistle, sing loudly, or test for echoes.  
- Do not knock on stone walls.  
- Do not move furniture without permission.  
- Catch dropped objects if you safely can, but do not lunge onto a garden or channel.  
- Close doors slowly against their pads.  
- Mute coughing and sneezing into thick cloth.  
- Keep conversations close and brief.  

If you accidentally make a sharp noise, stop moving. Do not compound it with startled apologies shouted into the chamber. Follow residents’ instructions; they may need to inspect droplets, channels, walls, or nearby gardens.

Be especially cautious with laughter. The inhabitants are not necessarily humorless, but they will have learned forms of amusement that do not burst outward.

### Moving through the city

The Still Ward is vertically dense. A roof may be a path, a garden may cover a water channel, and a stair may pass through what appears to be a private room. Ordinary ideas of street and building will not help much.

Always use a guide during your first days. Do not assume that the broadest route is public or that an empty ledge is safe to stand on. Pale surfaces conceal drops, shallow basins, translucent mineral crusts, and white steps worn into white floors.

Move slowly and keep one hand free, but do not steady yourself on walls unless invited. The wall may carry reflective plaster, a family’s moss patch, hidden moisture, or a vibration-sensitive lintel. Use designated rails, ropes, or shoulder-height grip stones.

Carry as little as possible. Backpacks should be narrow and soft-sided. A rigid pack can strike balconies, scrape mica from arches, or sweep drying plants from racks. Remove long objects before entering alleys.

Do not explore channels, crawlspaces, lower wet chambers, or roof terraces on your own. Some routes may be reserved for water keepers, gardeners, children, or animals. Others may simply become too narrow to turn around.

### Courtesy and exchange

Bring gifts that add value without adding noise, contamination, or maintenance. Good possibilities include:

- Fine wool felt  
- Quiet, strong thread  
- Clean woven cloth  
- Medical gauze and simple medicines, if requested  
- Small noncorroding needles in padded cases  
- Magnifying lenses  
- Durable pigments or writing materials  
- Knowledge of low-impact water conservation  
- Repair materials compatible with limestone and clay  

Do not present a metal tool merely because metal is rare. Its rarity may be deliberate, and a steel blade, hammer, or saw could be socially disruptive as well as acoustically dangerous. Ask what is wanted before bringing technological gifts.

Expect exchange to happen through gesture, placement, and soft speech. Do not bargain theatrically. Water, fuel, moss, plaster, nesting space, and labor may carry values invisible to an outsider. A tiny bundle of mint may represent careful tending across an entire season.

Offer practical service if permitted. You may be asked to sort shoots, hold a felt screen, pass water cups, turn a counterweight slowly, or sit beside a channel and report changes in its dripping. Do not improvise improvements. In the Ward, a crooked gutter may be crooked because six dependent systems have grown around it.

### Photography and recording

Ask before making any image. Bright flashes are unacceptable, and even a camera shutter may be intrusive. Equipment can also encourage you to step backward without looking—particularly dangerous on crowded terraces.

Audio recording deserves even greater caution. Silence may have ritual, legal, and personal dimensions beyond simple noise control. The residents may not wish private close-spoken conversations preserved or carried to other worlds.

Never sample stone, plaster, moss, water, shells, insects, or plant cuttings. Nothing is merely decorative. Even loose fragments may have a place in reflection, drainage, or repair.

### Sleeping and daily rhythm

Expect close quarters, little privacy, and a schedule governed by light and droplets rather than clocks. You may sleep beside several people on a raised platform around a shared hearth pit. Keep your belongings contained within the space assigned to you.

When the daylight enters, the Ward will become brilliantly active without becoming loud. After it withdraws, movement may diminish quickly to conserve lamps and heat. Adapt to that rhythm. Do not ask hosts to stay awake for your convenience.

At meals, take small portions and wait before accepting more. Foods are likely to be cold, fermented, bitter, mineral-rich, or strongly herbal. If you have dietary restrictions, communicate them before arrival rather than refusing scarce food after it has been served.

### Emergencies

Agree on a silent emergency signal with your guide—perhaps pressure on the arm or a repeated hand motion. Audible alarms should be a last resort.

The most likely immediate dangers are:

- Slipping on wet limestone  
- Becoming lost in layered passages  
- Hypothermia  
- Contaminating or damaging a water route  
- Blocking a narrow evacuation path  
- Causing a sharp vibration through dropped gear  
- Sudden seepage after rain on the plain  

Check surface weather before descending. Heavy rain may alter channels and cistern behavior below even if the sky is invisible from the Ward. During an influx, residents may need every route clear.

If evacuation is ordered, leave possessions behind unless told otherwise. Move single file, softly and steadily. Do not rush the worn steps.

### The proper attitude

The chief mistake would be to treat the Still Ward as primitive because it lacks metal, engines, spacious streets, or abundant fire. Its sophistication lies in relationships too gradual and distributed for a visitor to see: one drop passing through six uses, one wall serving as home, path, reflector, garden, and reservoir boundary.

Arrive ready to observe rather than solve.

Do not straighten what appears crooked, clear what appears overgrown, dry what appears damp, illuminate what appears dim, or accelerate what appears inefficient. The city has survived by accepting the exact pace of stone, water, cold, and living growth. Your task is to pass through without forcing any of them to accommodate you.

If you leave having made no loud sound, displaced no water, introduced no organism, and consumed no more warmth than your hosts freely shared, you will have traveled well.
