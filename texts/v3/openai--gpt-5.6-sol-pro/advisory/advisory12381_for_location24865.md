## Overall assessment

Treat this city as an active environment, not a ruin. Its principal hazards are not hostile inhabitants but cold, hypoxia, mineral dust, unstable orientation, nonreciprocal routes, and architecture that changes when observed, approached, counted, or described.

An ordinary human should not enter unless each traveler has:

- sealed life support,
- independent thermal protection,
- an extraction method carried on the body,
- training in climbing, low-oxygen emergencies, and navigation without ordinary geometry,
- and the discipline to abandon the visit immediately when local rules begin changing.

Do not plan to “explore the city” in any comprehensive sense. Plan a short incursion along its outer terraces. The highest districts, eastern abyss, needle streets, unsupported plazas, and absent-column stair should be considered specialist objectives with an extreme likelihood of nonreturn.

The city does not seem malicious. That distinction will not protect you.

---

## 1. Arrive self-contained

Nothing local should be considered usable.

There is no water, combustible material, edible life, breathable guarantee, or dependable shelter. Apparent channels do not carry water. Mineral “fungus” is not food. Basins are civic shapes, not reservoirs. Rooms may become inaccessible, vanish, enlarge, or cease to be inside the city.

Carry:

- A positive-pressure sealed suit with full-face protection.
- Independent oxygen rather than a filter alone. Elevation tides can reduce available oxygen without warning.
- A heated underlayer and active glove and boot warming.
- A closed drinking system with more water than the temperature suggests. The air will desiccate you even when you do not feel sweaty.
- High-calorie food that can be consumed inside the suit.
- Redundant carbon-dioxide monitoring.
- Eye protection within the helmet in case the outer visor seal is compromised.
- A nonlocal emergency return device, if multiversal travel permits one.

The mineral suspension below the parapets must be assumed dangerous to lungs, eyes, seals, bearings, and electronics. Never open a helmet, even in apparently clear air. Corridors of clarity left by the drifting angular animals are not proof that the air is safe.

Use as little exposed metal as possible. Metal tools are acceptable if insulated, but bare metal will rapidly conduct heat from the hands. Controls should be operable with thick gloves.

## 2. Do not leave your means of return at the entrance

A portal, vehicle, beacon, or gateway fixed only to a local street may cease to share a district with you. It may also remain exactly where it was while the intervening route becomes one-way.

Every traveler should carry an individual extraction capability. If that is impossible, establish several redundant extraction points:

1. One beyond the city’s outermost architectural influence.
2. One on a broad exterior stone shelf.
3. One mobile relay carried by the expedition leader.
4. One emergency relay carried by the rear guard.

Anchor return coordinates to the traveler or to an external universe reference, not to “the third plaza,” a tower, altitude, compass bearing, or measured distance. Those are all mutable properties here.

Do not place the base camp:

- under an arch or lintel,
- inside a room,
- beside a dry channel,
- on a bridge,
- near polished migration scars,
- in one of the ancient sockets where towers settle,
- near the eastern walls,
- or within sight of a tempting “obvious” route upward.

The best available site is a wide, bare shelf outside the organized streets, with multiple unobstructed exit directions and no nearby parapet.

---

## 3. Travel as a small, disciplined team

A team of four is preferable:

- **Lead:** tests terrain and thresholds.
- **Navigator:** records transitions and gravity changes.
- **Observer:** watches architecture, dust, animals, and shadows.
- **Rear guard:** confirms that the path behind still exists.

Do not travel alone. Do not form a large group; additional people create extra shadows, extra threshold crossings, conflicting counts, and more opportunities for someone to describe distance aloud.

Each person must be capable of independent extraction. If one traveler disappears through a changed doorway, the others must not automatically follow. The city can convert one casualty into an entire lost expedition through the ordinary human urge to pursue.

Agree in advance on rescue limits. A person out of sight beyond a nonreciprocal threshold is not to be chased without a verified alternative route.

---

## 4. Use silent communication

Ordinary speech performs poorly here and leaves persistent effects. It may become visible, lodge in rooms, and later enter someone else’s mouth as remembered language.

Use:

- hand signals,
- suit-to-suit text,
- tactile signals through a short contact line,
- icon panels,
- or narrow-beam digital communication.

Do not rely on shouted warnings. A cry may travel only a few paces.

Avoid speaking distances altogether. Do not say “ten meters,” “three streets,” “far,” “closer,” or “we have a long way to go.” If distance increases when named, even casual estimates may alter the route.

Use operational symbols instead:

- **A:** continue,
- **B:** turn around,
- **C:** threshold hazard,
- **D:** altered gravity,
- **E:** extraction now.

Refer to locations by arbitrary symbols rather than spatial descriptions. “Site Violet” is safer than “the upper plaza two kilometers east.”

Do not speak back to old conversations suspended in rooms. Words that appear in your mouth after crossing one of those bands should be treated as environmental contamination, not as memory, prophecy, or instruction. Record them only after leaving.

---

## 5. Navigate topologically, not geographically

A conventional map will rapidly become dangerous. The same tower can remain three streets away regardless of how long you walk. Districts exchange positions. Streets can acquire new avenues. Compass directions may cease to correspond to gravity or previous motion.

Make a route record as a chain of events:

- crossed pale gate,
- passed one lintel animal,
- gravity rotated toward dark wall,
- entered hexagonal square,
- left by violet arcade,
- avoided channel,
- reached bare shelf.

This is more useful than bearings and distances.

Use inertial instruments, but do not trust them exclusively. Carry:

- redundant gyroscopes,
- suit attitude indicators,
- line-of-sight optical markers,
- a mechanical inclinometer,
- disposable test masses on short cords,
- and cameras recording continuously.

A falling object is only a probe, not a definition of down. Nothing falls forever, and some things do not fall downward. Before crossing a gate or entering a new stone district, test orientation with several independent methods.

Do not navigate by the skyline. Do not chase a tower that seems perpetually nearby. Do not assume the visible return route is the route you used.

Physical trail markers may move with the district. If used, give each a timestamp and unique identifier so that a displaced marker is recognizable as displaced rather than reassuring.

---

## 6. Treat every threshold as one-way

Doorways are among the city’s most dangerous features.

A doorway crossed from one direction may not permit return from the opposite side. The direction involved may remain tied to the city rather than to your compass. Therefore:

- Avoid buildings whenever possible.
- Never enter a room merely for shelter.
- Do not cross a threshold unless another exit is visible or individual extraction is available.
- Cross one person at a time.
- Do not pause beneath a lintel.
- Record the direction of crossing using suit-relative inertial data.
- Do not “test” reversibility with a human.

A drone may be used for initial testing, but its successful return does not prove that a person can do the same. The city’s rules may respond to approach, observation, shadow, or intention rather than mass alone.

Lintel animals should be left undisturbed. Do not touch, sample, illuminate at close range, or attempt to remove one. Their contraction when a person passes is a response, even if it is not aggression. If every lintel animal shortens simultaneously, treat that as a citywide alarm.

---

## 7. Do not count casually

Counting appears to be an active interaction.

In particular, never count a staircase twice. The safest procedure is to avoid counting steps at all. If parity is operationally necessary, appoint exactly one designated counter, who counts once and records only **odd** or **even**, not the total. No one else verifies the count.

Do not count colonnade pillars. The sequence is close enough to comprehensible to invite fixation and apparently includes quantities the human visual system cannot stably represent. Trying to resolve the uncountable number may produce distraction at precisely the kind of civic junction where orientation changes.

Do not repeat measurements “to be sure.” In this city, verification may create a second condition rather than confirm the first.

---

## 8. Manage shadows carefully

Rooms with two shadows are larger than their exteriors. Rooms with no shadows are outside the city regardless of their walls. Artificial light may therefore alter more than visibility.

Inside any enclosed space:

- Use one primary light source.
- Avoid multiple lamps casting separate shadows.
- Keep backup lights off until the primary has failed.
- Check the number and direction of shadows before entering.
- If a second shadow appears, stop. Do not walk toward the newly implied space.
- If all shadows disappear, do not assume you have found a safe exit. Activate external-reference navigation or extraction.

A no-shadow room might offer a route beyond the city, but it might equally be “outside” in a sense incompatible with human survival. It is not an emergency shelter.

Interior surfaces sometimes become luminous. If this removes your shadow, withdraw by the last verified route without running.

---

## 9. Avoid edges, bridges, and narrow elevated streets

The ordinary danger of falling is severe enough before accounting for changing gravity.

Near any precipice:

- Remain farther back than feels necessary.
- Stay low.
- Use a body harness and two independent attachment systems.
- Use breakaway couplings so a moving wall or bridge cannot drag the entire team into the abyss.
- Never attach every traveler to a single local anchor.
- Keep lines short enough that a gravity reversal cannot turn them into long pendulums.

Natural-looking bridges are not automatically stable. Their worn central paths show that something has crossed them repeatedly, not that they are safe for humans. Bridges migrate and may leave their anchors while occupied.

The needle districts should be avoided. Streets too narrow to walk comfortably are wholly unsuitable when gravity may rotate at a gate or elevation tide may impair balance.

Unsupported plazas should also be regarded as flight environments. Enter only with personal maneuvering capability or a verified extraction field. A plaza’s lack of visible support may be normal, but “normal” here does not imply that it will continue supporting a human.

## 10. Recognize environmental warnings

### Elevation tide

Signs:

- sudden shortness of breath,
- rapid heart rate,
- narrowing vision,
- a sensation that the sky is closer,
- hairline fractures appearing overhead,
- distant peaks seeming to sink.

Response:

1. Switch immediately to maximum closed-loop life support.
2. Stop climbing.
3. Sit or lie low to prevent falls.
4. Reduce exertion and communication.
5. Prepare for extraction if oxygen or cognition continues to decline.

Do not attempt to “descend” during a tide. The altitude change may not correspond to physical elevation.

### Pressure season

Signs:

- empty rooms becoming difficult to enter,
- increased resistance at doorways without wind,
- a sense of heaviness focused in vacant spaces.

Response:

- Stay outdoors.
- Do not force a door.
- Do not place an arm or tool into the resistance.
- Withdraw from courtyards that are beginning to roof themselves.

An empty room whose emptiness has gained mass may crush, trap, or immobilize without any visible moving surface.

### Absence season

Signs:

- masonry vanishing without debris,
- streets continuing across gaps,
- doors opening onto the backs of walls,
- structures returning misaligned.

Response:

- Treat every new gap as a precipice, even if paving continues across it.
- Do not walk on unsupported continuity.
- Move to broad, visibly continuous stone.
- Extract before the returning structures close around you.

### Passage of the eastern entity

Signs:

- distant districts being concealed in sequence,
- upward-flowing darkness in channels,
- simultaneous contraction of all lintel animals.

Response:

1. Leave the eastern walls and all parapets immediately.
2. Get away from channels and thresholds.
3. Move to a broad open shelf.
4. Crouch and secure yourself to two separate surfaces.
5. Shut off unnecessary lights and active emissions.
6. Do not attempt to photograph, illuminate, signal, or approach the body.
7. Extract if the concealment advances toward your district.

Whether the entity is an animal, a pressure front, or a moving region of different geometry does not matter operationally.

## 11. Do not trust the silence

You may not hear:

- a teammate falling,
- a wall collapsing,
- a bridge detaching,
- an animal moving,
- or a person calling from a few paces away.

Maintain visual or instrument contact at all times. Give every suit a vibration alarm for loss of proximity. Use tactile pulse codes for urgent warnings.

The suppression of sound will also affect morale. People may begin whispering, then shouting, then doubting whether companions are real because they cannot hear expected movement. Train for silent operations beforehand.

A clap producing only pain is not evidence of deafness. Do not repeatedly test hearing.

---

## 12. Do not collect samples during the first visit

The pale wafers, moving dust, crack networks, lintel animals, and violet mineral are all scientifically valuable and operationally unknown.

Do not:

- break stone,
- scrape crystal-filled cracks,
- bottle the moving dust,
- touch lintel animals,
- intercept drifting angular fauna,
- or bring home material shaped like rooms that never existed nearby.

The city rearranges matter and architecture across separated districts. A sample may remain topologically connected to its source or may begin reconstructing absent structures in storage.

If sampling is ever authorized, use an unmanned, disposable mission with quarantine outside the home universe.

After any visit:

- vacuum and encapsulate the outer suit layer,
- do not brush dust into the air,
- isolate all equipment,
- compare pre- and post-visit masses and dimensions,
- review recorded speech anomalies,
- and quarantine personnel until intrusive phrases, orientation disturbances, and false spatial memories have ceased.

Do not take visible speech bands home if they adhere to equipment.

## 13. Set conservative turnaround rules

Do not use a landmark as the turnaround point. Do not use a stated distance. Use consumables and events.

Extract immediately if:

- anyone’s suit pressure falls,
- two independent navigation systems disagree,
- a team member cannot identify the last three route transitions,
- a doorway no longer matches its recording,
- the same landmark appears in two incompatible directions,
- a second shadow appears unexpectedly,
- all shadows vanish,
- dust begins assembling into architecture around the team,
- a channel fills with upward-moving darkness,
- gravity changes twice within one district,
- a teammate speaks words they did not intend,
- or the return relay cannot be contacted.

Do not wait for certainty. The city’s most dangerous changes have rounded edges and may initially feel merely strange.

Use a strict consumables rule: spend no more than one quarter of oxygen, heat reserve, and extraction power on outward travel. Ordinary expedition rules that reserve one third for return are inadequate because the return route may be longer, nonreciprocal, or nonexistent.

## 14. Recommended conduct for a first visit

A responsible first visit would look almost disappointingly modest:

1. Arrive outside the architectural boundary on a broad shelf.
2. Establish multiple externally referenced extraction points.
3. Remain sealed.
4. Observe one complete light cycle from camp.
5. Record dust movement, shadow behavior, elevation effects, and district migration.
6. Enter only the first broad avenue.
7. Avoid all doorways and gates.
8. Visit no more than one open square.
9. Do not count its pillars.
10. Do not touch its basin or plinth.
11. Return by a preplanned alternative route rather than assuming exact retracing is possible.
12. Depart before the dimmest interval, when districts most often exchange places.

Do not seek the highest terrace on an initial expedition. The stair around the absent column is not an invitation. It is a demonstration of the city’s ability to make absence structurally functional.

## Mental preparation

The most difficult adjustment will be accepting that the city is orderly without being stable.

Human beings instinctively treat streets as promises, doors as reversible, walls as stationary, stairs as repeated units, and distance as something that can be checked by naming or measuring it. Here, each of those assumptions can become a physical hazard.

Do not become absorbed in understanding the city while inside it. The place is full of rules that feel one observation away from comprehension. That feeling is itself dangerous. The sequence of pillars, the migrating districts, the old conversations, and the empty plinths will all suggest that one more gate or one more terrace will make the pattern clear.

It will not. The city’s apparent final form is always one terrace higher.

Visit with the expectation of seeing very little, understanding less, and leaving early. In this place, restraint is not cowardice. It is the only form of navigation likely to remain valid.
