An ordinary human should not attempt to enter this city at ordinary size. To its inhabitants, a hand thrust beneath the leaf would be an earthquake, a heat source, a flood of salt and skin oils, and possibly a predator beyond comprehension. Even lifting the roof to look inside could change airflow, admit lethal light, drain condensation, or cause the leaf to dry and contract.

The first preparation, therefore, is **controlled reduction in size**—or a remote visit through a body or probe no larger than a local resident. Reduction must include mass, heat output, breath volume, and footfall energy. Merely shrinking dimensions while retaining human mass would destroy the plant crown.

## Before entering

### Arrange a local guide

Contact the water-keepers or builders before arrival if possible. They understand the plant’s pressure cycles and can tell you:

- which entrance is currently open;
- whether the Still Court is rising;
- where new shoots are expected;
- which streets can bear weight;
- whether predators are active;
- what substances the mother plant is presently reacting to.

Do not rely on a map more than a few hours old. Here, “the road has moved” is often literal. Learn route descriptions based on current conditions: *the white root has turned*, *the lower court is wet*, *the third shell is below the crease*.

If no contact can be established, do not open your own way in. Wait at an existing frost crack or abandoned animal passage and announce yourself with the locally accepted tapping pattern.

### Time the arrival

Enter during a stable interval after drainage, not while the plant is drinking from the marsh. The important warning is the Still Court reaching its third notch. Once the evacuation begins, strangers become a burden and low routes can disappear within minutes.

Avoid:

- flood onset;
- freeze-thaw transitions;
- mass hatchings;
- periods when a salamander is present;
- sudden outside weather changes;
- active emergence of a new ivory shoot.

Arrange extraction by time and pressure condition rather than by clock alone. A portal fixed to a “floor” may be buried, folded shut, or carried several body lengths sideways as the plant swells.

## Biological and chemical quarantine

This city is not merely built on an organism; it exists inside a living negotiation among plant tissue, fungi, algae, microbes, and animals. Human contamination could be disastrous.

Before reduction, undergo thorough decontamination. Bring no ordinary soil, pollen, seeds, fungal spores, mites, or gut waste into the pocket. Wear a sealed, non-shedding suit. The outer surface should be chemically bland and sterilizable without leaving disinfectant residue.

Particularly dangerous contaminants include:

- salt from sweat;
- blood;
- soap, perfume, sunscreen, and insect repellent;
- alcohol and solvents;
- smoke or combustion products;
- copper and other biologically active metals;
- petroleum oils;
- loose synthetic fibers;
- Earth microorganisms;
- food crumbs rich in sugar or fat.

A cut finger at local scale could release enough warm, salty blood to provoke the plant, feed a bloom, foul a channel, and attract scavengers. Carry redundant wound seals and isolate any injury immediately.

Waste must be retained in sealed containers. Never urinate, spit, wash, or discard food in the streets.

## Clothing and life support

Expect cold, saturation, darkness, and constant condensation. Ordinary waterproof clothing is insufficient because sweat cannot evaporate and any exposed moisture may freeze at the margins.

Wear:

- a sealed vapor-barrier underlayer;
- non-absorbent insulation;
- a smooth, low-friction outer suit that will not snag root hairs;
- soft articulated soles that distribute weight;
- waterproof gloves with strong tactile sensitivity;
- eye protection with anti-fog and anti-icing treatment;
- a close-fitting hood rather than loose fabric;
- a quiet closed-circuit breathing system.

The breathing system matters even if the air is technically breathable. Human exhalation is warm, wet, turbulent, and rich in carbon dioxide. At reduced scale it could alter a chamber’s atmosphere or stimulate living surfaces. Use a rebreather that captures carbon dioxide, moisture, odor, and excess heat. It should have no bubbling outlet and no clicking valves.

Carry chemical warming packs only if they are fully sealed and temperature-limited. Better yet, use low-output electrical heating spread over the torso. Never lean against a warm root without permission: these are civic resources, often supporting kitchens, hatcheries, or infirmaries.

Do not use flame under any circumstances.

## Light and navigation

Bring no broad-beam torch. A light bright enough for a human to see comfortably may disorient inhabitants, expose them to predators, heat tissue, trigger heat-folding plants, or stimulate unwanted growth.

Use:

- shielded, very low-power red or near-infrared illumination;
- passive low-light imaging;
- tactile route cords;
- a short probing whisker for detecting spider lines and drop edges;
- local shell and root-hair markings;
- vibration-based navigation calibrated to remain below predator thresholds.

Even infrared should be cleared with local guides. Some organisms respond to heat rather than visible light.

Practice moving in total darkness before the trip. Learn to identify intersections by texture rather than sight: polished scar, grooved vein, white root-hair border, husk scale, wet rind. Your primary navigation instruments should remain functional under condensation and without satellite, magnetic, or visual reference.

## Movement discipline

Walk as though every step is taken on the skin of a sleeping animal—because, in effect, it is.

Keep your center of gravity low. Place the whole foot softly rather than striking with heel or toe. Test surfaces before transferring weight. Do not assume that black leaf-skin is solid; it may cover water, decayed pith, a larval gallery, or a pressurized capillary.

Do not:

- jump;
- run unless directed during an evacuation;
- hammer in anchors;
- cut handholds;
- widen doorways;
- step over marked white hairs;
- brace against walls;
- cross an “empty” avenue casually.

The open spaces are functional. They give new growth somewhere to emerge, allow floodwater to pass, and create sightlines against predators. Camping in one is comparable to pitching a tent in a drainage culvert and fire lane simultaneously.

Use existing grass bridges one person at a time. Clip in only to designated structural fibers. A root that appears strong may be a water line, a growing shoot, or living tissue under tension.

## Sound and vibration

Human voices, tools, and footsteps may travel through the plant much farther than expected. Speak only in a whisper and preferably through close-contact communication inside your suit. Secure every buckle, container, and tool so nothing knocks against anything else.

Bring padded tools rather than hard cases. Disable audible alarms. Haptic warnings should be faint and localized.

Learn the distinction between civic tapping and accidental impact. A loud knock may summon help, panic a district, or attract something that hunts by vibration. If a guide signals stillness, become completely still. Do not ask why until movement is permitted again.

During a salamander incursion, silence is survival. Its head may block an entrance for hours. Do not illuminate it, touch it, feed it, or attempt to “scare it away.” To the city, this is weather with a mouth.

## Water precautions

Assume every wet surface is both a road hazard and part of the city’s infrastructure. A droplet at the local scale can be a deep, adhesive body of water. Surface tension may trap a reduced human more effectively than gravity pulls them free.

Carry:

- a hydrophobic outer coating;
- a compact flotation collar;
- a cutting hook accessible with either hand;
- a capillary-breaking mesh or fine dry cord;
- a sealed emergency air reserve;
- a line managed by a companion.

Never puncture a vein for drinking water. Water extraction is skilled civic work, and taking too much can collapse tissue or alter pressure elsewhere. Accept water only from a keeper-approved source. Treat it for local microbes without releasing sterilants back into the environment.

At the first evacuation signal, follow residents upward. Do not stop to retrieve equipment. Flooding here is not merely water entering a chamber: walls swell, passages constrict, floors soften, and hidden organisms emerge from the pith. A route that was open on the way in may cease to exist behind you.

## Wildlife

Most animals will not behave like familiar large animals. They live by touch, vibration, moisture, heat, and chemical traces.

Check for nearly invisible spider lines before entering warm passages. Do not brush springtail clusters aside; their sudden movement can spread alarm and may make footing treacherous. Avoid larvae visible as shadows within the leaf—stepping over them may rupture both animal and wall. Give snails the right of way on vertical routes, especially before a freeze or pressure change.

Do not use insecticide, repellent, glue traps, ultrasonic deterrents, or electric shock devices. A defensive tool should be mechanical, quiet, and highly localized: a soft barrier, shield, or guide-approved thorn pole. Killing even a small predator may change local ecology, release fluids into the plant, and create a scavenger gathering.

If something attaches to the suit, do not crush it against yourself. Enclose it, let a guide remove it, and inspect for punctures.

## Etiquette and trade

The inhabitants’ concept of ownership is likely conditional and temporal. Do not ask to buy a building or claim a campsite. Ask whether a place may be occupied *during the present state of the plant*.

Respect water rights, growth corridors, evacuation routes, fungal boundaries, and warm-root access. Never scrape away fungus merely because it looks hazardous; doorway colonies are maintained carefully and may serve as light, signage, food, or environmental indicators.

Useful gifts should be small, inert, and requested in advance. Appropriate possibilities include:

- tiny ceramic blades;
- chemically stable glass needles;
- non-corroding tool edges;
- sterile thread;
- quiet measuring instruments;
- magnifying optics;
- sealed cold-light markers.

Metal is valuable, but introducing the wrong metal could poison tissue or transform local power relations. Do not distribute steel hooks or needles casually. Give tools through whatever communal authority manages scarce materials.

Do not take shell markers, beetle cases, survey cords, resin crumbs, or “abandoned” pieces. In darkness, objects that appear decorative may be road signs, flood gauges, structural wedges, or inherited district tools.

## Emergency plan

Travel with at least one companion and a local guide. Establish:

1. a high refuge above the third-notch flood level;
2. two extraction points attached to stable external references, not only local tissue;
3. a silent distress signal;
4. a lost-traveler protocol;
5. procedures for suit puncture, immersion, freezing, constriction, and predator lockdown.

Your portal or return mechanism must tolerate movement. It should either track the living substrate without anchoring deeply into it or remain outside the pocket with a flexible transit link. Never leave a rigid gate braced between two walls; when the plant swells, it may become a wedge that tears the city apart.

If trapped by constricting tissue, do not cut immediately. Cutting can release sap, alter pressure, and trigger further contraction. Reduce body profile, stop producing heat and vibration, and allow local builders to determine which surface can safely be opened.

If evacuation fails, move to an approved high platform, seal your suit, tether yourself, and wait through the pressure cycle. Expect darkness, crowding, warmth, and living walls pressing close. Panic and forceful movement will endanger everyone around you.

## The proper attitude

Do not think of this as exploring a miniature ruin or a whimsical city beneath a leaf. It is a young settlement inside a cold, reactive organism, surrounded by animals for whom its citizens are prey. Its apparent fragility is matched by extraordinary adaptation, and its emptiness is intentional.

Move slowly. Touch little. Ask before taking water, heat, space, or light. Accept that addresses are temporary, streets are seasonal, and the city you leave will not be exactly the city you entered.

When the tools begin tapping again after the flood, resist the urge to interpret the sound as a return to normal. Change is the normal condition here. Your task as a visitor is not to overcome it, but to pass through without giving the plant—or anything hunting through it—a reason to remember you.
