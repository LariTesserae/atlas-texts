Prepare for this town as you would for a working industrial district, a wetland, and a greenhouse at the same time. It is not especially hostile, but nearly everything is damp, moving, slippery, electrically suspect, or part of somebody’s livelihood.

## Before crossing over

Arrange a local contact if possible: a market porter, workshop clerk, canal keeper, or lodging-house proprietor. Addresses are likely to depend on landmarks—trees, pumps, bridges, yards, and channels—rather than durable street numbers. A guide will also know which galleries are public, which narrow tracks carry carts, and which apparently harmless drains can rise without warning.

Confirm:

- the current rain and flood conditions;
- shift-change times, when lanes and rail tracks become crowded;
- whether your lodging is above the historical flood marks;
- what currency or barter goods are accepted;
- local rules for photography, factory access, and collecting plants;
- whether your biology is compatible with local food, medicines, spores, and microbes.

If you have multiversal medical screening, use it. A place can look biologically familiar while containing unfamiliar molds, plant toxins, waterborne organisms, or allergens. Do not assume that clear water, recognizable fruit, or a familiar medicinal leaf is safe for you.

## What to wear

Choose light, breathable clothing that dries quickly, but do not expose too much skin. The main problems will be abrasion, plant sap, oily water, hot pipes, insects, and contaminated cuts rather than dramatic animal attacks.

Wear:

- quick-drying long trousers;
- a loose long-sleeved shirt;
- a brimmed rain hat rather than an umbrella;
- thin work gloves;
- closed footwear with excellent wet traction;
- gaiters or boots high enough for ankle-deep water.

Do not bring smooth-soled city shoes. Stone, algae-dark brick, wet timber, metal bridge plates, and crushed husks will all be slick in different ways. Heavy waterproof boots may become unbearably hot and can fill from above; breathable wetland boots or secure draining shoes may be more practical unless floodwater is polluted.

Avoid dangling scarves, loose coat hems, jewelry, uncovered long hair, and shoulder straps that can catch in belts, carts, hooks, vines, or low pipework. In active yards, tie back hair and remove anything that might be drawn into machinery.

A compact rain cape is useful outdoors, but never wear loose poncho fabric near shafts, conveyors, or flywheels.

## Essential equipment

Pack for persistent moisture, not merely rainfall.

Bring:

- several small roll-top dry bags rather than one large bag;
- waterproof document and money pouches;
- silica gel or another regenerable desiccant;
- a sealed flashlight or headlamp;
- spare batteries in individual waterproof wraps;
- a walking pole for testing submerged footing;
- a refillable bottle with a reliable purifier;
- oral rehydration salts;
- antiseptic, waterproof dressings, blister care, and antifungal treatment;
- any prescribed medicine in sealed duplicate supplies;
- hearing protection;
- eye protection for workshops;
- a respirator capable of filtering fine plant dust, mold, smoke, and fibers;
- a nonconductive whistle;
- a paper notebook and soft pencil;
- a local map protected under clear film.

Protect advanced electronics. Warm condensation may form inside devices when you move between exposed sunlight, shaded workshops, and cooler storage rooms. Keep equipment sealed until it reaches ambient temperature. Assume that open charging points are damp, poorly grounded, and subject to voltage fluctuations. Use isolated converters and surge protection, or rely on your own power supply.

Leave drones and conspicuous scanning devices behind unless specifically authorized. The canopy, overhead wires, steam, narrow courtyards, and private industrial spaces make them both unsafe and socially intrusive.

## Health precautions

Drink only treated water. The channels are industrial infrastructure as well as drainage: even apparently clean flows may contain tannins, oils, fermentation products, plant alkaloids, boiler residue, or sewage from farther upstream. Do not wash cuts in them.

Clean even minor scratches promptly. Wet brick, rusty fittings, cane fibers, and bark splinters can produce small wounds that remain damp and become infected. Keep your feet clean and dry whenever you reach lodging. Carry a second pair of socks and change them during the day.

Wear respiratory protection around:

- bark stripping;
- fiber beating;
- leaf drying;
- moldy storage;
- resin heating;
- pulp or starch dust;
- husk-fired boilers.

“Natural” industry does not mean harmless industry. Saps, gums, dyes, wood dust, fermentation gases, and essential oils can irritate or poison. Never lean over an open vat to smell it. Avoid low enclosed rooms, covered tanks, and sumps: carbon dioxide, methane, hydrogen sulfide, steam, or oxygen deficiency may collect there.

Heat illness may arrive quietly because the air is not scorching. Drink regularly, replace salts, and rest in moving air. Sweating will be less effective under the canopy’s humidity.

Mosquito control appears competent, but use repellent anyway, particularly near the outskirts at dusk. Do not touch oil-treated sumps or disturb the small fish used for control.

## Moving through town

Walk slowly. The town’s hazards are mostly ordinary things in unexpected places:

- a submerged edge beside a lane;
- a narrow-gauge cart approaching behind machinery noise;
- a bridge plank polished by wet feet;
- a low pipe at head height;
- a hot condensate line that looks like an ordinary railing;
- a root hump beneath standing water;
- a floor opening hidden by fiber or leaves;
- a load moving overhead.

Treat embedded rails as active even when no cart is visible. Step across them rather than along them, and never stand between a cart and a wall. If workers begin signaling sharply or blowing whistles, stop and look for direction instead of continuing.

Do not use headphones. Hearing the pumps, bells, belts, relief valves, and approaching carts is part of staying safe. In especially loud workshops, wear hearing protection but remain where you can see the workers’ hand signals.

Use handrails cautiously; some may be hot, electrically live, weakened by concealed rust, or simply wet. Test unfamiliar stairs before committing your weight. Do not trust mats of duckweed: they may disguise a deep channel.

An umbrella will be awkward under cables, galleries, vines, and awnings. It may also dump water onto goods or into somebody’s workspace. A hat and fitted rain layer are better.

## Learn the town’s water etiquette

Water management is the settlement’s civic order. Never obstruct a gutter, culvert, drain mouth, channel, spillway, or narrow bridge, even briefly. Do not place your bag on a grating. Do not discard peelings or paper into flowing water. A small blockage can flood a workshop or divert contaminated runoff into someone’s yard.

If you notice water rising, changing color, carrying fresh foam, or flowing in the wrong direction, tell a local immediately. Likewise report a stopped pump or an unfamiliar silence from machinery that had been operating steadily. Residents may recognize such changes as early warnings.

Before entering a ground-floor room, note the flood-height pole and the nearest upward route. During heavy rain, follow local behavior rather than your own intuition. People may evacuate valuables before persons because they know the water will rise slowly—or abandon a lane that still looks passable because they know a gate upstream is about to open.

Do not wade through moving, opaque, or warm industrial water. If flooding reaches electrical switches, motors, or cables, assume the water is energized.

## Factory conduct

This is a town of patient processes. Do not touch, rearrange, taste, or “help with” anything laid out to soak, dry, settle, season, ferment, or thicken. A tray that appears neglected may represent several days of controlled work. A bundle in a channel may be deliberately weighted there. Even fallen leaves may be sorted material.

Ask before crossing a workshop yard. Stay within marked passages and behind guards. Old equipment may operate more safely than it appears, but its safety depends on workers anticipating every movement around it.

Never:

- step over a moving belt or shaft;
- reach through mesh;
- rest against tanks or pipes;
- enter a boiler room uninvited;
- stand beneath suspended loads;
- smoke near fibers, oils, dust, resins, or drying sheds;
- use an open flame;
- assume an idle machine has been disconnected.

Workers’ voices may not carry. Learn a few basic hand signals from your host: stop, wait, danger, permission to cross, and move aside. If uncertain, make eye contact and wait for an unmistakable invitation.

The shift bell will affect traffic. When it sounds, move to the side of the lane and let workers and carts pass before attempting to navigate.

## Social behavior

The settlement is practical rather than picturesque to the people who maintain it. Admire its ingenuity, but do not describe active workshops as ruins or the town as having been “reclaimed by nature.” The trees, vines, roots, channels, buildings, and machines are managed infrastructure, not accidental overgrowth.

Ask permission before photographing:

- workers;
- proprietary equipment;
- production vats and recipes;
- private yards screened by hedges;
- flood records;
- medicinal goods.

Do not pick plants. A vine on a pole may be a crop, a shade screen, a boundary, or part of a stabilization system. Moss, floating plants, fallen branches, and gutter growth may also be harvested or monitored.

Expect conversation to happen at close range. Speak briefly and clearly, and do not be offended if workers rely on gestures. In quieter market spaces, take time to ask what a product is and how it should be used. Do not ingest unfamiliar oils, powders, medicinal leaves, dyes, or resins merely because they are plant-derived.

Useful gifts include corrosion-resistant hand tools, quality needles, waterproof labels, sealed pencils, durable cord, gasket material, or accurate small measuring instruments—provided local law permits technology transfer. Avoid introducing seeds, live plants, insects, untreated wood, or soil. In this ecology, an invasive organism could be catastrophic.

## Food and lodging

Choose food from busy stalls where ingredients are kept raised, covered, and separated from drainage water. Cooked starches, steamed foods, and freshly prepared dishes are safer than raw leaves or cut fruit washed in local water. Ask about fermented foods: some may be perfectly safe for locals but biologically unfamiliar to you.

A good lodging should have:

- sleeping rooms above ground level;
- mosquito screens;
- cross-ventilation or a fan;
- secure dry storage;
- a visible flood plan;
- drinking water kept separately from utility water;
- an escape route that does not depend on a single wooden stair.

Never leave baggage on the floor. Hang it or place it on a rack. Inspect bedding for dampness and dry your footwear overnight. Keep doors and windows screened at dusk, but do not seal a room so tightly that mold and fumes accumulate.

## Navigation and communication

The canopy will interfere with celestial orientation, aerial landmarks, and possibly interdimensional or satellite signals. Chimneys and tanks are useful landmarks but may disappear behind foliage after a single turn. Record your route as a sequence of practical features:

> market grove — bronze bell yard — double-pump crossing — fig-root bridge — blue resin shed.

Mark safe high ground and broad bridges on your map. If carrying a return beacon, place it above historical flood height, clear of moving machinery, and away from power cables or large metal tanks that might interfere with it. Tell your host where it is. A mysterious sealed device attached to communal infrastructure is likely to be removed, repaired, or incorporated into something else.

Have a physical rendezvous plan in case communications fail.

## Emergencies

The most likely emergencies are falls, infected cuts, heat illness, industrial injury, sudden drainage failure, fire in dry plant material, or localized flooding.

If you hear an unfamiliar alarm:

1. Stop moving.
2. Look to workers and follow their signals.
3. Move out of rail lines and away from machinery.
4. Do not descend to retrieve possessions.
5. Avoid channels, boiler rooms, and electrical equipment.
6. Head toward known high masonry or an upper gallery only if locals do so.

In a fire, remember that wet surroundings do not make the town fireproof. Dried fiber, oil, resin, husks, timber, and dust can burn fiercely, while narrow lanes and overhead pipes complicate escape. Move crosswind if possible and avoid smoke trapped beneath the canopy.

If lost, do not wander into quieter industrial yards. Return to a staffed market stall, bridge keeper, pump station, or bell yard. These are likely to be better-known civic reference points than official offices.

## How to experience the place well

Give yourself more time than the map suggests. A town only a few streets across may take hours to traverse because routes bend around channels, active loads, private yards, and flooded sections. Do not schedule the visit as a quick stop.

The best introduction would be:

1. arrive after full daylight, not at dawn or dusk;
2. meet a local guide at the dry edge of the market;
3. learn the bell and hand signals;
4. walk the central lanes before entering any workshop;
5. visit one industry slowly rather than many superficially;
6. finish at the marsh edge before sunset;
7. return while landmarks are still visible.

Stand occasionally in a safe recess and listen. The town’s machinery is not background noise so much as a working map: pumps identify the low ground, carts announce the transport lanes, bells organize time, and changes in the sound of water tell residents what the weather is doing.

Come prepared to be damp, to wait, and to ask before acting. The settlement rewards attention. Its apparent disorder is a highly maintained compromise among roots, masonry, labor, water, and time—and a considerate traveler should move through it as one more temporary load the system has agreed to carry.
