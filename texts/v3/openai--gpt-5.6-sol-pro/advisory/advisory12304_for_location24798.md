## Best way to visit

Treat the Under-Court Reservoir as a functioning municipal utility, not as a swimming place. An ordinary traveler should arrange the visit through its keepers and enter on a scheduled cleaning day, after the gates have been locked shut and the chamber drained. This is by far the safest option, and it reveals the constructed “landscape” more clearly: settling steps, ceramic fins, grit pits, patched plaster, pipe collars, and the central dividing ridge.

A submerged visit is an overhead-environment dive. There is no internal water surface at which to breathe, and the glowing light shafts are capped with glass, not exits. Warm, shallow-looking water does not make the chamber safe.

## Before arriving

- **Secure permission.** The water feeds public baths or other town facilities. Unauthorized entry will be regarded as contamination or sabotage.
- **Learn the gate schedule.** Avoid the morning draw entirely. Request a period of balanced or reduced flow, with all relevant gate ropes physically secured against movement.
- **Ask about access.** Use the maintenance hatch or stair, never a light shaft, inflow pipe, or outlet.
- **Confirm the valve condition.** The southern valve’s usual pattern—three knocks, pause, one—is operational information. If it stops unexpectedly, leave immediately.
- **Arrange a keeper escort.** At least one operator should remain above at the gates, and another should supervise the access point.
- **Do not depend on local workers as diving rescuers.** Their divers may be highly experienced in this particular chamber, but their equipment is simple. Bring support appropriate to your own physiology and technology.

If local authorities object to unfamiliar equipment, choose the drained visit rather than improvising.

## For a drained visit

Wear:

- clean, soft-soled footwear with good grip;
- a light helmet without exposed metal edges;
- gloves suitable for wet brick and sharp tile;
- eye protection against falling lime grit;
- simple clothing that can get wet and warm.

Expect oppressive humidity, strong echoes, and slippery films near the shafts. Do not step into circular grit pits without probing them, and do not trust low ridges as convenient walkways: many are narrow, glazed, or designed to direct residual flow. Keep away from unsupported timber braces and repaired pipework. Fresh lime can irritate skin and eyes.

Even drained, the reservoir should be treated as a confined space. Confirm ventilation before descending. Water removal does not guarantee breathable air; gases can collect in low chambers, and the normal water-filled condition means the room was not designed for human occupancy. Carry an atmosphere monitor if your travel kit permits it.

## For a submerged visit

An ordinary untrained traveler should not attempt one. If the experience is essential, prepare as for a short cave or wreck penetration, despite the reservoir’s modest size.

Use:

- a full-face mask or securely retained regulator;
- redundant breathing gas, including a genuinely independent bailout supply;
- a continuous guideline fixed at the proper maintenance entrance;
- a harness with no dangling straps;
- two compact lights, despite the daylight shafts;
- clean thermal protection appropriate to skin-warm water;
- smooth gloves and knee protection;
- a cutting tool tethered close to the body;
- a low-profile helmet if it will not interfere with breathing equipment;
- direct communications with the surface team, if available.

Surface-supplied air is preferable only if the hose can be routed without crossing gates, grates, fins, braces, or currents. Otherwise it becomes an entanglement line. Conventional open-circuit equipment may also introduce oils, corrosion products, or loose components that the keepers will not accept. All gear should be scrubbed, rinsed, and approved before entry.

Do not wear or carry:

- jewelry;
- unsecured cameras;
- coins, keys, beads, or charms;
- oil-treated leather;
- loose weights;
- trailing fabric;
- magnetic or adhesive markers that could remain behind;
- chemicals, soaps, insect repellents, or skin oils likely to foul the water.

Cover open wounds with waterproof dressings. Do not drink the water merely because it is filtered and clear.

## Timing the immersion

The least hazardous submerged period is likely around noon, when the gates are nearly balanced and broad circulation replaces the stronger morning draw. Even then, obtain a direct confirmation from the keeper; do not infer conditions from the hour alone.

Avoid:

- **Morning:** the western outlets draw strongly, creating the greatest entrapment risk.
- **Any gate movement:** pressure changes can be abrupt and affect the entire room.
- **Cleaning transitions:** water level changes can expose obstacles, disturb silt, or create suction through partially opened routes.
- **A sudden cessation of the valve knock:** evacuate at once.
- **Periods of poor sunlight:** the bright shafts are useful orientation references, though never exits.

Establish hand, line, and emergency signals beforehand. Underwater speech and sound direction will be unreliable because the roar travels through water and masonry. A noise that seems nearby may be conducted from another wall.

## Hazards inside

### Outlets and currents

The bronze-grated western mouths are the principal danger. A grate prevents a person from entering a pipe, but it does not prevent a limb, hose, garment, or line from being pinned against it. Stay well clear, especially during draw periods.

The incoming eastern jets can roll or disorient a swimmer. Do not cross directly in front of them. The angled slabs beneath them produce complex downward flow, and their bubble clouds may obscure the wall.

Slots, fins, and low partitions can catch equipment. Do not squeeze through openings simply because they appear wide enough.

### No breathable refuge

The silver pockets beneath bricks are trapped bubbles, not usable air spaces. They may contain stale air or gases released from masonry and water. Never remove a regulator to test one.

Likewise, swimming upward toward a luminous shaft will end at thick glass. Learn the route back to the maintenance entrance before moving far into the chamber.

### Heat and exertion

Skin-temperature water feels comfortable at first but can promote overheating during exertion, especially in a drysuit or heavy protective garment. Use minimal appropriate insulation, move slowly, and keep the visit short. Dehydration is still possible underwater; drink beforehand, but not excessively.

### Brightness and visual distortion

At midday the glazed floor may produce severe glare. Bring a mask with a lightly tinted or glare-reducing lens if this does not compromise low-light vision. Distances will appear larger, and partitions may resemble drop-offs. Use the guideline and known masonry features rather than visual intuition alone.

Fine silt in the settling troughs can quickly erase the chamber’s famous clarity. Maintain careful buoyancy and avoid kicking near the floor.

### Sound

Expect the chamber to be physically loud. The inflows, valve knocking, grains crossing tile, and your own breathing will all be magnified. Hearing protection must not interfere with pressure equalization or communications. Do not use sound alone to locate the exit.

### Surfaces

The structure is new and therefore sharp. Tile edges, ceramic fins, tool marks, and unfinished repairs can cut skin or hoses. Do not pull on pipe collars, timber wedges, reeds, or mortar seams. The pale reeds beneath the central shaft are water-quality instruments, not decoration; disturbing them could force the keepers to test or flush the entire system.

## Conduct during the visit

Follow the reservoir’s own rules:

1. Nothing enters untethered.
2. No gate moves while a person is inside.
3. No one approaches the central or western grates during active draw.
4. No scraping, collecting, feeding, or rearranging.
5. No bubbles should be deliberately trapped beneath masonry.
6. Do not touch the green films or moss merely to examine them.
7. If normal sound or current changes, turn back immediately.
8. If visibility deteriorates, follow the line out rather than seeking a light shaft.
9. Keep the party small; two visitors and one qualified guide are ample.
10. Account for every tool and object before the system is returned to service.

Move gently. The reservoir’s visual effect comes from clarity, bright tile, and disciplined flow. A few careless fin strokes can turn the first settling trough into a brown cloud.

## Personal preparation

Before a submerged attempt, be competent in:

- mask clearing and regulator recovery;
- zero-visibility line following;
- gas sharing in a confined space;
- entanglement release;
- buoyancy control without touching the floor;
- responding to current and loss of primary light;
- calm navigation when the apparent “up” direction is not an exit.

Claustrophobic travelers should choose the drained visit. The chamber is bright, but brightness does not lessen the psychological effect of being completely enclosed by water and masonry. The constant bodily roar can intensify panic.

Afterward, rinse yourself and all equipment away from the reservoir. Report any contact with plaster, reeds, grates, or repairs, however minor. The keepers care less about blame than about knowing what may have entered or shifted within their system.

Most importantly, do not arrive expecting a buried lake. Prepare for a machine large enough to enter: warm, radiant, beautiful, and entirely indifferent to the fact that a human body cannot breathe inside it.
