## General assessment

Treat this world not as a forest but as a **living, saturated, three-dimensional continent with no dependable ground**. Its chief dangers are unlikely to be dramatic predators. They are structural failure, disorientation, humidity, contaminated air and water, slow flooding, unnoticed biological exposure, and the temptation to mistake something ancient and load-bearing for an ordinary tree.

An ordinary human should visit only as part of a supported expedition. A first excursion should last hours, not days, and remain within a secured return corridor.

## Before crossing

### Verify the atmosphere

Do not infer safety from the presence of familiar-looking leaves or animals. Send sterile instruments first and continuously measure:

- Oxygen and carbon dioxide
- Carbon monoxide, methane, hydrogen sulfide, and fermentation gases
- Airborne spores, pollen, microbes, and volatile plant compounds
- Temperature and humidity at several heights
- Pressure and gas composition inside hollows and root chambers

The open air may be breathable while depressions, towers, shafts, and under-mat galleries are not. Warm gases rising from decay can displace oxygen or carry toxins. Flowering events may also change the air abruptly.

Even if preliminary readings are favorable, wear a **positive-pressure respirator** with an independent emergency air supply. Unknown pollen should be regarded as both an allergen and a possible bioactive substance.

### Use strict biological quarantine

This ecosystem appears ancient, interconnected, and efficient at incorporating foreign material. Do not introduce Earth soil, seeds, fungi, insects, food scraps, untreated wood, or ordinary clothing fibers.

Everything crossing should be:

- Sterilized without leaving persistent toxins
- Smooth-surfaced and easy to decontaminate
- Double-contained during transit
- Inspected for attached spores and microscopic organisms on return

Do not bring anything home casually—not water, seedpods, bark, fungal cords, shed feathers, or dirt from a boot seam. “Dead” material here may be dormant, symbiotic, or only one stage of a distributed organism.

## Personal equipment

Wear a lightweight sealed field suit designed for heat rather than cold. It should include:

- Active cooling and moisture management
- Positive-pressure hood or full-face respirator
- Eye protection against sap, spores, and fine root hairs
- Cut-resistant but flexible gloves
- Soft, broad-soled footwear with removable sterile overshoes
- A full-body climbing harness worn at all times
- Redundant tether points
- A flotation layer or compact buoyancy aid
- Chemical and biological exposure sensors
- A personal inertial tracker
- Several days of sealed water and concentrated food, even for a short trip
- Antihistamines, epinephrine, broad first-aid supplies, and treatment for heat illness
- A compact emergency breathing cylinder
- A nonflammable thermal bivouac

Avoid spikes, crampons, pitons, machetes, and anything intended to bite deeply into the terrain. Such tools may sever vascular structures, release irritants, or turn a stable surface into a tear.

Keep every item physically attached to you. A dropped tool may fall through several ecological levels, vanish into mulch, or become inaccessible while remaining an enduring contaminant.

## How to move

### Assume every surface is suspended

The upper layer may resemble soil but behaves more like a wet roof stretched over unknown voids. Probe ahead with a broad, blunt staff equipped with pressure and acoustic sensors. A taut, resonant area is not necessarily strong; resonance may indicate a large hollow beneath it.

Travel roped in small teams, but never attach the whole group to a single natural anchor. Use distributed, nonpenetrating slings around several separate trunks or roots. Since apparently separate supports may belong to one organism—or one failing mat—anchors should also be separated by direction and layer.

Cross questionable areas with weight spread widely:

- One person at a time
- Low center of gravity
- No jumping
- No tightly grouped loads
- No camping on taut mats, fresh folds, waterlogged terraces, or over buried groaning

If the land begins to vibrate or emit a descending wooden groan, stop. Do not run. Spread weight, clip into independent anchors, and retreat one person at a time after the movement ends. A local settlement of only a few centimeters may rupture hidden channels or initiate a larger collapse nearby.

### Think vertically

A “clearing” may be an unfinished bridge over a gulf. A marsh may occupy the crown of a tree. A river may pass below, within, or above your route. Mark every position by:

- Horizontal coordinates
- Elevation
- Canopy or substrate layer
- Distance and direction from the return line
- Structural description of the supporting growth

A conventional map will be dangerously incomplete.

Use a continuous physical or electronic breadcrumb system. Inertial navigation, short-range ranging beacons, and tightly spaced relay nodes are preferable to satellite assumptions. Radio may be weakened unpredictably by water-rich vegetation; sound will not carry far enough for dependable signaling. Use vibration or wired-line alarms as backups.

Do not follow waterways as if they were roads. They disappear into roots, divide without warning, and may end in shafts or hidden drainage seams.

### Expect routes to close

Vines may noticeably occupy disturbed gaps even during a visit, and wet foliage can descend under accumulated water. Never allow the only return route to depend on one bridge, branch, or opening. Maintain two extraction paths where possible.

For any expedition longer than a day, the portal or return vehicle should be placed on a surveyed, load-distributing platform with independent suspension. Do not simply set it on the leaf-felt and assume it will remain at the same elevation.

## Water, food, and camp

Drink none of the local water without laboratory analysis and multistage treatment. Clear droplets may contain dissolved alkaloids, spores, symbionts, animal larvae, or mineral concentrations unsuitable for humans. Warm tea-colored streams are especially biologically active.

Eat no local organisms. Familiar shapes—reeds, fruits, mushrooms, tubers—are meaningless evidence of compatibility. Do not taste sap to identify a plant.

If camping is unavoidable, choose a well-aerated, structurally surveyed ridge rather than a basin or enclosed chamber. Use a suspended sleeping platform distributing weight across multiple supports. Establish gas monitors both at face level and below the camp, because dangerous gases may pool in hollows under the mat.

Never use open flame. The visible forest is wet, but buried fermentation gases, dry internal fibers, pollen clouds, or oxygen-rich pockets could still ignite. A deep fire in the rootwork might be impossible to extinguish and ecologically catastrophic.

Waste must be sealed and removed, including human waste, wash water, filters, and shed medical dressings.

## Weather precautions

Humidity will be nearly continuous. Humans may overheat even at modest temperatures because sweat cannot evaporate. Active cooling is essential; simply drinking more water will not prevent heat illness.

Treat all four forms of rain as operational weather:

- **Sky rain** reduces visibility and increases surface loading.
- **Canopy rain** may begin long after the sky clears.
- **Upward rain** can wet equipment from below and conceal gas movement.
- **Flowering rain** may carry unusually concentrated pollen or volatile compounds.

If leaves begin reversing their surfaces, animals descend, and ambient light steadily thickens, leave. These are better storm warnings than the sky. A month-long rain event could make extraction impossible and transform a safe ridge into a shallow wetland.

After major rain begins, do not enter tunnels or descend beneath root mats. Water may rise from below rather than arrive from upstream.

## Animals

Most animals described are not overtly aggressive, but that should not be confused with harmlessness. Move slowly and do not touch apparently inert bark, moss, or lichen; it may be a resting colony.

Give broad-backed canopy grazers an enormous margin. Their ordinary movement may deform the supporting terrain or redirect water. Never stand downhill or directly beneath one.

Pale gliders may approach silently on updrafts. Secure loose equipment and use low-intensity perimeter sensors rather than loud deterrents. Noise may panic animals into damaging the structure supporting you.

Check suit seams frequently for burrowers, soft-shelled grazers, eggs, and threadlike organisms. Do not crush attached creatures against the suit; isolate them under a clear containment patch and remove them during decontamination.

## The towers and other apparent structures

Approach the seven towers as both **archaeological sites and living organs**. Do not assume that an absence of tool marks means an absence of intention.

Before entry:

1. Monitor gas exchange for at least one full local day.
2. Map airflow and structural vibration remotely.
3. Confirm that the fibrous floor carries distributed human weight.
4. Install a retrieval line anchored outside the structure.
5. Record the slow pulse of warm air from the shaft and determine whether it is changing.

Do not descend the central shafts on an initial expedition. They may be respiratory vents, drainage organs, access routes into kilometers of unstable compost, animal passages, or parts of an architecture whose purpose is still active. The seasonal pulse suggests that conditions below can change on a timescale no human observer will readily recognize.

Avoid touching the black seedpod plaques, clearing alcoves, tracing grooves with bare instruments, or cutting samples from repeated patterns. If these are inscriptions, reproductive structures, sensory tissues, or ecological control surfaces, physical sampling could be both destructive and dangerous.

Photogrammetry, chemical readings taken from the air, and noncontact imaging should come before excavation of any kind.

## Psychological preparation

The environment is likely to distort human judgment. There is little horizon, weak daylight, muffled sound, ambiguous scale, and no stable distinction between building, organism, and terrain. Travelers may feel sheltered while standing over a valley-sized cavity, or feel stationary while the supporting layer subtly moves.

Expect:

- Loss of time sense in the green dusk
- Claustrophobia despite immense spaces
- Vertigo when an apparent floor is understood to be canopy
- Anxiety caused by infrasonic groans and vibrations
- Difficulty estimating distance through mist
- A strong impulse to interpret recurring patterns as either obviously artificial or obviously natural

Neither conclusion is justified. Keep observational logs separate from interpretations. Use scheduled check-ins, artificial day-night lighting, and explicit turnaround times.

## Conduct

The safest etiquette is to assume that the plateau is not scenery but a **world-sized community of living systems**, some potentially sentient on timescales or spatial scales humans do not naturally recognize.

Therefore:

- Cut nothing unless required to save a life.
- Do not leave markers embedded in growth.
- Do not drain pools or redirect streams.
- Do not imitate recurring patterns as an experiment.
- Do not trigger flowering, heat vents, or air pulses.
- Do not take “souvenirs.”
- Walk around small organisms rather than assuming they are expendable.
- Record changes caused by your own weight and presence.

A footprint here fills with water and closes, but that does not mean it was unnoticed.

## Recommended first visit

The first human visit should be limited to a sterilized four-person team, supported by remote observers, with no overnight stay. Establish a platform beside the crossing point, survey a radius of only a few hundred meters, and spend most of the time studying load-bearing behavior, air chemistry, and local biological responses.

The most important rule is simple:

**Never go farther into the plateau than your secured route back.**

Its apparent stillness is the product of immense, continuous activity. Nothing is hurried, but everything is being incorporated. A human who becomes lost, trapped, or merely left behind may not encounter a dramatic end. The forest will soften equipment, fill spaces, grow across paths, and receive the traveler layer by layer—making the accident, in time, part of the ground.
