## Preparing to visit the shifting basin town

Treat this as a high-altitude, cold-wet expedition into a living hydraulic structure—not as a visit to a conventional town. The greatest dangers are unlikely to be dramatic avalanches or attacks. They are glare, hypoxia, silent flooding, unstable footing, cold water inside clothing, and the false assumption that a route or room will remain where you found it.

Above all, arrange for a local guide before entering the basin. A map will become obsolete; a resident’s reading of crabs, light, stone pressure, and drainage is far more valuable.

### Before transit

**Acclimatize if your physiology requires oxygen.** Spend several days at increasing altitude before crossing the pass. Carry supplemental oxygen if you have any history of altitude illness, but use a quiet, nonventing system if possible. Headache, clumsiness, confusion, and unusual fatigue are especially dangerous here because movement requires constant balance and spatial judgment.

Have your gateway or return point established **outside the basin**, preferably on stable bedrock below the pass. Do not anchor it to a building, stair, ice wall, or central plaza. The town physically migrates, and its unusual water behavior may interfere with machinery, coordinates, and causal timing.

Leave a travel plan with someone beyond the pass. Agree on return windows rather than an exact minute; local routes can disappear within moments.

### Clothing and equipment

Wear layers suited to simultaneous cold, wet, wind, and intense reflected sunlight:

- A close-fitting moisture-wicking base layer
- Warm insulation that retains heat when damp
- A flexible waterproof outer shell without dangling straps
- Waterproof gloves with excellent grip and removable liners
- Soft, high-traction boots with insulated soles
- Gaiters or tightly sealed trouser cuffs
- Spare socks and gloves in double waterproof bags
- A warm hood rather than a loose hat likely to fall into a drain

Avoid rigid crampons in the settlement unless a guide directs otherwise. They can damage polished channels, slip on bare wet stone, and catch while climbing through narrow slots. Carry compact microspikes that can be removed quickly.

The glare may be incapacitating. Bring glacier-rated eye protection with side shields and a spare pair. A face covering and high-SPF broad-spectrum sunscreen are essential; UV exposure will be severe even in cold weather and shade.

Pack small. A conventional frame backpack may wedge in passages or strike roofs, nests, and stacked stones. Use a narrow, soft-sided pack that can be passed hand to hand. Secure every tool. Dropped equipment may vanish into a drain, contaminate a cistern, or alter a delicately balanced wall.

Useful items include:

- A headlamp with red and low-output settings
- A compact thermal bivouac sack
- A closed-cell sitting pad
- Waterproof medical supplies
- Water purification that does not depend only on freezing or boiling
- High-calorie food requiring little preparation
- A short, soft rope and textile sling
- A bone, wood, or polymer walking staff with a broad tip
- Chalk or removable cloth markers, used only with permission
- A nonmetallic cup with a lid

Do not rely on magnetic compasses, ordinary drainage logic, or exposed electronics. Waterproof all devices against immersion from below. Water may enter places that are above or apparently sealed.

### Gifts and trade

Useful gifts are better than decorative ones, but avoid transforming the local economy with inappropriate technology. Likely welcome offerings include:

- Strong woven cord
- Leather-working needles made from bone or another noncorroding material
- Fine cloth, felt, or waterproof membrane in modest quantities
- Sealed salt, fat, lamp oil, or durable high-energy food
- UV-protective eye coverings
- Small copper sheets or simple repairable copper tools

Ask before offering iron or advanced adhesives. Iron is rare and may be valuable, but it may also corrode rapidly, stain water, damage stoneworking traditions, or become socially disruptive. Mortar, expanding sealants, and rigid fasteners are particularly unsuitable in architecture designed to move.

Bring nothing biologically active: no seeds, untreated wood, live food cultures, soil, feathers, or unsterilized animal products. The basin’s ecology is sparse but intensely interconnected. Clean your boots and equipment before arrival.

### Entering the town

Pause at the rim and let your eyes adjust. Do not descend while dazzled. Watch residents’ route choices: what appears to be a roof may be a path, while what looks like a stair may be a drain about to flood.

Move slowly and follow exactly where your guide places hands and feet. Never assume a fitted stone is load-bearing. Do not lean against walls, pull on projecting slabs, or widen a gap for convenience. The settlement stands through carefully distributed weight and friction; an apparently loose stone may brace an entire chamber.

Local movement is intimate. Be ready to:

- Turn sideways through passages
- Crawl or kneel
- Cross low roofs
- Step over active gutters
- Pass your pack ahead of you
- Yield to people carrying stone
- Climb immediately when water begins to bead through paving

Keep three points of contact where possible, but ask before gripping polished gutter edges. Their smoothness may indicate an accepted handhold—or a critical water surface that should remain clean.

Do not travel alone, especially after dark. The wall animals can negotiate spaces you cannot, and their presence does not mean a gap is human-passable.

### Noise and manners

Speak softly and at close range. Do not shout across the basin, clap, whistle sharply, ring bells, use audible alarms, or play amplified music. Whether or not sound truly loosens the mountain, the belief is practical, ancient, and easy to respect.

Set electronics to silent. Replace emergency whistles with a locally agreed visual or tactile signal. A bright cloth, repeated hand motion, or tug line may be more appropriate, although intense flashing lights should also be cleared with residents because light affects water and glass-gnat activity.

Expect little personal distance. Buildings, paths, work, animals, and bodies are compressed together. Close speech is normal and should not be mistaken for secrecy or aggression.

Ask before:

- Photographing people, graves, water devices, or the central hole
- Entering a warm chamber
- Moving any stone
- Taking ice, water, moss, shell, feather, or mineral deposit
- Feeding animals
- Helping with reconstruction

A visitor’s instinct to “repair” something may be disastrous. A bowed wall, blocked-looking drain, or dismantled doorway may be functioning exactly as intended.

### Reading the water

Water is the principal immediate hazard. It may emerge through floors, rise uphill, adhere vertically, or abandon an open vessel. Never camp or sleep in a low passage, even if dry. Do not place critical equipment directly on stone.

Learn the local warning signs:

- **Crabs climbing upward:** lower routes may soon flood.
- **Crabs burying themselves in drains:** water may rise from roofs or upper channels.
- **Beading between paving stones:** move upward immediately.
- **Sudden coordinated movement by residents:** follow first and ask later.
- **Glass gnats thickening around falling water:** conditions may be nearing a freeze–thaw inversion, though only locals should interpret this precisely.
- **Birds leaving niches without audible alarm:** regard it as significant.

When flooding begins, do not run blindly. Climb to an indicated shelf or roof, keep out of narrow door bends, and do not fight the flow merely because it appears shallow. Knee-deep water can rapidly numb the legs, conceal shifting paving, or freeze around footwear.

Never open, close, or remove a diversion plug unless directed. A small obstruction may redirect water through homes, geothermal rooms, graves, or storage chambers.

Carry drinking water from outside until residents show you a safe source. Clear local water is not necessarily potable, and ancestral mineral seepage may be culturally acceptable without being biologically or chemically suitable for an off-world human. Use a sealed vessel: an open bottle may fill, empty, or freeze unpredictably.

### Cold and shelter

The cold is invasive rather than merely severe. Water under the paving continually draws heat from the feet, and the town’s narrow drafts can cool one part of the body while another overheats from climbing.

Monitor for:

- Loss of dexterity
- Slurred or unusually quiet speech
- Stumbling
- Apathy about wet clothing
- White or waxy fingers and toes
- Altitude headache or confusion

Change wet layers early. Do not wait until you are shivering violently.

Use inhabited shelter rather than pitching a tent. There may be no safe flat ground, and stakes could puncture a drainage channel. Local rooms trap still air through bent entrances; crawl through them carefully and avoid blocking the air path. Geothermal chambers may be warm but could accumulate gases unfamiliar to you, so use them only with residents present.

Fire is likely inappropriate. Fuel is scarce, smoke would collect in low chambers, and rapid heating may fracture ice or stone. Bring chemical heat packs only if they are leakproof and pack every used one out.

### Animals and ecology

Do not handle the white crabs. They are indicators, scavengers, and part of the drainage system. Crushing a mass of them underfoot would be both ecologically damaging and socially offensive.

Do not block wall gaps occupied by the many-jointed animals. They warm the masonry and appear to be essential residents rather than vermin. Keep food and leather gear sealed, but respond to theft by securing possessions—not by trapping or poisoning animals.

Avoid bird niches, particularly eggs. Their communication may be infrasonic or otherwise perceptible through pressure in the teeth; discomfort, nausea, or anxiety near a flock may therefore be sensory rather than psychological. Hearing protection may not block it.

Do not attempt to capture glass gnats. Given the absence of remains in feeding birds and their apparent association with anomalous phase changes, they may not be organisms in the ordinary sense. Keep them out of sealed equipment if possible, but do not spray insecticide or swing at them. If they gather densely around you, stop moving and let a local assess the light and water conditions.

The few plants and lichens are exceptionally precious. Never step on a screened grass blade or scrape moss from a drainage mouth.

### The dead

The pale forms behind ice at the basin edge are graves, not mineral curiosities. Do not touch, chip, sample, warm, illuminate for prolonged photography, or drink runoff from them without explicit invitation.

Because graves also function as retaining structures, even respectful physical contact may be unsafe. Treat fitted stones around them as both funerary architecture and active engineering.

Residents may speak of ancestors in literal hydraulic terms. Avoid dismissing this as metaphor. In a place where water, mineral deposition, architecture, and bodies materially merge, the distinction may not be useful.

### The central hole

Do not approach the central aperture alone. Stay outside any boundary indicated by stones, body position, or avoidance behavior. Do not place fingers, tools, cameras, thread, liquids, or sensors into it. Do not throw anything down it.

Most importantly, do not listen to it without understanding the local consequences.

If it connects to earlier configurations of the town—or merely produces convincing sounds that seem to do so—it presents psychological, temporal, and navigational risks. A traveler may hear familiar voices, construction from another era, or apparent evidence that encourages intervention. Establish beforehand:

- Your current date and personal chronology
- A written record of recent memories
- A recognition phrase known to your companions
- A firm limit on proximity and listening time
- A rule against acting on information obtained from the hole until outside the basin

Do not establish a portal near it. Do not attempt to synchronize a multiverse transit device to its noon inhalation or dawn exhalation. The aperture may encode position by historical arrangement rather than ordinary space, making “sideways” displacement dangerously ambiguous.

If you experience missing time, doubled memories, sounds continuing after you leave, or certainty that a vanished route still exists, tell your guide immediately and withdraw from the plaza.

### Staying overnight

Sleep in a resident-approved chamber above the current predicted waterline. Keep boots, eye protection, gloves, and pack within reach, not piled in a doorway. Tie critical items loosely to your body or sleeping mat, never to the walls.

Before sleeping, learn:

1. The nearest upward route  
2. The secondary upward route  
3. Which wall may be dismantled in an emergency  
4. Which walls must never be disturbed  
5. The local silent alarm  
6. Where your host expects the floor and waterline to be by morning  

Do not be alarmed if the room shifts slightly, ice grows through a seam, or a passage has changed by dawn. Do be alarmed if residents evacuate, animals abandon the walls, or all nearby crabs move in the same direction.

### Departure and decontamination

Leave with everything you brought, including food wrappers, medical waste, broken cord, metal filings, and human waste if local disposal has not been explicitly arranged. Do not take “souvenirs.” A loose slab may be a family marker, a hydraulic weight, grave material, or the future component of a house.

Outside the basin:

- Dry and inspect equipment for crabs, eggs, moss, gnats, or mineral films
- Quarantine wet gear
- Test water and biological samples only if sampling was formally permitted
- Reconfirm your chronology and portal coordinates
- Monitor for altitude illness, frost injury, unfamiliar infection, and persistent auditory or dental-pressure sensations

Allow extra time before multiverse transit. Water trapped in equipment may behave normally again—or may not. Do not carry an active inversion into a portal system.

## The right attitude

Arrive without the expectation that stability is a sign of health. In this town, permanence would likely be catastrophe. Its people do not merely endure collapse and freezing; they have made continuous adjustment into architecture, economy, memory, and kinship.

The most respectful visitor is therefore not the one who stands apart and tries to preserve everything as found. It is the one who stays attentive, travels lightly, keeps quiet, follows the water, and accepts that by the time they leave, the room in which they slept may already be becoming a roof, a drain, or someone else’s wall.
