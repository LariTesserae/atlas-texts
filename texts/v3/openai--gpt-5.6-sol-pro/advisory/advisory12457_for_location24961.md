## General approach

Treat the basin as **a functioning factory that happens to be alive**, not as wilderness reclaiming a ruin. Its motions are slow, but the forces involved are industrial: crushing, entanglement, sudden impact, hydraulic release, electrical discharge, and unstable footing. It does not appear predatory, yet it continually tests and incorporates unattended objects. A visitor can be harmed without ever being attacked.

An ordinary human should make this a **short daylight survey**, ideally no more than four to six hours. Do not camp in the basin.

## Before arrival

### Choose the right conditions

Visit:

- After several relatively dry days.
- In full daylight.
- With no rain forecast during the visit or for several hours beforehand.
- With enough time to leave well before dusk.

Rain charges the bladder-vines, fills pipes, activates runoff machinery, and produces prolonged secondary dripping. Dusk also closes flowers that serve as valves, so pressure and pathways may change around sunset.

Do not assume dry weather makes the works dormant. Sun-warmed flowers can activate mechanisms of their own.

### Establish the exit outside the rim

Place the return portal, vehicle, or extraction point on firm ground **beyond the basin’s rootwork**, not inside it. Keep its controls attended. Vegetation should never be allowed to touch an open interdimensional threshold.

A surface team should remain outside with:

- Continuous communication.
- A fixed return deadline.
- Medical and lifting equipment.
- An independent way to close the portal remotely.
- A contamination enclosure for returning personnel.

If no trustworthy extraction point can be established outside the vegetation, do not enter.

### Team size

Use a team of two or three. No one should enter alone, but a large party would increase trampling, noise, entanglement, and route congestion.

Stay within sight of one another. The vegetation absorbs sound directionally, while machinery can suddenly overwhelm speech.

## Clothing and equipment

Wear smooth, close-fitting equipment with no dangling straps or exposed loops.

Recommended protection includes:

- A ventilated waterproof coverall or dry-suit-style outer layer.
- Broad-soled, electrically rated boots with good wet-bark traction.
- Sealed gaiters.
- Cut-resistant waterproof gloves.
- Helmet with chin strap.
- Full eye protection.
- Hearing protection that still permits radio communication.
- A respirator suitable for spores, aerosols, and unknown pollen.
- A personal flotation aid only if deeper water has been confirmed nearby; bulky vests can snag in dense growth.

Although the water is usually shallow, it may conceal rails, pipe mouths, moving components, or holes beneath root mats.

Carry:

- A blunt, nonconductive probing staff. Never stab it through the mat.
- Sealed lamps even during daylight.
- Inertial navigation and local mapping equipment.
- Compact thermal and humidity monitors.
- Voltage and electric-field detectors.
- A guarded rescue cutter for cutting clothing or your own tether.
- First-aid supplies brought from home.
- Drinking water and electrolytes.
- A small emergency air supply if the canopy can seal tightly.
- A vibration-resistant camera rather than loose notebooks.

Avoid natural-fiber ropes, wooden tools, absorbent packs, exposed foam, or porous equipment. The basin may recognize such material as readily usable tissue. Smooth synthetic and ceramic surfaces are preferable, though nothing should be considered immune to exploration.

Do not leave conventional trail markers. If navigation beacons are necessary, count them and recover every one. A lifeline can be useful near the entrance, but it must be kept taut on a managed reel: a slack line could enter a pulley, become woven into a wall, or transmit a sudden mechanical pull to the team.

## Moving through the basin

Assume every surface may be:

- Alive.
- Load-bearing only temporarily.
- Part of a pressure system.
- A component waiting to move.
- Electrically active when wet.

Walk slowly on broad, mature root plates. Test each step gently. Avoid pale stolon mats, swollen vines, folded leaf cups, apparently loose seed gravel, and places where warm water wells upward.

Never:

- Step over a belt or cable.
- Pass beneath a counterweight.
- Stand between converging trunks.
- Put a hand into a gear housing, pipe, leaf mound, or warm pool.
- Use machinery as a handhold.
- Crawl under anything that could descend, however slowly.
- Stand on narrow-gauge rail where roots have exposed it.
- Assume a rusted component is weak or inactive.

The mechanisms can spend hours storing force and release it in a fraction of a second. A belt moving almost imperceptibly may still be under enough tension to sever a limb.

Use three points of contact only on surfaces already tested. Even then, do not lean your full weight against a trunk: some trunks are hydraulic actuators rather than structural plants.

## Recognizing a basin-wide stroke

The tremendous “knock” is likely the greatest acute hazard. Warning signs may include:

- The ground becoming noticeably firmer or tighter.
- A rise in deep vibration.
- Water climbing visibly through stems.
- Bladder-vines becoming taut.
- Belts trembling or changing pitch.
- Leaves tilting in the same direction.
- A sudden pause in smaller drips and creaks.
- Metallic latches withdrawing below the surface.

If these signs appear:

1. Leave ankle-deep water immediately.
2. Move away from the pumping house, exposed rails, suspended weights, belts, pipe mouths, and taut vines.
3. Choose a broad root pad with no overhead fruit, metal, or pooled leaves.
4. Crouch low with knees flexed rather than locking your legs.
5. Protect your head and maintain a secure grip on your own equipment.
6. Do not grab nearby vegetation.
7. After the impact, remain in place briefly. Secondary weight drops, showers, electrical discharges, and pipe surges may follow.

Do not run unless a specific component is about to strike. The footing is too irregular, and the basin’s hazards are mostly pinch points rather than pursuers.

## Electricity and water

The pale vines linking ceramic insulators should be treated as live conductors. Blue sparks indicate more than decorative bioluminescence.

In damp conditions:

- Remain well clear of poles and connected vines.
- Do not touch metal and vegetation simultaneously.
- Avoid standing in water near electrical growth.
- If nearby sparking begins, retreat with short, shuffling steps rather than taking long strides.
- Expect radio and compass interference.

Do not attempt to ground, cut, sample, or reroute the conducting vines. Their voltage may be modest while their current, stored charge, or biological effect is unknown.

## Interaction with the vegetation

The safest behavior is to be **brief, clean, and uninteresting**.

Do not feed, prune, poison, burn, lubricate, repair, harvest, or “free” any machine. Do not remove bolt-shaped fruits, serial-numbered bark, patterned stems, moth remains, or apparently dead fiber. Here, dead material is still part of an active recycling process.

Do not place tools on the ground. If something is dropped, retrieve it immediately if safe. Tendrils may not seize an object within minutes, but the habit of leaving equipment unattended is dangerous. Anything left overnight should be presumed incorporated or contaminated.

If a tendril touches you:

- Do not jerk away.
- Step back slowly while a companion watches for connected tension.
- If it has entered clothing or equipment, sacrifice the clothing or strap before cutting the plant.
- Never pull against a vine that may connect to a counterweight or pressurized bladder.

Plant sap should be treated as a chemical and biological hazard. Hydraulic punctures can inject material under the skin even when the wound looks small.

## Navigation

The basin’s closeness will make distances feel unreliable. The pumping house is an obvious landmark but should not become the team’s only reference; arching trunks and curtains may hide it after a few meters.

Map continuously. Record turns, elevation changes, exposed rail segments, pole locations, and distinctive machinery sounds. Do not carve marks into bark or tie ribbons to plants.

The stronger deep vibration near the rim might provide a rough directional cue, but it should not be followed blindly. It may lead toward buried casing, unstable peat, or the most mechanically active part of the perimeter.

If lost, stop and reconstruct the route. Do not force a straight path through vegetation. “Breaking through” a wall may mean entering a belt chamber, pressure vessel, or living conduit.

## Sampling and biosecurity

The basin is an exceptionally serious cross-world contamination risk. Its plants can use pipes for seed distribution, imitate manufactured parts, and incorporate foreign material. A single fruit or root filament might establish itself inside plumbing, machinery, or a portal facility.

The preferred policy is **no biological sampling**.

If sampling is essential:

- Obtain only tiny quantities under a prewritten protocol.
- Use sealed, rigid, triple-contained vessels.
- Never open them outside a high-containment laboratory.
- Keep samples away from water systems and mechanical equipment.
- Record every item and mass balance.
- Assume water, mud, condensation, and “rust” all contain viable propagules.

On departure:

1. Inspect one another before crossing the rim.
2. Remove visible tendrils and seeds without dropping them outside.
3. Enter a containment enclosure.
4. Bag outer garments and equipment separately.
5. Collect all wash water.
6. Inspect seams, boot treads, filters, cable sockets, and hollow tools.
7. Quarantine equipment long enough to reveal slow growth.
8. Destroy any unaccounted biological material under controlled conditions.

Never rinse equipment into local ground or drains.

## If an overnight stay becomes unavoidable

Avoid sheltering in the pumping house. It contains the greatest concentration of machinery, pollen vents, belts, hidden shafts, and conductive vines.

Choose the driest, least mechanically active area near the rim. Use a smooth, self-supporting emergency shelter kept slightly pressurized, with all gear inside. Maintain watches throughout the night. Inspect the shelter perimeter frequently and shift any exploratory tendrils gently before they begin testing load or attachment.

Even then, request extraction immediately. By morning the basin may already have begun deciding whether the shelter is a wall, a tool, a nutrient source, or an obstruction.

## Mental preparation

Expect a place that feels crowded without feeling hostile. There will be no horizon, little animal movement, and no obvious operator to explain the work. The slow machinery may encourage complacency, while its crashes provoke panic. Neither reaction is useful.

Move patiently, but never forget the accumulated force around you. The basin does not need to hurry. You do.
