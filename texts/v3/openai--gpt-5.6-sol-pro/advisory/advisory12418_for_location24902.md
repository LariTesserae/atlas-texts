## Prepare for two environments, not one

This is simultaneously a high-altitude summit and a cold-water dive site. Reaching the crater may expose you to thin air, strong ultraviolet light, rapid weather changes, and exhaustion before you enter the water. Entering the town adds cold immersion, pressure, entanglement, limited breathing gas, and the danger of damaging a structure that is also a living organism.

An ordinary traveler should not attempt the visit alone. Arrange for both a mountain guide and a resident water-guide, preferably one trained to escort air-breathing outsiders. Do not arrive at the rim and simply jump in: there will almost certainly be designated crossing and entry points chosen to avoid injuring the root seal.

Acclimatize to the summit altitude before diving. High-altitude diving requires different decompression calculations, and climbing immediately after a dive can further increase decompression risk. Bring a dive planner or computer that supports the lake’s actual elevation, and build in conservative surface intervals.

## Essential equipment

Use equipment designed for cold, elevated freshwater:

- A well-fitted drysuit with insulating underlayers
- Hood and gloves that preserve dexterity
- Full-face mask if available, both for warmth and communication
- Redundant breathing supply
- An altitude-capable dive computer
- A closed-circuit or semi-closed rebreather, if you are already properly trained to use one
- Compact buoyancy equipment with no loose straps
- Two cutting tools in accessible sheaths, for emergency entanglement only
- Waterproof lights with broad, low-intensity beams
- Underwater slate or local signal board
- Sealed communications equipment compatible with your guide’s
- A small emergency flotation device for the surface
- Personal oxygen and a high-altitude first-aid kit at the summit staging point

A rebreather is preferable because it conserves gas, reduces noisy bubble streams, and is less likely to disturb delicate membranes or trapped-air pockets. It introduces its own serious hazards, however; do not learn to use one here. If using ordinary open-circuit scuba, ask where exhaled bubbles may safely collect. Bubbles trapped under broad leaves or against bladder-vine walls could alter buoyancy, stress tissue, or displace air residents rely upon.

Streamline everything. Dangling gauges, buckles, hooks, fins, and camera arms can catch guide-ropes or tear soft walls. Avoid sharp metal fittings where possible. Cover hard tank edges and clips with smooth guards.

## Do not rely on the town’s air pockets

The inhabitants travel from one natural breath reservoir to another, but an outsider should not imitate them.

A pocket beneath a leaf may contain too little oxygen, too much carbon dioxide, plant gases, decay products, or air compressed to the surrounding depth. Some pockets are inhabited by frogs or maintained for specific workers. Others may be essential to the buoyancy of a roof. Never breathe from, vent, enlarge, or “top up” a pocket without explicit instruction.

Likewise, do not enter an air-bladder building casually. Its interior pressure may match the surrounding water, and its plug hatch may require a precise sequence to avoid flooding, membrane damage, or sudden pressure change. Wait for a resident to operate every door.

Even in a dry chamber, keep your breathing apparatus close. Fires, fermentation, crowded occupants, and plant respiration can change the atmosphere. Carry a compact oxygen and carbon-dioxide monitor if local custom permits it.

## Biosecurity matters enormously

The root collar is the lake’s dam, filter, and life-support system. An imported fungus, snail egg, alga, or bacterium could be more dangerous than a structural accident.

Before arrival:

- Clean and disinfect all dive equipment according to local guidance.
- Use gear that has been completely dried since its last immersion.
- Remove seeds, soil, spores, and organic debris from boots and clothing.
- Avoid sunscreen, insect repellent, soaps, oils, and anti-fog products unless confirmed safe for the grove.
- Do not bring live bait, untreated wood, food plants, aquarium organisms, or unsealed biological samples.
- Disclose recent visits to other aquatic ecosystems.

Human waste, food scraps, blood, vomit, and dead organisms must be handled according to settlement rules. Carry sealed waste containers. Nothing should be abandoned in the water, and absolutely nothing dead should be allowed to lodge against the outer root wall.

If you become injured, tell your guide immediately. A small bleeding wound is not merely your problem in a closely managed aquatic ecology.

## Learn the town’s rules before entering

The local laws are practical survival measures, not picturesque customs.

You should understand at least these principles:

- Never cut, scrape, sample, or tie anything to a living structure.
- Never anchor to the outer root wall.
- Do not widen a channel by pushing vegetation aside.
- Do not block a spillway, ventilation tube, or circulation lane.
- Do not cross planted moss beds or breeding pools.
- Yield every air source to children and deep workers.
- Stay away from the central throat.
- Do not obstruct shafts of noon light.
- Never attempt to “repair” a leak yourself.
- When gardeners order an area cleared, leave immediately.

A root that resembles an ordinary handhold may support a house, tune a bell, carry water, or regulate the summit seal. A translucent wall may look thick enough to lean on and still be dangerously easy to bruise. Keep neutral buoyancy and fold your arms when passing close to architecture.

Ask before touching guide-ropes. Some may be routes; others may carry loads, control doors, mark restricted growth, or transmit signals.

## Arrange a local guide and learn the bell language

Underwater visibility may be excellent, but orientation will still be difficult. Streets exist in three dimensions, buildings sway, old channels end in thickets, and familiar landmarks change when viewed from above or below.

Before the dive, learn the most important bell-root signals:

- Structural breach or rim crack
- Storm recall
- Current reversal or cold-water turnover
- Missing swimmer
- Restricted channel
- Evacuation to upper chambers
- All-clear

The bell-roots also sound accidentally. Do not act on a single tone unless your guide does. Local residents distinguish civic sequences from fish strikes, settling stone, and current hum by rhythm and context.

Agree on hand signals for cold, low gas, entanglement, separation, panic, and immediate ascent. Sound may travel far but be difficult to locate. Maintain visual contact.

## Cold is likely to be the limiting factor

The clear water may look gentle, especially in sunlit areas, but the shaded channels and lower streets can numb an unprotected hand quickly. Plan shorter excursions than your gas supply would theoretically permit.

Watch for:

- Loss of finger control
- Uncontrolled shivering
- Confusion or irrational calm
- Jaw fatigue
- Difficulty operating valves or hatches
- Increased breathing rate
- Drysuit squeeze or buoyancy problems

Do not descend deeper merely because the town remains visible. The central throat is likely colder than the inhabited levels and may contain unfamiliar currents, gas chemistry, or geological hazards. Its apparent openness is deceptive: it is the settlement’s most important forbidden space.

## Move as though visiting a glass workshop suspended in a forest

Use small, controlled fin strokes. Frog-kicks or gentle hand-assisted movement in open water are safer than broad flutter-kicks near walls. Avoid stirring the root mat; dark silt can obscure channels, smother moss, and reveal fragile buried tissue.

Do not:

- Stand on the lake floor
- Swim directly beneath workers carrying loads
- Chase animals from air pockets
- Reach into abandoned bulbs
- Follow otters into tunnels
- Put fingers near grazing fish or defensive blue crabs
- Approach needlefish groups at speed
- Enter sealed chambers in the old quarter

The abandoned district is not an archaeological playground. Its trapped air may be unstable, and cutting into it could wound the central grove. Photography should be done from established lanes. Obtain permission before photographing translucent homes; their walls provide less privacy than an outsider may first realize.

The clear vertical wells and empty spaces are deliberate infrastructure. Do not mistake them for unused building sites or convenient shortcuts. Their emptiness delivers light and allows water to circulate.

## Expect unfamiliar social rhythms

Residents who grow their buildings over decades will not treat time, property, or maintenance as you do. A bent shoot may represent years of planned construction. A gardener may inspect a wall for an hour before making a cut no longer than a fingernail. Do not hurry them, offer unsolicited tools, or describe slow work as primitive.

Metal gifts may be inappropriate. Copper is precious but corrodes, and a strong modern blade could bypass safeguards embedded in local custom. Bring only gifts requested in advance. Likely useful offerings include verified noncontaminating cordage, sealed medical supplies, optical instruments, durable measuring tools, or knowledge about water chemistry—but even these may alter local power and labor arrangements. Ask first.

Fire is hazardous and probably ceremonial. Never light a flame, activate a heating coil, smoke, or open a hot battery pack inside an air chamber without permission. Carry electrical devices in pressure-rated, leak-proof housings.

Meals may feature managed fish, snails, crabs, aquatic plants, and preserved foods. Inform hosts about allergies, but do not demand that scarce dry storage or air be used for elaborate accommodations. Bring compact food that produces little waste and can remain sealed underwater.

## Surface and evacuation planning

Because there is no shore, the surface is not automatically safe. Floating leaves, root growth, wind, and the crater rim may make exit difficult. Identify in advance:

1. The authorized entry opening  
2. The normal exit opening  
3. A storm exit  
4. A place where an unconscious diver can be lifted from the water  
5. The nearest dry shelter  
6. The route down the mountain  
7. The nearest recompression or advanced medical facility  

Leave emergency gas, warm clothing, shelter, oxygen, and communications equipment at the summit staging point. Weather may remain clear over the lake while lower routes disappear into cloud or heavy rain. A medical evacuation could require both aquatic rescue and a mountain descent.

If the rim is reported damaged, do not surface near it or attempt to inspect it. Follow residents toward designated safe zones. A leak that appears to be a harmless thread of water may be under immense load, and the roots’ response must not be obstructed.

## Best way to experience the settlement

For a first visit, remain in the upper and middle town. Enter through the established route, spend time acclimating in a robust guest bladder, and take several short guided swims rather than one long expedition.

Visit the market platforms from the perimeter. Watch gardeners work without hovering over their hands. Listen to the bell-roots while holding still in open water. Look down at noon, when the settlement resolves into layers and the pale root mat becomes visible beneath the avenues. If invited into a home, move slowly, keep sharp equipment sheathed, and do not press against the green membrane simply to feel the lake outside.

Most importantly, resist the urge to explore every depth. This town has survived by leaving spaces open, structures untouched, and processes slow. A good visitor should imitate that restraint: carry their own breath, disturb as little water as possible, and leave no mark that the grove must spend years growing around.
