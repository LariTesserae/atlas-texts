Prepare for this settlement as you would for a cold, wet mountain expedition—but remember that you are entering an inhabited watershed, not merely a forest. Water, paths, trees, and bridges all carry social meaning. Your greatest dangers will be mundane: slipping, getting chilled, contaminating a spring, blocking a drain, or trusting the wrong branch.

## Before crossing into this world

### Arrange biological quarantine

This basin would be exceptionally vulnerable to foreign organisms. Mud, seeds, fungal spores, insects, and microbes will thrive in its perpetual damp.

Before arrival:

- Scrub and disinfect boots, pack seams, walking poles, tents, and clothing.
- Bring no untreated seeds, fresh fruit, soil, live wood, feathers, or animal products.
- Do not release pets, bait, aquarium organisms, or “helpful” agricultural species.
- Keep alien medicines, soaps, fuels, batteries, and bodily waste out of the drains.
- If you may carry unfamiliar respiratory or intestinal pathogens, quarantine before entering. A crowded settlement with shared water and limited medical resources could be devastated by an ordinary traveler’s infection.

If multiverse transit itself requires sterilization, complete it somewhere outside the watershed rather than washing residues into a local stream.

### Learn enough language to be safe

At minimum, learn phrases for:

- “May I cross?”
- “Where is the public path?”
- “Is this water for drinking?”
- “The drain is blocked.”
- “The bank is moving.”
- “The tree is leaning.”
- “I hear something above.”
- “Where is high ground?”
- “I need a healer.”
- “I do not understand this boundary.”

Learn the local alarm bells and call-and-response used on bridges in fog. A pleasant greeting is useful; the word for “landslide” is essential.

## What to bring

Pack light. Every kilogram will have to be carried up wet stairs, across springy bridges, beneath low branches, and through passages barely wide enough for one person.

### Clothing

Bring:

- Several thin wool or synthetic layers rather than one heavy garment
- A breathable rain cape or poncho that leaves your hands free
- Quick-drying trousers
- Calf wraps or close-fitting gaiters against leeches
- Two or three pairs of wool socks, stored separately in waterproof bags
- A warm sleeping layer reserved exclusively for indoors
- A broad-brimmed rain hat
- Light gloves for rope, thorn, and rough bark
- Dry sandals or soft house footwear, if local custom allows them indoors

Avoid cotton. Once wet, it will remain cold and clammy. Avoid long loose straps, scarves, and dangling ornaments; they will catch on thorns, branches, pegs, and bridge lashings.

Bright white clothing will immediately become stained by mud, tannins, smoke, fruit, and moss. This is not a place to preserve an immaculate appearance.

### Footwear

Use boots or trail shoes with:

- Excellent traction on wet roots and polished stone
- Flexible soles that can feel narrow steps
- Fast drainage
- Secure lacing
- No deep cavities that trap seeds and mud

Heavy rigid mountaineering boots may be awkward on ladders and woven walkways. Whatever you wear, test every step. Moss is not merely slippery ground; it may conceal a gutter, rotting plank, frog, or missing stair.

Carry a short walking staff only after asking what material and length are appropriate. Do not cut one casually. A “convenient” sapling may be part of a living building, slope reinforcement, boundary, or crop.

### Essential equipment

Useful items include:

- A waterproof roll-top pack
- Several smaller dry bags rather than one large liner
- A hooded headlamp with warm or red light
- Spare batteries in sealed containers
- A compact first-aid kit
- Oral rehydration salts
- Water treatment suited to protozoa, bacteria, and viruses
- Antifungal powder or cream
- Blister care
- Leech-bite dressings
- Insect repellent known not to poison aquatic life
- A compact sitting mat
- A sleeping bag appropriate for cool, saturated conditions
- Earplugs, though you may still feel monkeys and rain through the walls
- A whistle, used only according to local emergency convention
- Cord for your own gear—not for modifying local structures
- A notebook made of waterproof material, if writing is culturally acceptable

Do not rely on electronics for navigation. Dense vegetation, steep relief, moisture, and multilevel paths will defeat maps and satellite positioning. Keep devices sealed against condensation, not only direct rain.

## Arrival and guides

Do not enter alone simply because the entire basin looks small from the ridge. Half an hour across on a map may mean hours of wrong turns through vertically layered paths.

Arrange for a local guide or host to meet you at the rim. Ideally, this person should know:

- Which bridges are public
- Which courtyards merely resemble paths
- Where floodwater rises first
- Which springs are drinking sources
- Current repairs and diversions
- Boundaries marked by trees
- Safe shelter during storms

At the first opportunity, ask your host to point out the settlement’s high-water refuges, emergency bells, communal halls, and routes out of the lowest terraces. Repeat the route yourself in daylight. Do not assume you will recognize it in fog or darkness.

A local guide is also your protection against accidental trespass. Here, a tree may be a legal document and a spring may establish kinship rights. An outsider cannot reliably distinguish a boundary marker from ordinary vegetation.

## Moving through the town

Adopt the local pace. It will feel unnecessarily slow until your first near-fall.

- Step over gutters; never place a foot in one.
- Keep one hand free.
- Test rails, roots, vines, and ladders before loading them.
- Look upward whenever birds, monkeys, squirrels, or residents do.
- Call before entering a narrow bridge in fog.
- Yield to people carrying poles, water, firewood, animals, or large baskets.
- Do not stand beneath suspended cages, drying racks, roof edges, or fruiting branches.
- Avoid bunching several travelers onto one bridge or platform.
- Never assume the route beneath you is unoccupied before dropping or pouring anything.
- If you dislodge soil or leaves into a drain, remove them immediately.

Expect paths to pass over and under one another. Instructions such as “take the upper crossing below the goat platform” may be more useful than compass directions. Memorize landmarks by sound and function: a clay bell, a frog cistern, a smoked-fish stall, a particular spring—not by temporary screens or vegetation that may be moved overnight.

After dark, ask for an escort until you know the routes. Frogs, rain, and mist can conceal both hazards and warning calls.

## Water etiquette

Treat all moving water as someone’s concern.

Never:

- Wash yourself, clothes, cookware, or medical equipment in a spring without permission
- Empty soap, oil, alcohol, fuel, blood, chemicals, or food scraps into a channel
- Move a stone to redirect flow
- Dam a gutter for amusement
- Step into a settling pool or reed bed
- Remove aquatic animals from wastewater gardens
- Build across even a seemingly dry channel

A shallow trickle may become a violent runnel during rain. A minor obstruction above could flood a house below.

Ask separately where to obtain drinking water, where to wash, and where to dispose of waste. These will not necessarily be the same place. Filter and disinfect drinking water unless your hosts can confidently identify a protected source and your biology is compatible with local microbes.

If you notice water backing up, changing color, carrying fresh soil, or emerging where the ground was previously dry, tell someone at once. Do not attempt a major repair alone. Water rights and drainage responsibilities are communal matters.

## Weather and landslide precautions

Rainfall is not the only warning sign. The dangerous period may come after the rain, when saturated soil begins to move.

Pay attention to:

- New cracks in banks or paving
- Doors or platforms suddenly out of square
- Trees leaning farther than before
- Roots lifting from the ground
- Muddy water entering a previously clear channel
- A drain that has gone silent during rain
- Deep creaks or snapping roots
- Stones falling without an obvious animal above
- A sour or freshly exposed earth smell

If alarm bells sound:

1. Stop asking questions and follow local directions.
2. Move uphill or to the designated tree platform, not merely into the nearest building.
3. Leave baggage unless explicitly told it is safe to retrieve.
4. Do not cross active runoff.
5. Keep clear of leaning trees and the downhill side of retaining walls.
6. Help only with tasks you understand. Unskilled pulling on a rope or opening the wrong sluice may worsen the failure.

Keep footwear, rain layer, light, and a small sealed emergency pouch within arm’s reach while sleeping. Your pouch should contain medicine, water treatment, warm layer, identification, and whatever means will return you across worlds.

## Animals and insects

The wildlife is habituated to human settlement, not domesticated.

### Monkeys and birds

Keep all food in latched, hard-sided, or locally approved containers. Simple knots may not deter monkeys. Never feed them, even once. Do not display brightly wrapped food on bridges or roofs.

Watch for falling fruit, nutshells, droppings, branches, and objects thrown by monkeys. Cover your drinking vessel whenever you are not actively using it.

### Dogs, pigs, goats, and poultry

Ask before touching animals. Dogs may be working, territorial, or reacting to unfamiliar multiversal scent. Give pigs room in narrow lanes. Keep clothing and pack straps away from goats; they may eat or damage both.

### Frogs, bats, and insects

Do not handle frogs with bare hands. Some may have irritating or toxic skin, and substances on your skin can harm them. Shake out footwear and clothing before use. Inspect bedding for insects without spraying indiscriminately.

Avoid sleeping directly beneath bat roosts. If bitten or scratched by any mammal, clean the injury promptly and seek local and expedition medical advice. Your normal vaccines may not correspond to local diseases, but pre-travel rabies vaccination or its multiversal equivalent is still sensible.

Leeches are chiefly unpleasant rather than catastrophic. Remove them gently rather than burning them against your skin, clean the bite, and expect prolonged bleeding. Check under calf wraps, waistbands, pack straps, and socks after every journey.

## Health and comfort

The persistent damp will work on you slowly.

Common problems will include:

- Chafing
- Trench foot and fungal infections
- Chills despite moderate temperatures
- Smoke-irritated eyes and lungs
- Infected cuts
- Gastrointestinal illness
- Sleep loss from frogs, rain, tools, bells, and roof animals
- Fatigue from constant climbing and balance

Dry your feet whenever possible. Rotate socks. Clean even tiny scratches, especially those caused by bamboo or thorns. Reserve one set of dry clothing for sleep and never wear it outdoors.

Cooking smoke may be more difficult than the altitude or cold. Choose lodging with ventilation and avoid sleeping directly under smoke-dark roof peaks, which may also house bats. If you have asthma, bring ample medication and discuss it with your host before entering a smoky dwelling.

Do not drink heavily. Wet stairs, open channels, and elevated walks are unforgiving to impaired balance.

## Food and trade

Eat what your host recommends and observe how it is served. Smoked fish, mushrooms, larvae, fruit, eggs, and fermented foods may all be ordinary, but unfamiliar species and preservation methods can affect an off-world digestive system.

Be particularly cautious with:

- Wild mushrooms
- Raw snails or fish
- Unboiled water
- Unfamiliar medicinal bark
- Ferments still actively bubbling
- Food exposed to monkey or bat saliva

Bring compact emergency rations, but consume them discreetly and pack out every wrapper.

Metal is scarce and socially significant. Small, high-quality iron or steel tools may make excellent gifts—but only if your crossing authority permits technological exchange and local leaders accept them. A knife can alter labor, status, conflict, and obligations. Do not hand out metal casually to children or individual households.

Safer gifts might include durable cord, sewing needles, plain cloth, noninvasive medical supplies, or repair services, again subject to local approval. Avoid disposable plastic, toxic coatings, batteries, and objects that cannot be repaired locally.

Ask prices rather than assuming barter. Pay guides, porters, craftspeople, healers, and hosts fairly. The apparent abundance of plants does not mean materials are free; nearly every useful stem may have an owner, purpose, or future role.

## Social conduct

The community’s deepest institutions are likely embodied in maintenance rather than monuments. Show respect by noticing work.

- Offer to help clear leaves or carry baskets, but wait for instructions.
- Do not alter knots, lashings, sluices, bridges, braces, or plantings.
- Do not pick fruit merely because it hangs over a path unless you are certain the path is public and the custom applies to outsiders.
- Ask before entering a living building; its walls and supports may be easily damaged.
- Ask before photographing, scanning, sampling, or mapping.
- Do not collect bark, moss, orchids, insects, carved markers, cords, or “abandoned” wood.
- Never cut a tree or vine without explicit permission.
- Avoid leaning packs against trunks used as shrines, boundaries, memorials, or structural supports.

Because agreements are remembered through trees, songs, cords, and recurring acts of repair, do not dismiss local records as imprecise. A resident who recalls who tended a spring for three generations may possess a more legally meaningful record than any map you carry.

If invited to a communal repair, participation may matter more than verbal thanks. Follow the rhythm of experienced workers and accept the least technical task.

## Using advanced technology

Keep conspicuous devices out of sight until their use has been discussed. Drones would disturb nesting birds, provoke territorial animals, invade courtyards, and become dangerous if they fell into a roof or drain. Bright lamps can disrupt insects and attract crowds. Sonic devices may mask alarm bells. Heaters and cooking equipment can cause fire even in a wet forest; dry thatch and smoke-blackened rafters still burn.

Do not make detailed scans of the water system, boundary trees, or escape routes without collective permission. Such information may be political, sacred, or dangerous in the wrong hands.

Your return portal or transit mechanism must not:

- Scorch roots
- Block a path
- Frighten livestock
- Discharge heat or chemicals
- Appear in a spring, wastewater garden, or living hall
- Remain open to insects, spores, or animals

Establish it on stable, agreed ground outside the basin if possible.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect never to become fully dry. Your skin will smell faintly of smoke, wet fiber, and leaf tannin. You will hear people before seeing them and feel crossings tremble under traffic on levels above your head. Privacy will be mostly visual; leaves hide faces, but walls transmit coughs, arguments, pestles, frogs, and rain.

The town may initially seem chaotic. It is not. Its order is distributed through watercourses, practiced movement, plant care, and remembered obligations. A bridge may look temporary yet be socially ancient. A hall may appear overgrown yet be under constant cultivation. A path that disappears may not have been abandoned; it may simply be resting, rerouted, or awaiting repair.

The best preparation is therefore not self-sufficiency but attentiveness. Watch where residents place their feet. Notice when they look up. Learn which water is clean, which trees are speaking legally, and which bells require immediate movement.

Travel lightly, keep your dry things dry, never block a channel, and assume that anything living may also be a wall, a crop, a record, or part of someone’s home.
