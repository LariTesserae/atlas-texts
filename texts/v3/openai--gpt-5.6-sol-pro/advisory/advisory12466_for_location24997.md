## General assessment

Treat the highlands as a flooded industrial complex in permanent, absolute darkness—not as a dark forest. Human sight will contribute almost nothing. The principal hazards are unstable ground, sudden basin-scale releases of water, spore-laden air, concealed machinery, acoustic deception by predators, electrical leakage, and loss of orientation.

An ordinary traveler should not attempt a solo crossing. Go with at least three people, all trained to travel blind, and maintain a rescue station outside the plateau. Assume that radio contact will be unreliable beneath wet vegetation, mineral rock, and machinery.

## Before arrival

### Learn to function without vision

Practice for several weeks in total darkness before the trip:

- walking on irregular wet terrain with a probe;
- using tactile maps and haptic compasses;
- identifying companions by touch codes;
- performing first aid and equipment repairs blindfolded;
- swimming and escaping entanglement without visual reference;
- sleeping and keeping a schedule without daylight;
- reacting to flood alarms without running.

Do not merely cover your eyes during training. Visual light appears to be physically unavailable here, and ordinary lamps, flares, glowing indicators, and heated objects will not provide reassurance. All essential equipment must have tactile, acoustic, or mechanical status indicators.

Agree on touch signals for **stop, retreat, water rising, unstable ground, animal nearby, machinery moving, and respiratory failure**. Spoken communication may be drowned by rain or may reveal your position.

### Test the atmosphere

The atmosphere is warm, dense, mineral-rich, and biologically active. Before anyone removes a sealed helmet, an entry probe should test:

- oxygen and carbon dioxide;
- total pressure and breathing resistance;
- toxic volcanic gases;
- airborne acids and metal aerosols;
- spore concentration;
- combustible gases near vents and industrial works.

Do not trust odor alone. Your nose will quickly become saturated by wet vegetation, minerals, animals, and lubricants.

A closed breathing system is preferable. At minimum, use a positive-pressure full-face respirator with replaceable particulate, biological, acid-gas, and organic-vapor cartridges. Cloth masks will become wet culture media within minutes.

## Essential equipment

Each traveler should carry:

- a sealed full-face respirator or compact rebreather;
- active hearing protection that suppresses thunder and machinery while preserving directional sound;
- bone-conduction team communications;
- an infrasound and ground-vibration detector;
- a short-range acoustic mapper with a haptic display;
- two probing poles, one long and one short;
- broad, articulated peat shoes or low-pressure oversoles;
- a buoyancy vest and compact emergency flotation bladder;
- several quick-release lines, never fixed permanently around the body;
- a tactile compass, inertial navigator, and mechanical watch;
- waterproof tactile maps and a raised-line route log;
- sealed chemical-resistant clothing, gloves, and anti-slip boots;
- redundant atmosphere, heat, electrical-field, and water-depth sensors;
- a rigid emergency breathing hood for each companion;
- antifungal wash, wound sealant, sterile dressings, and foot-care supplies;
- a buoyant emergency capsule or shelter pod;
- food that can be eaten through a suit interface or inside a sealed shelter.

All devices should operate without screens. Use raised controls, vibration codes, spoken readouts through an earpiece, and physical lockouts.

Shield power systems carefully. Local plants can draw current through insulation without visibly damaging it. Keep cables short, dry where possible, and disconnected when not in use. A dry ticking sound, metallic-rain odor, or hair lifting from the skin indicates an electrical field: stop, avoid nearby metal, and back away by the route you entered.

Never use an open flame. Flame gives no visible warning here, and neither hot metal nor fire will glow. Cooking and heating equipment must have automatic temperature, oxygen, and combustion-gas cutoffs.

## Movement through the plateau

### Choose hard ridges, not invitingly level ground

The safest routes are generally rounded volcanic backs and established maintenance approaches. The most dangerous surfaces are broad, apparently level vegetation mats. A mat may support a forest and still cover hundreds of meters of water. Test every step ahead and to both sides.

Warning signs of a floating mat include:

- slow ground waves after a footstep;
- roots moving independently of the underlying surface;
- hollow water sounds beneath vegetation;
- absence of firm mineral response to a long probe;
- distant movement arriving through the ground after a delay.

Spread the team out enough that one collapse does not take everyone, but keep each person within line reach. Lines must include quick-release clips: a submerged mat, opening gate, crawler rig, or migrating animal can turn a safety tether into a trap.

Do not trust rusted stairs, gantries, rails, or inspection platforms simply because machinery still uses them. Test every handhold for corrosion. Never stand between a machine and a drain, rail, valve, culvert, or body of water.

### Navigate primarily by passive sound

Listen before transmitting. Water, survey tones, pump rhythms, and echoes provide a stable map, but loud active sonar may attract predators or interfere with machinery.

When active mapping is necessary:

- use low-power, tightly directed pulses;
- vary the timing so you do not reproduce a machine code;
- take readings from several positions;
- compare the echo map with ground vibration and probe contact;
- avoid clicking repeatedly near rock, where local predators may interpret it as prey or a rival.

Survey towers can be useful landmarks. Record the timing and harmonic pattern of their returning notes rather than merely their direction. If the expected echoes weaken or disappear, stop. You may be approaching a sound-swallowing erosion basin. Do not enter such a basin merely to investigate it. Its soft walls defeat your best navigation sense, machinery avoids it, and animals behave cautiously around it.

Do not use distant echoes alone to judge cliff edges. Windblown waterfalls and hanging wetlands can produce misleading returns. Probe for changes in slope and airflow, and keep well back from any place where water noise has great depth.

### Preserve your sense of time

There is no sunrise, sunset, or visual fatigue cycle. Use strict watch schedules. Require meals, sleep, medical checks, and navigation resets at fixed clock times. Sleep deprivation in continuous darkness rapidly produces disorientation and false sound perception.

## Water and flood protocol

The most important local rule is also the safest human rule:

**Never obstruct water.**

Do not:

- dam a trickle to collect it;
- camp in a dry channel;
- lay ropes across drains;
- block a culvert with equipment;
- seal a leak;
- wedge open a gate;
- anchor a shelter over a spring;
- stand downhill from a maintenance drone clearing roots.

The industrial system may tolerate your presence, but it reacts forcefully to interference with drainage. Gates can open without warning and rearrange an entire basin.

Likely advance signs of a release include:

1. tremors traveling through pipes or rails;
2. changes in pump rhythm;
3. coded hammering or horns;
4. animals moving toward ridges;
5. water rising in root cups and shallow depressions;
6. a sudden pressure change or coordinated tightening of vegetation.

A horn by itself is not sufficient evidence. Predators can imitate safe-crossing and sluice codes. Confirm any alarm through at least two independent channels—ground vibration, machinery rhythm, water movement, or the behavior of several unrelated species.

When a release begins, move toward a known hard-rock ridge, perpendicular to the likely watercourse. Do not blindly follow a herd; false-gate predators exploit exactly that response. Do not take refuge on a floating forest, in a drainage tunnel, beneath a gantry, or inside a shelter whose doors may automatically open during storms.

Emergency shelters should be buoyant, smooth-sided, and capable of detaching from all anchors. A rigid capsule is far safer than a conventional tent.

## Wildlife precautions

Most animals will detect you through heat, vibration, pressure, and scent long before you detect them.

Move smoothly. Avoid rhythmic stamping, repeated pole taps, or regular mechanical noises. These may resemble herd signals or machine codes. Pause periodically with all equipment quiet and listen for:

- claws or clicks on stone;
- breathing close to the hand;
- vegetation moving against the rain;
- synchronized ripples;
- pulses that repeat too perfectly to be natural;
- a “safe” signal coming from above the route it supposedly controls.

Do not approach warm pipelines to rest. Prey gathers there, and predators know it. Avoid the uphill side of a route after hearing a possible sluice-opening signal; that is where a false gate is likely to wait.

Large grazers are not necessarily aggressive, but their feet can rupture mats and their movement can create waves across hectares. Give them a wide corridor. If puddles begin trembling from infrasonic calls, step onto firm rock and remain still until the herd’s direction is clear.

During storms, do not shelter beneath interlocked reed animals, rope fungi, hollow trunk groves, or membrane flyers. Storm-adapted organisms climb, contract, launch, and rearrange themselves as pressure changes.

Do not eat local organisms or drink untreated water. The ecology incorporates metals, industrial lubricants, fungal compounds, and possibly maintenance organisms intended to colonize pipes rather than human tissue.

## Machinery etiquette

The works are not abandoned in the ordinary sense. Assume that any door, platform, lift, valve, crawler, or drainage channel can activate.

When a maintenance drone approaches:

- stop moving;
- clear the nearest water route;
- place loose gear against firm high ground;
- do not touch the drone;
- do not enter anything it opens;
- let it finish and leave.

If it repeatedly repositions toward you, you are probably obstructing something you have not detected. Retreat.

Raised labels may be readable by touch, but scan them with a disposable glove or tactile camera. Cabinets can contain electrical-cleaning animals, hot components, fungal colonies, and scavengers waiting for warmth. Never reach into an open housing.

Do not board a functioning elevator unless your expedition has surveyed both the mechanism and destination. A several-kilometer descent introduces pressure, heat, toxic gas, entrapment, and communication problems. An operating lift is not evidence that humans are expected at the other end.

Rail lines are useful for orientation but poor campsites. They transmit warning blows, machinery vibration, and possibly moving equipment with little local notice. Cross them promptly and at right angles.

## Camps and rest sites

The best campsite is:

- on verified solid rock;
- above known watercourses;
- away from cliff spray;
- away from vents, substations, pipelines, rails, hollow groves, and drainage works;
- acoustically distinct enough to relocate;
- surrounded by multiple escape routes.

Before deploying shelter, listen through one complete local pressure cycle if time permits. Probe for buried water and check whether the “rock” moves slowly under load.

Keep a ground-vibration alarm active. Post a listening watch even inside a sealed shelter. Rain, respirator noise, and the shelter wall can hide the first warning tremors.

Old concrete shelters may be usable only after checking every drain, door, and automatic mechanism. Never sleep in front of a molded door, floor channel, or wall opening. Something designed to remain dry only intermittently may deliberately flood.

## Medical and psychological concerns

Expect:

- fungal skin disease and respiratory exposure;
- trench foot and maceration;
- heat stress despite constant rain;
- ear and sinus injury from pressure and thunder;
- motion sickness from moving vegetation mats;
- sleep disruption and time disorientation;
- auditory hallucinations after prolonged exposure to repetitive industrial rhythms;
- panic caused by distant echoes returning long after their source sound.

Perform scheduled skin, foot, lung, and mental-status checks. No one should conceal confusion: a traveler who begins interpreting every pump or delayed echo as speech is no longer a reliable navigator.

Decontaminate before leaving. Spores, pipe-sealing roots, oxide-eating animals, and conductive moss must not be carried into another ecosystem or into your transport.

## Final rule

The plateau is survivable if approached with humility. It is neither wilderness nor ruin, and its apparent cooperation was not designed for human bodies. Stay on hard ground, breathe sealed air, trust no single signal, and yield immediately to moving water.

If the horns begin in sequence across the dark, do not wait to understand the message. Confirm the ground tremor, take the nearest known ridge, release all fixed lines, and move.
