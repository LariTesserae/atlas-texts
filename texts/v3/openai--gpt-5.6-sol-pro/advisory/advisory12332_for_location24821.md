## Overall assessment

This is not a city that an ordinary human should explore casually or on foot. It is a planetary-scale living machine whose streets, weather, organisms, and buildings participate in the same distributed civic system. Its apparent emptiness is functional space, not abandoned space. A traveler who camps in a vacant plaza, broadcasts a locator tone, dams a trickle of water, or walks across an unoccupied field may unknowingly obstruct migration, alter a weather process, or issue the equivalent of a false traffic command.

Visit only with recognized local sponsorship and a city-compatible guide or mediation device. Treat every landscape feature as potentially inhabited, instrumented, or legally significant.

## Before departure

Arrange the following:

- **A local guide familiar with human tolerances.** Ideally this should be a mobile being or interface able to negotiate with towers, root networks, transit systems, and weather machinery. Permission from one resident may not constitute permission from the city as a whole.
- **An authenticated acoustic identity.** Animals and structures navigate by sound. You need a beacon that announces your location in an approved frequency range without imitating fauna, rain, pool levels, bridge signals, or civic instructions.
- **Dynamic transit access.** Distances are enormous, landmarks move, and some bridges depend on invisible support fields. Do not assume that a structure which resembles a road is currently a road.
- **Event-based forecasts.** Conventional hourly weather reports will be inadequate. Ask where rain, darkness, gravity changes, migrations, pressure releases, and flood conditions are expected to travel.
- **Biological quarantine clearance.** The city is ecologically vigorous and engineered. Both you and the city need protection from accidental contamination.

Do not schedule a tight itinerary. A ten-kilometer route may become impassable because a storm has occupied it, a grove has shifted its boundary, or an acoustic corridor has been reassigned to a migration.

## Essential equipment

### Hearing and vibration protection

The soundscape is the most immediate human hazard. Bring:

- Active hearing protection with adjustable attenuation
- In-ear protection beneath over-ear protection
- Infrasonic exposure monitoring
- A chest or torso vibration monitor
- Bone-conduction communications
- A visual and tactile alarm system
- Anti-nausea medication suitable for vibration-induced sickness

Do not use total noise cancellation everywhere. Sound here carries traffic, weather, animal, and structural information. Your equipment should reduce dangerous intensity while preserving translated warnings.

Expect headaches, nausea, anxiety, disorientation, chest resonance, and profound fatigue even when your ears are protected. Pressure-gate concussions may be felt more than heard. Spend the first part of the visit acclimating in a shielded room rather than going directly into the open city.

Silent corridors create the opposite problem. Crossing into one may make tinnitus, breathing, joints, and clothing seem unnaturally loud. Some travelers may experience panic or loss of balance at the abrupt boundary. Pause before continuing.

### Clothing and environmental protection

Wear a lightweight sealed field suit with:

- Cooling and humidity control
- Replaceable filters for spores, aerosols, and unknown biological particles
- Eye protection
- Cut-resistant gloves and gaiters
- Electrically dissipative outer layers
- Minimal exposed metal
- A decontaminable surface

The constant warmth and humidity can cause heat illness despite the weak light. Condensation will wet everything, including electronics and clothing that would normally remain dry.

Boots should have puncture-resistant soles, strong ankle support, and traction suitable for wet glass. The young substrate is sharp enough to cut skin and may shed thin, brittle flakes. Never collect those flakes barehanded; a translucent edge is still an edge.

Bring no loose seeds, untreated wood, exposed soil, or unsealed food scraps.

### Navigation and survival

Carry:

- A city-linked moving map rather than a static coordinate map
- Short-range radar or lidar approved for local use
- Passive acoustic mapping
- A tether for gravity-variable districts
- A compact flotation aid
- At least two days of water and food beyond the planned journey
- A respirator independent of suit power
- Chemical and biological water testing equipment
- A sealed emergency bivouac that can be anchored without drilling

Satellite positioning, if available at all, may tell you where you are geometrically but not what the place around you has become. A tower’s former coordinates are not evidence that it remains there.

Do not use active sonar, amplified music, sirens, or unsanctioned animal-call playback. In this city, those can amount to false signage.

## Movement through the city

Use designated silent roads and authorized transit whenever possible. Walking between distant structures is impractical and dangerous. A landscape that looks empty may contain sharp ravines, temporary waterways, concealed field boundaries, or reserved airspace.

Observe these rules:

1. **Never step onto an unsupported span until the city confirms it is active.** Some arches are unfinished; others may lower, move, or alter gravity.
2. **Do not approach fresh escarpments or ravine edges.** The rock has not weathered into stable slopes, and sudden water releases can cut through them.
3. **Do not travel along dry channels.** They can become violent streams with little local warning.
4. **Do not shelter beneath ventilation mouths, towers, bridges, or very large leaves.** All may generate wind, condensation, falling water, field changes, or biological activity.
5. **Do not assume a mathematically straight vegetation boundary is merely botanical.** It may mark a controlled zone, field edge, chemical transition, or active civic interface.
6. **Stay out of apparent vacant lots unless routed through them.** Open space is operational infrastructure.
7. **Keep your acoustic beacon active unless instructed otherwise.** In poor visibility, being silent may make you effectively invisible to both animals and machines.

When a low concussion crosses the plain, crouch, protect your head, and wait for your guide’s signal. Vegetation bending in sequence can reveal the wave’s approach.

If foil-winged flocks rise nearby, stop rather than running. Their mass movement may be a navigation response, not aggression, and panicked motion could push you into their flight corridor.

## Weather and flooding

Weather here is mobile geography. Ask not “Will it rain today?” but “Which rain is approaching this route?”

Localized showers may form around you with little regional warning. More dangerous are compact storm columns and the temporary river system that follows major events. Carry a sensor that listens for the pitched chord of approaching rain and translates it into direction and intensity.

At the first flood warning:

- Leave channels, basins, low plazas, and transit underpasses.
- Move to a certified elevated refuge, not merely the nearest bridge.
- Do not cross new water, even if shallow.
- Expect emerging aquatic animals and pursuing predators.
- Remain away from temporary pool margins, where spawning, grazing, and hunting concentrate.

Do not drink local water. Pools may contain larvae, luminous microbial mats, engineered signaling organisms, or dissolved products from the young substrate. Boiling alone may not make it safe.

Lightning within a storm column may move slowly, but that does not make it harmless. Do not stop to watch from exposed ground.

## Biological precautions

Assume that no plant or animal is edible and that no apparently passive organism is safe to handle.

Broad leaves, flowers, fungi, root curtains, and pools may be parts of the city’s sensing and computation. Touching them could be less like touching a wild plant and more like interfering with a public terminal.

Particular cautions:

- Do not enter a grove when its root hum changes suddenly.
- Do not stand in a meadow during a soil pulse; synchronized opening may involve electrical, chemical, or mechanical discharge.
- Avoid airborne pale hunters, especially when they begin venting steam or descending.
- Give amphibious grazers broad access to water.
- Never separate young animals from a temporary pool.
- Avoid luminous algae unless local authorities explicitly permit contact.
- Do not follow small browsers into dense growth; they may use routes unsuitable for human bodies.

After every excursion, undergo spore removal and full decontamination. Monitor for rashes, respiratory irritation, fever, unusual photosensitivity, persistent auditory sensations, and changes in sleep or time perception.

## Civic etiquette

The city’s governing intelligence is distributed. You may never meet a single person who can explain everything happening around you. Practice conservative etiquette:

- Ask before recording or replaying local sounds.
- Do not alter shorelines, channels, puddles, or vegetation boundaries.
- Do not leave markers, cairns, stakes, or buried objects.
- Do not sample water from the archival lakes.
- Do not release drones into air used by migratory fauna or cloud harvesters.
- Do not assume an unoccupied room is unused.
- Do not speak loudly into resonant shafts or ventilation structures.
- Pack out all waste, including shed biological material where feasible.

The seven civic lakes are an archive, not a park. Wading, boating, skipping stones, or even producing repeated waves could damage or rewrite information.

Likewise, a bridge lowering in front of you is not necessarily an invitation. It may be responding to a herd, a root message, or weather elsewhere.

## The central mirror basin

An ordinary human should view the basin only from an authorized observation site, at the distance specified by local guides.

Do not:

- Walk onto the mirror
- Throw anything across it
- Fly a drone over it
- Touch the molecular water layer
- Approach the pillars
- Collect rising seeds or condensation
- Shout, broadcast, or test echoes at the rim
- Remain during the pillars’ convergence cycle or storm formation

Birds refusing to cross is sufficient warning. The alteration and delayed return of sound suggests that the basin does not obey ordinary acoustic—or possibly causal—behavior. If you hear your own voice, a companion’s voice, or an emergency message returning from around the circumference, do not act on it without independent verification. Record it passively and leave interpretation to specialists.

When animals freeze, pools stipple, or spores begin rising in columns, withdraw immediately along the designated route. These are large-area transition signs.

Do not attempt to photograph the oldest pillars repeatedly from changing angles without permission. Their unstable appearance may reflect field effects capable of confusing rangefinding, balance, memory, or instrumentation.

## Emergency behavior

If separated from your guide:

1. Stop moving.
2. Leave your approved identity beacon active.
3. Switch navigation equipment to passive mode.
4. Move only if you are in a channel, exposed migration route, active vegetation boundary, or marked field hazard.
5. Do not follow unverified voices or echoes.
6. Wait for an authenticated silent corridor or transit response.

If gravity changes, get low, attach to a certified anchor, and keep limbs close to the body. Do not tether to plants, raw stone flakes, bridge railings, or a tower.

If a grove boundary begins moving toward you, retreat perpendicular to the boundary along the mapped evacuation vector. Do not try to outrun it through the vegetation.

If your acoustic beacon fails, use a city-approved visual distress signal rather than shouting or sounding an alarm.

## Psychological preparation

The scale will be difficult to process. Structures may be kilometers apart but still belong to one civic room. A plaza may have weather instead of pedestrians. A road may be a strip of silence through overwhelming noise. The city can appear abandoned while responding continuously to your presence.

Expect to feel watched without observers, lost despite visible landmarks, and physically insignificant in spaces designed for migrations and atmospheric events. You may also find the silence zones emotionally intense after hours of continuous vibration.

The safest mindset is neither “urban tourist” nor “wilderness explorer.” Think of yourself as a small, slow guest moving through the active organs of a vast organism. Announce yourself properly, touch almost nothing, trust no apparent emptiness, and let the city determine when there is room for you.
