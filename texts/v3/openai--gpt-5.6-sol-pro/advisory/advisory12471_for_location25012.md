## Preparing to visit the basin town

This is not a wilderness, but neither is it a city you can treat as inert scenery. Nearly every surface is alive, load-bearing, water-managing, or under stored mechanical strain. The central rule is simple:

**Assume the ground, walls, roofs, and street furniture are parts of one working organism.**

### Before crossing universes

Arrange entry through the town authorities, preferably with both a local host and the **Office of Strain**. Send them:

- Your approximate body mass and luggage weight.
- The dimensions of any cart, chair, mobility aid, or equipment.
- The materials in your clothing and gear.
- A complete list of seeds, foods, medicines, cultures, wooden objects, soil residues, and living organisms you carry.
- Your atmospheric, dietary, and medical requirements.

Do not assume that familiar-looking air, water, fungi, or plants are biologically compatible. Have the atmosphere and local water tested for human respiration and consumption. Bring a compact respirator and a closed drinking supply until compatibility is confirmed.

Biological quarantine will matter more than customs duties. The town’s ecology is unusually closed and largely performs animal functions by mechanical means. An introduced insect, fungal spore, bacterium, or seed could do serious damage. Clean boots, seams, pockets, bags, and hair before departure. Avoid untreated wood, cork, natural-fiber stuffing, potting soil, fresh fruit, loose spices, and anything capable of germination. Do not bring a pet.

If your transit method produces heat, radiation, corrosive exhaust, or significant vibration, do not arrive inside the basin. Use a designated point on the gravel flats and walk or take approved transport in.

## What to wear

Wear light, layered clothing suitable for cool dampness rather than heavy rain:

- A breathable water-resistant outer layer.
- Warm underlayers for mornings and winter visits.
- Gloves for cold iron railings and rough plant surfaces.
- Soft-soled, broad-tread shoes with good grip.
- Gaiters or close cuffs to keep fungal fibers and wet debris out.
- Eye protection if visiting factories.
- Hearing protection only for specific machinery; otherwise you need to hear strain warnings.

Avoid narrow heels, spikes, crampons, and hard-edged luggage wheels. The ground is firm but yielding, and puncturing it may damage water channels or living structural tissue. Smooth, broad soles distribute weight better. Check them after long walks: fine roots may catch in tread, and carrying local fragments out of the basin may be prohibited.

Bring a small hand towel, spare socks, and waterproof pouches. Condensation is more persistent than rainfall. Benches, rails, doorframes, and walls may all be wet even in apparently clear weather.

Do not wear dangling straps near vine elevators, root trams, belts, flywheels, or pressure machinery.

## Pack lightly

The town’s streets change subtly, doorways may narrow, and upper rooms are reached through structures that were not designed around standardized luggage. A soft bag is better than a rigid case. Keep your load compact enough to carry without dragging.

Useful items include:

- A dimmable flashlight with a warm or red setting.
- A paper notebook or an approved offline device.
- A local map issued that day.
- Chalk or marking tape only if the Office of Strain authorizes it.
- A sealed water bottle.
- Oral rehydration salts.
- Antifungal skin treatment and your usual allergy medication.
- A compact sitting pad, since the ground is often damp.
- A personal air and water monitor, if available.
- A whistle for emergencies—but use it only when necessary.

Do not hammer stakes, attach suction mounts, drive screws, carve initials, or tape things to walls. Adhesives can block pores; punctures can enter water vessels; even a harmless clamp may redirect growth.

## Arrival and orientation

Enter by a recognized gate or embankment path. Do not descend the undercut basin walls or follow a pipe as a handrail. The embankment is stable under ordinary use, not necessarily under climbing.

Your first stop should be the Office of Strain or a visitor station. Obtain:

1. A current street and load map.
2. The day’s closed routes.
3. Rain-state and pressure-release schedules.
4. Instructions for alarms and evacuation.
5. Approved lodging.
6. Permission before visiting factories, pressure groves, nurseries, cisterns, or roof levels.

Static maps are only approximations. A street that was open yesterday may be bowed, tightened, or raised today. Two buildings may have grafted, changing addresses and legal boundaries. Use landmarks, but confirm them: walls and courtyards can slowly alter shape.

Walk near the center of established dark-grained streets unless signs direct otherwise. The grain in the ground records habitual traffic and is usually the safest route. Pale, newly exposed tissue, fresh scoring, bulges, taut roots, iron collars, and areas marked by survey pins should be given a wide berth.

The slight yielding underfoot is normal. A rhythmic lift, sudden warmth, cracking, or tightening beneath both feet is not. Step calmly toward a worn route and notify a local official.

## Water etiquette

Water behavior is civic behavior here.

Never pour liquid onto the ground without permission—not drinking water, tea, wash water, disinfectant, or melted ice. A small quantity in the wrong place may contaminate a cistern, trigger growth, interfere with pressure storage, or introduce chemicals into the town’s circulatory system.

Use the correct drains. First runoff, clean water, kitchen water, industrial water, and nursery water may all have separate channels. Ask before washing clothing or equipment.

Never:

- Block a channel with a bag or foot.
- Drink from curled leaves, moss wells, hollow roots, or visible cisterns.
- Pick condensation-catching leaves.
- Open a valve or “help” a dripping pipe.
- Seal a leak yourself.
- Cover wet ground with a waterproof sheet.

After rain, expect local delays. Courtyards may swell, doors may jam, elevators may change speed, and pressure machinery may enter a different operating state. Do not force a door that has tightened in its frame; it may be structurally loaded.

## Moving around town

The root tram may feel unsettling because the rail itself moves in a muscular wave. Board only when directed, keep your feet clear of the edge, and secure loose belongings. Do not step onto a root-rail to test whether it is moving.

Vine elevators should be treated more like suspended lifting equipment than ordinary lifts. Their speed and stopping point may vary with moisture. Stand centrally, do not grab the living traction elements, and wait for an attendant’s signal before entering or leaving.

Allow generous travel time. Machines and infrastructure operate slowly by design. Trying to hurry them is both rude and dangerous. A lift that appears stalled may simply be between contraction phases; a factory press that has not moved for twenty minutes may still be fully energized.

Avoid shortcuts across open green intervals. A quiet square may be a nursery, a water-storage surface, a pressure field, or a future construction zone. The absence of fences does not mean the space is unused.

## Industrial safety

The factories are quiet enough to encourage dangerous complacency. Their machinery may make little noise while holding enormous stored force.

The greatest hazards are:

- Slow presses with crushing force.
- Counterweights descending through concealed shafts.
- Tensioned vines and roots.
- Pneumatic releases.
- Belts that move only intermittently.
- Floors that change level as trunks swell.
- Transparent leaf roofs that become slippery and fragile after rain.

Stay behind marked lines and never lean against a beam, trunk, pipe, or wall in an industrial area. Do not assume stationary machinery is de-energized. Ask specifically whether it is **relaxed, restrained, and drained**, not merely stopped.

A low groan like a timber ship is often normal. A sharp sequence of cracks, rapidly tightening wire, lifting floor grain, sudden pipe movement, or workers becoming abruptly silent should be treated as an emergency. Follow locals immediately. They understand the distinction between ordinary strain and impending release better than a visitor can.

## Lodging

Choose an upper chamber in a registered guest house. Expect:

- Cool, high rooms.
- Persistent dampness below.
- Living walls that may be slightly warm or irregular.
- Pipes emerging directly from the structure.
- Bark-tile floors.
- Modest lighting.
- Small changes in doors or fittings overnight.

Do not wedge doors open. Do not drive hooks into walls. Keep luggage away from damp seams and ventilation pores. Ask which surfaces are shelves, which are access panels, and which are living tissue.

If a door does not open in the morning, do not shoulder it. Moisture may have tightened the frame. Use the call system and wait until the structure relaxes or a caretaker trims it.

Some people may find the nearly imperceptible movement disturbing. The town creaks, warms, tightens, and settles, especially at night. If you are prone to motion sensitivity or claustrophobia, request a room near an open courtyard rather than one deep inside a grafted building.

## Health considerations

The main ordinary risks are damp exposure, slipping, mold or fungal allergy, cold stress, and unfamiliar food or water. The town’s pale fungal tissue is integral to waste processing, so avoid unnecessary contact and clean minor cuts promptly.

Bring all essential medication. Local pharmacies may understand human residents but not your universe’s formulations or physiology.

If the root mat is biologically incompatible with you, use approved barrier footwear and avoid bare-handed contact. Conversely, your skin microbiome may be a threat to the town. Observe whatever washing, clothing, or quarantine protocol locals prescribe.

Food may be plant- and fungus-heavy, with few animal products. Confirm nutritional compatibility rather than assuming that resemblance implies safety. Avoid eating decorative growth, roof membranes, metallic seeds, or anything collected from a water channel.

## Social conduct

The residents are accustomed to structures being collective, conditional, and alive. Ask permission before treating any part of the town as property.

Good conduct includes:

- Waiting rather than forcing.
- Reporting changes rather than repairing them yourself.
- Yielding to surveyors and maintenance crews.
- Respecting temporary closures.
- Asking before photographing industrial interiors, strain maps, nurseries, or water systems.
- Referring to a building as a supporting body when locals do, rather than merely as real estate.

Do not joke about cutting the town back, uprooting it, paving it, or “cleaning up” its weathered forms. Erosion is part of local design, and visible age is not necessarily neglect.

Never pick leaves, scrape moss, remove seeds, or take a fragment of wall as a souvenir. Some apparently dead pieces may still be connected to the basin. Buy only certified sterilized objects intended for export.

The scarcity of animals is culturally significant. If an animal enters the town, do not approach, feed, chase, touch, or photograph it aggressively. Stand back and follow local instructions. Such an event may become part of the Bestiary, and your interference would be remembered longer than you expect.

## Best time to visit

A mild dry interval is easiest for a first visit. Streets and doors are more predictable, roofs are less slippery, and the industrial quarter is easier to tour.

A visit just after rain is more beautiful and more operationally complex. The leaf roofs turn translucent, water sorting becomes visible, pressure groves swell, and the town’s hidden mechanics reveal themselves. Expect closures and strict supervision.

Winter offers silver condensation, low steam, and warmth rising faintly through the root mat, but mornings are cold and engines are slow. Bring insulation that tolerates dampness.

## Emergencies

Learn the local signals before setting out. Do not rely on sirens; warnings may use bells, wire vibrations, lamps, pressure knocks, or workers stationed at intersections.

If directed to evacuate:

- Leave luggage behind.
- Stay on designated dark-grained streets.
- Do not run across green open areas.
- Avoid pipes, rails, iron collars, and taut roots.
- Move toward the embankment or assigned rigid refuge.
- Do not shelter in a doorway, under a suspended beam, or beside a cistern.

If you become lost, remain on a traveled street and wait at an open courtyard or staffed platform. Do not follow pipes underground or enter a culvert.

## The right frame of mind

Visit slowly. The town’s most important motions occur over minutes, days, or generations. Spend time standing still in a square, watching condensation assemble on ironwork or waiting for a press to complete one stroke. Listen for the root strain under traffic. Notice how a gutter becomes a branch, how masonry survives because roots hold it, and how every apparent ruin has already begun another civic function.

You are not visiting a city overgrown by nature. You are visiting a city whose architecture, utilities, industry, and ecology have become inseparable. Behave less like a tourist in a botanical garden and more like a guest standing inside a patient, inhabited machine—one that drinks rain, remembers footsteps, and will quietly rearrange itself after you leave.
