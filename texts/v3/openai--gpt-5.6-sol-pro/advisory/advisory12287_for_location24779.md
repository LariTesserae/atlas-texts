This is not a wilderness in the usual sense. Treat it as a single exposed organism that is also a bog, a factory, an electrical system, and a region of unstable causality. An ordinary human should not visit alone, camp on it, or rely on conventional mountaineering practice.

The safest visit is a short, sealed, closely supervised excursion from a hovering or dimensionally anchored base.

## Before departure

### Establish a return that does not depend on local geography

Do not place your only portal, vehicle, or extraction beacon directly on the plateau. The ground can grow around machinery, reproduce forms carried by electricity, and revise the sequence in which events occurred.

Your return system should be:

- Anchored from outside the plateau’s reality.
- Operable remotely by someone who never enters the anomalous region.
- Connected by dielectric fiber rather than conductive wire.
- Capable of extracting the entire party without requiring them to reach a named place.
- Fitted with a sterile transfer chamber and an emergency severance mechanism.

Names, coordinates, and landmarks may cease to refer consistently to their objects. Give personnel tactile identity markers and cryptographic transponders, but do not assume these are infallible. Navigation instructions should be relational and continuously updated: “return along the verified path to Beacon 3,” not “go west to the black chimney.”

The off-site team must retain the authoritative expedition clock and personnel roster.

### Keep the expedition small

A suitable surface party is three to five people. Fewer cannot manage a casualty; more produce too much pressure, heat, contamination, and confusion.

No one should go down without:

- A medical and rescue specialist.
- A causality or anomaly monitor, if multiverse technology permits.
- A pilot or extraction operator remaining off the surface.
- At least one person whose only duty is to watch the terrain rather than gather data.

Do not permit solo deviations, even when another person appears to be visible nearby.

### Plan for hours, not days

Do not sleep on the plateau. There is no inert campsite, and prolonged contact gives the substrate time to react to body heat, weight, waste, electromagnetic emissions, and repeated movement.

A first visit should last no more than two to four hours. Set a hard external recall time. If local and external clocks disagree, obey whichever indicates that more time has passed.

## Personal equipment

Each traveler should wear a positive-pressure environmental suit with:

- Full skin and respiratory isolation.
- Spore, pollen, aerosol, and volatile-organic filtration.
- Active heating and anti-hypothermia layers.
- A smooth, nonporous exterior that can be discarded.
- Broad detachable footplates to distribute weight.
- Electrically insulating inner layers and a rounded dissipative outer shell.
- No sharp projections that could encourage upward discharge.
- Hearing protection that still permits directional awareness.
- Eye protection capable of reducing violet and bioluminescent glare.
- Continuous body-camera, ECG, temperature, and neurological monitoring.

Carry independent mechanical, electronic, and off-world synchronized clocks. Each person should also carry an analog written record sealed inside the suit. Record observations immediately; memory may not preserve their proper order.

Do not bring open flames, exposed heating coils, loose batteries, or unshielded transmitters. The buried conductive network may treat electrical patterns as instructions or anatomical templates.

Use low-emission equipment and optical communication where possible.

## Moving over the surface

Assume every apparent rock is tissue.

Do not test the ground with spikes, hammers, drills, crampons, or ice axes. Do not drive stakes. What resembles shale may peel, what resembles scree may contract, and an apparent cliff may be the exposed grain of a living structural organ.

Walk with:

- Broad, slow steps.
- Several meters between travelers.
- No jumping or synchronized marching.
- Frequent pauses to watch whether the surface wells, tightens, pulses, or changes color.

Follow routes used by the long-legged herds only cautiously. Their paths may indicate pressure-bearing vascular seams, but their bodies and weight distribution are not human. Never step merely because an animal did.

Avoid:

- Clear or translucent mats.
- Pools of any size or orientation.
- Blue-flashing cracks.
- White domes.
- Respiratory chimneys.
- Overhangs and cliff margins.
- Parallel grooves that may open as prepared folds.
- Any surface that responds repeatedly to footsteps.
- Places where droplets remain suspended.
- Terrain visible in two incompatible forms.

A lightweight pressure-distributing rescue raft should accompany the group. It should rest on wide flexible pads without anchors. Use it during medical treatment, whiteout, or local ground instability, but do not mistake it for a permanent safe platform.

## Weather and electrical activity

The rain is not merely weather. It participates in the plateau’s circulation and may move through anomalous directions or times.

Expect rapid hypothermia despite apparently mild air temperatures. Keep suits sealed. Never drink local water, even if it appears clear. Water may contain spores, conductive tissue, dissolved signaling compounds, or material from another sequence of events.

Treat the first blue flashes in exposed roots as an evacuation warning. Other warning signs include:

- Animals freezing simultaneously.
- A deep intake felt through the soles.
- Sudden synchronization among distant chimneys.
- Membranes drawing taut across basins.
- Pools trembling without wind.
- Tracks appearing before their makers.
- Reflections showing terrain absent from the horizon.
- Thunder preceding an upward flash.
- Any mismatch between external and local clocks.

Do not shelter beside columns during storms. They are breathing organs and likely electrical junctions. There is effectively no safe natural lightning shelter. Return to extraction before the storm fully develops.

## Living things

Do not classify local organisms too confidently. “Plant,” “animal,” “machine,” and “landform” are temporary roles here.

Do not:

- Touch, feed, collect, mark, or restrain fauna.
- Approach animals that imitate engines, voices, alarms, or familiar calls.
- Follow a flock into a crack.
- Stand between a herd and a flowering dome.
- Enter a chimney to escape weather.
- Interfere with beetles around electrical wounds.
- Approach an animal that resembles a previously injured or missing individual.

Predation may not end at death. A killed organism can be incorporated into the plateau and produced again in altered form. For a human, this means death could become biological and informational contamination rather than a discrete event.

Weapons should be a last resort. Prefer smooth barriers, suit-mounted air jets, bright nonpatterned deterrent light, and retreat. Injuring a local organism may activate surrounding tissue or cause the plateau to reproduce elements of the encounter.

## White domes and production events

Maintain several kilometers’ distance from any white dome that is swelling, lifting, or showing violet light. Its opening can produce:

- Ground-striking petals.
- Fountains of stored water.
- Concussion.
- Mass animal convergence.
- Pollen and aerosol release.
- Sudden local electrical activity.

If a dome begins opening, move crosswind only if the route is verified. Otherwise return directly to extraction. Do not stop to observe the interior.

Likewise, leave any basin that begins draining through multiple openings, inhaling, raising fins, warming, or manufacturing larvae. Mass production events can fill the air, change visibility, attract predators, and alter the basin’s local reality.

Seed capsules may be building-sized explosive organs. Give all swollen capsules an extremely wide berth. Hearing protection will not protect against the pressure wave of a nearby rupture.

## When sequence begins to fail

The first rule is: do not investigate the contradiction.

If a capsule is both exploded and unopened, a ridge both fallen and standing, or a companion appears in two places, stop advancing. Do not choose the more convincing version. Do not cross between versions of the terrain.

The group should:

1. Establish physical contact through short nonconductive straps.
2. Report exact personnel count to the external controller.
3. Compare body telemetry and clock readings.
4. Move only along a route still confirmed by the outside anchor.
5. Refuse instructions from any local duplicate of a person, beacon, or vehicle.
6. Extract immediately.

If two apparent versions of a companion exist, do not allow either to approach the group. The off-site team should perform identity verification, but even successful verification is not proof: the plateau can carry form through electrical patterns. Extract separately if possible and maintain indefinite isolation until the discrepancy is understood.

Do not attempt to “repair” incompatible terrain. The plateau grows connective tissue between contradictions; intervention may make you part of that tissue.

## Medical emergencies

Any suit breach is an immediate mission abort.

Local exposure may involve more than infection. Spores, pollen, conductive fluids, or living fibers could preserve and reproduce neural or anatomical patterns. Flood the outer breach with an inert sealant, place the casualty on the rescue raft, and extract without removing the suit.

A person who loses consciousness while in direct contact with the ground should be treated as potentially neurologically exposed, even if there is no visible wound.

If someone dies, recover the body immediately if this can be done without causing additional casualties. Do not leave human tissue, blood, hair, or equipment behind. If recovery becomes impossible, sever all conductive links and quarantine the entire expedition. Any later local appearance of that person is not to be accepted as a rescue.

## Contamination control

Nothing should be taken from the plateau, including:

- Water.
- Apparent stones.
- Dead organisms.
- Shed membranes.
- Pollen.
- Seeds.
- Air samples outside sealed sacrificial containers.
- Equipment that cannot be sterilized or destroyed.

All waste must remain sealed. Do not urinate, spit, discard food, or vent untreated suit atmosphere onto the surface. Human chemistry may become raw material for a temporary species.

After extraction:

- Isolate travelers from the home universe.
- Remove outer suits robotically.
- Incinerate or dimensionally quarantine disposable layers.
- Monitor for electrical anomalies, shared dreams, duplicated memories, pollen growth, and unexplained tissue structures.
- Compare each traveler’s memories separately before allowing them to speak with one another.
- Keep recordings offline until checked for self-propagating biological or informational patterns.

Quarantine should be measured in weeks, not hours.

## The escarpments

Do not approach the plateau’s outer edges.

The ground there is wet, vertically exposed living tissue crossed by root cables and waterfalls. Visibility is poor, distances are indeterminate, and enormous partial entities may ascend from below. Their appearance causes broad changes in the plateau’s behavior: fauna gather, chimneys quiet, and buried engines slow.

If this happens while the party is nearby, do not watch from the edge. Withdraw inland by the verified route, minimize electrical emissions, and prepare for extraction. A limb becoming a waterfall does not mean the event has ended.

## The most important attitude

Do not treat the plateau as hostile. Hostility implies that it distinguishes you clearly enough to oppose you. It may instead experience a traveler as pressure, weather, nourishment, injury, electrical form, or a new anatomical possibility.

Move lightly. Repeat as little as possible, because repeated shapes become organs. Leave no pattern the plateau can practice. Do not walk the same short path many times, pulse transmitters rhythmically, arrange equipment in regular arrays, or perform repetitive mechanical actions on the ground.

Above all, remember that erosion is how this place thinks. A footprint, a cut, a washed-away fiber, or a body incorporated into the substrate may become part of its thought process.

The safest visitor leaves before the plateau has had time to decide what the visitor is.
