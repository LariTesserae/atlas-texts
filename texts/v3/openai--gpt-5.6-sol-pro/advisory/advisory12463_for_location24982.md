## Overall assessment

Prepare for this city as you would for a warm, permanently wet industrial port, a high-altitude mining complex, and an immense animal habitat—all occupying the same streets.

The greatest dangers are unlikely to be spectacular predators. They are chronic exposure, contaminated water, slippery surfaces, machinery that never stops, slow-moving heavy animals, poorly marked drops, and becoming trapped by a migration or industrial cycle. Almost everything dangerous here is patient. Travelers should adopt the same patience.

Do not arrive casually or alone. Use a locally licensed guide for at least the first several days, preferably one familiar with both industrial access rules and animal movements.

## Before crossing over

### Verify basic environmental compatibility

Do not infer safety from the presence of local people. Before removing a sealed transit suit, obtain current measurements for:

- Oxygen and carbon dioxide
- Atmospheric pressure at plateau elevation
- Sulfur compounds, carbon monoxide, and refinery gases
- Metal-bearing aerosols
- Airborne microorganisms and reproductive particles
- Water chemistry
- Local background radiation
- Biological chirality and biochemical compatibility
- Any toxins produced by the microbial films or filter colonies

The air’s metallic taste should be treated as a warning, not local character. Even if acute toxicity is low, prolonged exposure may accumulate heavy metals in human tissues.

Arrange reciprocal quarantine. You may be vulnerable to the city’s microbes, but terrestrial skin flora, fungal spores, and gut organisms could also be disastrous in an ecosystem with no native vegetation and unfamiliar microbial checks. Clean boots, baggage, food packaging, and wastewater before entry and again before departure.

### Medical preparation

Bring or arrange:

- Vaccinations appropriate to industrial travel and contaminated water
- Broad wound-cleaning supplies
- Antifungal and antibacterial treatment selected by an expedition physician
- A heavy-metal exposure baseline and post-visit screening
- Respiratory fit testing
- Altitude-acclimatization medication if pressure warrants it
- Anti-nausea medication for barge tunnels, industrial vibration, and unfamiliar odors
- Waterproof dressings and skin-barrier cream
- Several days of personally compatible food and sealed drinking water

Do not depend on local pharmaceuticals being safe for human metabolism.

## Essential clothing and equipment

Wear clothing for warm rain rather than cold rain:

- A light, breathable waterproof shell with hood
- Quick-drying underlayers
- Waterproof trousers
- High-traction boots with ankle support and replaceable soles
- Gaiters or sealed cuffs to keep small swimmers and drainage organisms out
- Waterproof gloves, plus tougher work gloves for railings and ladders
- Eye protection
- A properly fitted respirator with particulate and chemical cartridges
- A compact oxygen and toxic-gas monitor
- Hearing protection that allows speech and alarm frequencies through
- A waterproof headlamp and backup light
- A physical map in a sealed case
- A locator beacon compatible with local rescue systems
- A short safety line for approved bridge and gantry travel
- A noncorroding walking staff for testing flooded pavement

Choose dull, easily cleaned materials. Avoid dangling straps, loose scarves, open pockets, and jewelry. The city is full of exposed moving parts, climbing animals, hooks, grilles, and cableways. Bright colors or reflective flashes may also have unintended significance to local fauna.

Carry everything in a sealed, buoyant pack. A bag dropped in a street channel may be carried into a reservoir or turbine system before anyone can recover it.

## Food and water

Assume all standing water is chemically and biologically unsafe. This includes rainwater collected from roofs: it may contain refinery aerosols, metallic dust, animal cells, and the drifting young of local species.

Do not eat microbial stains, canal animals, grazers, or local processed food until a qualified xenobiologist has established compatibility. “Edible to the locals” is not enough. Bring complete human nutrition, especially if the city has little experience with plant-derived foods.

Keep food sealed. Odor may attract gutter predators, herd animals, filter feeders, or scavenging canal life. Never discard scraps into drains.

## Moving through the city

### Use local routes, not the shortest routes

A map of streets will be inadequate. Routes can pass through turbine stations, reservoirs, animal corridors, freight yards, inhabited vaults, and half-flooded foundations without obvious boundaries. Obtain a current operational map showing:

- Active machinery
- Scheduled rail and freight movements
- Herd corridors
- Canal-crossing periods
- Flooded underlevels
- Gas-release zones
- Subsidence areas
- Protected nesting districts
- Plateau-edge exclusions
- Safe public refuges

Industrial access may be governed by timing rather than fences. An open passage is not necessarily a public passage.

### Walk slowly

Expect slick mineral films, sagging steps, polished rails, concealed channels, and shallow water that hides holes. Do not step over a resting animal unless a local explicitly directs you to. Do not assume an apparently solid “cobblestone” in a canal or flooded street is a stone.

Test flooded surfaces before committing your weight. Avoid bare-handed contact with white crusts, rust-colored deposits, or oily sheens. Some may be merely mineral; others may contain reactive industrial compounds or feeding colonies.

### Treat machinery as always live

An old mechanism may still be connected to a distant shaft, runoff channel, vibration source, or pressure system. Never lean on a wheel, chain, piston housing, cable, or abandoned-looking lever. Do not climb through a paused machine. Its cycle may be measured in minutes or hours.

Learn the local visual and acoustic warning signals. Machinery here is slow enough to seem harmless, but its force is likely enormous. A closing ore press or moving bucket chain may offer plenty of time to notice and very little opportunity to resist.

### Avoid the plateau margins in poor visibility

The cliffs descend through cloud and may not have conventional guardrails. Warm haze can erase depth cues, while runoff makes mineral terraces unstable. Visit overlooks only with local authorization, in clear conditions, and from maintained structures. Waterfalls that appear gentle can still sweep a person toward the edge.

## Conduct around animals

The safest rule is: **do not touch, feed, imitate, obstruct, or surprise anything.**

### Grazing herds

Allow them to flow around you if locals indicate that it is safe. Do not force a path through a herd. Their individual movements are slow, but their collective weight can pin a person against masonry or carry them toward drains and rail lines.

Keep away from injured or isolated animals. Six-legged hunters may be following them, and the herd itself may react to perceived interference.

### Filter colonies

Do not poke, brush, or walk directly beneath dense hanging colonies without head and eye protection. A mass closure can release fluid, particles, shed tissue, or startled young. The rolling clap may be harmless, but it can also mask alarms and destabilize someone on a ladder or wet stair.

Use your respirator near large colonies, especially during rain, molting, or reproductive periods.

### Canal animals

Never wade casually. Large armored swimmers can block exits, crush limbs against lock walls, or surge onto streets during rain. If a crossing begins, step onto a raised platform and wait. Do not try to hurry between individuals simply because they appear slow.

### Tower animals

Avoid exhaust vents, nesting shelves, and climbing routes on warm masonry. Do not reproduce their calls, whistle along with machinery, or play recorded industrial rhythms. Their vocal behavior is socially learned and tied to mechanical cycles; imitation may be interpreted as territorial, courtship, juvenile, or alarm behavior.

### Predators

Do not trust stillness. Gutter ambushers may be nearly indistinguishable from wet stone, and gliders may look like marks in the clouds. Follow local pavement markings and avoid placing hands in gutters, pipe recesses, or under railings.

If a predator is watching you, do not run unless your guide instructs you to. The local ecology favors slow assessment and deliberate pressure. Back away steadily toward an occupied, well-lit space.

## Lodging

Choose inspected accommodation in a district intended for off-world visitors. Ideally it should have:

- Rooms above ordinary flood level
- Filtered, slightly positive-pressure air
- Potable sealed water
- Secure waste containment
- No direct connection to nesting shelves or ventilation colonies
- Emergency lighting independent of the industrial grid
- Two clearly marked exits
- A local alarm interpreter
- Regular structural surveys for subsidence

Avoid sleeping in basement quarters, old foundations, or rooms beside warm exhaust shafts. Do not request that resident animals be removed merely for comfort; displacement may drive them into vents or create more dangerous behavior.

The constant low noise may make sleep difficult. Use filtered ear protection or active noise reduction, but retain an alarm channel. Complete silence may be psychologically unnerving after several days and may also indicate an abnormal machinery stoppage.

## Social and civic etiquette

The inhabitants likely regard accommodation—not eradication—as basic civic competence. Complaining that the city should “clear out” its animals will sound naïve or hostile. Animal corridors, nesting shelves, and canal schedules are infrastructure, not quaint customs.

Useful habits include:

- Yield immediately when locals step aside for a herd or machine cycle.
- Ask before entering any doorway, low arch, maintenance barge, or service bridge.
- Never move eggs, shells, molted tissue, mineral growths, or apparently discarded machine parts.
- Do not collect biological or industrial samples without permits.
- Avoid flash photography around tower colonies and dark cisterns.
- Ask before recording machinery or animal calls; some rhythms may be proprietary, ceremonial, or operational.
- Respect district differences. A species protected in one area may be controlled in another, and outsiders should not intervene.

Learn the local signs for “live machinery,” “animal crossing,” “contaminated water,” “subsidence,” “gas,” and “do not interrupt cycle.”

## Weather and timing

Rain itself is not dramatic, but it changes the city quickly:

- Microbial films become active.
- Grazers concentrate on newly stained surfaces.
- Canal animals move onto streets.
- Drainage routes deepen or shift.
- Visibility falls.
- Electrical and traction hazards increase.
- Filter colonies may open widely into the moist air.

When rain begins, do not merely put up a hood and continue. Check the local movement forecast. A route that was open ten minutes earlier may become an animal corridor.

Carry extra water despite the humidity. Warm, wet air encourages dehydration because sweat does not evaporate efficiently.

## If something goes wrong

### If lost

Stop in a raised, occupied place away from tracks, canals, gutters, and machinery. Do not descend into maintenance chambers looking for shortcuts. Use your locator and wait for a local guide or civic responder.

### If exposed to canal or foundation water

Leave the water, remove contaminated outer layers, rinse with verified clean water, and seek medical assessment. Report even minor cuts. Do not use local rainwater to wash the wound.

### If caught in a herd crossing

Move laterally only if there is an obvious raised refuge. Otherwise remain visible, protect your knees and torso, and follow local wardens’ signals. Avoid falling; the greatest danger is likely trampling or being pressed against infrastructure.

### If machinery stops

Treat a citywide cessation as an emergency. The usual noise is evidence that ventilation, pumping, cooling, and drainage are functioning. Do not enter suddenly silent tunnels or cisterns. Move to a designated public refuge and await instructions.

### If alarms are unclear

Follow local people, not your intuition. In this city, an emergency response may involve moving toward an animal-filled hall, waiting through a seemingly open route, or boarding a maintenance barge rather than climbing upward.

## How to experience the place well

Plan fewer destinations than you normally would. Travel by district and by cycle rather than by clock. Spend time on a maintained upper bridge during light rain, with a guide who can identify the machinery rhythms and animal calls. Watch a herd crossing from above. Visit a public reservoir gallery rather than an active intake. Take a guided maintenance barge through inspected foundation chambers, but never an informal one.

The city will reward attention more than motion. At first it may look like an oppressive expanse of wet stone and slow industry. With time, its order becomes legible: each side arch, grille, pause in freight traffic, replaceable wall facing, and nesting shelf records an old negotiation.

The correct traveler’s posture is neither fear nor casual confidence. It is respectful patience. Nothing here is truly inert, nothing is completely separate from anything else, and almost every safe passage depends on allowing some older, slower process to finish first.
