## Overall assessment

This basin is physically survivable and socially inhabited, but it is an unusually serious **entrapment and identity hazard**. Its danger is not starvation, warfare, or obvious catastrophe. The danger is that it allows experience while refusing cumulative change. You may be able to walk, eat, work, make friends, and attempt to leave indefinitely without ever producing the condition called “having left.”

Do not treat the returning roads as ordinary loops in geography. Assume they express a local law: **departure does not become an enduring fact**.

An ordinary traveler should visit only with an extraction system anchored outside the basin and operated by people who remain outside it.

---

## 1. Arrange extraction before anything else

Your return plan must not depend on:

- following a road out;
- retracing your route;
- leaving cairns, paint, chalk, cut marks, or dropped beacons;
- constructing or repairing a portal after arrival;
- measuring elapsed days;
- exhausting supplies as a cue to turn back;
- local inhabitants recognizing that your visit is temporary;
- a device left unattended continuing to retain a novel configuration.

The basin gently abolishes innovations and restores disturbed arrangements. A portal frame assembled inside may become ordinary poles and stones. A beacon may return to your pack or lose its programming. A trench, anchor point, or marked threshold may be found pristine after attention shifts.

Use all of the following if multiversal transit technology permits:

1. **An externally powered portal or retrieval line**, with its essential machinery outside.
2. **A fixed extraction schedule controlled from outside**, not by your watch.
3. **Continuous monitoring by an external operator.**
4. **A dead-man retrieval condition:** if communication is lost, extract immediately.
5. **At least two independent exit mechanisms** based on different principles.
6. **A short initial exposure**, preferably measured in outside time as less than one hour.
7. **A physical safety tether whose far end remains outside the basin.**

Keep your return token, portal key, or locator in direct possession and under frequent observation. In this place, “I set it down for only a moment” may be operationally equivalent to losing it.

Before entering fully, test the boundary:

- Send in an expendable object carrying synchronized clocks.
- Alter it in a simple way inside and retrieve it immediately.
- Leave a second object unattended and compare the result.
- Test whether recorded data survives removal.
- Test whether a living subject’s memories and bodily changes persist outside.
- Confirm that the portal remains visible and traversable from both sides.

If the basin causes the entry aperture to migrate, close, become locally ordinary, or lead back into the basin, abort the visit.

---

## 2. Keep the visit short

The absence of sunset makes subjective duration unreliable. Smoke, water, animal movements, work rhythms, and even provisions may repeat without providing a dependable clock.

Set a conservative limit controlled from outside. On the first visit, do not sleep there.

If later visits include an overnight stay, use rotating watches so that:

- the extraction equipment is always observed;
- someone regularly verifies names, mission purpose, and departure procedure;
- nobody follows a road beyond immediate line of sight;
- nobody mistakes local routine for proof that only one normal day has passed.

Do not extend the trip merely because food remains abundant or you do not feel tired. Neither is evidence that little time has elapsed.

---

## 3. Clothing and physical equipment

The climate is cool, damp, and exposed. Dress for a high mountain basin even though severe weather appears absent.

Bring:

- layered wool or synthetic clothing;
- a waterproof, breathable shell;
- warm hat and gloves;
- sturdy high-ankle boots;
- cut-resistant gaiters;
- spare socks sealed against moisture;
- eye protection for windblown grit;
- a compact first-aid kit;
- antiseptic, dressings, splinting material, and personal medications;
- a helmet if approaching cliffs, quarries, or scree;
- trekking poles, used cautiously on sharp stone;
- insulated sitting pad;
- simple respirator for kiln smoke, ash, or dusty workshops.

The scree is exceptionally sharp and has not been naturally rounded. Ordinary soft leather can be sliced. Inspect footwear frequently while keeping it in hand or sight; do not assume damage or repairs will accumulate in a predictable way.

Avoid climbing the black cliffs. The lack of landslide scars does not prove the rock is safe in the moment. A fall can still occur even where its consequences do not become part of the landscape.

---

## 4. Food, water, and medicine

Carry your own initial food and water even though local resources appear inexhaustible.

Local spring water is clear, but clarity is not sterility. Filter and disinfect it until testing establishes that local microbes are harmless to travelers. The city may remain at equilibrium because its inhabitants and ecology belong to that equilibrium; you may not.

Eat local food only with permission and in modest amounts. Harvesting does not reduce abundance, but it remains socially meaningful labor and property. “It grows back” is not consent.

The principal medical uncertainty is whether the basin:

- heals injuries by restoring bodies;
- preserves injuries because they happened to a visitor;
- repeatedly reasserts an injury without allowing healing;
- resets medication levels;
- prevents physiological adaptation;
- restores pathogens as quickly as the immune system fights them.

For that reason, travelers with insulin dependence, seizure disorders, unstable heart conditions, severe allergies, or medications requiring precise timing should not join early expeditions.

Before a long visit, establish through remote or nonhuman testing whether digestion, drug metabolism, wound healing, sleep, and infection proceed normally.

---

## 5. Navigation

Within the city, navigate by prominent existing features:

- named springs;
- established gates;
- sacred stones;
- market halls;
- watch platforms;
- distinctive ridgelines.

Do not rely on newly placed markers. Maps may remain useful if kept on your person, but any annotations made inside could disappear, revert, or become ambiguous.

Never travel alone on an outward road. Roads through the passes apparently return by another gate regardless of direction. This may involve:

- curved space;
- topological closure;
- erased transitions;
- memory reconciliation;
- or the basin refusing an enduring state of absence.

A traveler who sets out should carry a companion and remain within a strict externally monitored radius. Do not attempt to “beat” the loop by leaving the road, crossing a ridge, following the river underground, or climbing above the rain veil. Those attempts add exposure, falls, hypothermia, and cave hazards without addressing the underlying law.

If you unexpectedly return by another gate:

1. Stop.
2. Do not immediately try a second route.
3. Report exact memories while they are fresh.
4. Compare clocks and recordings with the external team.
5. Proceed directly to extraction.

---

## 6. Choose equipment the basin is likely to tolerate

Simple equipment is preferable. Bring devices that work without needing to be assembled or modified locally.

Useful items include:

- mechanical compass;
- sealed electronic recorder;
- multiple watches using different mechanisms;
- paper notebook and graphite pencils;
- preprinted observation forms;
- handheld environmental sensors;
- compact binoculars;
- sealed camera with write-protected storage;
- passive identification tags;
- simple cordage and blankets.

However, do not assume records made inside will remain changed. Record observations redundantly:

- speak them over a live link to an external recorder;
- have the outside team transcribe them in real time;
- carry local copies;
- repeat key facts verbally during extraction.

Treat any advanced tool as temporary while inside. A sensor may continue functioning in your hand yet revert when set down. Software updates, field repairs, custom configurations, improvised antennas, and newly assembled mechanisms are especially suspect.

Bronze, copper, wood, stone, ceramic, bone, and cord are established local materials, but copying local technology does not guarantee persistence. The basin seems to preserve forms that “belong,” not merely materials.

---

## 7. Conduct among the inhabitants

Approach as a guest, not as a rescuer.

The inhabitants do not necessarily experience their condition as imprisonment. Avoid announcing that they are trapped, frozen in time, primitive, or in need of progress. Their culture has adapted around exactness, recurrence, and the discovery of forms that can endure.

Good gifts are likely to be:

- food shared and consumed in company;
- songs, stories, and performances;
- labor offered under local supervision;
- wool, resin, or other materials in familiar forms;
- respectful copies of established objects, if invited.

Poor gifts include:

- advanced weapons;
- engines;
- seeds from other ecologies;
- self-replicating technology;
- plans for radical political or industrial reform;
- monuments intended to prove you were there;
- inventions whose disappearance would humiliate their recipient.

Ask before photographing shrines, underground streets, workshops, council halls, or burials. Do not excavate. The city explicitly refuses excavation, and repeated attempts may be regarded as sacrilege even if the ground restores itself.

When trading, pay fairly despite renewable abundance. Value here may attach less to scarcity than to correctness, social obligation, skill, ritual placement, and remembered form.

A particularly respectful opening might be:

> “We come briefly from beyond the basin. We do not wish to alter your foundations. We ask permission to enter, observe, trade, and depart.”

Do not promise that you can take residents elsewhere until you have tested whether a person can cross the boundary and remain outside. A failed rescue could be psychologically devastating or could place both traveler and resident in an unstable transit condition.

## 8. Psychological preparation

The city may initially feel peaceful: no decay, no depletion, little noise, abundant food, and an apparently permanent afternoon. Over time, that peace can become deeply disorienting.

Possible effects include:

- loss of urgency;
- inability to estimate duration;
- compulsive attempts to create something permanent;
- frustration when records or constructions revert;
- fixation on finding a “true” exit;
- derealization;
- grief over actions that occur but leave no mark;
- temptation to remain because ordinary consequences seem suspended.

Carry a written statement prepared before entry containing:

- your name;
- home reality;
- names of trusted people;
- purpose of the visit;
- planned extraction time;
- explicit instruction not to extend the mission from inside.

Read it aloud at scheduled intervals. Pair travelers and have each check the other for altered priorities, resignation, or obsessive behavior.

Remember that meaningful experience need not leave a physical mark. Trying to force permanence is precisely the struggle this place defeats most effectively.

---

## 9. Scientific precautions

The highest-priority questions are not geological but causal:

- What counts as “attention leaving” an object?
- Does observation by a camera count?
- Does external observation count?
- Can information accumulate in human memory?
- Do scars, fatigue, pregnancy, illness, or aging accumulate?
- Does an object altered inside retain the alteration once outside?
- Can an inhabitant leave?
- Can a visitor’s presence become part of the city’s established form?
- Does repeated visiting make the visitor harder to extract?

That last question is critical. The basin may incorporate recurring people as readily as it incorporates buildings. If locals begin saying that you have “always” occupied a particular room, or if your equipment develops an apparently ancient local storage place, extract immediately and suspend further visits.

Do not conduct destructive experiments on residents, animals, buildings, or terrain. Restoration is not proof that no suffering occurred. Events occur even where change does not accumulate.

---

## 10. Warning signs requiring immediate extraction

Leave at once if:

- the entry point is no longer visible from its expected location;
- outside communications repeat, lag, or contradict themselves;
- clocks agree too perfectly after separate disturbances;
- your written mission statement changes or appears locally aged;
- locals claim to remember you from before your arrival;
- your supplies become indistinguishable from local goods;
- an injury recurs after treatment;
- you cannot remember the appearance of your home;
- you feel no concern about missing extraction;
- companions disagree on whether anyone has slept;
- a road returns you to the city without a perceived turn;
- a device ceases to have a recognizable manufactured purpose;
- the outside team reports that no time is passing, or far more time is passing than you experience.

If ordinary extraction fails, do not begin building a new exit. Stay together, remain near the last known boundary, maintain continuous observation of essential equipment, and communicate outward for rescue. Random travel is likely to return you to the city while creating only the experience of danger.

---

## Final recommendation

Visit only as part of a professionally supported, externally anchored expedition. Wear mountain gear, carry independent food and medicine, respect local customs, and assume that every locally created route, marker, repair, or innovation may be undone.

Most importantly, go with a clear idea of what the trip is for and let people outside decide when it ends. The basin offers safety from decay, but not necessarily a safe return from permanence.
