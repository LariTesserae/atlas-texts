An ordinary human can visit, but should treat the city as a high-altitude industrial site built inside an active geographic anomaly—not as a picturesque mountain town. The greatest dangers are mundane ones compounded by unreliable space: hypoxia, cold, wet surfaces, machinery, falls, disorientation, and being confidently guided somewhere that is no longer adjacent to where you began.

## Before departure

### Arrange a local receiving party

Do not arrive without:

- A named local guide licensed for **pedestrian routes in the current season**
- A confirmed station, district, and bell code
- Lodging reachable without freight-only lifts or unguarded ladders
- A local contact who will notice if you fail to arrive
- The current pedestrian map and, if visiting during wet weather, the rainfall map

“Near the central station” is meaningless here. Specify the station’s full name, face of the mountain, operating pressure, and year of elevation. Ask your host which entrance currently leads to the lodging; the front door may not.

Have them send a physical token, stamped transit chit, or other locally recognized proof of destination. Addresses alone may be insufficient when streets can be discovered retroactively.

### Prepare for altitude

The inhabited shelves are high, cold, and thin-aired. Spend several days acclimatizing at an intermediate elevation if your physiology is human-standard. Bring any prescribed altitude medication only after consulting a clinician familiar with you.

Do not travel upward if you have:

- Unstable heart or lung disease
- A recent serious respiratory infection
- Severe anemia
- Poorly controlled vertigo
- A condition likely to be worsened by sustained vibration or interrupted sleep

Arrange supplemental oxygen through your host. Cylinders and valves should be certified for the city’s industrial environment; leaking oxygen near furnaces, slag works, or hot condensate equipment is a serious fire risk.

Learn the signs of dangerous altitude illness: worsening headache, vomiting, confusion, unusual clumsiness, breathlessness at rest, or a wet cough. The mountain’s strange acoustics and distances are not an excuse to explain away neurological symptoms.

### Insure for recovery, not merely rescue

A normal evacuation policy may promise transport to “the nearest facility,” which can become an argument when proximity has several definitions. Your coverage should specify:

- Local high-angle rescue
- Industrial-site evacuation
- Altitude treatment
- Search across discontinuous districts
- Recovery from anomalous routes
- Accommodation during prolonged transit closures

Leave your itinerary in both chronological and district order. They may not remain the same thing.

## What to bring

Pack as though for a wet alpine construction site.

### Clothing

Use layers:

- Moisture-wicking base layers
- Warm midlayers
- Waterproof, breathable outer shell
- Insulated gloves with good grip
- Warm hat that fits beneath a helmet
- Spare socks in sealed bags
- Waterproof boots with excellent traction and ankle support

Avoid loose scarves, dangling straps, wide sleeves, and jewelry. There are cables, flywheels, ladders, intake mouths, and exposed mechanisms everywhere.

Wear dark or industrial-neutral clothing rather than blue outer lights or green inspection colors. Blue lamps mark unreliable water, and green lights may indicate moving maintenance equipment. You do not want to resemble either signal in fog.

### Protective equipment

Bring or rent locally:

- A certified climbing-style industrial helmet
- A fall-arrest harness compatible with municipal anchor lines
- Two locking attachment points
- Eye protection
- Work gloves
- A compact respirator for mineral dust and smoke
- Hearing protection that reduces damaging noise without blocking bells and speech
- A headlamp with warm or white light
- Spare batteries stored warm and dry

Do not rely on noise-canceling headphones. The city communicates through bells, pipe knocks, sirens, and changes in vibration. Total acoustic isolation is unsafe.

A walking pole can help on ordinary stone, but ask before using metal-tipped poles near magnetized pipes, exposed electrical works, or luminous seams.

### Navigation and identification

Carry:

- A paper pedestrian map in a waterproof sleeve
- A separate rain-route map
- A notebook and grease pencil
- A mechanical watch
- Chalk or removable route tags approved by the city
- A whistle with a local rescue pattern
- Your lodging and guide details written in the local format
- A photograph or sketch of your intended station entrance
- A small pressure gauge if locals recommend one
- A vibration or bell-code card

Electronic positioning should be considered advisory at best. Mark your progress by named structures, bell sequences, air pressure, machinery rhythm, and staffed checkpoints—not by straight-line distance.

Wear identification on your body, not only in your luggage. Include baseline medical information, oxygen needs, home-world contact details, and explicit notice that you are unfamiliar with noncontiguous architecture.

### Food and water

Carry enough food for a delay of at least one full day, even on a short outing. A tower across a ravine may be visually close and operationally several days away.

Bring water from a municipal tap or sealed source. Never drink from:

- Temporary streams
- Blue-lit pools
- Hovering droplets
- Water entering a luminous seam
- Rain gathered near vent stacks
- Condensate pipes unless marked potable
- Flooded shafts, however clear they appear

The danger is not merely contamination. Some water does not reliably obey the contour you are standing on.

## On arrival

Move slowly for the first day. The city’s pace is deceptive: machines turn slowly, but their mass is enormous, and a “quiet” mechanism may still crush a building.

Check in with a district office or staffed transit point. Obtain:

- The day’s closed bridges and staircases
- Pump schedules
- Active bore and stamping zones
- Expected shelf shifts
- Rain warnings
- Newly opened or withdrawn streets
- The nearest altitude clinic
- The current meaning of local bells

A route used yesterday may be physically present but unsafe today. Conversely, a worn, well-lit street may exist without being accepted into the pedestrian system. Age and apparent maintenance are not proof of authorization.

Do not plan a demanding ascent immediately after arrival. Stay on a lower inhabited ledge, eat lightly, hydrate, and sleep before moving higher.

## Moving through the city

### Always use the appropriate map

Freight, pedestrian, bird, and rainfall routes are not interchangeable.

A cableway visible overhead may be carrying ore. A bridge used by goats may not support your version of gravity. A door through which workers emerge may connect only under a pressure condition you have not noticed.

Follow your guide’s exact route even when it appears indirect. Never shortcut across bare stone simply because your destination is visible.

### Count stairs—but do not resolve discrepancies yourself

Local codes require stairs to be counted from both ends. If you happen to count a staircase and obtain a different total from a posted number, back away and tell an attendant.

If the top and bottom counts agree and the stairs are closed, do not assume the closure is bureaucratic nonsense. Agreement is apparently a recognized warning condition.

### Treat unnecessary doors as safety equipment

Every building has at least one door that appears purposeless. Do not open it casually, block it with luggage, lock it, or hang clothing from its handle. It may be a contingency entrance for a district that has not yet become adjacent.

Likewise, never walk onto a spare bridge span pointed into open air. It is preparation, not a viewing platform.

### Use staffed transit whenever possible

Funiculars and cable cars are safer than improvising on stairs and ladders. On oblique elevators:

- Confirm both the destination and the expected exit face
- Stay aboard if the doors open onto bare rock, cloud, or an unannounced station
- Do not force doors
- Note the pressure and bell sequence at departure
- Let locals exit first

If the elevator emerges somewhere unexpected, remain with the car unless there is an immediate mechanical hazard. Its return route is usually a better prospect than whatever apparently nearby structure you can see.

### Respect machinery boundaries

Painted lines, chains, empty floor areas, and polished patches around machines are there for a reason. Geological-speed equipment may have movement cycles measured in minutes. A flywheel that appears stationary may already be moving.

Never:

- Step over a pipe because it looks cold
- Sit on a support column
- Touch luminous seams
- Lean against condensers
- Enter a lift shaft to inspect blue water
- Cross under a suspended load
- Stand on rails for a photograph
- Walk near an intake because it seems inactive
- Approach a pump after its warning stroke begins

When the great pumps wake, brace yourself away from railings and wait for pipe pressure to stabilize.

## Listening and city etiquette

The city pauses for sustained notes from shifting stone. Do the same.

If conversation stops, stop speaking. Stand still, watch the locals, and keep clear of glass, hanging tools, and edges. The pause is partly courtesy and partly situational awareness. Do not fill it with jokes, applause, or recordings played aloud.

Learn at least:

- Evacuation bells
- Pump-start warnings
- Lift departure signals
- Shelf-shift warnings
- The low bell announcing new ridge formation

Bird calls can imitate sirens. Do not decide for yourself that an alarm is “probably birds.” Follow workers to the assembly point. A false evacuation is embarrassing; ignoring a real warning is worse.

Keep music and private audio low. Sound travels strangely through the young rock and may return much later from an unexpected place. Avoid shouting into hollows for amusement.

## Weather and water

Fog is not merely a visibility problem. It hides edges, changes apparent distance, feeds machinery, and may coincide with altered routes.

When fog thickens:

- Clip into marked handlines
- Reduce your pace
- Keep the next route marker in view
- Do not follow silhouettes unless they answer in an agreed manner
- Expect surfaces to become slick without visible rainfall
- Avoid crossing open gantries alone

When rainfall begins, consult the rainfall map or enter the nearest staffed shelter. Do not assume water will travel downhill. Temporary streams may stop, climb, divide, or disappear into open-air seams.

A blue lamp means an unreliable water condition. It does not mean scenic pond, public fountain, or safe boundary. Give blue-lit areas a wide berth.

## Plants, food, and animals

The heat-wall orchards and market produce are likely your safest introduction to local food. The small dark fruit may taste metallic without being unsafe, but buy it from a vendor rather than gathering it. Industrial sediments are often the only soil, so contamination standards matter.

Do not eat wild plants from clinker heaps, condensate valves, or green ravines. Some may accumulate metals; others may react to vibration or close around fog—and possibly fingers.

### Small animals

- Do not feed pipe-walking goats. They may follow you onto routes you cannot use.
- Give maintenance crews time to move salamanders before entering damp works.
- Keep moths away from respirator valves and lamp vents.
- Do not swat silver insects over chasms; their apparent surface is not necessarily yours.
- Treat cliff birds as unreliable alarms, not pests.

### Cloud herds

If a large grazing-shaped animal steps out of the cloud:

1. Stop.
2. Move away from edges and traffic lanes.
3. Do not touch, feed, photograph with flash, or walk beneath it.
4. Do not attempt to step where it steps.
5. Follow local instructions quietly.

Its feet are using elevations, not surfaces. The route it demonstrates is not available to an ordinary human.

Do not stare through its body to navigate by the buildings visible within. Those structures may represent a different age or condition of the city. Note the sight if you wish, but do not act on it.

Rain tends to gather where these animals pause. Expect sudden wetness and new blue-lamp restrictions afterward.

## Lodging

Choose lodging in an established lower district with:

- A staffed entrance
- Interior access to food and medical help
- Supplemental oxygen
- Handrails and emergency lighting
- A room away from lift shafts, stamping mills, and resonant support columns
- Clearly marked ordinary and contingency doors

Ask which doors and windows may be opened. A window that looks onto the same courtyard each evening is preferable to one facing an active gap.

Sleep may be difficult. The city is loud, but conventional earplugs can hide alarms. Use locally approved filtered protection or a room with vibration-linked alarm lights.

Place boots, helmet, warm layers, map, respirator, and identification beside the bed. A shelf shift or pump incident may require a quick move to an internal refuge.

## If you become lost

Do not continue simply to make progress. In this city, movement can increase topological rather than geographic distance.

Use this procedure:

1. Stop in a stable, lit place away from machinery, water, and edges.
2. Note the last staffed point, bell sequence, pressure reading, and route marker.
3. Check whether rainfall has begun.
4. Contact your guide or district control.
5. Describe what you can hear and feel as well as what you can see.
6. Do not enter an unlisted door or newly discovered street unless directed.
7. Do not follow pipes, rails, birds, goats, or apparent shortcuts.
8. Conserve warmth, oxygen, and batteries.

If you find a paved street with working utilities that is absent from your map, treat it as an active civil anomaly, not a lucky shortcut. Stay at its threshold and report it.

If a familiar structure disappears from one viewpoint, return to the last confirmed marker rather than circling around it. It may remain accessible from the side you left.

## During a shelf shift or new ridge event

A deep note sustained across the district is not necessarily an immediate collapse, but it deserves complete attention.

- Stop on firm ground.
- Keep away from windows, suspended roads, cliff edges, and unsecured machinery.
- Do not use lifts unless directed.
- Watch condensation: locals may use its behavior as a cue.
- Follow staff to a refuge if factories slow or bells answer the stone.
- Expect transport disruption lasting days or longer.

If a new dark edge begins gathering in the fog, do not join the surveyors. Freshly arrived basalt may look solid while remaining warm, incompletely connected, or gravitationally unsettled. Let rails, moss, and birds establish the order of visitation.

## The right mindset

Do not try to solve the city.

Its residents have turned impossible geography into codes, inspections, spare spans, blank pipe junctions, and redundant doors. Follow their procedures even when the explanation sounds metaphysical. To them, it is infrastructure, and practical respect will keep you safer than either skepticism or wonder.

Travel slowly. Allow enormous margins. Ask before crossing, touching, opening, drinking, or descending. Accept that a short outing may become an overnight journey. Listen when the city listens.

The best visit will not be the one in which you conquer the heights. It will be the one in which you learn to move at their pace—and return by a door that still leads home.
