A first visit to the Highwater Terraces should be treated as a deep-ocean expedition, a diplomatic mission, and an entry into a living construction site. An unprotected human would not survive there. Do not assume that because the water is warm and calm it is gentle.

## Do not arrive by open-water teleportation

The depth is sufficient to make pressure the first danger. Appearing directly in the water with ordinary diving equipment could cause immediate injury, equipment collapse, gas toxicity, or loss of consciousness. Conventional recreational scuba is wholly inadequate.

The safest options are, in order:

1. **Visit through a remotely operated body or immersion proxy.**
2. **Arrive inside a quiet one-atmosphere diving shell** rated far beyond the estimated depth.
3. **Bring a sealed, pressure-independent habitat** and establish it only at a site selected by local engineers.
4. Use saturation methods only with expert medical support and a guaranteed extraction route. Never improvise decompression in a city whose streets cross water layers of differing density and chemistry.

Your arrival system must match the surrounding pressure without releasing a violent pressure wave. It should not discharge bubbles, hot exhaust, ballast, or wash from thrusters. A noisy or turbulent arrival could damage gardens, shake mineral crusts loose, or announce you throughout the mountain.

Arrange to be met. The unfinished crown platform may resemble a landing site, but it is a garden, a civic mystery, and possibly sacred ground. Do not descend upon it without invitation. From the citizens’ perspective, objects falling from overhead may be omens from another world; arriving in the same manner could create religious and political consequences before you have spoken a word.

## Essential equipment

Your suit should be compact, flexible, and exceptionally quiet. Ordinary atmospheric diving suits may be too bulky for root-curtained passages and too rigid for buildings designed to flex.

Carry:

- A pressure-independent breathing system with several isolated redundancies.
- A silent emergency extraction beacon that does not depend on reaching the surface.
- Independent depth, pressure, temperature, salinity, and water-density instruments.
- Chemical sensors for narcotic, toxic, or oxygen-rich water layers.
- Low-power maneuvering assistance with no exposed propellers.
- Two independent buoyancy systems, each able to compensate for abrupt layer changes.
- A mechanical anchor suitable for rock, plus soft plant-safe attachment loops.
- A cutting tool accessible with either hand. Use it only for entanglement emergencies.
- Tough gloves and puncture-resistant outer layers. The young basalt and black glass are extremely sharp.
- A dim, shielded lamp, preferably adjustable through red, amber, and blue-green wavelengths.
- A tactile navigation device and inertial recorder; visual landmarks may be obscured by murk.
- A writing slate or sealed display that can show symbols and color panels.
- Closed-loop food, drinking water, sanitation, and waste storage.
- A sterile sampling kit, if sampling has been explicitly permitted.
- Medical supplies for pressure injury, panic, disorientation, punctures, burns, and toxic exposure.

Secure every item. Nothing should dangle, drift, rattle, or be capable of falling into a lower layer. Arrange your equipment so that you remain upright or deliberately neutral rather than being turned by a badly balanced tool.

Do not rely on a simple “drop weight to rise” emergency system. Buoyancy in the Terraces is layered and anomalous. Below the city, objects that ought to rise may cease rising at all. A falling traveler may not strike bottom; they may simply become a distant, motionless speck.

## Plan extraction sideways, not upward

There is no known shore, and the surface is still hundreds of body-lengths above the highest towers. “Go to the surface” is not a viable emergency plan.

Your exit should be through:

- a stable return portal,
- a pressure-safe transit chamber,
- or a vehicle parked at the same depth and attached to a known anchor.

Have at least two extraction methods, one of which works while unconscious. Program a hard depth boundary into your maneuvering system. If you cross it, the system should resist further descent without depending solely on inflatable bladders.

Never descend below locally recognized warning markers merely to look into the abyss. The people who live here understand the water better than you do, and even they lose explorers.

## Learn to move in three dimensions

There is no single street level. A square may be a marked volume with no floor. A market may hang above you, sideways from your perspective; a doorway that looks like a skylight may be its normal entrance.

Local addresses use **depth, warmth, and anchor**, not north and street number. Record directions exactly:

> three spans above the Split Vent; coldward of the red garden; beneath the fifth bell.

Ask your guide to identify:

- safe depth corridors,
- density boundaries,
- warm and cold landmarks,
- private root curtains,
- communication ropes,
- structural lines,
- and descent limits.

Move slowly across water layers. If you become suddenly light, heavy, cold, sleepy, euphoric, or confused, stop immediately and retreat along your exact previous path. Sudden drowsiness should be treated as poisoning or narcosis, not fatigue.

Do not grab the first rope you see. It may be a structural brace, a crop support, a message line, a property boundary, or a musical and civic instrument. Tugging it could send a signal across several districts.

## Be quiet

Sound carries unusually well, and quietness is both etiquette and public safety.

Before arrival:

- Pad hard components.
- Disable audible alarms in favor of tactile and visual alerts.
- Cover buckles and tool edges.
- Test your suit for pump hum, valve clicks, and joint squeaks.
- Avoid sonar unless specifically authorized.

Speak softly and at close range. Better yet, learn the local hand signs and color-panel conventions. Do not shout to attract attention, clap, strike stone, or test an echo. A dropped metal tool could ring through the foundations and be heard far below.

When a mason taps the rock and waits, remain still. The silence after the tap is part of the measurement.

## Follow the citizens during a mountain movement

The mountain is geologically active. Its movements may begin with a low vibration felt in the ribs, a deep groan, or dust lifting from ledges.

If this occurs:

1. Stop swimming.
2. Do not rush for a doorway.
3. Follow the nearest resident’s gestures.
4. Place a hand or soft brace against an indicated stable surface.
5. Keep clear of pale mineral crusts, glass columns, convex rock blisters, and taut structural ropes.
6. Wait after the movement ends; delayed fractures and drifting debris may follow.

Do not shelter inside a warm hollow merely because it appears solid. Some chambers are thin-walled volcanic blisters or active gas pockets. Do not investigate cracks with tools, lights, or released gas.

## Respect the gardens as infrastructure

Vegetation here is not decoration. It produces food, controls buoyancy, filters water, marks rooms, supports buildings, enriches enclosed water for breathing, and may have been cultivated across generations.

Never:

- cut a root to clear a path,
- puncture a gas-fruit,
- push through a root curtain without permission,
- tie yourself to a living stem,
- scrape glow-slime for a souvenir,
- move a crop across a water layer,
- or “clean” seedlings from an apparently abandoned room.

An overgrown chamber may now be a filtration grove. A vine may be carrying structural load. A gas-fruit may be holding up part of a roof.

Use screened lights around dark leaves and luminous gardens. Bright lamps may disrupt growth cycles, dazzle residents, or attract attention from open water. Ask before touching any plant, even one growing on an object that appears discarded.

Maintain strict biological quarantine in both directions. Local spores and microbes may colonize your suit, while organisms from your home world could become invasive in a place with few animals and slow ecological turnover. Keep skin covered, consume no raw local food on the first visit, and decontaminate all equipment before departure.

## Do not sample the “breathable” fruit casually

Gas-fruit are not substitute air tanks. Even if their gas supports local respiration, it may have unsafe oxygen, carbon dioxide, sulfur, or volatile-organic concentrations for a human. Pressure changes make apparently harmless gases more dangerous.

Likewise, do not drink the surrounding water. Bring your own food and water until samples have been analyzed under local pressure. Local cooking methods involving sealed pots and thermal vents are ingenious, but unfamiliar foods may contain minerals, microorganisms, or biochemistry unsuitable for humans.

Do not release your own air or waste into the city. A bubble that seems trivial to you may alter the lift of a planted roof, lodge in an enclosed chamber, or contaminate a breathing garden.

## Expect psychological disorientation

The Terraces have no horizon, no visible sky, and almost no true darkness or daylight. “Up” is a direction rather than a destination. Buildings float above narrow anchors, people pass beneath rooms, and distant ridges appear and disappear without seeming to move.

Travelers prone to claustrophobia, thalassophobia, vertigo, or panic in low light should practice beforehand in a large dark-water environment. Your suit should provide:

- a private horizon indicator,
- a body-orientation display,
- timed sleep lighting,
- tactile route guidance,
- and a simple one-command request for extraction.

Do not interpret the slowness of the place as inactivity. Plants bend over days, streets drift between visits, and masonry projects are planned across generations. Local conversation and decision-making may likewise be deliberate.

## Social conduct

Assume that precision is valued more than speed. The citizens live amid structures that must bend rather than break and a mountain whose answers may take years.

Good conduct includes:

- Waiting to be guided rather than swimming ahead.
- Asking before crossing curtains, marked ropes, or thermal boundaries.
- Treating gardeners and masons as highly skilled technical experts.
- Keeping close appointments; searching for a lost visitor is dangerous.
- Giving depth and anchor references whenever reporting a problem.
- Remaining still when a rare animal enters a public or private space.
- Accepting pauses caused by omens without ridicule.
- Avoiding confident claims about the unseen surface unless you can prove them carefully.

A small fish or spiral crab may suspend work or civic proceedings. Do not chase it for a photograph. Veils are never to be hunted, approached, tagged, illuminated aggressively, or sampled. If one passes overhead, follow local behavior and enjoy the dimming in silence.

Do not call the technology primitive. The inhabitants possess precise knowledge of pressure, layered fluids, heat, flexible construction, and controlled buoyancy. Their tools are adapted to an environment where metal corrodes, noise is hazardous, and rigid architecture fails.

## Trade and gifts

Metal is extraordinarily valuable. Do not display a large quantity casually, offer steel tools to random individuals, or introduce a new material without understanding its social effects. A pocketknife that is ordinary at home may be a dynastic treasure here.

Safer first-contact gifts might include:

- clear, pressure-stable optical material,
- quiet measuring instruments,
- nonreactive ceramic blades,
- durable fiber samples,
- sealed color panels,
- or accurate maps made in cooperation with local surveyors.

Even these should be negotiated through civic authorities. Avoid giving seeds, live organisms, adhesives, buoyant foams, batteries, or reactive chemicals. A foreign material that expands, corrodes, floats, or roots unpredictably could damage the city.

Never remove fallen objects from above without permission. Such objects may be public property, sacred evidence, or the focus of long-standing disputes about the nature of the ocean.

## Where to stay

Do not expect an air-filled guest room. Rare gas pockets are industrial resources, and creating a human-sized bubble could impose major structural and buoyancy loads.

The best lodging is a small sealed habitat attached at a site approved by local builders, with:

- soft distributed anchors,
- no rigid connection across a moving crack,
- no released bubbles,
- quiet life-support pumps,
- screened light,
- and enough reserve power to survive a geological movement.

Have the attachment inspected daily. A doorway or bridge that aligned yesterday may not align tomorrow.

## The most important rules

Remember these even if everything else is lost:

- **Never visit unpressurized.**
- **Never depend on swimming upward to escape.**
- **Do not descend beyond your guide.**
- **Cross water layers slowly.**
- **Secure every object.**
- **Make as little sound as possible.**
- **Treat plants as food, walls, lungs, property, and ancestry all at once.**
- **When the mountain groans, stop and follow the locals.**
- **Do not arrive from overhead without permission.**
- **Do not mistake stillness for safety.**

If properly prepared, the Highwater Terraces would be less like touring a drowned city than entering a patient, vertically folded civilization growing in partnership with new stone. You will spend much of the visit dimly lit, carefully balanced, and conscious of the abyss below. You may also see streets made from buoyancy, orchards hanging over black volcanic cliffs, and a city adjusting itself to geological change with a calm measured in generations.

Leave no bubbles, no loose tools, no foreign seeds, and no reason for the citizens to fear the world above.
