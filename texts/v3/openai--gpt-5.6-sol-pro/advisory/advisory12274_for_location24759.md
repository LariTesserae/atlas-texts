## Essential assessment

Treat the Crown as an active, flooded industrial organism—not as an abandoned underwater ruin.

An ordinary human cannot safely enter it in ordinary diving equipment. There is no breathable refuge, pressure may vary between adjacent compartments, the water chemistry is engineered, and almost every apparent plant or architectural feature is part of the tower’s regulation system. The greatest dangers are not predators or violent machinery, but drowning, pressure failure, contamination, sharp surfaces, abrupt buoyancy changes, entanglement, and being mistaken for a defect by the maintenance system.

## Before traveling

### Confirm the arrival point

Do not translate directly into the Crown unless you possess a current volumetric map. The district has almost no unused space; a small coordinate error could place you inside pipework, roots, a pressure wall, or the central impeller shaft.

The safest arrival is:

- inside a portable pressure-rated transfer capsule;
- in a known service bay large enough to receive it;
- well away from transparent pressure shells, maintenance arms, planted joints, and density wells;
- with a return gate that does not depend on the tower’s power or communications.

Send a sterile mapping probe first. “Recently surveyed” should mean recently by the tower’s maintenance cycle, not by local calendar years. Panels, roots, and access routes can be replaced without the overall map visibly changing.

### Do not use conventional scuba

Bring a compact rigid atmospheric diving suit or one-person micro-submersible rated substantially beyond the highest measured local pressure. A soft suit, rebreather, or liquid-breathing apparatus leaves the traveler exposed to unknown pressure gradients and engineered water chemistry.

The vehicle should have:

- completely closed-loop breathing, with no bubble exhaust;
- redundant oxygen and carbon-dioxide control;
- independent heating and cooling;
- at least double the planned life-support duration;
- smooth external surfaces with guarded joints and no loose hoses;
- puncture-resistant protection against knife-sharp terraces and mold edges;
- corrosion-resistant ceramic, titanium, or compatible polymer surfaces;
- adjustable ballast capable of responding quickly to sudden changes in water density;
- low-wake, reversible microthrusters;
- mechanical handholds and emergency anchors;
- passive optical navigation backed by low-power short-range sonar;
- inertial orientation instruments and several independent gravity sensors.

Bubbles are regarded as faults here. Venting gas may activate sealant growth, maintenance arms, collection currents, or unknown alarms. Test every external fitting for bubbleless operation before entry.

### Sterilize everything

The tower contains a deliberately closed biological system with no animals and apparently no uncontrolled food web. A few ordinary skin microbes, fungal spores, insect eggs, or traces of terrestrial algae could be more destructive than a broken pipe.

Use full ecological quarantine:

- sterilize the exterior of the suit, capsule, tools, and sample containers;
- trap all waste, condensation, shed fibers, and lubricants;
- use no exposed organic rope, leather, wood, paper, food, or biodegradable packing;
- inspect all cavities for insects and spores;
- do not discharge ballast water brought from another universe;
- carry nothing with viable seeds.

On departure, quarantine yourself and the equipment again. The tower’s microbial films may be equally dangerous to an outside ecosystem, even if they are harmless to humans.

## Moving through the district

### Expect underwater “falls”

Gravity still matters, but buoyancy may change abruptly. Some shafts hold denser water that will pull an otherwise neutral vehicle downward. Others contain buoyant brines that can throw it toward the ceiling or underside of a bridge.

Before crossing any visible chemical interface:

1. stop outside it;
2. sample density, temperature, conductivity, and pressure;
3. secure a breakaway line to a verified structural member;
4. adjust ballast before proceeding;
5. cross at minimum speed, nose first, with reverse thrust ready.

Do not trust sediment as an indicator of “down.” Material may collect on walls, ceilings, or fluid boundaries. Keep a continuous gravity display inside the helmet.

A tether is useful but dangerous in the vegetation and cablework. Use a smooth, self-retracting line with remote release at both ends. Never use a long free-floating rope.

### Move slowly and touch almost nothing

The Crown has room for machinery and plants, not necessarily for a suited human. Travel speed should be closer to careful cave diving than swimming. Thruster wash can bend sensor plants, lift conductive mineral dust, draw dying leaves away from digestion grilles, or induce compensating behavior elsewhere in the tower.

Do not grip:

- clear bamboo stalks;
- mosslike pressure-joint cushions;
- roots entering ceramic sockets;
- valve-flowers;
- vines covering light panels;
- capillary bundles;
- surfaces that appear soft, swollen, translucent, or newly printed.

Use only confirmed load-bearing metal members. Even old handrails may now be biologically integrated or inaccessible. Never cut vegetation to open a route. A root may be carrying nutrients, heat, electrical signals, or structural strain data.

The young, sharp architecture is a serious suit hazard. Protect shoulders, elbows, thruster shrouds, viewports, and life-support connectors. A slow scrape can be more dangerous than an impact.

### Treat all machinery as live

The builders’ absence does not imply that the tower is dormant. Pumps, impellers, sealants, strain forests, and reconstruction arms remain active on timescales poorly matched to human attention.

A brace that appears fixed may move several centimeters while you are resting beside it. A harmless-looking grille may begin drawing in material. A panel may be scheduled for melting and reprinting. Before using any location as a staging point, observe it remotely for a full local operating interval if that interval can be determined.

Do not enter the central well. A quarter-turn of one stationary-looking impeller could trap, crush, or carry a traveler far down the tower. The displacement is gentle only at room scale; close to the blade it may be irresistible.

## Reading the tower’s behavior

The plants are part of the instrumentation. Their changes may provide more warning than your equipment.

Watch for:

- glassy bamboo clouding on one side;
- valve-flowers closing in sequence;
- leaves leaning toward the central well;
- joint moss tightening or changing color;
- black vines sealing luminous apertures;
- synchronized root or frond movement;
- mineral dust rising without nearby thruster activity.

A gradual inclination of leaves toward the shaft probably precedes or accompanies an impeller displacement. Stop moving, retract loose tools, establish neutral buoyancy, and remain clear of grilles and narrow passages until the water becomes still again.

A deep note felt in the teeth and joints may indicate anchor adjustment, shell loading, or another large structural event. Do not assume it is merely ambient sound. Suspend travel and watch the strain-stalks. If they remain clouded, pressure readings diverge, or valve closures propagate toward you, withdraw to the transfer capsule.

The soft metallic thermal ticks are likely normal. Repeated ticks, sharp acceleration in their frequency, visible joint movement, or bubbles are not.

## Instruments and communications

Ordinary depth gauges may be misleading inside a manufactured tower with pressure-separated flooded chambers. Record absolute pressure, fluid density, temperature, and local gravity separately.

Bring:

- chemical sensors for dissolved metals, oxidants, acidity, and biological activity;
- high-resolution pressure sensors at several points on the vehicle;
- an independent mechanical pressure indicator;
- inertial mapping and dead reckoning;
- low-light cameras with polarized illumination;
- a passive hydrophone;
- vibration sensors attached to the suit frame;
- a fiber-optic or tightly directional communication system.

Powerful active sonar may interfere with sensors or provoke system responses. Use it sparingly. Radio will be unreliable through water, metal, and mineral-rich fluids.

Maintain a physical or optical route record. The repeating pipework and gradual lighting changes make disorientation easy, while the tower’s fractional sway prevents any single plumb reference from remaining psychologically convincing.

## Human factors

The place will feel still, but it is not restful. Expect:

- loss of ordinary horizon and scale;
- uncertainty about up and down;
- exaggerated awareness of your heartbeat and breathing;
- unease caused by lush growth with no animals;
- the sensation of being watched despite the absence of eyes;
- impatience with systems that move too slowly to observe directly;
- sudden alarm when a structure believed stationary finally moves.

Keep a quiet internal audio channel, timed orientation checks, and a strict mission clock. Do not let the tower’s slow rhythm tempt you into extending the visit. Life-support consumption remains human-paced even when everything outside seems to operate over weeks.

Travel with a second person in a separate vehicle if possible. Do not connect both vehicles to the same tether or anchor. If one is caught by a density transition or maintenance mechanism, the other must remain free.

## Conduct in the Crown chamber

The Crown deserves the greatest caution. Its overlapping shells carry uneven external pressure, and the living strain gauges suggest that structural loads are continuously monitored there.

Remain away from the pressure windows and do not illuminate them with high-powered heating lamps. Never strike, scrape, sample, or brace against a transparent shell. Do not approach apparent cracks; a “crack” may be a layered optical seam, while a genuine defect may already be surrounded by sensitive sealing tissue.

Stay outside the central well’s exclusion zone. When one impeller begins its quarter-turn:

- halt immediately;
- face the vehicle into the induced displacement;
- close tool ports;
- release any tether that begins loading;
- avoid fighting the flow with full thrust;
- wait for the leaves, dust, and root hairs to settle.

The district appears designed around gentle forces. Introducing a sudden, violent correction may cause more damage than drifting a short distance.

## Emergency priorities

If bubbles appear from your equipment, return immediately. Do not attempt a prolonged repair in place.

If caught in a density shaft:

1. do not discard ballast reflexively;
2. determine whether you are sinking from excess density, flow, or mechanical capture;
3. use reversible ballast and controlled thrust;
4. avoid deploying an inflatable device, which may fail under pressure or create forbidden bubbles;
5. release entangled tethers before they pull you against sharp structures.

If the tower begins sealing around you, do not cut your way free unless death is otherwise imminent. Reduce movement, stop all leaks and exhaust, retract projections, and reverse along your entry path. The system may be responding to you as foreign debris, a leak, or a damaged component.

If a pressure wall shows active deformation, abandon exploration. Do not seek safety by passing through an unknown membrane or opening a hatch. Retreat to your own rated capsule and depart.

## What to bring back

Take observations, not souvenirs. Photography, fluid readings, thermal maps, and passive recordings are preferable to physical samples. If sampling is essential, collect only microscopic quantities with presterilized, hermetically sealed tools, and never sample a root socket, strain stalk, joint cushion, or active valve structure.

Above all, leave no appetite behind. The tower’s strange completeness depends on the exclusion of grazers, parasites, and uncontrolled decay. A human visitor is biologically closer to a catastrophe than to an inhabitant.

Visit quietly, remain enclosed, and depart before the tower has reason to decide what you are.
