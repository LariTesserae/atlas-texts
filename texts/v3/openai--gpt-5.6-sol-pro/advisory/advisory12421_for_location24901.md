## Preparing for the Low Galleries

Treat the Low Galleries neither as a cave nor as a ruined city. They are a living building without an exterior: warm, wet, crowded, continuously altered, and governed by water more strictly than by walls. An ordinary traveler can visit safely, but only by accepting that local people understand the place far better than any map can.

Your most important preparations are simple:

1. **Arrange a resident guide before entering.**
2. **Learn the water rules exactly.**
3. **Carry little, and carry nothing long or rigid.**
4. **Assume rain can begin at any moment.**
5. **Do not open, pry, excavate, or “investigate” the structure.**
6. **Never drink from an unapproved vessel merely because the water looks clean.**

### Before crossing worlds

If your method of travel allows reconnaissance, establish that the Galleries’ air is compatible with human respiration. The atmosphere is warm, humid, dusty, and rich in smoke, spores, animal matter, and mineral salts. Even if its oxygen content is ordinary, it may be difficult for anyone with asthma, mold allergies, chronic lung disease, or impaired mobility.

Use whatever cross-world medical screening is available. At minimum, prepare for:

- gastrointestinal infection;
- infected cuts and abrasions;
- fungal skin conditions;
- insect bites;
- smoke and dust irritation;
- diseases carried by rats, birds, goats, standing water, or unfamiliar arthropods.

Do not arrive visibly ill. A place dependent on shared cisterns and densely packed rooms has good reason to distrust strangers with fever, vomiting, rash, or coughing. If possible, undergo quarantine and disinfection on your own side rather than forcing the inhabitants to invent precautions for you.

Arrange translation in advance. Acoustic conditions will make machine interpretation less reliable: echoes overlap, rain can erase speech, and people may shout messages through multiple rooms. Learn a small set of local words or hand signs regardless of your translator:

- water;
- clean or drinkable;
- first flow, second flow, third flow;
- rain;
- danger;
- stop;
- guide;
- lost;
- cistern;
- warm vent;
- unstable wall;
- permission;
- above and below.

Directions will probably rely on sounds, shafts, trades, cistern names, and habitual paths rather than compass bearings. “Past the double echo and under the goat bridge” may be more useful than any geometric address.

### What to wear

Dress for heat, dampness, dirty water, low beams, and abrasive masonry.

Wear:

- a light, breathable long-sleeved shirt;
- durable trousers that allow deep steps and crouching;
- closed shoes with soft, high-grip soles;
- thin socks that dry quickly, plus a spare pair;
- a close-fitting cap or padded cloth head covering;
- washable gloves;
- a compact waterproof outer layer that can be put on quickly.

A hard industrial helmet may be useful in unstable districts, but choose one without a projecting brim. It will catch on beams and hanging cords. A padded cap is more practical in occupied passages unless local guides insist on stronger protection.

Avoid:

- trailing coats;
- scarves;
- loose straps;
- large backpacks;
- rigid frame packs;
- wide hats;
- jewelry that can snag;
- expensive footwear you are unwilling to soak in gutter water;
- anything that sheds toxic coatings or fibers into cisterns.

Wear your pack on the front or use a narrow shoulder bag that can be slid around your body. In many passages you will need to turn sideways. Keep both hands available for ladders, ropes, and sudden steps.

A respirator or well-fitting particulate mask is sensible near potters, fungus cupboards, demolition work, smoky kitchens, or freshly opened walls. Do not assume locals are unconcerned about dust simply because they live with it; they may know which dusts are ordinary and which mean that a wall has become dangerous.

### What to carry

Keep the load compact. A useful kit would include:

- two independent lamps;
- spare power or fuel;
- a dim red or amber light for occupied sleeping areas;
- a short personal line, clearly different from local route ropes;
- chalk or removable markers, used only with permission;
- a whistle with an agreed emergency signal;
- a sealed water bottle filled before arrival;
- purification tablets or a compact filter;
- oral rehydration salts;
- basic wound-cleaning supplies;
- antifungal cream;
- insect repellent compatible with local animals and water;
- a small towel and dry cloth;
- a waterproof pouch for documents and electronics;
- a noncontact gas detector if multiversal equipment is permitted;
- modest trade goods.

Do not bring a powerful floodlight and sweep it casually through rooms. It will blind residents adapted to alternating glare and shadow, disturb roosting animals, and make deep pockets of darkness harder to see once it is switched off. Use low, controlled light and aim it at the floor or wall.

Do not bring geolocation equipment as your only means of navigation. Satellite systems are unlikely to function, magnetic readings may be distorted by metal fittings or unfamiliar planetary conditions, and the Galleries change too often for a static floor plan to remain dependable.

A compact thermal or air-quality sensor could be valuable, but it must never replace a resident’s warning. Locals know which warm breath is an ordinary conduit and which one means that a sealed chamber should not be approached.

### Gifts and trade

Bring things that are useful without upsetting the local economy or water system. Good offerings may include:

- strong needles;
- durable thread;
- fine cord;
- small corrosion-resistant blades;
- plain cloth;
- simple medical dressings;
- sealed pigments known to be nontoxic;
- compact mirrors;
- replaceable tool edges;
- illustrated information, if welcomed.

Ask before offering metal tools. Bronze is scarce, and a visibly superior alloy may carry political implications. Do not distribute medicines, pesticides, waterproof sealants, or construction compounds casually. A substance that kills gutter larvae may also poison crabs, birds, cistern biofilms, or people downstream. A sealant applied to one drip may deprive an entire lower chamber of water.

Pay guides, hosts, and water keepers separately if that is the custom. Hospitality should not be treated as ownership of whatever a household gives you access to.

## Entering and moving through the Galleries

Secure a guide from the district you intend to visit. The Galleries are small in overall extent but effectively labyrinthine. A wall may contain an old doorway known to one neighborhood and be understood as solid by another. Routes change when a cistern fills, a floor is shored, goats occupy a ramp, or repairs close an arch.

Never wander off because you can see your destination through the pillars. The apparent direct route may cross:

- a fragile floor;
- a household boundary;
- a water channel;
- an animal pen;
- a roof over an occupied room;
- a cul-de-sac that becomes dangerous during rain.

Move slowly. Test each step visually before committing your weight. Worn brick may be polished smooth, algae can make wet tile extremely slippery, and shallow water may conceal a sudden stair or drain. Use handrails only after seeing that they are meant to bear weight. Some cords suspend baskets, regulate gates, brace timbers, or mark water rights.

Do not lean on walls. Do not sit on cistern rims without invitation. Do not move jars “out of the way.” A small vessel on a ledge may collect a household’s dependable daily drip.

Learn to duck when your guide ducks, even if you cannot yet see the beam. Generations of head contact may have polished its underside, but that does not make it forgiving.

Because sounds travel deceptively, do not chase cries or hammering on your own. Ask your guide whether the sound is nearby. A call heard from behind a wall may have crossed three chambers through a shaft.

### Navigation discipline

At the beginning of each excursion, agree on:

- the destination;
- the expected return time;
- a meeting point if separated;
- a whistle or knocking signal;
- what to do if rain begins;
- which rope, if any, is the recognized route line;
- whether you should remain still or move toward a named cistern if lost.

If separated, usually stop rather than exploring. Move out of active gutters and away from shafts, but do not pass through closed doorframes or descend stairs in search of a “main level.” There may be no main level.

Do not leave thread through passages like a maze explorer. It can obstruct traffic, interfere with animals, become tangled in mechanisms, or be mistaken for a rain route. Any marking system must be approved by the neighborhood.

## Water and rain

Water etiquette is survival etiquette.

Memorize the rule of flows:

- **The first flow cleans the shafts and belongs to the drains.**
- **The second belongs to animals.**
- **Only the third may be gathered for people.**

Do not decide for yourself that enough time has passed for the water to be clean. The distinction may depend on the particular shaft, recent dry weather, bird nesting, roof dust, and where the runoff travels.

Never:

- drink from a decorative-looking basin;
- wash hands over a gutter;
- empty your bottle into a cistern;
- step into pooled water unnecessarily;
- rinse equipment near a collection jar;
- block or redirect a drip;
- take water from a lower chamber because you saw it fall cleanly above;
- lower a container into a cistern without permission.

Your imported bottle may itself be considered a contamination risk. Ask where you may refill it and whether it must be washed first.

If rain begins, obey immediately. The warning may be only a ticking overhead or a change in the air. Residents may suddenly roll mats, lift animals, open gates, or run with vessels. Do not stand beneath a shaft to admire the spectacle. Do not block a passage to photograph it.

Move where directed, even if the chosen shelter appears lower or less comfortable. Locals know which channels overflow and which apparently dry rooms become sumps.

During heavy rain:

- fasten your bag;
- keep your lamp accessible;
- stay within arm’s reach of your guide;
- avoid doorsills and stair wells where water can deepen suddenly;
- do not grip route ropes with both hands if the current is moving debris;
- keep cuts out of floodwater where possible;
- assume the noise will make speech useless.

Afterward, wash and disinfect any wound exposed to runoff. Dry your feet carefully. Standing water may contain sewage, roof filth, animal waste, fungal spores, larvae, or unfamiliar organisms even when it is clear.

## Structural hazards

The architecture is not inert. It is a continuous compromise among weight, dampness, heat, repair, and use.

Take special care around:

- fresh cracks with pale edges;
- bulging plaster;
- newly installed props;
- sagging wicker-and-plaster partitions;
- floors that sound hollow;
- walls sweating unexpectedly;
- warm wet spots;
- areas with a strong rotten, sulfurous, solvent-like, or sharply mineral smell;
- sealed openings under active repair.

Never remove even a loose-looking tile, timber, wedge, cord, or potsherd. It may be functioning as a shim, drain lip, ownership marker, pest barrier, or visual warning.

Most importantly, **do not dig or pierce a wall for curiosity**. This prohibition is practical, not quaint. Behind a handspan of plaster may be:

- water under pressure;
- foul or oxygen-poor air;
- hot vapor;
- an animal nest;
- loose fill;
- a load-bearing void;
- another family’s room;
- a route still in use from the other side.

If tapping comes from behind a wall, do not answer automatically. Notify a resident or structural worker. A reply could initiate expectations or alarm people whose relationship to your side of the wall is complicated.

If a collapse occurs, suppress the instinct to pull debris away at once. In layered masonry, removal can enlarge the failure or release water. Call local repairers, mark the location, listen for trapped people, and touch nothing until someone who understands the load path arrives.

## Health and sensory strain

The Galleries are likely to be exhausting even without an accident. Warmth, close quarters, constant noise, repeated ducking, smoke, poor sight lines, and lack of a visible sky can produce headaches, irritability, nausea, or panic.

Plan short outings at first. Rest in a place your host identifies as appropriate. Do not occupy a shaft-lit courtyard simply because it feels pleasant; it may be needed for drying food, warming water, growing plants, or community work.

Use hearing protection sparingly. Full earplugs may protect against mallets and shrieking fowl but deprive you of warnings, water sounds, and your guide’s voice. Filtered acoustic plugs are preferable.

If you are prone to claustrophobia, practice beforehand in warm, noisy, low-ceilinged environments. The Galleries are shallow, but that fact may not comfort you. There is no visible horizon, and stairs that climb may terminate in ceilings rather than lead outside.

Protect against dehydration even though the environment feels damp. Sweat may not evaporate efficiently. Drink only approved water and replace salts during long walks.

Food should be eaten cooked and hot until your biology has been cleared for local organisms. Fungi and translucent post-rain mushrooms should be regarded as unsafe unless a knowledgeable resident serves them. Do not assume familiar-looking onions, beans, goat milk, eggs, or crabs are chemically or microbiologically identical to those of your home universe.

## Animals

Most animals will be hazards of nuisance rather than attack, but their routes matter.

- Give goats right of way on narrow ramps and beneath shelves. A startled goat can knock down jars or people.
- Do not grab the long-legged fowl, even when they appear tame.
- Keep food sealed against rats and beetles.
- Do not corner cats in wall gaps.
- Avoid touching pale drain crabs.
- Do not stand beneath swift shafts at dawn or evening without eye and mouth protection.
- Never introduce food to an unfamiliar animal population as a gesture of friendliness.

Shaft-swifts may descend in large spirals. When they do, stand still near a wall, lower your head, and let them navigate around you. Swinging your arms is more dangerous than the birds.

Ask before using insect repellent. Smoke, oils, and chemicals can alter insect behavior and affect the birds and geckos that control them.

## Social conduct

Privacy will not correspond neatly to walls. A wicker partition may be more socially absolute than a brick arch. Conversely, a room that appears enclosed may function as a public route at certain hours.

Always ask before entering:

- sleeping rooms;
- cistern chambers;
- growing bays;
- fungus cupboards;
- repair sites;
- animal platforms;
- sealed or newly reopened spaces.

Do not photograph water stores, structural weaknesses, children, shrines, or hidden routes without permission. A map can be politically sensitive in a place where access shifts and water rights pass through other people’s rooms.

Avoid describing the Galleries as primitive, buried, trapped, ruined, or unfinished. Their inhabitants are maintaining an extraordinarily complex habitat with constrained materials. “Why don’t you clear this out?” or “Where is the original building?” would reveal that you have misunderstood the place.

Do not ask casually what lies above. Residents may have theological, historical, political, or simply uncertain answers. The absence of an agreed surface is not necessarily a mystery they want a visitor to solve.

Respect repair work. Mallets and plaster are not background folklore; they are the activity that keeps rooms inhabitable. Step aside for baskets of brick, reeds, jars, tools, and water before expecting anyone to make way for you.

If invited to help, take only simple tasks under supervision: passing tiles, holding a lamp, carrying small baskets, or turning a wheel. Do not demonstrate advanced cutting tools on a structural wall.

## Where to sleep

Choose accommodation in an inhabited, well-known chamber rather than seeking solitude. Your sleeping place should be:

- above known flood channels;
- away from active gutters;
- not directly beneath a ventilation shaft;
- clear of goat routes and wall gaps used by cats or rats;
- near, but not beside, an approved latrine route;
- ventilated without lying in a cold condensation stream;
- within easy reach of your guide or host.

Keep your lamp, shoes, outer layer, and bag together. Rain or repair work may require rapid movement. Hang nothing from an existing peg without asking; it may operate a shutter or mark a drip.

Expect the Galleries to remain noisy. People work late, animals answer one another, water moves invisibly, and sound arrives from rooms you cannot locate. A familiar cloth over the eyes and filtered ear protection may help, but retain enough hearing to notice an alarm.

## Emergency priorities

If there is sudden flooding, fire, collapse, or foul air:

1. Follow local commands.
2. Do not run blindly.
3. Take your lamp and leave possessions behind.
4. Move across water paths rather than along them when directed.
5. Stay off newly wet stairs and unsupported platforms.
6. If people begin covering their mouths or extinguishing lamps, treat it as an air emergency.
7. Never enter a sealed room to rescue someone without atmospheric protection and structural guidance.
8. Do not assume “up” leads to safety.

Fire may be especially dangerous because smoke can migrate through hidden voids and shafts. Some ventilation conduits will feed flames while others carry smoke into distant rooms. The nearest open-looking shaft is not necessarily an exit.

For evacuation, use the established route chosen by residents. The Galleries’ ordinary entrance may be inaccessible during certain winds, rains, repairs, or conflicts. If your multiverse portal is portable, do not activate it in a passage, under a load-bearing arch, beside a cistern, or where displaced air and heat could harm people. Establish a cleared portal room in advance.

## The right attitude

The best visitor arrives with restraint.

You are entering a place where every surface has consequences downstream and where an object that looks abandoned may still collect water, brace a floor, guide an animal, or preserve someone’s claim. Curiosity is welcome only when separated from interference.

Spend time learning how the daylight moves. Listen for the warm vents and the characteristic echoes. Watch where residents put their feet and hands. Notice how they distinguish a harmless drip from a valued one, an old crack from a dangerous one, a public obstruction from a private threshold.

If you adapt well, the Galleries will stop seeming like a maze of debris. Patterns will emerge: light courts, wet routes, animal levels, quiet sleeping recesses, resonant cisterns, repair fronts, and the narrow social passages connecting them. You may never see farther than twenty paces, but you will begin to understand that distance there is measured less by length than by permission, water, sound, and the number of corners through which a thing must pass.

Above all, remember the local principle:

**Nothing is merely in the way. Nothing is merely underneath. And no room is only itself.**
