## First recommendation: do not enter by conventional diving

The visible fissure is no wider than a hand. An ordinary human cannot pass through it, and widening it would likely destroy the pressure, chemical, and spatial balance that sustains the chamber.

The safest meaningful visit is therefore a **remote-presence survey from the outer seafloor**. Send several small probes before considering bodily entry. If personal entry is essential, use a multiversal transfer system that places you directly inside a previously mapped pocket of open water. The transfer must target coordinates fixed to the surrounding basalt—not apparent visual distance within the chamber.

Do not phase through the enclosing rock. It is young, glassy, chemically active, and slowly changing. Material overlap would be an especially poor way to discover that the chamber’s anomalous extent applies to solids as well as water.

## Reconnaissance

Use at least three probes of different designs:

- A soft, neutrally buoyant chemical sampler.
- A tactile crawler that remains in contact with the basalt.
- A disposable free-swimming probe attached to an abrasion-resistant physical line.

Do not rely on sonar mapping. Sound acquires delayed repetitions here, and a reflected signal may return from an accumulated path rather than the nearest surface. Optical rangefinding is also unreliable around luminous films and spores. Build the initial map from:

- direct mechanical contact,
- tether payout,
- pressure,
- temperature,
- water chemistry,
- and the known geometry of the enclosing rock.

Probe for hydrogen sulfide, dissolved metals, acidity, microbial activity, volcanic gases, and temperatures within the warm seeps. Determine the ambient ocean pressure before sending a person. A cool chamber can still contain localized fluids hot or corrosive enough to damage a suit.

Reconnaissance should continue long enough to establish that the chamber is not approaching one of its chemical “seasons.” In particular, do not enter during the transparency event. The larger outlines may be harmless, but this is not a hypothesis an ordinary human should test from inside.

## Personal equipment

Wear a fully sealed, puncture-resistant pressure system. Exposed skin is unacceptable: the basalt is sharp, volcanic glass projects from the walls, and the biological films may contain unfamiliar chemosynthetic organisms or concentrated metals.

The suit should have:

- Closed-loop life support with far more reserve than the apparent size of the chamber suggests.
- Redundant mechanical and electronic clocks.
- Independent carbon-dioxide and toxic-gas monitoring.
- A smooth exterior without loose straps, buckles, or exposed hoses.
- Cut-resistant gloves with tactile feedback.
- Small, diffuse, low-intensity lights mounted close to the body.
- Passive chemical and thermal imaging.
- A physical compass or inertial reference, while recognizing that neither establishes distance.
- A reel of exceptionally thin, glass-resistant guide line.
- An automatic line cutter accessible to either hand.
- A direct hardwired data connection through the transfer boundary, if possible.
- A small reserve propulsion system that can operate without noisy cavitation or strong jets.

Avoid ordinary scuba bubbles. Bubbles in the Drowned Interval do not reliably rise, and a bubble trail will not indicate the way out. Exhaled gas may wander into folded distances, disturb the forests, or remain in the chamber for an extremely long time.

If the visit requires exposure to ambient pressure, plan decompression according to the depth of the outer seafloor—not according to the chamber’s apparent dimensions. Preferably, remain in a pressure-neutral system and avoid decompression entirely.

## Navigation

The critical fact is that **turning around does not make the return journey short**.

Ordinary cave-diving rules assume that distance already crossed can be crossed again in roughly comparable time. That assumption is unsafe here. A route that takes twenty minutes inward may take hours outward, even if nothing has visibly changed.

Accordingly:

1. Remain within immediate reach of your extraction boundary.
2. Advance only along the contiguous basalt enclosing the entrance.
3. Never swim freely into the black frond forest.
4. Do not enter pale spore clouds or mineral-rich layers. These are where distance appears to thicken most strongly.
5. Keep the guide line under low, constant tension, but do not attach it to vegetation, shelves, shells, or miniature structures.
6. Mark progress by actual life-support consumption and line payout, not by sight.
7. Withdraw after the first unexplained disagreement between instruments.

A tether is useful but not infallible. It may accumulate length inside a visually tiny gap, snag on glass, or cross a region in which the line is physically much longer than it appears. It is therefore a route indicator, not a guarantee of rescue. No outside team should attempt to haul you back forcibly; the line could cut through organisms, slice the suit against basalt, or apply force through an anomalously extended path.

Your extraction system should be based on **rock-relative coordinates and a standing return gate**, not on retracing the route. The gate should remain open for the entire visit and be stabilized from outside the chamber. Do not trust a system that must reacquire you by optical or acoustic ranging.

## Communication

Use a physical data line where possible. Acoustic communication is particularly unsuitable. Spoken words, clicks, and locator pings may return minutes later from the wrong direction or be perceived through the body rather than the ears.

Keep speech minimal. Arrange simple status signals in advance:

- one tactile pulse: well,
- two: withdrawing,
- three: immediate extraction,
- continuous pulse: line or geometry failure.

Your own breathing, heartbeat, and suit noises will become intrusive in the quiet. Delayed copies of those sounds may be mistaken for another swimmer. They are not evidence of company.

## Conduct inside

Move very slowly. The chamber is not empty water containing plants; it is a completely occupied living matrix. Even a gentle fin stroke could snap mineralized branches that have taken decades to grow.

Do not:

- touch the empty shells,
- pass through the small arches,
- step on the basalt rooms,
- press the dark depressions,
- collect fronds or nodules,
- follow the ribbon swimmers,
- enter a ring of released luminescence,
- or approach a sideways-moving bubble.

Touching one shell causes all the others to respond, and the vegetation subsequently changes orientation. Whether this is communication, pressure transmission, or a chamber-wide reflex is immaterial. A visitor should not trigger it.

The dark depressions in the miniature rooms deserve particular restraint. They seem to display a later state of the chamber, but their surfaces are physically yielding and cold. They may be sensory organs, temporal interfaces, regions of altered material state, or something for which those categories are inadequate. Observation should be entirely passive. Do not insert a probe unless it is expendable and the visit is already ending.

Do not attempt to “correct” visual inconsistencies. If a wall looks farther away than your hand says it is, believe your hand for collision avoidance and believe neither measurement for navigation. If a fracture casts a shadow toward the light, record it and move on.

The images along the ribbon swimmers should likewise be treated as unverified visual phenomena. Do not follow a reflected sky, coastline, tree line, or human figure. The swimmers may be showing inherited information, another environment, or expectations supplied by your own visual cortex.

## Abort conditions

Leave immediately if any of the following occurs:

- The pale entrance filaments stand upright.
- All nearby fronds begin leaning in one direction.
- Empty shells close.
- Your guide line pays out while you are stationary.
- A companion appears visually but is absent from the physical communications line.
- Repeated sounds arrive from within your suit or bones.
- The water becomes visibly richer in metallic particles.
- Your hand or instrument takes longer than expected to cross a small visible gap.
- The transfer boundary appears curved, displaced, duplicated, or farther away.
- Organisms begin losing opacity.
- Large stationary outlines become visible.

Do not investigate an abort condition. The Drowned Interval rewards patience on geological timescales, not human curiosity on an air supply.

## Psychological preparation

Expect profound claustrophobia without the usual sense of enclosure. You will know the chamber is only a few paces across while experiencing it as potentially unending. There is no horizon, no open ground, and almost no unoccupied surface. The vegetation will constantly brush or nearly brush the suit. Dim biological hazes will reveal objects at incorrect depths. Sounds may recur after you have forgotten making them.

Before traveling, train in:

- flooded cave environments,
- sensory deprivation,
- zero-visibility line procedures,
- delayed auditory feedback,
- and tasks performed while visual and tactile distance disagree.

Do not enter alone, but do not place two people close together inside unless the extraction system can identify them independently. A second swimmer is as likely to become another obstruction or ambiguous silhouette as a source of help. The preferred arrangement is one traveler inside, one rescue operator at the transfer boundary, and a full team outside the anomalous water.

Establish a strict mission script beforehand. Curiosity expands to fill available time, and the chamber contains inexhaustible reasons to take “one more look.”

## Contamination and return

Bring nothing back casually.

The organisms derive energy from iron, sulfur, mineral gradients, and possibly electrical differences between adjacent materials. A trace colony introduced into a ship’s plumbing, battery compartment, life-support system, or metal hull might find an abundant habitat. Conversely, ordinary surface microbes could irreversibly alter the chamber.

After exit:

- isolate the suit without opening it,
- sterilize the exterior chemically and thermally where materials permit,
- quarantine all water and sediment,
- inspect equipment for mineral growth and electrical anomalies,
- compare elapsed time across multiple independent clocks,
- and retain the traveler under observation for delayed auditory, visual, or spatial aftereffects.

Pay special attention to water trapped in seams. A visually tiny droplet may contain more internal path length than its volume suggests. Do not pour chamber water into an ordinary drain.

## What a successful visit should look like

A prudent human visit would be brief and, by adventurous standards, disappointing. You would enter near the outer margin, remain in physical contact with the same piece of basalt, observe the black fronds and one or two temperature boundaries, and leave before reaching the shells or miniature rooms. Total penetration might be less than the length of your body.

That restraint is not failure. The chamber’s apparent center may be only a few steps away in rock and functionally unreachable through water. Its deepest lesson is that proximity is not access.

Go there to witness the boundary: the pale filaments, the cold exhalation, the first impossible excess of depth between two leaves. Do not go there expecting to conquer or cross it. The Drowned Interval is slowly entombing itself, and it has no need to distinguish a visitor from one more object to be enclosed.
