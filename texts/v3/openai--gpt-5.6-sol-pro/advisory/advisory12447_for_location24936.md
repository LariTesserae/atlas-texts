## First recommendation: do not arrive unannounced

This city is survivable for an ordinary human only as a **hosted, tightly controlled expedition**. It is not a place to explore with a lamp, a map, and cold-weather clothing.

Arrange for:

- A native guide or a specialist already recognized by the city’s inhabitants and maintenance systems.
- Arrival inside a sealed, thermally buffered chamber—not on a street, roof, bridge, or open plaza.
- A current route measured in local structural vibrations, not geographic coordinates.
- Permission to emit heat, light, radio, sound, or electrical fields.
- A rescue plan that does not rely on searchlights, sirens, aircraft, or hot-running machinery.

The city moves too slowly to notice and too much for old maps to be trusted. Verify your arrival coordinates against the present basin configuration. A century-old landing point may now be inside solidifying rock or below a breeding cistern.

## Environmental protection

### Wear a sealed, cold-surface suit

The air may or may not be chemically breathable, but even if it is, breathing it directly would be unwise. It is saturated, near freezing, biologically crowded, and likely carries mineral aerosols, animal secretions, conductive droplets, and unfamiliar microorganisms or biochemical fragments.

Use a sealed suit with:

- A warm, dry internal layer.
- A nonmetallic, electrically insulating outer skin.
- A hydrophobic surface that sheds condensation slowly rather than dripping.
- No exposed conductive fasteners, tools, or wiring.
- Internal oxygen and carbon-dioxide management.
- A smooth exterior without crevices in which shell animals or mites can settle.
- Redundant pressure integrity, since sharp mineral growths and rasping fauna are common.

The suit must keep **you** warm while keeping its **outer surface cold**. Ordinary heated expedition clothing would create a conspicuous thermal target. Heat should be stored internally in phase-change reservoirs or transferred gradually to an approved heat sink inside a protected station. Do not use an exterior radiator in the open city.

Expect batteries to perform poorly in the cold. Keep power cells inside the insulated layer.

### Treat your electrical signature as a scent

The shell colonies feed on voltage differences. Your equipment should have:

- Excellent electrical isolation.
- No exposed charging contacts.
- Minimal static accumulation.
- Shielded cables and sealed connectors.
- Low-voltage, low-leakage systems.
- A way to shut down nonessential electronics without disabling life support.

Do not ground yourself to walls or paving without native supervision. The wet stone, metal, animals, and city systems form one electrical ecology. A trivial human voltage may be food, a signal, or an interruption.

Anyone with a pacemaker, neurostimulator, insulin pump, cochlear implant, or other critical electronic implant should obtain specialist clearance. The city contains strong, persistent mechanical and possibly electromagnetic fields.

## Light discipline

Bring no ordinary flashlight.

Bright light is not merely rude or conspicuous; it is a biological release mechanism and an attractant. A white lamp could become buried under animals within minutes. Camera flashes, helmet lamps, glowing screens, laser rangefinders, hot filaments, and emergency flares are all inappropriate.

If your hosts permit visual aids, use only certified equipment with:

- Extremely low intensity.
- A wavelength matched to the existing blue seams.
- Narrow shielding so no light escapes upward or sideways.
- Automatic shutdown if uncovered.
- Displays enclosed inside the helmet rather than projected into the environment.

Prefer passive imaging based on the city’s existing light. Do not introduce infrared illumination on the assumption that sightless animals cannot detect it; many local species are sensitive to heat.

Become comfortable with darkness before the trip. Practice walking, working, and remaining calm without useful vision.

## Sound and vibration discipline

The low tones are infrastructure. Do not treat them as background noise.

Do not:

- Use active noise cancellation that emits counter-vibrations into the environment.
- Operate sonar, ultrasonic rangefinders, loud motors, alarms, or acoustic beacons.
- Hammer on a wall to test it.
- Interrupt or cover a vibrating panel.
- Lean equipment against resonant ribs, columns, or floors.
- Assume a tone from an “empty” building can safely be blocked.

Use passive hearing protection calibrated to reduce harmful intensity while preserving the ability to detect changes. The lowest frequencies will enter through your feet, sternum, jaw, and suit rather than your ears. A mouth guard may help if prolonged vibration causes tooth pain, but it must not prevent communication or breathing.

Before departure, undergo a cardiac, vestibular, and hearing examination. People prone to arrhythmia, severe migraine, panic attacks, or motion sickness may find the combined resonance and variable gravity intolerable.

Learn the local warning tones. A change in pitch may indicate structural stress, water movement, animal traffic, or a gravity-management event.

## Navigation

Your eyes will be your least useful sense.

The natives travel by pressure, touch, and mapped vibration. A visitor should carry an approved interface that translates wall information into private tactile signals inside the glove or suit. Learn the basic patterns for:

- Safe passage.
- Closed route.
- Deep water.
- Dense animal traffic.
- Structural strain.
- Local gravity reduction.
- Shelter or pressure lock.
- Do not touch.
- Remain still.

Only place a hand on designated contact surfaces. Walls may be structural organs, communication channels, electrical habitats, or parts of the city’s resonant machinery.

Do not navigate by slope. A descending street is not evidence of an exit, lower district, or transit route. It may end in:

- A flooded animal chamber.
- A pressure-control cavity.
- A newly opened drainage course.
- Soft, unfinished stone.
- A district now being buried.
- A route whose lower end has migrated elsewhere.

Maps should be considered short-lived suggestions. Use a guide, a live local route record, and a physical return tether only if your hosts have approved where the tether may touch. Never clip a line casually to a parapet, shell colony, pipe, or architectural rib.

## Water rules

Assume every flooded surface is inhabited.

The water may be perfectly clear while being filled with transparent animals. Never wade because you can see the paving beneath it. In particular:

> Do not cross water that has become still.

Stillness may mean the channel is packed solid below the surface or that a large organism is suppressing visible flow.

Use raised, currently marked routes. If crossing water is unavoidable, do so in a host-provided enclosed carrier. Do not launch probes, poles, floating markers, or sampling bottles without permission.

Avoid drinking or touching local water. It is part of the city’s electrical, structural, and biological circulation, not merely runoff.

## Conduct around animals

The basic rule is to treat the animals as parts of the building.

Do not scrape, collect, feed, relocate, or step on colonies. Some animals seal cracks, distribute electrical charge, transmit structural information, or contribute minerals. Removing a handful may be equivalent to cutting a cable or opening a leak.

Specific precautions include:

- **Shell colonies:** Never pry them from walls. Keep exposed voltages away from them. If they begin accumulating on your suit, stop and notify your guide rather than brushing them off.
- **Street grazers:** Give them wide clearance. Their apparent lack of a head does not imply they cannot sense you. Do not stand between one and a shell-covered surface.
- **Ribbon swarms:** Treat a channel filled with them as closed, even if the animals seem soft or slow.
- **Bridge fliers:** Pass beneath roosts only when instructed. Avoid abrupt motion, heat, sound, or light. A mass release can knock a person down, obscure all routes, and overload a suit with impacts.
- **Six-limbed wall grazers:** Never approach one while it is listening. Its vibration may be physically harmful, and the wall it bites may be under active structural management.
- **Reflective mites and tunnel animals:** Do not respond to an infestation with lamps, heaters, pesticides, or electrical discharge.

If animals begin converging on you, do not run unless your guide directs it. Reduce emissions, remain controlled, and withdraw into the nearest approved enclosure. Swatting may damage load-bearing organisms or trigger a larger response.

## Structural and geological hazards

This is a city on actively forming terrain. Hazards do not always announce themselves with sudden movement.

Watch for:

- Soft ash-like stone that cannot support a human despite looking solid.
- Mineral ridges emerging through paving.
- Plate gaps that slowly open under distributed strain.
- Bridges lengthening or changing alignment.
- Gravity-reduction zones around failing structures.
- Roof ice released during subsurface warming.
- Areas where runoff has recently chosen a new path.
- Chambers being enclosed by growing basalt.

Never enter a district simply because it appears abandoned. Buried and enclosed districts may remain active infrastructure.

If you hear a prolonged groan from above during a warmer interval, move under designated cover. Clear ice sheets can slide from roofs with little visible warning.

Do not approach a slowly failing tower. Its collapse may take years, but the surrounding gravity fields, receiving trench, and redirected stresses are immediate hazards. Human intuition about falling structures is not useful there.

## What to carry

Keep equipment compact and externally inert. A suitable personal kit would include:

- Sealed cold-surface environmental suit.
- Redundant closed-circuit breathing system.
- Passive low-light visor.
- Host-approved tactile navigation interface.
- Passive vibration and structural-stress monitor.
- Mechanical backup compass only if local fields do not make it useless.
- Inertial and local-gravity monitor.
- Internal medical telemetry with no external alarm.
- Nonconductive hand tools.
- Self-sealing suit patches.
- Internal chemical heat storage, not external heaters.
- Several days of food and water in sealed containers.
- Closed waste storage.
- A host-compatible silent distress transponder.
- A physical written or embossed emergency code sheet.

Leave behind:

- Drones.
- Flares.
- Firearms and energy weapons.
- Open flames.
- Hot engines.
- Flash photography.
- Ultrasonic scanners.
- Chemical repellents.
- Adhesive markers.
- Rock hammers.
- Specimen jars intended for casual collecting.
- Anything disposable.

All waste, skin flakes, food residue, and damaged equipment should leave with you. The city has never supported plants; introducing seeds, spores, soil organisms, or terrestrial biofilms would be a serious contamination event.

## Emergency behavior

Agree on emergency procedures before leaving the arrival chamber.

If separated from your guide:

1. Stop moving.
2. Extinguish all unapproved light.
3. Reduce nonessential electrical and thermal output.
4. Stay out of water.
5. Do not shelter beneath a bridge roost or against an occupied wall.
6. Use only the approved tactile distress signal at a designated contact point.
7. Wait.

Do not improvise a signal by pounding, shouting, heating a surface, or broadcasting low-frequency sound. You may accidentally imitate a structural warning, interrupt a stabilizing tone, or attract animals.

If you fall into water, do not thrash. Seal the suit, minimize heat leakage, and allow the guide or rescue system to extract you. If something is moving beneath you, violent movement is unlikely to improve the situation.

If your exterior becomes covered in small animals, do not remove the suit in the field. Enter a controlled isolation lock and let local specialists determine whether the animals are feeding, sealing damage, or using you to carry information.

## Psychological preparation

The city will feel larger, darker, and more alive than it first appears.

There is no dawn by which to orient your body. You will seldom see a whole room, street, animal, or tower. Water will form on everything. The city’s lowest notes will remain in your bones even while you sleep. Apparent masonry will move when watched long enough, and apparent silence will contain millions of small clicks.

Train for:

- Extended darkness.
- Confined routes.
- Cold, wet surroundings.
- Unclear boundaries between architecture and animal life.
- Continuous low-frequency vibration.
- Long periods of waiting without visible change.
- Dependence on a guide and tactile instructions.
- Suppression of the instinct to illuminate, investigate, or touch.

Keep visits short at first. Human circadian rhythm, balance, and threat perception may degrade rapidly in a place with no daylight, no horizon, and no truly inert surfaces.

## The most important mindset

Do not think of the basin as a ruined city invaded by animals and water. It is a functioning collaboration among stone, machines, weather, and living bodies. A wall may be growing. A street may be migrating. A colony may be carrying its load. A tone may be holding a mountain in agreement with itself.

Move slowly. Emit as little as possible. Touch only when invited. Trust no route merely because it looks built for walking.

Above all, remember that to the city, an ordinary human is a bright, warm, electrically noisy, fast-moving event. Your preparation should be devoted to becoming, for a few hours, something quieter.
