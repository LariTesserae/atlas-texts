This is not a city to visit casually with a paper map and a raincoat. It is a moving hydraulic habitat whose streets, weather, animals, and utilities continually negotiate with one another. An ordinary human can probably travel there safely, but only by becoming—temporarily—another registered component of its infrastructure.

## Before departure

### Confirm basic human compatibility

Do not infer safety from the presence of inhabitants. Obtain current measurements for:

- Atmospheric composition and pressure
- Gravity
- Wet-bulb temperature and heat index
- Local day length and ultraviolet exposure
- Common aerosols, industrial gases, spores, and microbial films
- Potability standards and food biochemistry
- Human-active pathogens, venoms, and allergens
- Medical response compatibility

The equatorial warmth, permanent damp, and heavy industrial metabolism make heat stress, fungal skin problems, respiratory irritation, and contaminated-water exposure more likely than dramatic poisoning. Have any cuts, dental problems, or chronic ear conditions treated before arrival. Bring several days of your own medication in waterproof, corrosion-resistant packaging.

Arrange entry through a certified receiving station. Never emerge through an unsurveyed portal onto a roof, plaza, tunnel, or apparently vacant platform. A plaza may become a reservoir; an empty corridor may be a pressure route; a puddle may be working machinery.

### Secure a local interface

You will need more than translation. Arrange for a guide, municipal liaison, or trusted navigation intelligence that can communicate with multiple district protocols. It should provide:

- Live route geometry
- Tide and storm forecasts
- Flood-state and pressure-zone warnings
- District border status
- Human-safe food and lodging
- Transit authorization
- Local alarm interpretation
- Air, water, and surface-contamination alerts

Static maps will be historical documents by the time you use them. Download an offline emergency map, but expect ordinary navigation to depend on continuous civic updates.

Your identity, payment credentials, medical profile, and access permissions should be carried on a local-compatible beacon. In this city, being electronically invisible may cause doors, bridges, and traffic systems to treat you as debris or wildlife.

## Biosecurity

The total absence of plants is probably maintained rather than accidental. Assume that seeds, pollen, soil, untreated wood, plant fibers, and ordinary food scraps are regulated biological contaminants.

Arrive with:

- Decontaminated clothing and luggage
- No loose seeds, fruit, soil, or untreated wooden objects
- Sterile or certified food supplies
- Sealed waste containers
- A clear inventory of all living cultures and medications you carry

Do not bring a souvenir houseplant. Do not scatter crumbs for animals. Do not pour drinking water, soap, or waste into a convenient gutter. The city sorts fluids with great precision, and putting the right substance into the wrong channel may be treated as contamination or sabotage.

Expect to be cleaned both on arrival and departure. Some of the drain films and service-vault fungi may be chemically harmless to locals but highly irritating—or ecologically disastrous—elsewhere.

## What to wear

Dress for warm rain, salt, abrasion, sudden immersion, and constant transitions between humid streets and chilled interiors.

A practical outfit includes:

- Lightweight, breathable, quick-drying clothing
- A close-fitting waterproof shell with sealed pockets
- Non-slip, electrically insulating boots with ankle protection
- Gaiters or sealed cuffs against small drain animals
- Gloves for railings and wet surfaces
- Eye protection
- A brim or visor for rain and glare
- Strong sun protection for clear intervals
- A compact flotation collar or vest
- A waterproof respiratory mask with particulate and chemical cartridges
- Hearing protection that passes recognized alarms
- A bone-conduction communicator or throat microphone

Avoid loose coats, hanging straps, umbrellas, and unsecured hats. Corridor winds can lift objects, moving structures can catch them, and transit pressure wakes may be violent. Tether essential equipment to yourself with breakaway fasteners.

All electronics should tolerate salt water, condensation, immersion, and repeated decontamination. Bring redundant power storage, but have unfamiliar batteries certified; waste heat and energy storage are likely regulated.

## Noise preparation

The soundscape may be one of the greatest immediate stresses. Ordinary conversation will often be impossible in the open, and major pressure exchanges can be felt through your ribs and teeth.

Use active hearing protection, but never block all environmental sound unless your local interface can translate civic alarms into visual or haptic signals. Learn the patterns for:

- Flood sealing
- Canal traffic
- Pressure release
- Structural movement
- Storm shelter
- Animal migration
- District-border closure

Expect “quiet” rooms to feel uncanny. Engineered silence may remove the roar while leaving low vibration in the floor. Some travelers experience dizziness, anxiety, or phantom noise. Spend your first day in a moderately quiet acclimation space rather than going directly into major freight districts.

## Moving through the city

Think in terms of **water state, route state, and jurisdiction**, not merely street names.

When asking directions, include the time and expected weather. “How do I reach this address?” is less useful than “What is the human-safe route there during the next tide and storm cycle?” A destination may be accessible from above during a flood, from below at low water, or not at all while neighboring districts are disputing transit priority.

Follow these rules:

1. **Use live routing at all times.**  
   A bridge can divide, retract, or become a conduit. A building entrance may relocate.

2. **Treat markings as current instructions, not decoration.**  
   Color is used sparingly and probably carries operational meaning.

3. **Do not stand in long, clear corridors without checking traffic.**  
   Their emptiness may mean they are reserved for high-speed transit, wind, rainfall, pressure discharge, or migrating fauna.

4. **Never assume a dry ledge is pedestrian space.**  
   Shadows, wall recesses, and undersides of stairs may be leased nests, cooling surfaces, sensors, or maintenance access.

5. **Do not step on active repair material.**  
   Silver matter flowing toward a crack is machinery, not spilled metal.

6. **Keep off pavement that rises, ridges, pulses, or changes texture.**  
   It may be redistributing load or signaling a jurisdictional conflict.

7. **Use handrails cautiously.**  
   Confirm they are meant to be touched; walls and rails may carry heat, information, fluids, or electrical functions.

8. **Do not cross retracting borders.**  
   If doors begin relocating, drains reverse, or bridges withdraw, retreat into the district you came from and request a negotiated route.

A local escort is particularly important below street level. “Underground” chambers may also be underwater, pressure-managed, or intentionally flooded. Never enter a lower corridor simply because its door is open.

## Water and flooding

Rain itself may be relatively clean, but by the time water is on a roof, gutter, plaza, or wall it may belong to a specific treatment stream. Do not drink any local water without certification.

Carry:

- A sealed personal water supply
- Electrolytes
- A chemical contamination indicator
- A compact flotation aid
- A waterproof emergency light
- A cutting tool suitable for straps, not structures

During high tides, do not panic merely because a lower neighborhood floods. Controlled flooding is ordinary. Watch the locals and civic indicators. Doors will seal, floors may rise, and traffic will shift upward.

If an unexpected flood warning occurs:

- Stop moving toward lower levels.
- Follow designated upward routes, not improvised stairs.
- Keep clear of doors while they seal.
- Do not swim through streets or canals.
- Enter the nearest human-rated pressure shelter.
- Secure yourself before major gates close; pressure changes may arrive as physical blows.

Flood marks on walls are calibration records, not necessarily evidence that the place is abandoned or unsafe.

## Weather

Carry storm protection even under a clear sky. The city creates its own weather, and a short journey can cross from hard sunlight into a stationary downpour.

The most dangerous conditions are likely to be:

- Extreme wet-bulb heat before rain
- Sudden corridor winds
- Debris from unsecured cargo
- Lightning and conductive spray
- Pressure pulses from gates and transit
- Flash flooding at district boundaries
- Near-zero visibility in dense rain

If a storm begins, do not shelter under a random bridge. Bridges may separate, carry nests, or become water routes. Use a marked shelter or doorway with an active human-safety symbol. Roofs are especially hazardous because they tilt and may become roads, collectors, or spillways.

Plan shorter journeys than the distance suggests. Twelve visible kilometers of corridor may look straightforward but can involve several weather systems, district protocols, and vertical flood transitions.

## Animals

The animals are not wilderness creatures; they are participants in the city’s energy and waste economy. They may be protected, owned, semi-managed, or infrastructurally necessary.

General precautions:

- Do not touch, feed, ride, photograph at close range, or obstruct them without permission.
- Stay away from drainage grilles when rain begins.
- Do not approach warm machinery where amphibious predators sleep.
- Keep clear of ferry-beast mouths and cargo harnesses.
- Avoid standing beneath translucent airborne scavengers.
- Seal food, bloodied dressings, and metallic scraps.
- Check boots and clothing before entering sleeping quarters.

The small pale scavengers may squeeze through tiny openings. They are more likely to investigate warmth, salt, food residues, or shelter than to hunt you, but verify whether they bite or carry irritants.

Engineered midges may occur in enormous numbers near treatment basins. Use eye protection and a respirator there. Insect repellent from your home world may be ineffective or environmentally prohibited.

If animals suddenly leave an area, take notice. Their disappearance may indicate power loss, cooling failure, chemical changes, or an impending infrastructure shutdown. Follow them only if your guide confirms their route is human-safe.

## Food

There are no farms or conventional plant-derived local foods. Expect cultured tissue, microbial products, processed substrate, marine-derived material, and foods produced from industrial nutrient loops.

Do not judge safety by appearance or by another species eating it. Request food certified for baseline human digestion and check:

- Amino-acid chirality
- Toxic trace metals
- Hormone and neurotransmitter analogues
- Allergenic microbial proteins
- Salt content
- Local symbionts or living cultures
- Whether the food continues metabolizing after preparation

Bring emergency rations, but consume them only in places where your packaging and waste can be properly collected. Even organic scraps have assigned destinations.

## Lodging

Choose accommodation specifically rated for unmodified humans. It should have:

- Independent breathable-air reserve
- Controlled humidity and temperature
- A dry sleeping surface
- Noise reduction with alarm passthrough
- Flood and pressure seals
- Escape access to an upper route
- Wildlife exclusion
- Potable water certification
- A local communications node
- Manual releases you can physically operate

Avoid the cheapest deep-level rooms until acclimated. Lower corridors combine condensation, machinery chill, pressure changes, fungal scales, and psychologically difficult confinement. Also verify that your room remains a room throughout your stay; in a reconfigurable building, space-use schedules matter.

## Etiquette

The safest guiding principle is: **nothing is merely background**.

Do not sit on a conduit, lean luggage against a wall, occupy a doorway, pour out a drink, move a barrier, or use an empty alcove without checking. Every surface may have several jobs.

Other sensible courtesies include:

- Ask before recording infrastructure or negotiations between districts.
- Pay for water, shade, silence, and nesting-space access if required; these may all be leased resources.
- Do not complain that a route is inefficient because it changes. It may be serving flood control, animal passage, and structural balance simultaneously.
- Never disable a civic sensor for privacy. Rent a certified private space instead.
- Keep your beacon current so the city can route around your physical limitations.
- Do not call the fauna vermin or the microbial systems filth. Much of the city survives through them.
- Do not interpret infrastructural automation as evidence that nobody owns or cares about a place.

## Emergency behavior

If you lose connectivity, do not continue wandering. Move to the nearest marked human refuge and remain visible to civic sensors.

Carry a physical card displaying:

- Your species and atmospheric requirements
- Medical conditions and allergies
- Safe temperature and pressure ranges
- Emergency contact
- Translation key
- Consent or refusal for local medical nanotechnology
- Instructions not to immerse you in unfamiliar fluid media

If the pavement moves, walls begin folding, or bridges divide, do not try to outrun the transformation blindly. Move perpendicular to active seams if directed, keep away from closing gaps, and follow haptic instructions from your guide.

If absolute silence occurs outside a designated quiet zone, treat it as an emergency. It may indicate power failure, pressure isolation, or loss of local traffic systems. Check air quality, activate your beacon, and seek a sealed shelter.

## What the visit will feel like

Prepare psychologically for a place without relief into “nature.” There will be no park, grassy verge, treeline, or vacant edge where the city stops. Even the sea rests over foundations. The lack of plants will make the geometry feel harsh and distances unnaturally exposed. The constant wetness may make everything seem unclean even where water is precisely managed.

At the same time, resist seeing it only as oppressive machinery. Watch how a roof turns to meet a storm, how animals follow heat through the canals, how old stone has been polished by thirty centuries of use, and how traffic resumes before thunder has finished. The city’s beauty is not in monuments or skyline. It is in fit, adaptation, and uninterrupted metabolism.

Your best preparation is humility. Walk as if every puddle has a purpose, every wall is busy, every animal knows a route you do not, and the ground beneath you is listening and deciding what shape it needs to become next.
