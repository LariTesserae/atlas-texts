## Overall recommendation

Treat this as an industrial acoustic hazard, a desert expedition, and a first-contact visit simultaneously. An ordinary human can probably visit the inhabited outer districts for a short time with local guidance. Do **not** attempt the central basin, the wind courts, hidden ducts, or districts marked with red fiber.

Plan to enter near evening, stay no more than one or two local cycles at first, and retain an independent route back to the southern slit.

## Before departure

### Medical screening

The greatest immediate danger is not audible noise but low-frequency pressure transmitted through bone and soft tissue. Anyone with significant cardiac, pulmonary, vestibular, neurological, or inner-ear problems should not enter. Pregnancy and implanted electronic medical devices are additional reasons to avoid the city.

Expect possible:

- nausea and vertigo;
- headache or visual disturbance;
- irregular heartbeat;
- chest discomfort;
- dental pain;
- temporary or permanent hearing damage;
- disorientation caused by continuous vibration.

Arrange a baseline hearing test before the journey and another afterward.

### Learn nonverbal communication

Spoken language will be useless outdoors for much of the day. Before arrival, learn whatever local hand and facial signs your contacts can provide. At minimum, establish signs for:

- danger;
- stop;
- follow me;
- exit;
- pain;
- fire;
- permission;
- water;
- “I cannot understand”;
- “the vibration is harming me.”

Carry a slate or matte writing board. Screens may be difficult to read in the glare, and vibrating electronics may fail. Do not assume local pavement-drumming codes are intuitive; an accidental pattern could be interpreted as an announcement or alarm.

## Essential equipment

### Acoustic and vibration protection

Bring:

- properly fitted earplugs plus over-ear industrial defenders;
- a soft, nonresonant protective hood;
- a dentist-fitted bite guard;
- shock-absorbing boots or sandals with excellent grip;
- a thick vibration-isolating sleeping mat;
- a chest strap or support garment recommended by a medical specialist, but nothing that restricts breathing;
- a small vibration meter and sound-pressure monitor rated for infrasonic frequencies.

Ear protection alone is insufficient. Much of the city’s “sound” enters through the floor, jaw, ribs, and skull. Leave any area immediately if you experience chest fluttering, confusion, sudden fear, blurred vision, or a sensation that your organs are oscillating.

Do not wear a rigid helmet unless it has been tested for resonance. A hard shell can amplify particular frequencies.

### Heat, light, and air

Carry:

- wraparound mineral-dust goggles;
- a P100-class particulate respirator or equivalent;
- pale, close-fitting clothing with a hood;
- gloves;
- sealed electrolyte water;
- dry, packaged food;
- a passive cooling vest if available;
- skin moisturizer and saline nasal gel.

The atmosphere appears breathable, but test oxygen, carbon dioxide, and unusual mineral aerosols before removing respiratory protection. The orchards release gases in the evening, and their composition may vary.

Carry at least **five to seven liters of water per person per intended day**, plus an emergency reserve. There is no dependable local water source. Do not rely on bladder-fruit until both its pulp and internal gas have been analyzed for human compatibility.

### Fire-safe expedition kit

Fire is an existential threat there. Use only sealed, nonsparking equipment. Bring no matches, lighters, open heating elements, candles, smoking materials, or exposed high-energy batteries.

Useful items include:

- a mineral-felt fire blanket;
- sealed sand pouches;
- intrinsically safe lights;
- low-temperature chemical light sources;
- fire-resistant clothing.

Ask permission before activating any unfamiliar electronic device near warm orchard gases, kilns, ducts, or enclosed rooms.

### Navigation and access

The interior violates ordinary geometry, so do not trust GPS, compass bearings, exterior distance estimates, or inertial navigation without local confirmation.

Bring:

- a local guide;
- physical maps on tough, matte material;
- chalk or removable fiber markers, used only with permission;
- a return line for the entrance passage;
- a climbing harness and redundant anchor system;
- a mechanical watch;
- a personal locator at the southern slit and another at the external base camp.

Do not tie route lines to the city’s existing ropes. Those ropes are structural, communicative, and musical infrastructure; changing their tension may damage a bridge or alter a signal.

## Choosing the time of entry

The safest ordinary visiting period is likely **late afternoon into evening on a normal day**. The main roar is then breaking into separate currents, citizens are active, and reflected light remains adequate.

Avoid:

- midmorning through afternoon, when conversation is impossible and whole-body pressure peaks;
- the wind courts at any hour;
- the nine-day salt-blossom event until you understand its footing and chemistry;
- dry rain without a hood, goggles, and overhead shelter;
- the thirty-three-day dim cycle on a first visit;
- any forecast period when the foundational note is expected to stop.

The dim cycle may seem attractive because of reduced glare, but it is also when the silence below the grille is most clearly associated with the deliberate “inhalation.” Do not schedule scientific curiosity around that event.

## At the southern slit

Establish an external camp well away from the direct airflow. The salt flat is hot, reflective, abrasive, and visually deceptive. Secure shelters against wind and mark the hill from several directions so it can be found if tracks vanish.

Before entering:

1. Sample the outgoing air.
2. Confirm that the slit is exhaling steadily.
3. Send no loose object into it.
4. Secure clothing, hair, straps, and equipment.
5. Maintain a belayed connection until the internal footing is known.
6. Make certain someone outside knows the return deadline.

A reversal in airflow may turn the entrance into an intake. If the current changes direction, withdraw rather than investigating.

## First contact and etiquette

A visitor from another universe is, very literally, “the Outside.” Since the inhabitants believe an unfinished city attracts the Outside, your appearance may be interpreted as proof, accusation, disaster, or religious fulfillment.

Do not enter an apparently empty street and begin exploring. Wait for recognized inhabitants or intermediaries near the entrance. Keep your hands visible and allow them to decide how you are announced.

Important courtesies:

- Do not call the city empty, wasted, excessive, or unfinished.
- Do not ask why they continue building as if the practice were irrational.
- Do not offer to “complete” a structure.
- Do not touch mirrors, roof vanes, ropes, ceramic mouths, speaking bowls, plaques, or planted roots without permission.
- Never drum casually on walls or pavement.
- Do not rearrange weights, even loose-looking stones.
- Do not open sealed doors or cross red-fiber barriers.
- Treat upper rooms bearing named shutters as funerary spaces.
- Ask before photographing or recording dust patterns in a wind court; these may constitute laws, judgments, or sacred speech.

A good gift would be durable cord, fireproof textile, a nonferrous hand tool, mineral pigment, or a compact manual device that can be repaired locally. Metal is rare, so even an ordinary needle may be socially weighty. Offer gifts publicly and through a host rather than handing rare metal to an individual in secret.

It may be wise to ask for a small, harmless building task. Participating respectfully in repair could frame your arrival as contribution rather than fulfillment of a threatening prophecy.

## Water, food, and biological contamination

The city’s ecology is built around dryness. Liquid water may be biologically disruptive, culturally alarming, or dangerously valuable.

Therefore:

- drink privately from sealed containers;
- never pour water onto the stone or plants;
- do not wash in public;
- contain urine and all other waste;
- pack out food crumbs, skin dressings, and used filters;
- avoid touching vegetation with wet hands;
- do not introduce soil, seeds, insects, mold, or damp fabric.

Use full biosecurity on both arrival and departure. The silver roots, crystal-feeding plants, moss grazers, and airborne organisms may be harmless locally but invasive elsewhere. Dust from the city may also react unexpectedly with water once you leave.

Eat local plants only after chemical and biological testing. “Dry” does not mean nontoxic. Hot resins, mineral crystals, aromatic vapors, and floating-fruit gases may be irritants, narcotics, oxidizers, or incompatible with human metabolism.

## Moving through the city

Stay on inhabited routes and walk rather than ride improvised sledges. Polished floors and elastic stone can be unexpectedly slippery, especially during vibration peaks. Do not stand beneath rawhide bridges, suspended loads, mirrors, or ropeways when the afternoon pressure rises.

Empty buildings are not necessarily abandoned in the legal or ritual sense. They may belong to a household, the dead, a future generation, or the old plans. Never enter merely because a door is open.

Avoid sleeping in upper rooms. Shutters can be torn away by currents, and many such rooms contain funerary remains. Accept lodging arranged by your hosts, preferably in a low interior room away from ducts and resonant façades.

Take regular quiet-rest intervals, even if you do not feel injured. Low-frequency exposure can impair judgment before the traveler recognizes it.

## Specific hazards

### Wind courts

Do not enter. Wax ear packing and jaw bindings are ritual adaptations, not proof that the courts are human-safe. Concussive pressure can cause injury without obvious pain.

Observe only from a locally approved distance and never step into the moving dust rings.

### The central basin and grille

The basin should be considered a lethal exclusion zone.

Its hazards include:

- unbearable acoustic pressure;
- intense heat;
- high-velocity gas through the paving;
- unknown structural or dimensional behavior;
- possible biological activity below;
- religious sensitivity surrounding the added weights.

Do not approach the grille, move a millstone, insert an instrument, lower a camera, collect gas, or attempt to communicate through it. No ordinary scientific objective justifies this.

### Traveling silence

If the city suddenly becomes perfectly quiet:

1. Stop immediately.
2. Do not speak, drum, transmit sound, or activate machinery.
3. Move away from walls and ducts only if a local guide directs you.
4. Do not investigate the approaching silent zone.
5. Keep lights low and movements deliberate.
6. Follow inhabitants without argument.
7. Leave the affected district once normal sound returns.

The silence is not safety. It marks the passage of something large enough to suppress airflow and alter plant behavior inside the stone. If locals avoid the district for a season, do the same.

### Dry rain

Transparent flakes may have sharp edges and can shatter into inhalable particles. Shelter under a sound roof, wear goggles and a respirator, and do not collect specimens without permission. Material falling from the vault may be economically important.

### Salt blossoms

Do not step on them. They may cut through footwear, release dust, damage local growth, or mark active mineral pressure beneath the floor. Wait for residents to clear paths.

### Kite-creatures and moss grazers

Do not capture, feed, touch, or stand beneath the kite-creatures. Their lack of visible mouths suggests an unfamiliar metabolism.

The flat grazers are easy to crush between fitted stones. Watch where you place equipment, especially around red moss. Their pellets are useful pigment and may therefore be property rather than waste.

## If the foundational note stops

Treat a complete cessation of the roar as an emergency, even if the inhabitants merely stand and wait.

- Freeze and follow local behavior.
- Do not approach the basin.
- Do not mistake the silence for an opportunity to converse or record.
- Keep your respirator on.
- Prepare for abrupt airflow, pressure change, or an intake event.
- Stay away from loose doors, shutters, bridges, and hanging vines.
- If near the entrance and the route remains safe, withdraw only on your guide’s signal.

The reported inhalation implies that the city’s ventilation may be coupled to something with a cycle, agency, or both. A resumed note does not necessarily mean all districts are immediately safe.

## Length of stay and departure

For a first visit, remain only through one evening and quiet period, then depart before the next major rise in pressure. Extend later visits only after reviewing medical data and learning local signs.

On departure:

- submit to dust removal using dry methods;
- seal clothing and samples;
- count all tools and metal objects;
- leave no markers or route lines behind;
- verify that no plant fibers, grazers, pellets, spores, or mineral flakes are attached to equipment;
- quarantine specimens from water and ordinary soil;
- undergo hearing, cardiac, and respiratory checks.

Most importantly, leave before exhaustion makes the endless vibration feel normal. The city’s inhabitants have adapted culturally and perhaps biologically over generations. A human traveler has not. Respecting that difference is the best way to return alive—and to avoid becoming another name cut into an upper shutter.
