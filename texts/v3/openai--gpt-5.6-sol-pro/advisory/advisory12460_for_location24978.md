## Overall assessment

This is not a place an ordinary human should enter casually, or alone. The primary dangers are not the visible animals. They are:

- extreme dry heat and rapid dehydration;
- an atmosphere containing unknown concentrations of sulfur compounds, hydrogen, carbon monoxide or dioxide, and metallic aerosols;
- hot arsenic-, sulfur-, and vanadium-bearing crystal falls;
- unstable footing among sharp, heated spires;
- an unguarded drop at the open side of the cleft;
- low-frequency ground vibration capable of causing disorientation;
- contamination in both directions;
- ecological reactions to footsteps, machinery, heat, electrical fields, and vibration.

A traveler without a sealed environmental suit should remain outside the basin. Holding one’s breath, wearing a filter mask, or carrying extra water is not adequate.

## Reconnaissance first

Place your arrival point well outside the cleft, preferably on stable rock below the rim and upwind of the vents. A portal, vehicle, or transporter may itself produce vibration, electromagnetic noise, exhaust, or heat. Opening one inside the basin could provoke the entire ecosystem and might deposit moisture or foreign organisms directly onto its foundation species.

Observe for several local days before entering. Establish:

- the pre-dawn and midday temperatures;
- atmospheric composition at several heights;
- oxygen concentration and flammability;
- the timing of vent pulses;
- the frequency of mineral-needle falls;
- whether fault vibrations have recognizable precursors;
- the safest route through the spires;
- the location of the open precipice under low light;
- how long the inhabitants remain frozen after unfamiliar tremors.

Use passive instruments wherever possible. Avoid rotor drones, sonic rangefinders, hammer probes, drilling, and anything that lands repeatedly. Thermal imagers and passive optical cameras are preferable. Even a wheeled rover may be ecologically louder than a walking person because it produces continuous lattice vibration.

Do not assume silence in the air means your equipment is unobtrusive. Pumps, cooling fans, motors, suit joints, and hard-soled boots may all be conspicuous through the rock.

## Personal equipment

### Sealed suit

Wear a fully enclosed, positive-pressure environmental suit with:

- closed-cycle breathing apparatus;
- redundant oxygen and carbon-dioxide scrubbing;
- passive or exceptionally well-isolated cooling;
- a heat-reflective, chemically resistant outer layer;
- replaceable sacrificial oversuit panels;
- sealed eye protection;
- dry mechanical repair patches;
- broad, compliant, heat-resistant soles.

Do not rely on local air, even if it contains enough oxygen. Fine metallic and sulfide dust may be dangerous at concentrations too low to see, and unknown alien biochemistry should be treated as both toxic and potentially reactive.

Passive phase-change cooling is preferable to a loudly vibrating pump. Any active machinery should be vibration-isolated and tested on basalt before the visit. The suit must contain sweat and exhaled moisture completely. A leak would endanger you and create a chemically disruptive wet patch that the local salts would immediately attack.

Use intrinsically safe electronics. Hydrogen near vents may create explosive mixtures, and mineral dust can conduct electricity. Avoid exposed contacts, hot filaments, sparks, and static discharge.

### Water and medical preparation

Carry more water than the planned excursion requires, but keep it in multiple sealed internal reservoirs. Never open a drinking vessel outside the suit. Even a small spill would be an ecological event.

Before traveling, prepare for:

- high-altitude exertion;
- heat stress;
- sulfur and heavy-metal exposure;
- low-frequency vibration sickness;
- panic or vertigo in cramped, dark passages;
- emergency extraction while wearing a rigid suit.

Anyone with significant respiratory disease, heat intolerance, balance disorders, or a medically sensitive implant should not enter. The slabs’ vibrations are felt through teeth, knees, and sternum; hearing protection will not prevent that.

### Navigation and safety

The basin is dim, cluttered, and bordered by a lethal drop. Carry:

- a passive thermal map;
- an inertial navigator;
- low-intensity, non-strobing visible lights;
- a physical cliff-proximity alarm that does not use loud ultrasound;
- a line of high-temperature, chemically resistant fiber if safe anchoring is possible;
- a second, independent locator for the outside team.

A tether can save you from the precipice, but it can also scrape spires, transmit vibration, and entangle wildlife. Anchor it outside the basin through a shock-isolating system and keep it suspended or carefully paid out. Do not hammer pitons into the cleft.

## Timing the visit

Enter shortly before dawn, after the night minimum but with enough natural light to navigate. At that time:

- the spires are least hot;
- thread-hunters may be more mobile, but midday thermal conditions are avoided;
- wall colonies are less likely to be sealed against peak heat;
- metallic salts are less actively sublimating;
- the kite canopy may sit lower, so overhead clearance must be watched.

Leave well before midday. Do not make a schedule that requires crossing the basin during its hottest period. The apparent smallness of the cleft is misleading: narrow aisles, pauses, and cautious foot placement will make progress slow.

Only one person should enter at a time, with at least two people monitoring from outside. A second person immediately behind the first would double the vibration, restrict retreat, and make rescue harder.

## How to move

Assume the first footstep announces you to nearly everything in the basin.

Before crossing the boundary, stop all unnecessary motors and secure every loose buckle, tool, and hose. Communicate by text or low-power optical signals rather than speech. Speech itself may matter less than footsteps, but it can vibrate a helmet, suit frame, or rigid tether.

Step only on mapped lava high points in the established aisles. Never step on:

- black film;
- disk herds;
- mineral crusts;
- the bases of spires;
- circular walls;
- the area inside a circle;
- loose shed scales.

Use slow, flat-footed steps. Do not test the ground by stamping or tapping. Avoid touching spires for balance: they are hot, inhabited, responsive to pressure, and may carry vibrations directly to concealed hunters.

After the first step, expect the basin to stop.

Stop with it. Do not interpret stillness as safety or absence. Wait until motion resumes in widening rings before proceeding. A good rhythm may be one or two deliberate steps followed by a long pause. This will make the visit frustratingly slow, but haste would create a continuous disturbance recognizable across much of the cleft.

Do not run unless remaining still is immediately fatal. A fall among iron sulfide and ceramic spires could breach the suit, and rapid retreat might drive animals into the aisles or toward the precipice.

## The inhabitants

Do not attempt to touch, feed, collect, or attract anything.

The kite-like animals are efficient diving predators. A human is probably not prey, but reflective equipment, moving cables, loose mineral flakes, or an exposed pack could interfere with a strike. Keep your silhouette compact and do not raise poles or antennas into the canopy.

The thread-bodied hunters should be treated as invisible. They sense stress through shared stone. Do not rest a hand or knee against an occupied spire, even if nothing appears to be there.

The wall animals may release metallic reproductive material. Do not stand directly beneath dense colonies during a fault event. Their apparent lack of eyes is irrelevant; they perceive the world through other channels.

The tall, folding animals deserve an especially wide berth. Their careful avoidance of the spires suggests precise route knowledge. Never place yourself between one of them and a vibrating slab, and never block a crack from which one might emerge.

## The circles and slabs

Treat the circular structures as protected sites whether they are ruins, biological constructions, or geological formations.

Do not cross their walls. The refusal of grazers to enter, the thicker black films, and the restricted behavior of predators all indicate a meaningful boundary. It may be chemical, electrical, ecological, or cultural. A human body and suit could bridge gradients the native life keeps separate.

Do not lean against the slabs or place sensors on them. Their vibrations coordinate basin-wide reproduction. Even a light contact could alter their resonance, and sampling grooves from the surface would destroy context that may be older than any visible organism.

Photograph from outside the circle with passive equipment. Leave interpretation for later.

## If the mountain begins to vibrate

Signs may include:

- pressure in the teeth or sternum;
- a common tilt in the kite canopy;
- wall colonies closing;
- disk herds streaming into aisles;
- changes in the slabs;
- emergence of the tall animals.

If this begins while you are near the exit and have a clear route, withdraw slowly. If you are deep in the basin, running for the rim may be worse than remaining still.

Move to a previously selected recess beside an aisle, not inside a circle and not against a spire. Crouch to reduce obstruction, secure the tether, and wait. Do not lie flat on the rock; that increases both heat transfer and mechanical coupling.

Expect a reproductive storm and falling material. Keep the helmet sealed and avoid looking upward for long periods, since crystal needles may damage external optics. Let the tall animals pass. Do not attempt to record their slab contact with active sonar, contact microphones, or electrical probes during the event. Passive suit accelerometers are sufficient.

Resume movement only after the tall animals have withdrawn and normal local traffic begins to return.

## If mineral needles begin to fall

A glittering fall is not harmless weather. It is hot, chemically aggressive, and potentially capable of puncturing flexible suit components.

- Do not shelter beneath wall colonies or overhanging spires.
- Turn the helmet’s most resistant surface upward.
- Cover joints and hoses with your arms if necessary.
- Remain still rather than stumbling through obscured aisles.
- Withdraw once the fall ends, even if the suit appears undamaged.

Any visible red or violet residue should be assumed to contain arsenic and other toxic metals.

## Emergency rules

Leave immediately if:

- suit pressure drops;
- cooling becomes unreliable;
- gas alarms disagree;
- vision is impaired by mineral deposition;
- the route to the rim becomes uncertain;
- spires begin behaving differently around your equipment;
- the canopy descends enough to obstruct the exit;
- you experience confusion, chest pressure, severe nausea, or loss of balance.

Do not patch a serious breach inside the basin unless retreat is impossible. A leaking suit introduces moisture and air while exposing you to local chemistry. Use a dry clamp or mechanical patch and move toward extraction.

Do not remove the helmet under any circumstances.

## Decontamination

Build the decontamination station outside the cleft and away from runoff or wind that could carry wash products back into it.

Initial cleaning should be dry and contained:

1. Vacuum or electrostatically collect loose crystals in a sealed chamber.
2. Remove the sacrificial outer layer robotically.
3. Inspect folds, boot treads, tethers, and radiator surfaces.
4. Seal all debris as chemically toxic and biologically unknown.
5. Only then use liquid decontamination, within a closed recovery system.

Nothing collected should be casually returned to your home environment. The black film and its grazers are adapted to gases, metals, heat, and electrical gradients; even if they cannot survive elsewhere, their chemistry may be hazardous. Quarantine samples separately from the traveler.

Likewise, leave nothing behind—not fibers, skin cells, food, markers, pitons, or spilled coolant. This basin appears geographically isolated and chemically exact. A gram of foreign material could persist as an anomaly for generations.

## What the visit will feel like

The strongest psychological hazard may be the mismatch between abundance and silence. The place will appear crowded beyond reason, yet your own breathing and heartbeat may seem like its loudest sounds. When everything freezes after your arrival, resist the instinct to fill the stillness with speech or motion.

You are not entering an empty ruin inhabited by a few creatures. You are stepping onto the sensory surface of a single, tightly coupled ecosystem. The floor, spires, walls, airborne layers, vents, and ancient circles all exchange pressure, charge, heat, and material.

The safest way to behave is not like an explorer claiming ground, but like a brief fault in the weather: contained, slow, dry, and gone before the mountain reaches its hottest hour.
