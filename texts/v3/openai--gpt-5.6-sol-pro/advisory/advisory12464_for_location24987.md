Prepare as if visiting a cool, damp industrial settlement rather than a wilderness—but remember that its streets, water, and machinery obey local rules you may not recognize.

### Bring

- **Puncture-resistant, high-ankled work boots.** Black slag, rail debris, sharp ceramic fragments, slick moss, and subtly shifting ground are the chief everyday hazards. Soft walking shoes will not last.
- **Layered, close-fitting clothing:** wool or synthetic base layers, a light waterproof shell, and trousers that tolerate oil and clay. Avoid loose scarves, dangling straps, and wide sleeves near fans, belts, gears, and slow freight carriages.
- **Hearing protection with adjustable attenuation.** Use filtered earplugs that reduce the fans and pumps without entirely masking alarms, bells, or approaching machinery. Do not wear active noise-canceling headphones while walking through the works.
- **Eye protection and work gloves** for tower tours, quarries, rail yards, or repair halls.
- **A compact headlamp** with an amber or red setting. Empty lots remain genuinely dark between the town’s islands of light.
- **A walking stick or probing cane** if you intend to explore spoil heaps, drainage margins, or the quarry rim. Apparently firm ground may have settled since the last survey.
- **A small dry bag** for documents and electronics. Moisture collects on cables, railings, windowsills, and anything left near a cold surface.
- **Ordinary drinking water in an approved local-style container**, enough for your arrival period. Do not assume that visible water is public, potable, or available for sale to outsiders.
- **A paper notebook and soft pencil.** Machinery can make speech and audio recording unreliable, while dampness is unkind to cheap electronics.
- **Any routine medicines, tetanus protection, and basic wound supplies.** Cuts from slag or rusted fittings should be cleaned promptly.

Pack lightly enough to keep one hand free on stairs and service walkways.

### Before arrival

Arrange lodging in advance, preferably in an inhabited house near Valve Square rather than in one of the isolated workshops or unfinished blocks. Ask whether the room has:

- a secure cistern connection,
- shutters or heavy curtains for sound,
- a threshold channel for coherent water,
- and an exterior stair in good repair.

Obtain a current route map. Old maps may show straight streets that have since bent, doors that no longer close, and lots whose levels have shifted. The town is small, but overhead pipes, incomplete plazas, rail cuts, and acoustically misleading engine noise make direct navigation surprisingly difficult.

Learn the local **colored notice system** before entering industrial areas. Spoken warnings are unsuitable here, and a traveler who waits to hear instructions may miss them entirely. Find out the meanings of pressure, sluice, rail movement, restricted-water, and unstable-ground boards. Never assume a signal is unimportant because a nearby starling reproduces it convincingly.

### Water etiquette and safety

Water is not picturesque background here. It is infrastructure, property, food security, and civic duty.

Do not:

- wash beneath a public downpipe,
- empty a bottle into the street,
- touch tower gutters or collection screens,
- sit on cistern covers,
- throw anything into a sump or settling bed,
- pick plants whose roots mark a suspected leak,
- or redirect runoff, even briefly.

Ask before using a household tap. Roofs, statues, yards, workshops, and gardens may belong to different parts of a carefully accounted system. A host may freely offer tea while still objecting to a long bath.

Treat quarry pools, marsh beds, cooling ponds, puddles, and open reservoirs as **non-potable** unless a local authority explicitly says otherwise. Clear water may contain industrial residue; oily-looking water may be part of a functioning biological treatment bed. Neither appearance is a reliable guide. The unexplained eels are a further reason not to improvise.

The snail-altered “coherent water” deserves particular caution. It appears as separate, exceptionally round droplets that resist merging. Do not collect it as a curiosity, drink it, carry it across worlds, or seal it in a jar. A spill may migrate through clothes, luggage, flooring, and wall cavities toward the lowest point. If you encounter it indoors:

1. Do not chase individual drops with cloth.
2. Keep bags and shoes out of its downhill path.
3. Open or clear the nearest copper threshold channel.
4. Notify the building keeper or municipal collectors.
5. Let trained residents screen and route it.

Wash your hands after handling the translucent snails, and do not export live specimens. Their effect may be chemical, biological, or reality-specific; local familiarity is not proof of multiversal safety.

### Moving around

Stay on established paths unless accompanied by a resident. The principal dangers move slowly enough to look harmless:

- freight carriages that barely seem in motion,
- wheels completing a turn over many minutes,
- sluice mechanisms that remain still until they close violently,
- saturated banks slipping only a hand’s width,
- and pressure systems whose next cycle is not obvious to a visitor.

Never rest against a carriage, pipe, gantry, wheel, or counterweight. “Slow” is not the same as “safe.” Children walking beside the freight cars have learned their rhythms; you have not.

Give condensation towers a generous perimeter. A tank reaching capacity can produce a sudden concussion, falling runoff, startled birds, and automatic movement elsewhere in the system. Moist ceramic fins and steel stairs will be slick.

At road and rail crossings, **look rather than listen**. Quarry echoes can multiply one engine into ten or place it on the wrong side of you. The same effect occurs, less dramatically, between brick walls and tanks. If sound seems directionless, stop in a safe open place and watch fixed visual signals.

Avoid spoil heaps and the bowl’s rim after rain. Small slips are ordinary, but a modest movement can still break an ankle or carry someone into slag. Do not trust leaning survey pegs as handholds. At the quarry, remain well back from all four edges and do not descend without an authorized guide.

### Living with the noise

The town will be tiring before it feels dangerous. Constant low-frequency machinery can cause headaches, irritability, poor sleep, and a false sense that something urgent is always happening. Schedule quiet intervals indoors, preferably behind thick doors and soft hangings. Do not spend your first full day touring the towers.

Conversation is conducted close to the face. This is practical, not aggressive or intimate. Face the speaker so both of you can read expression and lips. Keep questions short near machinery and move indoors for complicated discussions.

You may occasionally feel a market signal through the paving before hearing it. If residents pause, look up, or step aside, imitate them first and ask why afterward. Conversely, do not react to every perfect warning note from a bird. Confirm alarms through colored boards, worker behavior, and machinery indicators.

Bring earplugs for sleeping, but first learn whether your lodging uses vibration, light, knocking, or some other emergency signal. The dawn chorus may be louder and stranger than the works themselves.

### Social conduct

The people are likely to value patience, maintenance, and accurate observation more than speed. Do not treat the town as unfinished in a dismissive sense. Empty lots, incomplete streets, exposed pipes, and settling doors are part of a place still accommodating itself to the ground.

Expect transactions to pause for pressure alarms, collection work, meals, or repairs. Showing impatience will make you seem unserious. Leave room in your schedule for a purchase or appointment to conclude the next day.

Useful gifts are preferable to ornamental ones: durable needles, fine files, corrosion-resistant fasteners, filter cloth, pencils, fruit preserves, or compact hand tools—provided local customs and cross-world quarantine permit them. Avoid gifts requiring water to maintain, and do not bring unknown seeds, aquatic organisms, snails, or soil.

Ask permission before photographing:

- cistern inlets,
- tower internals,
- pressure controls,
- workers at repair tasks,
- private runoff systems,
- and children racing coherent droplets.

The condensation works is not merely a scenic machine. It is the reason the settlement remains viable.

### Food, plants, and animals

The bakery and market near Valve Square are sensible places to begin. Eat cooked foods until you understand local water treatment. Preserved fruit, eggs, bread, cabbage dishes, sour cherries, currants, and pears are likely safer choices than uncooked wetland produce.

Do not forage from settling beds or buried-pipe thickets. Lush growth may indicate nutrient-rich water, but it may equally indicate a sewage leak, cooling discharge, or contaminated industrial seep. Water mint is not an assurance of clean water.

Do not feed foxes, voles, frogs, eels, or birds. Starlings nest inside signal equipment, and encouraging them around machinery is unhelpful even if residents tolerate their presence. Inspect boots and bags before departure for snails, beetles, seeds, and duckweed.

### A sensible first visit

On your first morning, begin at Valve Square with a local guide or municipal contact. Learn the notice colors, public-water rules, safe rail crossings, and current unstable areas. Visit the market and repair hall before approaching the towers.

See the condensation works from an authorized observation point in the afternoon, when you are rested and visibility is good. Watch one complete pump or sluice sequence before moving closer; the long pauses are part of the mechanism, not proof that it has stopped.

Visit the old settling beds from a maintained path. Their concentrated greenery will explain the town better than either the factories or apartments alone. Save the northern quarry for dry weather and daylight, with hearing protection loosened enough to stay aware of companions.

At night, stay initially within the linked pools of amber light. Empty concrete courts and sidings can look navigable while concealing rails, pits, channels, and sudden drops. A slow walk back through the lit paths is preferable to a shortcut.

Above all, adjust your sense of time. Do not wait for a dramatic event to explain the place. Attend instead to the repeated pump stroke, water beginning to bead on ceramic, rust at a bolt seam, roots following a buried line, and a street that is no longer perfectly straight. The town rewards visitors who can recognize accumulation as action.
