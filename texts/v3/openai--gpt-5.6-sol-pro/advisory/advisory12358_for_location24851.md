## First principle: this is not a human-sized cave

An ordinary traveler cannot bodily enter the cleft. Its chambers are fist- to helmet-sized, separated by unstable basalt splinters and already occupied. Treat it as a living fracture to be visited from its mouth with instruments—not as a passage to be widened.

Do not pry away the loose plate, excavate debris, insert a hand, or “improve” the opening. The plate may be structural, and a displacement of only a few millimeters could crush animals, release bats, or collapse much of the interior.

## Before approaching

Coordinate with the quarry operator and obtain:

- The blasting, drilling, hauling, and crusher schedule
- Permission to enter the face and exclusion zones
- Confirmation that no loose-rock scaling is planned
- A spotter familiar with the quarry
- A reliable all-clear procedure; never rely on hearing a warning over machinery
- Information on protected bat species and local wildlife regulations

Inspect the face from a distance first. Recent basalt is treacherous: drill scars and angular blocks can conceal detached plates with no visible support. If there has been a blast, strong vibration, unusual cooling, rain, or a fresh fall of rock, postpone the visit.

The safest period is usually a cool, quiet interval when machinery is shut down and the wall has begun releasing the day’s heat. However, bat activity complicates timing. Survey the cleft thermally or acoustically before choosing an hour. Do not block the entrance around dusk or dawn.

## Personal equipment

Bring ordinary quarry and climbing protection:

- Helmet with chin strap
- Eye protection sealed against dust
- P100 or equivalent particulate respirator
- Hearing protection, preferably plugs beneath earmuffs
- Gloves for external rock contact only
- Long sleeves and durable trousers
- Heat-resistant, high-traction boots
- Climbing harness, independently anchored rope, and a competent belayer
- Water and electrolyte replacement
- Personal gas monitor if your face may approach the opening
- Two-way communication that does not depend solely on quarry radio coverage
- Compact first-aid kit, including supplies for eye irrigation and animal scratches

Do not use the loose rock around the cleft as a handhold or anchor. Establish anchors in verified sound rock well away from the fracture.

For observation, bring:

- A very thin flexible endoscope with a rounded, nonabrasive head
- Infrared or extremely low-level illumination
- Thermal imaging
- A contact microphone or geophone mounted outside the entrance
- A small temperature, humidity, and airflow probe
- A camera with remote focus
- Soft guards around any cable that might scrape rock or animals

Avoid bright white lamps, hot light sources, ultrasound, compressed air, smoke, chemical markers, bait, glue traps, and exposed adhesives. Do not leave fibers, food, moisture, or equipment inside.

## Biological precautions

Assume the bats can carry rabies or analogous pathogens even if they appear healthy. Do not touch them, provoke flight, or position your face where startled animals must pass. Their compressed colony may also conceal significant quantities of pulverized guano. Disturbing it can aerosolize fungal spores, mites, and fine mineral dust.

Never reach into the seam. A flat gecko is harmless compared with the possibility of a concealed snake, sharp glassy stone, or a shifting plate. The pale book scorpions and mites are minor hazards at human scale, but they are part of a tightly rationed ecosystem. Even brushing dust from a surface may destroy shelter or eggs.

Clean and disinfect external equipment afterward, but do so away from the wall. Keep equipment used in this cleft isolated from other cave and bat sites to avoid carrying fungi, mites, or other pathogens between worlds.

## How to conduct the visit

Approach with only the minimum number of people. One person works near the seam; another remains on belay and watches the face, quarry traffic, and communications.

Pause before installing instruments. Put a gloved hand against sound rock nearby and feel the machinery through it. Learn the ordinary rhythm of crushers, conveyors, and trucks. This matters because the animals’ distinction between routine thunder and actual displacement should become yours as well.

Stop immediately if you notice:

- A new draft, especially a cool one
- Dust emerging from the opening
- A sharp change in temperature
- A crack followed by unusual silence
- Pebbles or flakes falling
- The loose plate changing position
- Bats becoming agitated or beginning to pour from the seam
- A change in the quarry’s operating state
- Any uncertainty about an impending blast

A sudden draft is especially important. In this place it may mean that an internal partition has failed or another crack has opened. Retreat from the entire face, not merely from the entrance.

Insert probes slowly, no farther than necessary, and never force them around an obstruction. If a beetle, gecko, or bat contacts the instrument, stop and withdraw only when doing so will not trap or scrape it. Expect cables to snag on fresh basalt. Your retrieval plan should allow you to abandon a cheap probe rather than tug hard enough to rearrange the cleft.

Keep the visit short. Heat stress will build even in shade because the wall radiates stored solar energy. The respirator and hearing protection will make fatigue harder to notice.

## What it will feel like

From the outside, the seam may remain visually disappointing. Most of the landscape is inaccessible to your eyes at once; it will appear piecemeal through a camera—an armored beetle filling a “valley,” a dust grain falling like a boulder, the ceiling resolving into overlapping bat bodies.

The striking sensation will be vibration. Airborne quarry noise will seem secondary to the pressure coming through your boots, rope, hands, and teeth. Contact microphones may reveal several simultaneous worlds: a deep engine pulse, ceramic ticking among fragments, ant footfalls too faint to hear unaided, and the soft collective adjustment of bats. Do not mistake dramatic sound for immediate collapse, but never become casual about it.

There may be a dry mineral odor, warm stone, old guano, and animal musk, but do not remove your respirator to experience it more clearly. Nothing about the smell is worth the exposure.

## If travel technology reduces you to the cleft’s scale

An ordinary human should not enter even if dimensional or scale-changing technology makes it physically possible. At that scale, the cleft becomes an extreme expedition environment:

- “Dust” becomes unstable rubble.
- Beetles and geckos become megafauna.
- Bat movement becomes structural impact.
- Ant traffic becomes a territorial hazard.
- A quarry vibration can throw or bury you.
- Fine particles can obstruct breathing equipment.
- Radiant and conductive heat can overwhelm a small body rapidly.
- There is effectively no accessible water.
- A millimeter-scale rock shift can erase an entire chamber.
- Surface chemistry, gas exchange, and heat loss may not scale safely with you.

Only attempt such entry with a sealed life-support suit, active cooling, independent water and oxygen, full-face abrasion protection, redundant localization beacons, vibration-triggered retreat alarms, and a retrieval system anchored outside the wall. Do not depend on walking back: the interior has no stable floor, no guaranteed route, and no space in which to turn around. Your presence would also be ecologically enormous. A footprint, breath condensation, food crumb, or drop of water could transform the local economy.

The ethical and practical choice is remote visitation.

## Departure

Account for every item and fragment of equipment. Photograph the entrance from the same position before and after to confirm that the loose plate and surrounding grains have not shifted. Record temperature, quarry activity, bat behavior, and any fresh airflow without marking the rock.

Leave no water, bait, shade cloth, sensor, or commemorative object. In a place where every empty crevice is occupied and every trace of moisture has value, even a well-meant gift is an invasion. The best visit will leave the hidden country exactly as narrow, crowded, dry, and undiscovered-looking as it was before you arrived.
