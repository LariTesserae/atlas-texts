## Overall assessment

Treat this as a polar, low-visibility, high-current seafloor expedition—not as an ordinary recreational dive. The plain looks subdued, but its main dangers are invisible: near-freezing water, reversing tidal flows, dense bottom brine, shifting sediment, grounded ice, and a complete lack of dependable landmarks.

An ordinary human should visit from inside a pressure-rated vehicle if at all possible. Walking or swimming on the bottom should be reserved for trained commercial or scientific divers with surface support. Arriving directly in the water through a multiverse transition would be extremely dangerous.

## Choose the safest form of visit

In descending order of safety:

1. **Observe remotely by ROV**, from a ship or stable ice camp.
2. **Use a compact polar-rated submersible** with sonar, inertial navigation, redundant ballast release, and several days of emergency life support.
3. **Dive from a dynamically positioned support vessel**, using a tether, heated suit, full-face mask, and surface-supplied gas.
4. **Independent scuba** should generally be rejected. A free-swimming diver can be swept away, buried in suspended sediment, trapped beneath moving ice, or lose the ascent route within seconds.

If the object is simply to experience the place, a submersible provides nearly everything that defines it: the darkness, bottom-hugging sediment, stone fields, ice scars, tidal rolls, and profound isolation. Leaving the vehicle adds enormous risk while revealing comparatively little.

## Arrival through the multiverse

Do not set an arrival point merely by coordinates. The bottom is changing, and a coordinate that once opened into water may later lie inside gravel, bedrock, grounded ice, or a moving iceberg keel.

Arrive in one of the following:

- A sealed pressure capsule already at ambient pressure tolerance.
- A submersible hovering several meters above the predicted bottom.
- A surface vessel well away from pack ice and known tidal races.
- A portal chamber that first sends probes through and confirms pressure, salinity, depth, temperature, free volume, and current velocity.

Send an expendable sensor package ahead. It should return:

- Water depth and pressure
- Clearance in every direction
- Current speed and direction
- Temperature and salinity profile
- Dissolved-gas and chemical readings
- Sonar imagery of nearby ice and seabed
- Confirmation that the exit aperture is not intersecting solid matter

Keep the return mechanism physically attached to you or your vehicle. Do not rely on relocating an invisible portal on a featureless bottom.

## Vessel and equipment

A suitable support vessel needs an ice-strengthened hull, accurate local charts, side-scan or multibeam sonar, a current profiler, and a crew familiar with polar tidal waters. Positioning cannot depend on GPS alone; GPS tells the ship where it is, not where a diver has gone under a shearing current.

For a submersible, bring:

- Forward- and downward-looking sonar
- Doppler velocity log and inertial navigation
- Acoustic transponder network
- Powerful lamps mounted away from the viewing port, reducing backscatter
- Mechanical guards over thrusters
- Redundant propulsion and electrical systems
- Jettisonable ballast that cannot be jammed by gravel or anchor ice
- Emergency oxygen, carbon-dioxide scrubbing, thermal protection, food, and water
- A pinger and independent emergency beacon
- A means to cut or abandon a tether
- Sampling tools rather than manipulators that require the craft to settle on the bottom

Do not land the vehicle casually. The apparent pavement may conceal soft mud, loose gravel, brine-filled hollows, or sediment that will engulf skids and blind the pilot.

## If a person must enter the water

This should be a tethered operation with a professional surface team. The diver should have:

- A drysuit rated for polar water
- Electrically heated undergarments with redundant power or a hot-water suit
- Full-face mask or diving helmet
- Surface-supplied breathing gas, with an independent bailout cylinder
- Hard-wired communications
- A harness and abrasion-resistant umbilical
- Two cutting tools placed where either hand can reach them
- Helmet lights plus a low-mounted hand light
- Sonar or acoustic homing equipment
- Depth, gas, and decompression instrumentation
- Thick gloves that preserve enough dexterity to operate releases
- Fins suited to current, though swimming against the principal flow should never be part of the plan

A conventional regulator can free-flow in very cold water. All breathing systems must be environmentally sealed and specifically prepared for freezing conditions. Keep equipment dry before immersion; moisture inside valves can freeze.

Cold incapacitates before it necessarily feels dramatic. Watch for loss of dexterity, slowed thought, unusual calm, slurred speech, and fixation on simple tasks. End the dive at the first sign. The bottom offers no warm refuge.

## Timing the expedition

Work around the tide, not the clock. There may be only a short interval near slack water when a bottom visit is practical. Slack at the surface may not coincide with slack near the bottom, and flow can reverse at different times on opposite sides of a ridge.

Before committing personnel:

1. Measure a complete tidal cycle with an acoustic current profiler.
2. Map downwelling and upwelling zones.
3. Identify eddies behind ridges.
4. Track moving and grounded ice.
5. Obtain a conservative weather window extending well beyond the planned operation.
6. Establish abort criteria for current, visibility, ice motion, and surface-vessel drift.

Do not assume that returning with the reversed tide is safe. The reversing flow may form rolls, vertical jets, or opposing layers rather than a simple current in the other direction.

Storms need not sound severe underwater to be dangerous. Their pressure pulses can mobilize sediment, move stones, alter ice, and make recovery at the surface impossible.

## Ice hazards

Ice is dangerous here in several distinct ways.

**Pack or surface ice** can close the ascent route or prevent vessel recovery.

**Iceberg keels** can reach the bottom unexpectedly. A grounded berg may pivot or lift with the tide. Never work beneath or immediately downstream of one.

**Anchor ice** can adhere to gear, collect sediment, foul valves, increase drag, and alter buoyancy. Brittle growths may look harmless until a sheet tears loose and tumbles through the current.

**Fresh iceberg trenches** can channel flow and conceal unstable ridges of pushed stone. Avoid entering them unless sonar has shown an exit and the current is negligible.

Maintain continuous upward-looking sonar. Visual inspection is inadequate in turbid water.

## Navigation on the plain

Do not navigate by remembered boulders, furrows, or kelp. They are repetitive and mutable. Visibility may collapse from tens of meters to nearly zero as soon as a vehicle or diver disturbs the bottom.

Use several independent systems:

- Tether or physical guideline
- Acoustic beacons
- Inertial track
- Sonar
- Recorded depth and heading
- Surface tracking

A guideline should be laid above the bottom where possible. A line dragged through gravel can snag under slabs, become buried, or collect anchor ice. Mark the route with retrievable acoustic transponders rather than permanent stakes.

Stay at least a few meters above the seabed while traveling. Move slowly. Thruster wash or fin kicks can create an opaque cloud that persists and may travel around a ridge to overtake you later.

If visibility is lost:

1. Stop moving.
2. Stabilize buoyancy without descending.
3. Confirm tether and acoustic bearing.
4. Report to the surface.
5. Retreat along the known route.
6. Do not search visually by circling; there may be no visible reference at all.

## Bottom brine and hollows

Treat depressions as distinct water bodies. Dense, colder brine may be nearly invisible and sharply layered. Entering it can cause:

- Sudden additional cooling
- Changes in buoyancy
- Optical distortion
- Reduced visibility
- Altered equipment performance
- Disorientation as the vehicle or diver crosses the density boundary

Do not “walk” into a hollow simply because its surface appears calm. Probe it with temperature and conductivity sensors first. A submersible should cross above the lip rather than descend to the bottom.

Soft mud basins are especially deceptive. They may look safer than stone fields but can swallow tools, trap a boot, clog intakes, and become instantly opaque.

## What to expect psychologically

The place will not feel grand in the usual sense. It has no cliff, ruin, forest, or conspicuous creature to reward attention. Its scale appears gradually. The same low ridges repeat until direction loses meaning. Lights illuminate mostly sediment and blunt stones. Beyond the beam there may be no visible boundary at all.

The violence is likely to be felt before it is seen: pressure through the body, vibration in the hull, a tether pulling sideways, gravel ticking against metal, or a low resonance in the jaw. A diver may feel that nothing is happening while being displaced rapidly.

Prepare for sensory monotony, claustrophobia, and time distortion. Give every excursion a written task sequence and a strict turnaround time. Do not extend the visit merely because the bottom seems quiet.

A useful objective is to remain still at a safe hover and watch one patch for several minutes. Sand ribbons, tiny amphipods, a nearly horizontal kelp blade, or a fish holding station against the flow may then become visible. Hurried travel will reveal little and create its own obscuring silt.

## Biological and archaeological precautions

The sparse life is easy to damage because it grows slowly and is repeatedly stressed by ice. Avoid collecting living kelp, overturning slabs, or placing equipment on algal films. A track that seems trivial may erase growth accumulated over decades.

Do not dig casually. Packed stones can conceal soft sediment beneath, and disturbing one layer may destabilize another. Buried driftwood, shell beds, or human-made objects may also have scientific value.

If you find a bone hook, worked stone, sink-stone, or other possible artifact:

- Photograph it in place with scale and orientation.
- Record exact depth and acoustic coordinates.
- Do not clean or move it.
- Notify an appropriate archaeological authority, if one exists in that world and era.

If this is a prehistoric period, prevent biological contamination in both directions. Sterilize sampling equipment, avoid releasing modern microbes or food waste, and quarantine anything brought home.

## Contact with people on the coasts

Coastal inhabitants may understand the strait far better than a technologically equipped visitor. Their knowledge of seal movements, dangerous surface patterns, and tidal timings could be lifesaving. Do not mistake an absence of structures on the seabed for an absence of ownership, meaning, or customary use.

If contact is possible:

- Approach through culturally appropriate intermediaries.
- Do not display advanced equipment unnecessarily.
- Trade only after considering historical contamination.
- Do not introduce modern pathogens, metals, fuels, or manufactured objects.
- Ask before sampling waters or animals they rely upon.
- Take local warnings literally, even if expressed through unfamiliar names or stories.

Their map may consist of currents, ice behavior, animal surfacing places, and weather signs rather than fixed seafloor landmarks. For this environment, that may be the more accurate map.

## Emergency planning

Have plans for at least the following:

- Tether severance or entanglement
- Loss of visibility
- Diver cold injury
- Regulator or valve freezing
- Thruster fouling
- Navigation failure
- Current exceeding recovery limits
- Ice closing the surface station
- Submersible trapped under a keel
- Ballast release failure
- Portal or return-device malfunction

A diver should never have to make a free ascent to an uncertain surface. A submersible should carry enough life support to wait through at least one major weather or tidal delay, preferably much longer.

Recovery personnel need active warming, oxygen, medical monitoring, and evacuation capability. If decompression is required, ensure the chamber is operational before anyone enters the water. Near-freezing exposure and decompression stress are a particularly unforgiving combination.

## Minimal personal checklist

Before departure, confirm:

- You are entering in a pressure-safe vehicle, not directly into seawater.
- Depth and ambient pressure are known.
- Current has been measured through a full tidal cycle.
- Ice above and nearby has been mapped.
- The return route has at least two independent locating systems.
- Thermal protection has redundancy.
- Surface support can track you continuously.
- There is a chamber if the dive profile requires one.
- Abort thresholds are written and understood.
- Nothing essential depends on visibility.
- Nothing essential depends on a landmark remaining where it was.

The central rule is simple: **do not interpret flatness as safety or stillness as calm**. This plain is dangerous precisely because its forces are broad, repetitive, and difficult to see. Visit slowly, touch almost nothing, and always retain a route back that does not depend on the seabed remembering you.
