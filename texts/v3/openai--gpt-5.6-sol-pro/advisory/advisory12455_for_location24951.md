Treat this city as three hazards superimposed: a confined mine, an active industrial plant, and a desert heat chamber. Its silence is not evidence of safety. The machinery is still energized by heat, pressure, and moving water, and much of it starts without warning.

## Do not go alone

An ordinary traveler should enter with at least one companion and leave a third person outside or at the transit point. Establish:

- A fixed return time.
- Scheduled radio checks.
- A route log and physical trail markers.
- A rescue threshold: if contact is missed twice, the outside watch initiates extraction rather than waiting.
- A transit beacon or other independent means of returning to your home reality.

Ordinary radios may perform poorly among dense masonry, metal ducts, and mineral-rich rock. Use repeaters or breadcrumb relays as you advance. Do not depend on the city’s wiring, lamps, maps, or control systems.

Before committing to the visit, send in disposable sensors or a small drone. Test the atmosphere at several heights and in both upper and lower districts. The air near the floor, inside cistern galleries, and around the central shaft may differ greatly.

## Time the visit carefully

The best window is probably during the local night or early morning, when the shallow roof has lost some heat and thermal machinery is least active. Do not assume it is entirely inactive: the city’s mechanisms are out of synchronization and may be driven by stored pressure rather than current surface temperature.

Avoid entering:

- At or near noon.
- During desert rain.
- For several days after known heavy rain, until delayed infiltration has passed.
- While the central shaft is heating strongly.
- During the upward migration of moisture in the lowest quarter.
- Whenever numerous pressure-responsive lamps begin brightening at once.

Rain on the surface may take hours or days to arrive underground. A clear sky during the visit does not mean the city is dry. Obtain recent regional weather information, including storms over nearby higher ground. Expect water to arrive suddenly through fractures and then activate machinery throughout the city.

Learn to recognize its cycle. Silver branching dampness climbing salted brick, droplets forming on the upper sides of pipes, unusual silence, and plants stiffening toward the walls all indicate the upward phase. This is not a picturesque moment to linger. Pressure is accumulating. Withdraw to a dry, structurally sound main route before the relay sequence begins and the indoor rain falls.

## Wear equipment for heat, steam, dust, and sharp machinery

Recommended personal equipment includes:

- A positive-pressure respirator or full-face breathing system with an independent air supply.
- Heat-resistant, moisture-wicking clothing beneath a light protective outer layer.
- A cooling vest with a closed-loop system; evaporative cooling may be unreliable in humid lower districts.
- A helmet with neck protection and a mounted lamp.
- Sealed goggles, even if using a respirator.
- Heat-resistant gloves with enough dexterity for ladders and tools.
- Hard-soled, high-ankle boots resistant to hot metal, salts, caustic liquids, and sharp debris.
- Hearing protection that can be engaged quickly without blocking communications.
- A personal gas monitor measuring oxygen, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, combustible gases, and volatile chemicals.
- A temperature, humidity, and radiant-heat meter.
- A small dosimeter and basic chemical detector, since the city’s fuel, refractory materials, glaze, and insulation are unknown.
- At least two independent light sources per person.
- More drinking water and electrolytes than a normal underground walk would require.
- Burn dressings, sterile eyewash, trauma supplies, and a way to cool a heat casualty.
- A compact escape hood or backup air bottle.

Do not use open flames. Fine dust, old fuel residues, or accumulated gases may be combustible despite the general mineral sterility.

The city contains narrow shoulder-width alleys, hanging pipes, steep galleries, overlapping roofs, and corroded stairs. Keep your pack small and close to the body. Avoid dangling straps that can catch in linkages or valve handles. A person wearing bulky expedition gear may simply not fit through some routes.

## Assume the atmosphere is hostile until repeatedly proven otherwise

The absence of animals is one of the strongest warnings in the description. It may mean heat alone, but it may also indicate chronic oxygen deficiency, toxic mineral vapor, heavy-metal dust, acidic aerosols, or gases periodically released by the deep engines.

Never remove respiratory protection because a district “smells fine.” Some lethal gases have little odor, and heat quickly reduces the usefulness of smell as a warning. Do not enter tanks, cisterns, kiln chambers, pump housings, dead-end tunnels, or rooms below street grade without confined-space procedures and a lifeline.

The central shaft deserves particular caution. Rising warm air can conceal combustion products or draw air so strongly that neighboring chambers become oxygen-poor. Stay behind existing guards and maintain enough distance that a sudden draft cannot pull loose clothing, dust, or equipment toward it.

## Regard every machine as live

Painted arrows tell you the intended direction of flow, not whether a conduit is safe. Pipes may contain:

- Pressurized steam.
- Superheated water.
- Hot brine.
- Caustic condensate.
- Compressed gas.
- Mineral slurry.
- Moving chains or rods hidden behind walls.

Do not lean on pipes, sit on housings, turn handwheels, throw knife switches, or “test” a mechanism. Do not place fingers beneath cups, floats, catches, shutters, louvers, counterweights, or belt drives. A machine that has been motionless for decades may still complete one stroke when temperature or pressure crosses a threshold.

Watch especially for:

- Valve stems beginning to rise.
- Chains losing slack.
- Counterweights lifting.
- Condensation lamps brightening.
- Cork-black machinery mounts compressing or shifting.
- Pings that begin occurring in a regular sequence.
- Dust moving from joints in a pipe or vessel.
- A cold line appearing on hot masonry.
- Sudden quiet after scattered mechanical sounds.

These may be the only warnings before activation.

Never step across a belt, chain, piston rod, or linkage, even if it appears seized. Go around it. If there is no safe route around, turn back.

## Move as though the roof is failing

The cavern is shallow, recently excavated, thermally stressed, and already shedding mineral glaze. Repeated heating and cooling can loosen slabs without obvious cracking. A helmet will protect against flakes, not a roof plate.

Avoid:

- Areas where fresh pale dust has accumulated beneath a crack.
- Buckled ceilings.
- Sagging braces.
- Glass visibly warped in its frame.
- Orange corrosion below bolts or anchor plates.
- Exploratory tunnels at the northern fault.
- Drill-cut faces with embedded cutting equipment.
- Rooms where doors or windows have become tightly pinched.
- Places where the ceiling ticks rapidly or sheds grit after a temperature change.

Do not touch or climb roof supports. Do not hammer on the rock. Avoid loud impacts and unnecessary vibration. The city’s own machinery was isolated to preserve the roof; visitors should show the same restraint.

The northern fault and unfinished tunnels should be treated as strictly off-limits. They are geologically fresh, unsupported, and may collect gases. Embedded cutting teeth are not evidence that excavation can resume; they may mark the point where conditions became impossible.

## Choose routes by district, not by apparent convenience

The city is small enough to cross quickly in principle, but its internal density will make straight-line movement deceptive. Galleries can dead-end, stairs can terminate at machinery, and a route that looks open may become impassable when shutters close.

A conservative first visit should remain on major routes between the eastern sector and western condensers, without descending into the deepest quarter or climbing into upper habitation blocks.

### Upper districts

These are hottest and closest to thermally stressed roof rock. Visit only during the coolest period, and limit exposure. Sagged glass and curled paint suggest repeated temperatures capable of injuring an unprotected person. Rooms may trap even hotter air than streets.

### Lower streets

They are cooler but more humid, corroded, and vulnerable to sudden water movement. Floors may be slick with salts or dark paste. Metal railings may fail under body weight. Use masonry where possible for balance, but first inspect it for heat.

### Western condensers and cisterns

Expect damp air, corrosion, oxygen displacement, locked grilles, and sudden pump activity. Do not enter cisterns or open access covers. “Cool” air issuing from a grille is not necessarily breathable.

### Central shaft and industrial ring

This is likely the most mechanically active region. Keep the visit observational. Use marked walkways if they remain sound, and do not pass beneath suspended vessels, counterweights, or pipe bridges.

### Ash Gate

Do not attempt to cut through fused sand or force the freight incline open. The seal may be supporting loose material or excluding surface heat and dust. Disturbing it could create a chimney draft, sand collapse, or uncontrolled secondary exit.

### Preserved standing niches and plazas

These voids may once have served as thermal refuges, crowd recesses, or pressure-clearance zones. They can be useful places to pause only after checking temperature, airflow, overhead stability, and nearby moving parts. Do not assume a person-sized niche was intended as a shelter. It may have been kept empty precisely because a valve arm or burst path crosses it.

## Prepare for the water reversal

The lowest quarter’s capillary cycle is predictable in concept but dangerous in practice. Water migrating upward can alter traction, cool and crack hot materials, change pressure in sealed systems, and release automatic catches. When it falls, even a small quantity can become scalding steam on hot surfaces.

If caught near the cycle:

1. Do not run blindly through narrow alleys.
2. Move away from the central shaft and visibly salted porous walls.
3. Avoid standing beneath gutters, balconies, pipe bundles, or collection roofs.
4. Do not shelter in a low room where water or steam can collect.
5. Find a broad, mapped landing with two exits and a high, stable floor.
6. Protect eyes and exposed skin.
7. Wait for the main valve and flywheel sequence to finish before moving.
8. Continue monitoring oxygen and temperature; steam may displace breathable air.

Never collect the water for drinking. It has passed through salted brick, corroded metal, industrial conduits, and biological films. Even after ordinary filtration it may contain dissolved metals or chemicals. Bring all water from home.

## Avoid biological and cross-world contamination

The plants and green-black films should be treated as unknown organisms, even if the city appears nearly sterile. Do not touch, sample, eat, or inhale material from them. The translucent leaves may be chemically defended or may concentrate metals from pipe condensation.

Take special care around rotating cutting blades that maintain vegetation near grilles. They may start slowly and almost silently.

Use clean entry and exit protocols:

- Keep suits and boots sealed.
- Carry samples only in approved, triple-contained vessels.
- Do not take loose artifacts as souvenirs.
- Vacuum or bind dust before departure rather than brushing it into the air.
- Decontaminate equipment on the city side of the return point.
- Quarantine exposed biological samples and clothing.
- Do not introduce food scraps, seeds, insects, or microbes.

This city’s ecology is extremely sparse. A single foreign mold, ant colony, or bacterium could transform its cistern district. Conversely, its organisms may behave unpredictably in your home environment.

## Navigation and emergency planning

Bring a paper map board as well as electronic mapping. Metal infrastructure and thick rock may disrupt compasses and signals. Mark each junction with removable, noncontaminating tags indicating the way out. Number them rather than relying on color alone; orange rust, amber light, and mineral glaze can distort color perception.

Continuously record:

- Time since entry.
- Ambient and radiant temperature.
- Remaining breathing gas.
- Water consumption.
- Direction and strength of airflow.
- Machinery activation.
- Moisture-cycle indicators.
- Recent route closures.

Use a conservative turnaround rule: begin exiting after consuming one-third of breathing gas, cooling capacity, or water, reserving one-third for the return and one-third for delays.

If someone collapses from heat, do not remove their respirator until the local air is verified. Move them to a cooler stable area, begin active cooling, and extract immediately. Heat illness in this environment can progress faster than a rescue team can navigate the packed streets.

If a pipe ruptures, do not attempt to cross the plume. Steam can be invisible near its source. Retreat around at least two corners and seek a route that does not pass above or below the failed line.

If the lights brighten in sequence or valves begin snapping across multiple districts, leave by the shortest confirmed route. Do not wait to see the full mechanism operate.

## What to expect psychologically

The city will tempt you into treating it as abandoned architecture. It is better understood as an organism whose inhabitants have vanished but whose reflexes remain.

Long silences will make each metallic knock feel close. In the narrow alleys it may be difficult to determine whether a sound came from behind a wall, overhead, or several streets away through a pipe. The orange lamps, shoulder-wide passages, and repeated person-sized niches can encourage the sensation that someone has just stepped out of sight. There is no need to indulge that impression, but do not dismiss its effect. Heat, dehydration, dim light, and isolation degrade judgment.

The most dangerous moment may be when the city becomes beautiful: after water arrives, when black brick shines, silver moisture maps spread up the walls, and translucent plants catch the maintenance lamps. That is also when traction worsens, pressure redistributes, shutters close, and dormant machinery begins to move.

Go slowly. Speak quietly. Touch almost nothing. Leave well before you feel exhausted.

A successful first visit should be modest: establish atmospheric safety, map one dependable route, observe one complete thermal cycle from a protected distance, and return with every traveler and every piece of equipment accounted for. The city is small enough to inspire overconfidence, but it is not inert. It is still conducting its business, and it will not notice that you are in the way.
