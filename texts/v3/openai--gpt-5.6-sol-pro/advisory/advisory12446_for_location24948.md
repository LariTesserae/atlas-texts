## First principle: treat it as an ocean, an archaeological site, and an uncontaminated biosphere

An ordinary traveler should not arrive here alone, unsealed, or directly underwater. There is no shore to swim toward, no local food, no dry refuge, no animal life to indicate toxicity, and no reliable natural landmark. The apparent calm is deceptive: the chief dangers are cold, pressure, entanglement, disorientation, structural failure, and the immense distances involved.

The safest visit is made from a self-contained floating base, with underwater exploration conducted by remotely operated vehicles and a one-atmosphere submersible. Ordinary scuba should be restricted to shallow, open-water work near the base.

## Before crossing

### Establish the arrival point

Place the transit aperture or arrival vehicle:

- Several metres above the water, not beneath it.
- Over an open paving plain or clear reservoir, not a dense district of posts and vegetation.
- Well away from any visible sluice, wall crest, roof field, or floating mat.
- With a guaranteed return route independent of local weather and visibility.

Do not assume the “surface” is safe merely because it is open. Permanent mist may conceal obstacles until impact, and the underlying pavement may lie just below the water or thousands of metres down.

Send probes first. They should test:

- Atmospheric pressure and composition.
- Water temperature, salinity, pH, dissolved oxygen, and toxic gases.
- Microbial and chemical hazards.
- Depth and bottom structure.
- Current direction at many levels.
- Weather, wave height, and electrical activity.
- Whether local chemistry attacks seals, metals, glass, or polymers.

Never taste the water despite its apparently mild mineral character.

### Use strict biological quarantine

The complete absence of animals is the most important ecological fact about the place. A few imported organisms might alter it irreversibly. Humans carry mites, microbes, spores, eggs, skin cells, and gut organisms everywhere.

Travelers should therefore use:

- Fully sealed suits and habitats.
- Sterilized exterior surfaces.
- Closed-loop sewage and gray-water systems.
- Filters capable of retaining microorganisms and reproductive spores.
- Double containment for food, soil, fibers, wood, and biological samples.
- A prohibition on releasing ballast water, wash water, bodily waste, bait, pets, seeds, or untreated air from living quarters.

Nothing collected should be eaten or handled bare-skinned. Nothing local should be returned home without quarantine. The black fronds and bacterial films may be harmless, toxic, allergenic, corrosive, or biochemically incompatible with human life.

## The surface base

A floating habitat should be enclosed, heated, and highly visible at close range. Visibility may be too poor for distant flags or lights, so fit it with radar reflectors, powerful fog signals, strobes, and acoustic beacons.

Bring redundant power. Solar generation will be weak beneath permanent cloud, and wind generation may suffer from wet, corrosive conditions. Fuel cells, sealed batteries, or another dependable non-solar source are preferable.

The base should carry:

- Multiple inflatable life rafts.
- Immersion suits rather than ordinary rain gear.
- Heated recovery equipment for hypothermic personnel.
- Desalination and purification equipment, even if the basin appears fresh.
- Several independent compasses and inertial navigation systems.
- Sonar and downward-looking depth sounders.
- Acoustic transponders for underwater navigation.
- Long-range radio if propagation permits, plus a guaranteed interworld distress channel.
- A weather mast and instruments for detecting slow pressure changes.
- Spare seals, cables, thrusters, filters, and heaters.

Avoid dropping conventional anchors blindly. An anchor may punch through an ancient roof, lodge beneath masonry, or damage unique structures. Use dynamic positioning where possible. If mooring is essential, survey the bottom first and use broad, recoverable pads on confirmed solid paving.

The rain and featureless horizon will erode orientation. Mark every exterior walkway, use safety lines, and require flotation and thermal protection whenever anyone goes outside.

## Going below

### Prefer machines

Begin with small expendable probes, followed by tethered ROVs. Map a district before sending people into it. Sonar will be more useful than cameras in deep or silty water, though enclosed masonry may create confusing echoes.

Human visits should use a one-atmosphere submersible equipped with:

- Forward and imaging sonar.
- Mechanical cutters for vegetation and old rope.
- Protected thrusters that cannot ingest fronds.
- Skids broad enough not to break softened paving.
- Multiple lights at different angles.
- Inertial navigation and an acoustic route home.
- Emergency drop weights.
- Independent life support and heat.
- A physical tether or guideline for entry into dense districts.

Do not rely solely on a tether. It may wrap around posts, screens, arches, or mineralized rope. Do not rely solely on inertial navigation either; small accumulated errors become lethal in a repetitive labyrinth. Use layered navigation: mapped geometry, acoustic beacons, inertial tracking, and a recoverable line.

### Limit ordinary diving

The shallow water may look benign, but diving hazards are severe:

- Near-freezing water.
- Curtains of vegetation.
- almost no ambient light.
- Silt that can reduce visibility to zero for months.
- Overhead rooms with no direct ascent.
- Fragile roofs and concealed shafts.
- Old ropes and frames at every depth.
- Narrow ducts that can produce unexpectedly strong suction.
- No shore or local rescuers.

If scuba is used at all, divers should remain in open water, use full-face masks and dry suits, carry redundant gas, and stay on a tended line. No solo diving. No penetration of chambers, galleries, roof fields, or submerged “woods” without cave-diving training and a specialized team.

Deep regions require one-atmosphere vehicles. Saturation diving would impose enormous logistical and decompression risks for little advantage.

## How to move through the ruins

Travel slowly. The environment changes on geological timescales, but a vehicle can still trigger a local collapse in seconds.

Do not:

- Land on sagging roofs.
- Pass beneath unsupported slabs.
- Force through frond canopies.
- Enter narrow sluices whose downstream geometry is unknown.
- Touch luminous films.
- Pull on doors, ropes, beams, or screens.
- Assume a smooth floor is solid.
- Follow a cold current into a lower chamber.

A wall may be retaining colder, denser water. Opening or breaking a blockage could produce a local flood even where the basin’s general currents are mild. Similarly, apparently sealed chambers may contain water or gas at a different pressure.

Maintain clearance above the bottom. Thrusters will raise mortar dust and biological mats, destroying visibility and possibly burying inscriptions or organisms. Use low-power lateral movement and avoid hovering directly over delicate surfaces.

The most navigable districts will probably be:

1. **Open paving plains**, though they are exposed, cold, and visually monotonous.
2. **Large circular reservoirs**, where clear water may simplify observation.
3. **Broad submerged roads**, if their continuity has been verified.

The worst districts are post forests, collapsed galleries, dense roof-fields, and any place where old rope and vegetation occupy the full water column.

## Clothing and personal equipment

At the surface, wear:

- A waterproof immersion suit or heavily insulated dry gear.
- A harness clipped to the habitat.
- Gloves that preserve grip when continuously wet.
- A hood and eye protection against rain.
- A personal light, locator beacon, whistle, and compact breathing hood.
- Anti-nausea medication if prone to seasickness; there is no fixed horizon.

Inside a submersible, retain thermal emergency clothing. A minor power loss in this water can become a fatal cooling event long before air runs out.

Each traveler should carry a physical slate or tactile route record. Screens can fail, and the repeated human-scale dimensions of the ruins make one corridor easy to mistake for another.

## Psychological preparation

This place will not feel like a conventional ruin. There are no birds, fish, insects, waves on a beach, or distant mountains. The rain is nearly constant, the horizon is erased, and most movement occurs too slowly to notice. Underwater, even a large chamber may appear only as a few metres of wall emerging from darkness.

Expect:

- Loss of time sense.
- Claustrophobia despite the basin’s enormous size.
- Vertigo at the surface.
- Unease caused by complete animal silence.
- A strong temptation to interpret every repeated structure as a sign or route.
- Fatigue from navigating a world with few distinct landmarks.

Use short watches, paired decision-making, and scheduled rest. Name mapped features rather than relying on descriptions such as “the second wall” or “the dark gallery.” Keep clocks synchronized with the base; daylight will provide little help.

## Food, water, and medical planning

Bring all food. The local vegetation should be regarded as poisonous until proven otherwise, and harvesting it may damage a unique ecosystem.

Bring all drinking water initially. Even if purification tests succeed, unknown dissolved minerals and microorganisms warrant caution. Distillation or highly redundant purification is preferable to ordinary filtration.

Medical provisions should emphasize:

- Hypothermia and cold-water immersion.
- Decompression illness.
- Drowning and aspiration.
- Crush injuries.
- Chemical burns and unknown biological exposure.
- Eye and respiratory irritation from spores or aerosols.
- Anxiety, disorientation, and sleep disruption.

Have a heated decompression-capable treatment unit if any diving is contemplated.

## Sampling and conduct

This is an artifact on a planetary scale, not a supply of salvage. A rounded slab or strand of frond may encode more history than an intact monument elsewhere.

Sample minimally:

- Photograph and map before touching.
- Take loose material rather than cutting attached growth.
- Record exact depth, temperature, current, and structural context.
- Preserve water surrounding biological samples.
- Avoid scraping inscriptions or cleaning masonry for visibility.
- Never dismantle a joint merely to learn how it was made.

Do not mistake the absence of inhabitants for absence of ownership or significance. If evidence of current intelligence, active maintenance, deliberate water control, or recent construction is found, withdraw and reassess rather than initiating contact inside a confined underwater structure.

## Emergency rules

Every excursion needs a fixed turnaround reserve of power, heat, air, and navigation margin. If any two navigation systems disagree, return immediately.

Abort for:

- Unexpected current acceleration.
- Rapid temperature or density change.
- Persistent silt loss of visibility.
- Tether snagging that cannot be resolved remotely.
- Structural tapping or shifting nearby.
- Unexplained gas bubbles.
- Frond mats beginning to detach.
- Failure of cabin heat, sonar, or communications.
- Loss of the surface base’s position reference.

A detached garden can behave like a vast submerged net. If one begins to lift, do not try to pass through or under it. Retreat laterally into mapped open water.

Above all, never count on swimming to safety. In a world without shores, “the surface” is only another hostile location. Safety means returning to a heated sealed habitat with a functioning way home.

The best visit would be patient and restrained: establish a small clean base, map one district thoroughly, descend only in protected vehicles, and leave the drowned works almost exactly as they were found. This world rewards slowness. Its greatest hazards begin when a visitor tries to move faster than the water.
