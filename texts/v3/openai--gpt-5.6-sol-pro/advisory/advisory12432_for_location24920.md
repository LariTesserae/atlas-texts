This is not a city to explore casually on foot. Its principal hazards are not spectacular; they are cumulative and mundane: heat, contaminated water, unstable surfaces, industrial traffic, insects, animal bites, toxic gases, and sudden flooding. An ordinary traveler should treat it as a working port, a tropical wetland, and a decaying industrial site simultaneously.

## Before crossing over

Arrange a local contact, preferably someone familiar with the drainage system, tides, and industrial districts. Do not arrive unannounced in a yard, tunnel, warehouse, or apparently abandoned building: obsolete structures are still used for pipes, storage, animal habitat, and informal access.

Confirm in advance:

- Local weather, tide, and flood forecasts
- Which roads remain passable during heavy rain
- Current industrial alarms and restricted zones
- Laws concerning photography, respirators, radios, and protective equipment
- Whether local pathogens, pollutants, or atmospheric chemistry differ from your home reality
- A secure indoor arrival and departure point above known flood levels

If multiverse travel can carry organisms between worlds, observe quarantine in both directions. Bring no loose soil, plants, or animals, and return with no mud, insects, feathers, water, or food residue on your equipment.

Obtain appropriate medical advice and vaccinations for a hot coastal industrial city. At minimum, consider protection relevant to tetanus, hepatitis, rabies exposure, mosquito-borne illness, contaminated water, and animal bites. Carry records in both physical and digital form.

## Clothing and protective equipment

Wear light, loose, quick-drying clothing that covers the arms and legs. Cotton will remain wet; technical fabric is preferable. Add:

- Waterproof safety boots with puncture-resistant soles and strong ankle support
- Several changes of socks
- A broad-brimmed hat
- Safety glasses or sealed goggles
- Nitrile gloves beneath tougher work gloves
- A high-visibility vest or jacket
- A compact rain shell, although it will trap heat
- Hearing protection for industrial areas
- A headlamp and backup light, preferably intrinsically safe if visiting fuel or processing districts

Carry a well-fitted particulate respirator for dust, dried waste, mold, feathers, and aerosols. Understand its limits: an ordinary filter does **not** protect against hydrogen sulfide, ammonia, solvent vapor, oxygen deficiency, or other industrial gases. Do not enter drains, tanks, culverts, pits, ship holds, or enclosed abandoned buildings without trained personnel, atmospheric testing, and proper breathing equipment.

Use effective insect repellent on skin and permethrin-treated clothing where medically appropriate. A mosquito head net may look excessive but will be useful near canals and at dusk.

## Heat, water, and food

Assume that every outdoor route will take longer than its map suggests. Shade is sparse, humidity prevents efficient cooling, and rain does not meaningfully lower the temperature.

Carry more sealed drinking water than expected, along with electrolytes. Drink steadily rather than waiting for thirst. Know the signs of heat exhaustion: headache, nausea, unusual fatigue, dizziness, confusion, and loss of coordination. Confusion or collapse is an emergency.

Do not drink from taps, cisterns, canals, roof runoff, or standing water unless a trusted local authority has confirmed the supply. Ordinary portable filters may not remove fuel, heavy metals, dissolved industrial chemicals, or salt.

Keep food in rigid, sealed containers. Soft bags will attract rats, dogs, pigs, insects, and birds. Eat indoors when possible, and never leave scraps behind. The local animals are accustomed to associating industrial activity with sudden food pulses; opening food in the wrong place can draw attention quickly.

## Movement through the city

Travel in an enclosed, air-conditioned vehicle with a local driver whenever possible. Choose a vehicle with good clearance, but do not mistake clearance for flood capability. Brown water conceals missing pavement, open drains, sharp scrap, submerged curbs, and collapsed culverts.

On foot:

- Stay on designated raised routes.
- Avoid the edges of canals and concrete aprons, which may be undermined.
- Test nothing by stepping on it.
- Do not use corroded railings as your sole support.
- Never cross rail yards or loading areas without authorization.
- Give trucks, dredges, cranes, and articulated haulers enormous clearance.
- Wear high visibility even if roads seem empty.
- Keep out of the path between animals and water, cover, or food.

The emptiness is deceptive. Machinery may start remotely, trains may move with little warning, and a single vehicle can occupy most of a road. Reversing equipment is especially dangerous in rain and haze.

Carry an offline map, but do not rely on old street plans. Former roads may now be canals, flood barriers may block direct routes, and industrial compounds may have only one usable gate. Mark several elevated refuges and at least two return routes.

## Rain and flooding

Treat the first drops as a logistical warning, not a pleasant change in weather. Animals will begin moving, visibility will drop, surfaces will become slick, and low routes may fill before the storm appears severe.

When heavy rain approaches:

1. Leave mudflats, drainage basins, underpasses, culverts, and subsided roads.
2. Move to a known occupied building or engineered high ground.
3. Do not shelter beneath bridges containing bat colonies or above active drains.
4. Avoid touching wet electrical cabinets, fences, rails, or pipes.
5. Never walk through flowing water if you cannot see the surface beneath it.
6. Do not assume floodwater is only rainwater; it may contain sewage, blood, grease, chemicals, sharp metal, and displaced animals.

Carry essential items in roll-top waterproof bags. Keep identification, medicines, communications equipment, and the multiversal return mechanism on your body rather than in a vehicle that may become inaccessible.

## Animals

Most animals will avoid a calm adult who gives them room. The risk rises around food, nests, confined passages, and flood-driven movement.

- **Dogs:** Do not run. Avoid staring, shouting, or approaching a pack. Back away slowly and place a solid barrier between yourself and them.
- **Feral pigs:** Give them a very wide berth, particularly in narrow yards or near piglets. Never try to force one out of cover.
- **Rats:** Do not reach into pallets, conduits, rubble, or dark cabinets. Protect food and wash any bite or scratch immediately.
- **Birds:** Avoid nesting roofs and loading events. Dense flocks can obscure vision, foul equipment, and become aggressive near nests.
- **Crabs:** Wear puncture-resistant boots. Do not cross a mass movement at low light; footing beneath them may already be mud or broken glass.
- **Monitor lizards and snakes:** Do not corner, handle, or step over them. Inspect dry ledges, cable galleries, and warm sheltered spaces before placing hands or sitting.
- **Bats:** Never handle one, even if it appears dead. Avoid breathing concentrated dust beneath large roosts.
- **Insects:** Keep cuffs closed, shake out clothing and boots, sleep behind intact mesh, and avoid bright open lamps near your face.

Any mammal bite, bat contact, deep puncture, or wound exposed to canal water requires prompt medical care. Wash wounds copiously with clean water and soap, but do not let cleaning delay evacuation.

## Air and enclosed spaces

The city’s smells are not a reliable guide to safety. A sulfurous odor may indicate mud, industrial discharge, or hydrogen sulfide; at dangerous concentrations, hydrogen sulfide can deaden the sense of smell. Rendering plants, drains, refrigeration systems, fuel yards, and settling ponds may also release ammonia, methane, solvents, or oxygen-displacing gases.

If you experience eye irritation, sudden headache, dizziness, nausea, breathlessness, confusion, or an unusual metallic or chemical taste, move crosswind and toward open, elevated ground. Do not investigate the source. Never attempt to rescue someone from a pit or tank without proper equipment; confined-space rescuers commonly become victims themselves.

## Accommodation

Choose an occupied building with:

- Sleeping rooms above historical flood stains
- Screened windows and intact doors
- Reliable air-conditioning or ventilation
- Backup power
- Potable stored water
- A known evacuation route that does not pass through a basement
- No direct connection to open service tunnels
- A staffed entrance and secure food storage

Avoid ground-floor rooms beside canals, waste yards, or loading docks. Check bedding, shoes, bags, and bathroom drains for insects. Keep luggage elevated and closed.

Power failure matters here: refrigeration, ventilation, pumps, lighting, and insect barriers may all fail together. Keep a light, water, respirator, footwear, and evacuation bag within reach while sleeping.

## Suggested travel rhythm

Move early in the morning, before metal surfaces become dangerously hot. Spend midday indoors. If you go out again, do so before dusk and return before the main movement of bats, insects, dogs, rats, and storm-displaced animals.

The safest way to experience the city is from controlled edges: an occupied quay office, a raised service road, a working ferry, or an upper-floor window overlooking the drainage basins. Do not seek “authenticity” by entering abandoned mills, walking mudflats, following animals into culverts, or touring flood channels after rain.

## Essential kit

A practical minimum would include:

- Identification, permits, local currency, and emergency contacts
- Two independent communication methods
- Offline maps and tide tables
- Sealed drinking water and electrolytes
- Personal medicines and a substantial first-aid kit
- Waterproof bags
- Headlamp and backup batteries
- Particulate respirators
- Eye protection and gloves
- Insect repellent and mosquito netting
- Puncture-resistant boots
- High-visibility outer layer
- Wound-cleaning supplies
- Emergency shelter and signaling equipment
- A reliable, waterproofed return device, if applicable

A trained guide may also carry a calibrated multi-gas detector, but possession is not a substitute for knowing how to interpret it.

## Mental preparation

Expect scale, isolation, and monotony rather than constant spectacle. You may walk or drive for long periods through wet concrete, walls, tanks, and haze without seeing another person. Then a food discharge, tide change, or incoming storm may set thousands of animals in motion at once. Do not let the long quiet make you careless.

The city is not dead, nor is it simply ruined. It is a functioning ecosystem organized around drainage, waste, machinery, tide, and shelter. Its residents—human and otherwise—understand those rhythms better than a visitor will. Your safest posture is to remain clean, enclosed, visible to workers, uninteresting to animals, and always ready to move uphill before the rain.
