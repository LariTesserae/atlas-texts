## General assessment

This is not a wilderness that happens to contain abandoned cities. It is an active landscape whose ecology, infrastructure, geometry, and perhaps intelligence are inseparable. Treat the plateau as inhabited, even if nothing there walks or speaks.

For an ordinary human, the greatest dangers are not attack. They are:

- hypoxia, cold, ultraviolet exposure, and dehydration;
- biological contamination in both directions;
- flash floods and slow but powerful plant mechanisms;
- unreliable distance and interior volume;
- local differences in the rate of time;
- delayed or displaced sound;
- becoming separated from the return route while apparently remaining nearby.

The safest expedition is short, sealed, conservative, and preceded by robotic reconnaissance. Do not visit alone.

## Before crossing

### Establish whether a human can exist there at all

Send instruments through first and leave them for at least one local day-night cycle. Measure:

- atmospheric pressure and oxygen partial pressure;
- carbon dioxide and other gases, especially in depressions and buildings;
- temperature, wind, humidity, and ultraviolet intensity;
- airborne spores, pollen, dust, and volatile plant compounds;
- water chemistry;
- radiation and electromagnetic conditions;
- discrepancies among several independent clocks;
- whether radio, laser ranging, radar, and physical tethers agree about distance.

Do not infer breathability merely from the presence of green plants and an ordinary sun. The plateau may have ample oxygen but insufficient pressure, or chemically active aerosols harmless to local vegetation and dangerous to lungs.

Test whether equipment returns with the same elapsed time it reports internally. A probe that spends an hour in a square while ten days pass outside is not malfunctioning until proven otherwise.

### Quarantine is essential

The plateau apparently has no animals and may never have encountered animal-associated microbes. A human body carries bacteria, fungi, skin cells, mites, oils, salts, and organic waste that could permanently alter such an ecology.

Use a sealed environmental suit with closed-loop breathing and waste containment. Sterilize the exterior before entry. Nothing should be discarded—not food crumbs, exhaled moisture, blood, hair, sewage, packaging, or wash water.

The same caution applies on return. Local spores or reproductive particles may be inert on the plateau but invasive or toxic at home. Decontaminate equipment remotely and quarantine the travelers.

### Acclimatize and train

If the pressure is survivable but low, acclimatize at altitude beforehand. Nevertheless, carry supplemental oxygen; folded roads and temporal pockets can turn an intended afternoon walk into a much longer exposure.

Train the team in:

- cold injury and altitude illness;
- confined-space gas hazards;
- rope rescue without relying on verbal commands;
- visual signaling;
- sterile field practice;
- navigation without satellite positioning;
- recognizing flash-flood terrain;
- strict “stop rather than explore” discipline.

Agree in advance that any team member may end the expedition without debate.

## Personal equipment

At minimum, carry:

- a sealed, insulated suit with a full-face impact visor;
- independent oxygen and emergency reserve;
- active heating and strong ultraviolet protection;
- eye protection capable of resisting fired seeds and windborne grit;
- two or more clocks based on different mechanisms;
- redundant navigation instruments, including inertial tracking;
- a body camera recording continuously;
- line-of-sight optical communicators and hand signals;
- radio, but never radio alone;
- chemical air and water sensors;
- all food and drinking water needed for the maximum plausible stay;
- a compact insulated shelter;
- noncombustion heating and cooking equipment;
- climbing protection for broken streets, reservoir stairs, and plateau edges;
- a medical kit emphasizing hypoxia, frostbite, eye injury, and puncture wounds;
- several passive location markers that are sterile, retrievable, and visually distinctive.

Avoid loose cords around roots, levers, gates, and counterweights. Do not wear anything that can be caught by a structure moving too slowly to be noticed.

Bring hearing protection. A loud impact may be released much later and much closer than expected.

## Expedition organization

A team of three or four is preferable. Remain within direct sight of one another. Sound cannot be trusted, and a teammate’s footsteps may arrive before or after the teammate.

Leave a staffed base at the crossing point, preferably on bare rock beyond any city’s active root system. The base should maintain a continuous visual and instrument record of the portal or return mechanism. If possible, keep the route home physically open rather than assuming it can be found again.

Set limits according to the base team’s clock, the travelers’ clocks, and observed environmental cycles. If the clocks begin to disagree beyond a predetermined margin, return immediately.

Do not rely on a tether as proof of geometry. A road can loop while appearing straight, and a rope crossing different temporal rates may develop unexpected tension or deterioration. Tethers are useful only as supplementary evidence.

## Moving across the plateau

### Walk slowly and assume every system is active

The cities maintain themselves. A quiet gate may close when roots swell. A wall panel may tilt at frost. A counterweight may release when ice melts. A workshop branch may complete its annual cutting stroke while someone is standing beside it.

Avoid:

- counterweighted poles;
- root bundles under tension;
- ice-supported weights;
- channels carrying vines or capillary cords;
- flat stone panels aimed toward the sun;
- seed capsules during pressure changes;
- dry riverbeds;
- cistern mouths and underground conduits;
- root-grown gates near dawn, rain, or a freeze.

Do not camp in streets, gardens, workshops, counting courts, or beneath frost-driven structures. Use exposed, biologically sparse rock only after checking that it is not a road, roof, temporal boundary, or reflective anomaly.

### Respect the local markings

Tentatively interpret:

- **white stones** as indicating that a route currently behaves as it appears;
- **red ochre** as a rain-dependent destination warning;
- **three vertical cuts** as a prohibition on entering during frost.

But treat every mark as current advice, not permanent law. Examine whether pigment is fresh, whether lichen is actively renewing it, and whether rain or frost has occurred since it was last assessed.

Do not move the markers. Do not “correct” them according to your own map.

### Never enter anomalous interiors casually

Refuse any doorway, alley, reservoir stair, or seam that violates visible dimensions. An exterior wall smaller than its room is not an architectural curiosity; it is a boundary whose exit conditions are unknown.

At the summit city, do not step through freestanding doors. In particular, avoid any doorway showing:

- another weather system;
- another historical state of the city;
- movement that ceases under observation;
- darkness with air, odor, or roots emerging from it.

Observing such a threshold from outside is already an expedition objective. Crossing it should require a separate mission designed around the specific anomaly.

## Temporal safety

Learn to recognize a time boundary before entering it. Warning signs include:

- suspended condensation;
- streams or waves that remain fixed;
- plants cycling visibly fast;
- fresh organic material in an otherwise ancient district;
- neighboring shadows that move at different rates;
- inconsistent frost formation;
- disagreements among clocks or camera frame timing;
- gates placed where no defensive barrier is otherwise needed.

The gates are likely pacing devices. Obey them. Pause where the local design indicates a pause, but do not cross if instruments continue to disagree.

Never have one teammate enter a temporal pocket while others wait outside. “A moment” for the traveler may be years for the team, or the reverse. Tissues, oxygen supply, batteries, medications, and stored food may each respond differently.

If you accidentally cross a boundary, stop. Do not run back and forth through it. Compare clocks and visible motion, retreat once along the exact path of entry, and abort the mission.

## Water and food

Carry both. Do not eat the vegetation, even if it resembles ferns, reeds, fruit, or cultivated crops. Local plants may contain antifreeze compounds, mineral concentrations, signaling chemicals, or structural fibers toxic to animals. Eating them would also introduce saliva and gut microbes.

Do not drink from cisterns or pools. They are part of the plateau’s functioning water network and may be chemically or biologically unsuitable. Cisterns may also contain low-oxygen or carbon-dioxide-rich atmospheres.

Keep far from dry riverbeds when a silver rain front is visible anywhere in the drainage region. The rivers arrive from distant storms and can spread across channels kilometers wide without local warning. A shallow sheet can still knock a person down, carry equipment away, or strand the team behind a newly altered route.

## Navigation

Map events and conditions, not merely coordinates. A useful field entry would read:

> “After frost, before direct sun, white-marked north street from the third cistern reaches bare basin in 42 minutes.”

A simple line on a map may be false after rain, at another time of day, or when approached from the opposite direction.

At every turn, record:

- direction of sun and shadows;
- frost and moisture state;
- color and placement of route marks;
- visible plant orientation;
- clock readings;
- travel time in both directions;
- whether the return view matches the outward view.

If a road returns to its starting point unexpectedly, do not repeat it to “solve” the loop. Stop in open ground, wait for a stable weather state, and retrace only a route already verified under matching conditions. If no verified route remains, preserve oxygen and signal visually rather than exploring further.

## Conduct toward the cities

Assume the maintenance systems can perceive pressure, moisture, vibration, shading, temperature, and chemical change even if they cannot see you. A warm animal standing in a frost field may be interpreted as weather, damage, raw material, or a message.

Therefore:

- do not cut plants or roots;
- do not draw water;
- do not open or block valves;
- do not lift cistern lids;
- do not rearrange counting stones;
- do not ring copper plates or deliberately strike resonant stone;
- do not enter workshops;
- do not shade frost leaves for prolonged periods;
- do not make fires;
- do not leave offerings unless communication has been demonstrated.

The counting courts may be archives, forecasts, or active conversations. Moving one stone could be the equivalent of altering a civic record—or interrupting a sentence centuries long.

If attempting communication, begin only with observation. Record recurring changes from a distance. Introduce no pattern until you can distinguish a reply from frost, root growth, and maintenance.

## What the visit will feel like

The psychological strain will come from activity without companionship. Everything will look tended, yet no caretaker will arrive. You may wait beside a workshop for hours and see nothing happen, then compare recordings and discover that a branch advanced by a fraction of a millimeter.

The silence will not feel empty. It will feel withheld. You will see your boot strike stone and may hear nothing; much later, in clear sunlight, the missing footfall may arrive from beyond a wall. Team members must be prepared not to treat every delayed sound as an approaching being.

The scale will also be deceptive. The country will appear horizontal and almost domestic—gardens, gutters, paving, low roofs—while the cloud floor reveals that you are walking above most weather. Cities that seem close on a clear horizon may be days away, inaccessible by the road that visibly connects them.

The central summit should be regarded as the culmination of a later expedition, not the first destination. Its reflective ground appears to expose other skies or times, and the oldest city’s doors are active despite the loss of their walls. Ordinary human instincts—entering a doorway, following a road, stepping closer to see—are exactly the instincts most likely to cause disappearance there.

## Abort conditions

Return immediately if:

- any suit breach or biological contamination occurs;
- oxygen use exceeds the planned rate;
- two clocks diverge significantly;
- the sun or shadows no longer agree with the base station;
- the return route changes after rain or frost;
- a teammate is visible but cannot be reached along the apparent distance;
- route markers move while under direct observation;
- condensation stops falling around the team;
- vegetation begins cycling rapidly;
- roots, gates, or roads respond specifically to the travelers;
- the portal or base signal appears from more than one direction;
- a sound associated with the team arrives before its apparent cause.

If return remains possible, do not spend the expedition trying to decide whether the plateau is a civilization, a machine, an organism, or a world whose categories have eroded. That question is likely to lure travelers into touching the mechanisms that embody the answer.

Visit as one would enter the living tissue of something ancient: sealed, quiet, observant, and ready to withdraw before it notices—or before you become part of its maintenance.
