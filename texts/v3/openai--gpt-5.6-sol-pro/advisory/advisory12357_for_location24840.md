## General assessment

Treat the Dry Crown as three things at once:

1. **An extreme high-altitude environment**
2. **A single, continent-sized living organism**
3. **An inhabited city whose streets, utilities, buildings, and communication systems are biological**

An ordinary human should not visit independently. Arrange a local guide, a sealed habitat, and permission from the relevant civic root authority before arrival. The Crown is not overtly hostile, but nearly every human necessity—water, humid air, soft ground, ordinary food—is foreign to it.

You will be the wettest object for kilometers. Prepare accordingly.

## Essential life support

### Use a fully closed environmental suit

Do not trust the local atmosphere merely because native animals breathe it. Bring:

- Independent oxygen supply
- Pressure regulation suitable for a very thin atmosphere
- Full exhalation capture
- Closed-loop water recovery
- Internal heating with redundant power
- Radiation and ultraviolet protection
- Eye protection against abrasive dust and sudden reflected light
- A compact emergency pressure shelter

Your suit should retain perspiration, breath moisture, urine, and washing water. A leaking hydration reservoir may constitute both a survival emergency and a biological contamination event. Water could be toxic, disruptive, valuable, or all three to Crown organisms.

Do not vent waste of any kind.

### Bring all food and water

Nothing local should be considered edible or drinkable. “Sap,” resin, salts, graft tissue, and fruiting bodies participate in an ionic metabolism unrelated to human digestion. Even apparently inert mineral food may contain mobile defects, reactive charge gradients, or microscopic structures capable of integrating with local tissue.

Use sealed rations that produce few crumbs. Keep food preparation inside your habitat.

### Expect profound dryness

Ordinary seals, lubricants, adhesives, and flexible plastics may become brittle. Use vacuum-rated equipment and dry lubricants. Carry extra seals for your suit and shelter.

The dryness can also produce dangerous static potentials. Equipment should have controlled charge dissipation, but do not ground yourself directly into the living pavement. Use a locally approved electrical interface mat.

## Clothing and movement

The wind is permanent, strong, abrasive, and culturally meaningful.

Wear:

- A low-profile suit without loose straps
- Hard, broad-soled boots that will not pierce root-bark plates
- An aerodynamic pack
- A climbing-rated tether for exposed districts
- A retractable face shield
- Replaceable abrasion layers

Do not wear fluttering cloth, whistles, exposed cables, or anything that produces rhythmic vibration. Accidental sounds may be interpreted as traffic signals, civic messages, animal calls, or construction instructions.

Never drive spikes into the ground. Do not collect samples, cut stems, scrape pavement, or insert probes without permission. The “stone” beneath you may be skin, roadway, archive, sensory tissue, or someone’s wall.

Stay on routes marked by **orange nodules**. Treat **violet bands** as absolute exclusion markers. Do not assume an unmarked route is safe simply because it looks empty.

## Arrival

Arrange a designated arrival site. Rocket exhaust, hot landing gear, chemical propellant residue, or downward-thrusting engines could sterilize centuries of growth or accidentally stimulate the substrate. A shuttle should use an approved inert landing surface or transfer passengers from a distant hard pad.

On arrival:

1. Wait for the local guide to establish contact with the ground network.
2. Discharge static through an approved coupler.
3. Calibrate your suit to local pressure, radiation, wind, and ionic conditions.
4. Record the current traffic rhythms and emergency tones.
5. Confirm the nearest sealed human-compatible refuge.
6. Mark the plateau edge and migration grooves on your navigator.

The city is dispersed over enormous distances. A “nearby” market or residence may be several kilometers away.

## Sound protection and communication

The Crown is extremely loud, including at frequencies below ordinary hearing. Earplugs alone are insufficient because pipe-tree tones and root pulses can travel through your skeleton.

Bring:

- Active hearing protection
- Infrasound monitoring
- Chest and jaw vibration dampers
- A bite guard, especially if you have dental implants or loose fillings
- A translator capable of recognizing civic rhythms
- Bone-conduction communications that can distinguish human speech from ground pulses

Do not block all local sound. The city uses music for traffic control, public announcements, timekeeping, weather warnings, and emergencies. Your guide should provide a simplified vocabulary of essential patterns: stop, clear the route, shelter, animal migration, damaged tower, and approaching Listening.

Public time pulses may vibrate tools, teeth, suit frames, and implants. Secure anything loose before the hour.

During festivals, sound levels and structural resonances may change radically. Humans with heart arrhythmias, seizure disorders, severe tinnitus, vestibular problems, or vibration-sensitive implants should avoid festival districts.

## The wind and the Listening

If the wind begins to fail, do not celebrate the calm.

Follow local behavior immediately:

- Enter the nearest recognized shelter.
- Close and seal doors.
- Stop unnecessary movement and transmission.
- Lower your profile.
- Do not strike the pavement, drill, broadcast sonar, or attempt to answer the deep knock.
- Wait until local inhabitants resume normal activity.

The natives fear or respect windless intervals for good reason. Predators stop hunting, animals press themselves to the ground, and the city listens to something beneath its oldest layers. A human visitor should not be the loudest object during that silence.

If you hear the subterranean knock through your suit, simply log it. Do not synchronize equipment to it. Do not let automated systems transmit a reply.

## Local fauna

### Shardbacks

Give herds a wide berth. Their migration grooves are functioning roads, not convenient campsites. Do not stand between a herd and a grafting station. Their open lattice bodies do not mean they are fragile.

### Bell-winged creatures

These animals alter the pitch of towers by landing on them. A sudden chord change may be a warning created by the flock rather than by the city. If they gather on a structure near you, move under guidance rather than waiting to learn what they detected.

Never try to catch one.

### Ribbon-beasts and stationary hunters

Do not linger beside seams in the living pavement, particularly at night. A dead-looking trunk near a seam may be a predator that has waited years for movement.

If the ground flinches, do not run blindly. Step onto a marked reinforced path and follow your guide. Running across root plates may create the same vibration pattern as fleeing prey.

### Sailers

Avoid the bases and downwind approaches of western spires at dusk. Sailers navigate by city-song and may collide with structures when a tower is mistuned. During repairs, remain under a canopy or inside a shelter.

Do not use powerful acoustic equipment near their flight paths.

## City etiquette

Remember that almost everything is alive, inhabited, or part of public infrastructure.

- Ask before touching a wall.
- Do not tap structures to hear how they sound.
- Do not lean against translucent wall patches; they may be eyes.
- Do not tether yourself to conductive roots.
- Do not step on buds, capsules, or raised vascular lines.
- Do not refer to a civic spire as “just a tree.”
- Do not call construction “gardening” unless locals do so first.
- Do not complain that a building is unfinished merely because it is still growing.

Construction here takes years or centuries. A road rising a few millimeters may be an active public works project. The local idea of urgency is unlikely to match yours, so build generous delays into every itinerary.

The city may communicate through vibration rather than speech. Stand where your guide places you during introductions. Your posture, foot pressure, suit motors, and heartbeat may all be perceptible to the substrate.

## Electronics and biological interfaces

The root network carries rapid signals, civic memories, transit instructions, and perhaps personalities. Keep human systems isolated from it unless they were specifically designed for Crown contact.

Recommended precautions:

- Use optical isolation between local interfaces and your computers.
- Keep navigation and life support on separate circuits.
- Maintain offline, read-only backups.
- Carry paper or etched-metal records as well as digital ones.
- Disable automatic pairing, neural links, and adaptive implants.
- Do not allow a local root to “diagnose” your nervous system.
- Record a cognitive baseline before the trip.
- Conduct daily memory checks with a companion.

The city’s powerful ionic and vibrational activity may confuse compasses, biosensors, clocks, and implant telemetry. Bring redundant inertial navigation and visual landmarks rather than relying on one electronic system.

## Municipal transit

Do not personally use the bud-and-capsule transit network.

The system has produced duplicate instruments, lost memories, and displaced ancient organisms. Whether it copies, folds, reconstructs, or reroutes objects is irrelevant to an ordinary traveler: it is not safe by human standards.

If you must send cargo through it:

- Send nothing alive.
- Send nothing irreplaceable.
- Do not send your only oxygen reserve.
- Label both the object and its expected duplicate.
- Treat anything returned as potentially nonidentical.
- Do not place active transmitters inside unless the city requests them.
- Never enter a capsule to “see how it works.”

Local residents may complain casually about delays. Their familiarity is not evidence of human compatibility.

## Death and the blue flowers

Avoid sites where small blue flowers have appeared. They mark a recent death and feed on electrical disorganization rather than decay.

Their petals close near coherent memory. Do not test this response with recordings, neural implants, preserved brains, or grieving companions. The flowers may detect something that human science does not yet distinguish safely.

Before visiting, establish a plan for human remains. A human corpse contains an enormous amount of water by Crown standards and must not be left exposed. Repatriation or sealed cold containment is preferable. Do not permit local scavenger lichens to process a human body without explicit prior consent from the traveler and both jurisdictions.

## The Hollow Meridian

The Meridian should be treated as an active anomalous installation, not a monument.

Visit only in daylight, with civic permission, an experienced guide, and a fixed withdrawal time. Stay outside any locally marked inner boundary. Do not touch the polished trunks, cast lights onto them, or use their apparent shadows for navigation.

Avoid the Meridian entirely on the coldest nights.

If the trunks begin to turn or the gaps reveal another landscape:

- Do not approach the opening.
- Do not wave, signal, or answer distant songs.
- Do not assume the visible plateau is uninhabited.
- Do not step through, even if you can see familiar people or equipment.
- Do not extend a tether, drone, camera pole, or radio antenna across the apparent boundary.
- Withdraw by your previously marked route.
- Verify each party member’s identity and memory afterward.

A view of another Crown may be a portal, a transmission, a reproductive process, or a civic memory. None of those possibilities makes casual contact wise.

## Emergency planning

Carry at least two independent emergency systems:

- A radio beacon for your own expedition
- A local acoustic or vibrational distress device approved by the city

Also carry:

- Two days of reserve oxygen beyond the planned excursion
- A sealed bivouac
- Thermal backup
- A personal tether
- An abrasion repair kit
- Medical oxygen and pressure-treatment capability
- A nonpenetrating rescue sled
- Analog identity and medical records
- A route map showing shelters, plateau edges, migration grooves, and violet zones

Never pursue equipment blown toward the cliffs. The wind has had geological time to perfect its route over the edge; you will not outmaneuver it.

If separated, do not shelter beside something merely because it resembles a tree or building. Use only verified civic shelters. Some hunters are more patient than human architecture.

## Best way to experience the Crown

For a first visit, stay in one inhabited district rather than attempting the central wilderness. Spend several days learning the local rhythms before traveling far. Good experiences would include:

- Watching the towers turn their mirrored leaves toward the stars
- Listening to a civic announcement with a translator
- Visiting a market canopy with a guide
- Observing shardbacks from a reinforced overlook
- Feeling a public time pulse through an isolation platform
- Hearing a distant festival from inside a pressure shelter

The Dry Crown rewards patience. Its buildings may take years to answer a request, its predators may wait for decades, and its oldest structure may open only for a few cold hours beneath unfamiliar stars. Move slowly, carry your own atmosphere, keep your water to yourself, and never mistake stillness for inactivity.
