Treat this as a remote, high-altitude polar expedition, not as an unusually scenic hike. The basin offers little shelter, unreliable footing, near-freezing water, geothermal hazards, and almost no margin for an ordinary unacclimatized human. Its openness is deceptive: you may see your destination all day without reaching it.

## Before crossing over

### Confirm human compatibility

Do not assume that “breathable” means safe for sustained exertion. Send instruments or a sterile probe first and verify:

- Oxygen partial pressure, not merely oxygen percentage
- Atmospheric pressure and concentrations of carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and other volcanic gases
- Temperature, wind, humidity, and storm cycles
- Local day length, gravity, magnetic behavior, and solar or cosmic radiation
- Water chemistry and biological contamination
- Whether local organisms, spores, or mineral aerosols are hazardous to terrestrial lungs and skin

The geothermal field deserves special attention. Air that is harmless on the basin floor may become lethal in a low hollow where heavier gases collect. Equip every traveler with a continuously operating oxygen and multi-gas alarm.

Use strict quarantine protocols in both directions. This is a young, nearly pristine ecosystem whose first soils and microbial communities could be radically altered by microbes carried on boots, food, waste, or clothing. Likewise, unfamiliar microorganisms may not be visibly threatening but should not be brought home casually.

### Acclimatize elsewhere

The phrase “almost at the limit of breathable air” should be taken literally. Spend days or weeks acclimatizing at progressively greater elevations before arrival. Even fit travelers can develop acute mountain sickness.

Bring supplemental oxygen sufficient for:

- The initial arrival
- Sleep during the first nights
- Medical treatment
- A delayed retreat
- At least one equipment failure

Know the signs of dangerous altitude illness: worsening headache, vomiting, confusion, loss of coordination, breathlessness at rest, wet coughing, irrational behavior, or unusual drowsiness. Descent is the primary treatment. A pressure bag and appropriate expedition medication are sensible, but they are not substitutes for evacuation.

No one should travel alone.

## Clothing and personal equipment

The central problem is not extreme cold but continuous wet cold around freezing. That is excellent weather for hypothermia: rain and meltwater defeat insulation while wind removes heat.

Wear a layered system:

- Nonabsorbent base layers
- Warm fleece or wool midlayers
- A synthetic or carefully protected down insulation layer
- Fully waterproof, highly wind-resistant outer shell
- Waterproof overtrousers
- Insulated gloves plus waterproof shell mitts
- Several dry glove liners
- Warm hat, face protection, and eye protection
- Gaiters
- Spare dry clothing sealed in independent waterproof bags

Use insulated mountaineering boots with stiff soles and strong ankle support. They must cope with slick slabs, hidden water, sharp fresh rock, soft mineral mud, and repeated shallow crossings. Carry traction devices for polished or newly frozen surfaces, but remove them on fragile geothermal crusts and loose moraine where they may catch.

Take glacier-rated sunglasses or goggles even under overcast. Diffuse cloud light and snow glare can still damage the eyes, and clearing conditions may become dazzling without warning. Sunscreen remains necessary at altitude.

Every traveler should carry:

- Trekking poles
- Helmet
- Personal gas monitor
- Emergency oxygen
- Headlamp with cold-resistant spare batteries
- Whistle and radio
- Navigation instruments
- Waterproof bivouac sack
- Compact sleeping insulation
- High-energy food
- Thermos or insulated bottle
- Fireless chemical heat packs
- First-aid and altitude-treatment kit

Open flames are a poor choice near unknown vent gases and unreliable in the wet wind. Use sealed, tested cooking equipment only in well-ventilated, gas-safe locations.

## Shelter and camp

There are no trees, caves worth trusting, or dry natural camps. Rock overhangs will drip, collect gas, and may shed newly fractured stone. The ground that looks solid may be saturated directly beneath the gravel.

Bring a low-profile expedition tent designed for severe wind and sleet, with:

- A fully waterproof floor
- An oversized groundsheet
- Snow or deadman anchors for loose sediment
- Extra guy lines
- Repair sleeves and patches
- Closed-cell foam beneath inflatable sleeping pads
- A sleeping system rated well below the forecast minimum

Choose camp on a broad, slightly raised gravel bench. Avoid:

- Drainage channels, even dry ones
- The toe of scree and talus fans
- Moraine crests with steep sides
- Fresh slump cracks
- Depressions where gas can collect
- Pool margins
- Snow banks beneath cliffs
- Glacier fronts and terminal-lake shores
- Geothermal crusts
- Bird nesting, feeding, or resting areas

Probe the intended site. If water rises around your boot or trekking pole, move. A few centimeters of elevation may determine whether the tent remains dry when meltwater shifts overnight.

Mark the camp with reflective wands and record its coordinates. Mist can erase a tent from sight at surprisingly short range.

## Movement through the basin

### Plan by terrain, not apparent distance

Distances will be consistently underestimated because the land has few familiar scale cues. Dark ridges, animals, and vapor columns may be many times farther away than they appear. Set conservative turnaround times and assume travel will take at least twice the visual estimate.

Move in groups of three or more. Maintain visual contact in cloud, and use radios rather than shouting into broad wind. Establish waypoints for camp, safe water crossings, geothermal boundaries, and the transfer portal or extraction point. Do not rely on a magnetic compass until local magnetic conditions have been verified.

Carry redundant navigation:

- Topographic or probe-generated maps
- GNSS or equivalent positioning system, if local satellites or beacons exist
- Inertial navigation
- Altimeter
- Physical compass
- Reflective route markers
- Written bearings and return instructions

The floor is not simply flat gravel. Expect hidden channels, saturated silt, unstable moraine, polished slabs, thin ice, and pockets where the surface collapses under body weight. Test suspicious ground with poles. Spread the party out on questionable sediment so everyone is not standing on the same weak patch.

Freshly uncovered rock can be unusually sharp. Wear gloves when scrambling, and protect tent fabric, ropes, and inflatable pads from contact with it.

### Streams and wetlands

Streams may split, vanish, reappear, and change route within hours. A shallow braid can still knock a person down because the bed is slick and the water painfully cold.

Cross only where the bed is visible and stable. Unbuckle pack hip belts, use poles, and cross as a coordinated group if necessary. Do not step from one apparently fixed slab to another without testing them; meltwater may have removed the supporting gravel beneath.

Carry spare socks and foot insulation in a waterproof bag. Stop immediately to deal with soaked footwear. At this altitude and temperature, “walking it dry” can become cold injury.

Avoid trampling the gray marsh margins. Their first roots may be the beginning of the basin’s future soil, and damage that looks trivial could persist for many seasons.

### Glacier and terminal lake

Do not approach or travel on the glacier without a trained glacier team, ropes, harnesses, rescue equipment, and prior survey. Grit-covered ice can resemble rock. Crevasses may be hidden by thin snow or debris, and meltwater tunnels can collapse.

Stay well back from the glacier front and from the terminal lake. Calving can throw ice, create waves, break shore ice, and destabilize raw gravel banks. The fact that the lake is small does not make it safe.

Cliffs near the glacier are also subject to rockfall as ice support disappears and freeze-thaw loosens fresh fractures. Helmets reduce injury but do not make these zones suitable campsites.

## The geothermal field

The northern field is likely the most dangerous area despite its orderly appearance. Assume every colored or pale surface is suspect.

Hazards include:

- Thin silica crust over boiling water or mud
- Superheated steam escaping through nearly invisible cracks
- Sudden vent opening
- Acidic or mineral-rich water
- Toxic gas pockets
- Ground collapse
- Tremors and pressure releases
- Slippery microbial films
- Hot channels concealed by cold runoff

Approach only with remote thermal imaging and gas monitoring. Stay on surveyed mineral ground and use probes designed for geothermal travel. Never step on a white, yellow, orange, or unusually smooth crust merely because it supports scattered stones or moss. Do not trust animals as route finders; their tolerances and weight distribution may differ from yours.

Wind direction matters. Remain upwind of concentrated vapor. If a gas alarm activates, do not pause to investigate—move crosswind and then upslope. Never kneel or sleep in low terrain near vents.

Do not bathe in springs or drink from them. A clear pool may be acidic, arsenic-rich, nearly boiling beneath a cooler surface layer, or biologically active in unfamiliar ways. Keep metal equipment out of mineral channels unless it has been tested for corrosion.

The terraces are both fragile and potentially hollow. Observe from a distance wherever possible.

## Water, food, and health

Bring enough drinking water for the initial period. The abundance of surface water is misleading: glacial silt can overwhelm filters, geothermal solutes may not be removable by ordinary treatment, and bird concentrations can contaminate shallow pools.

For local water:

1. Collect from a surveyed cold source well away from geothermal flow, animal congregations, and marshes.
2. Let mineral silt settle.
3. Prefilter it.
4. Use a treatment system validated against the detected local biology and chemistry.
5. Boil only if atmospheric pressure and equipment permit reliable sterilization.
6. Reject water with unusual color, odor, warmth, oily films, or mineral rims.

At extreme altitude, appetite often declines while energy demands rise. Carry compact foods that remain edible when cold and require little cooking. Schedule food and fluids rather than waiting for hunger or thirst. Dehydration, exhaustion, hypothermia, and altitude illness can resemble one another and compound quickly.

Protect cuts immediately. Fresh rock, sulfurous water, mineral mud, and unknown microbes make even small abrasions undesirable.

## Animals

The wildlife is likely more vulnerable to you than you are to it, but keep distance.

Do not walk through flocks or between adults and young. Birds resting in the basin may be using their last reserves during migration; repeatedly forcing them to fly can be costly. Give grazing herds broad escape routes and never crowd them against streams, unstable slopes, or cliffs.

Predators may be difficult to detect because their apparent stillness covers great distances. Learn their local behavior before travel. Carry nonlethal deterrents suited to the species, keep food sealed, and do not leave blood, scraps, or human waste near camp.

Insect hatches may be brief and overwhelming. Bring a head net and skin coverage, though repellents should be used cautiously to avoid contaminating water and microbial surfaces.

## Environmental discipline

This basin is in the process of becoming a landscape. A careless camp could introduce nutrients, seeds, microbes, plastics, fuel, or erosion into a system with almost no resilience.

Use exceptionally strict minimum-impact procedures:

- Sterilize boots, poles, tents, and sampling tools before arrival.
- Bring no exposed soil, seeds, untreated wood, or live food.
- Pack out all waste, including human waste and wash water.
- Use containment toilets rather than digging into young sediment.
- Do not build cairns, trenches, windbreaks, or fire rings.
- Do not move stones casually; their undersides may shelter early communities.
- Do not pick lichens, mosses, or mineral crusts.
- Take no samples without a scientific and quarantine plan.
- Keep camps and walking routes away from pool margins and first-growth meadows.
- Avoid marking rock with paint or permanent anchors.

If route markers are needed, make them removable and account for every one before departure.

## Timing and evacuation

Visit during the most stable portion of the local warm season, after gathering enough observations to understand meltwater peaks, bird migrations, and geothermal behavior. “Summer” here will still involve sleet, ice, and sudden whiteout.

Plan short travel days. Establish hard limits for:

- Visibility
- Wind chill
- Oxygen saturation
- Gas concentrations
- Stream depth
- Time away from shelter
- Minimum oxygen and food reserves
- Turnaround time

Maintain a staffed refuge or transfer point outside the basin if possible. Your evacuation plan should not depend on aircraft: cloud, broad wind, weak visibility, and altitude may prevent flight. Cache sealed emergency shelters and oxygen along the retreat route, but remove them when the expedition ends.

If the multiverse crossing mechanism is not perfectly reliable, bring an independent return system or redundant portal hardware. A location this empty offers no rescue, repair shop, fuel depot, or local knowledge. A damaged boot, lost tent, or failed oxygen regulator can become fatal.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect the first hours to be physically unimpressive and psychologically immense. The mountains will seem near, the ground almost level, and the weather merely damp. Then your breathing will remain fast after small efforts, water will appear beneath apparently dry gravel, and an objective selected for lunch will still be distant at dusk.

You will hear more than you expect: stones rearranging under hidden streams, sleet ticking on thin ice, birds turning together, and occasional reports from the glacier that seem too large for the empty basin. The geothermal field may look engineered through the mist, but resist the urge to treat its rhythms as evidence of habitation. Its regularity is pressure and phase change, not machinery awaiting an operator.

Move slowly enough to notice the land without forcing yourself into it. The safest and most respectful visit will be one in which you remain a temporary, carefully contained presence—watching raw stone become wetland and slope, then leaving no new process behind.
