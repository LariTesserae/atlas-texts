## First principle: do not arrive directly in the water

An ordinary human cannot visit this world as a conventional diver. Several kilometers of water imply pressures of hundreds of atmospheres in many regions. The near-freezing temperature, conductive brine, permanent currents, electrical discharges, and planetary mechanical resonance make even a brief exposed excursion potentially fatal.

Arrive inside a **sealed, one-atmosphere pressure vessel**, preferably through a pressure-matched transit chamber. Never open a portal directly between this sea and an ordinary room or atmosphere. The resulting water jet, decompression, and pressure shock could destroy both the portal facility and anything nearby.

Your first crossing should be unmanned. Send instrument packages to determine:

- Exact ambient pressure and temperature
- Water salinity, dissolved metals, and toxicity
- Radiation levels near exposed ores
- Electrical conductivity and field strength
- Acoustic and seismic spectra
- Current velocity in three dimensions
- Local biological activity
- Whether the industrial structures react to foreign machinery
- Whether your portal remains stable under the standing vibration

Use redundant probes with optical communication. Ordinary sonar may be nearly useless beneath the continuous mechanical thunder.

## The appropriate vehicle

Think less of a submarine and more of a compact spacecraft built to operate underwater.

It should have:

- A rigid one-atmosphere cabin rated well beyond the measured pressure
- Layered acoustic and vibration isolation
- An interior cabin mounted on damped suspension rather than directly against the hull
- Fully closed-cycle air, water, waste, and heat-management systems
- Multiple independent power supplies
- Electrically isolated control systems and surge protection
- Nonconductive exterior sections where practical
- Protected thrusters with no exposed cables or delicate control surfaces
- Emergency ballast, anchors, and grapnels
- Inertial, optical, magnetic, gravimetric, and terrain-relative navigation
- Exterior floodlights whose brightness and spectrum can be carefully controlled
- Robotic arms for sampling
- A lock or docking collar compatible with your return portal
- Several days more endurance than the longest planned excursion

Avoid hull geometries with strong resonant cavities. This sea is already being driven like an enormous musical instrument. A poorly designed vessel may begin to vibrate sympathetically with a factory, cliff, or current band. That can loosen fittings, scramble instruments, exhaust occupants, or eventually fracture the hull.

The cabin must also protect the body, not merely the ears. Earplugs and active-noise headphones are insufficient when low-frequency energy travels through the hull, seat, skeleton, and internal organs. Use suspended seats, vibration-isolating couches, soft restraints, and continuous monitoring of heart rhythm, blood pressure, balance, hearing, and cognition.

If possible, begin on or above one of the highest summits. The water may be shallower there, reducing pressure and shortening the emergency return route. Do not assume such locations are safe, however: bubble-rich layers, collection funnels, fixed ice circulation, and industrial electrical activity may make summit regions mechanically complicated.

## Do not plan to swim

A normal wetsuit, drysuit, or flexible pressure suit is useless at depth. A human EVA requires a **one-atmosphere articulated diving suit**, effectively a person-shaped submersible. Even then, leaving the main vessel should be reserved for controlled work close to a secured shelter.

An EVA suit should include:

- A rigid pressure shell
- Powered joints
- A physical tether and separate optical data line
- Independent navigation beacons
- At least two closed breathing loops
- Whole-body heating
- Electrical-field alarms
- Vibration-dose monitoring
- Automatic retrieval capability
- A homing system that does not depend on hearing
- Smooth armor with no loose straps or loops

Never free-swim. Never rely on buoyancy alone to return. The currents occupy fixed routes and may include permanent upward and downward flows that are invisible until crossed. A traveler caught at a boundary could be held in circulation indefinitely or carried hundreds of kilometers from the vehicle.

## Navigation and scale

Your eyes will lie to you here.

The unusual clarity removes ordinary distance cues. A ridge that looks like a short crossing may be hundreds of kilometers away. Mountains remain visible long past the point at which terrestrial experience says they should have faded into haze. Do not estimate travel time visually.

Use surveyed coordinates and hard limits:

- Navigate by inertial position and mapped terrain.
- Measure every landmark rather than judging its apparent size.
- Mark the return route with recoverable optical transponders.
- Keep a strict turnaround reserve of at least fifty percent.
- Treat amber guide pylons as landmarks, not as promises of safety.
- Maintain a live route trace independent of the main computer.

Acoustic ranging will be distorted by density layers, resonant cliffs, and machinery. Magnetic navigation may also fail near magnetite-bearing animals, ore fields, cables, turbines, and metallic installations. No single system should be trusted.

Fixed currents may be reliable over immense spans of time, but learn their boundaries before entering them. A current that never changes is not necessarily gentle. Stable flow can be more dangerous than weather because there may be no natural pause in which to recover.

Do not enter the upward needle-ice cycles. The crystals may be thin, but a perpetual stream can abrade sensors, jam joints, puncture flexible surfaces, or carry a vehicle through the same hazardous loop repeatedly.

## Sound exposure

The continuous roar is one of the principal human hazards.

Expect:

- Bone-conducted noise
- Nausea and vertigo
- Loss of balance
- Headache and chest discomfort
- Sleep disruption
- Difficulty estimating time
- Auditory hallucinations
- Irritability and impaired judgment
- False perceptions of speech or approaching machinery
- Resonance-induced equipment fatigue

Communication inside the vehicle should be primarily visual and textual. Crew should use hand signals as a backup. Spoken commands may be misheard even if headsets function.

Build a “quiet cabin” suspended inside the main cabin, although true quiet may be impossible. Crew should sleep without rigid contact with the outer hull. Use artificial day-night lighting, scheduled meals, visible clocks, and frequent cognitive checks. No one should make critical decisions after prolonged vibration exposure without another person verifying them.

Record a baseline hearing and vestibular examination before travel. Establish mandatory exposure limits even if crew members insist they are adapting. The local animals evolved with the standing wave; humans did not.

## Cold and atmosphere

The water is near freezing everywhere. A pressure hull does not remove the thermal problem. Heating must be redundant, especially in suits, docking collars, optical ports, and battery compartments.

Use a completely closed breathing system. Do not vent exhaled gases. They will condense or form bubbles and then enter established current paths, potentially advertising your presence or affecting pressure-sensitive organisms.

Do not assume an industrial shelter contains breathable air merely because it has human-sized bunks, lockers, or handrails. It may be flooded, evacuated, filled with an inert gas, maintained at local water pressure, or contaminated with metal vapor and decomposition products. Test from outside before docking.

If you find a genuinely air-filled chamber, do not equalize it with your vessel until its pressure and composition are known.

## Electrical hazards

Water near installations may carry large, controlled discharges. Blue-white arcs that appear slow are not harmless. Conductive seawater can transmit faults through hull fittings, robotic arms, tethers, and sampling equipment.

Maintain generous distance from:

- Transformer yards
- Turbine chains
- Tower cables
- Drill shafts
- Pressure foundries
- Active intake shutters
- Bright discharge regions
- Metallic sulfide shelves connected to machinery

Use fiber-optic communication rather than conductive tethers whenever possible. Manipulator tools should be electrically isolated. Never connect your vessel directly to local power, no matter how compatible the voltage appears.

Some predators alter conductivity to divert prey. A human vehicle may resemble either a current boundary, an obstacle, or an unusually rich electrical source. Keep exterior emissions low. If instruments detect a conductivity gradient forming around the vessel, withdraw slowly along the route by which you arrived. Do not respond with a large electrical pulse unless hull survival requires it.

## Local life

The ecosystem may look sparse, but it is not empty. Its organisms are simply spread across enormous volumes and operate on unfamiliar timescales.

### Bell colonies

Give migrating colonies a very wide berth. A colony hundreds of meters long may be physically delicate but electrically active. Do not pass through one, illuminate it at close range, sample it with exposed metal, or allow it to drape over the vessel.

If a colony begins aligning with your electrical field:

1. Hold course briefly rather than making a sudden maneuver.
2. Reduce nonessential electrical emissions.
3. Dim exterior lights.
4. Retract manipulators.
5. Move away perpendicular to its migration line once a clear gap exists.

A broken colony could foul intakes or attract a raylike predator.

### Mineral grazers

Do not walk or land directly in front of grazing groups. Their metabolism is slow, but their ceramic mouthparts are designed to remove ore. Hull coatings, sensor windows, cable insulation, and pressure seals may be equally interesting.

The transparent microbial films they consume may be almost impossible to see. Treat all undisturbed stone as potentially alive. Sample only tiny areas, and sterilize tools without releasing chemicals into the water.

### Great rays

A stationary ray can be difficult to distinguish from the brightness until its red pressure organs become visible. Assume every major upwelling along an escarpment may contain one.

Stay many kilometers away. Do not cross between a ray and a bell migration. Do not linger in altered conductivity zones. A ray need not pursue you to be dangerous; it may change the surrounding water until your vehicle’s instruments, power systems, or buoyancy controls behave unpredictably.

### Eggs, shells, and microbes

Do not collect intact eggs. Their opening is pressure-dependent, and bringing one into a cabin or laboratory could trigger hatching.

Avoid shell drifts. They may be brittle, sharp, biologically significant, or positioned beneath currents frequented by larger animals. A “dead” shell that has remained unchanged for millennia may still contain dormant tissue.

Use stringent quarantine on return. Local organisms exploit metals, radiation, conductivity, and mineral-water boundaries. They may not require familiar organic food and could survive in batteries, reactor shielding, plumbing, electronics, or structural alloys.

## Industrial structures

Assume every installation is active until proved otherwise.

“Abandoned” has little meaning in a place without corrosion, sediment, weathering, or dust. A machine may wait centuries between externally visible operations. Empty conveyors and uncollected ingots do not establish that the operators are gone.

Initial visits should be external surveys only. Map:

- Electrical fields
- Vibrational nodes
- Moving components
- Intake and exhaust cycles
- Pressure-door timing
- Cable routes
- Active beacons
- Possible docking surfaces
- Any reaction to your lights, transmissions, or proximity

Never stand in front of a shutter, press, casting aperture, mechanical arm, or pressure door. Its cycle may be measured in hours or decades.

Do not remove tools, helmets, ingots, number plates, or objects found in shelters. A wrench suspended in an eddy may still be exactly where a maintenance system expects it to be. Moving it could trigger a retrieval process, alarm, or correction routine.

The human-proportioned spaces are especially dangerous psychologically. They will tempt you to infer hospitality, ancestry, or abandonment. None of those conclusions follows from a handrail or bunk. Enter only after:

- Several full machine cycles have been observed
- Pressure and atmosphere are measured
- Structural vibration is mapped
- An independent return line is secured
- Robotic inspection finds no active maintenance systems
- Your team has agreed in advance on a strict time limit

Do not attempt to stop a machine. The factories may generate, exploit, or regulate the equilibrium that keeps the ocean suspended. Disabling even a minor pump could have consequences far beyond the installation.

Likewise, do not synchronize your vessel with an unknown tower signal. A steady amber light may be a guide beacon, a hazard marker, a maintenance status, or bait for something adapted to follow it.

## Base-camp discipline

Your safest base is a sealed habitat brought from home and anchored to stable rock well away from:

- Current boundaries
- Escarpment upwellings
- Volcanic-glass fields
- Large concentrations of grazers
- Bell migration routes
- Industrial cables and drill holes
- Funnel stations
- Shell drifts

Mount it on vibration-isolating feet. Do not assume apparently solid ground is quiet; the rock carries machinery noise across planetary distances. Survey for resonant frequencies before anchoring.

Keep all waste, heat byproducts, gases, cleaning fluids, and biological material contained. This environment preserves disturbances. A discarded object may remain in place for ten thousand years.

Use paired excursions. Maintain a crew member at the habitat and another at the portal-side facility. Have an automatic recall protocol if communications fail.

## Psychological preparation

This world will feel less like an ocean than like being trapped inside a mechanism.

There is no visible sky, no natural silence, no soft ground, no weather that passes, and almost no familiar sign of aging. Human perception expects events to end. Here the storm returns through the same cracks, the machines repeat their cycles, and the noise never fades.

Travelers should prepare for:

- Claustrophobia despite enormous visibility
- Agoraphobia caused by immense unobstructed distances
- Compulsive staring at bell-colony pulses
- The sensation that landmarks are approaching or receding when they are not
- Loss of confidence in elapsed time
- Obsession with finding the absent operators
- The belief that repeating machine sounds contain messages
- A growing conviction that nothing done here has consequences

That final belief is particularly dangerous. The landscape may not visibly retain history, but your body, equipment, and local organisms can still be altered.

Travel in teams. Keep written logs rather than relying on memory. Rotate observation duty. Maintain artificial weather, music, changing light, and ordinary conversation inside the habitat. Anyone who begins interpreting the standing wave as personal communication should be relieved from external operations, without argument or shame.

## Emergency planning

There is no dependable “surface” rescue. An ascent could take hours, cross multiple violent current layers, or end at an unknown upper boundary. Your primary escape must be the same controlled transit system through which you arrived.

Prepare specifically for:

- Hull breach
- Power loss
- Resonant structural failure
- Electrical strike
- Thruster failure inside a fixed current
- Tether entanglement
- Biological attachment
- Navigation corruption
- Portal instability
- Crew incapacitation from vibration
- Unexpected machine activation

Every excursion should have a predetermined abort condition. Do not wait for certainty. In a world where most things move slowly, the fatal event may still be instantaneous.

## What to bring

At minimum:

- One-atmosphere submersible or pressure habitat
- Unmanned survey drones
- Hard-shell atmospheric diving suits
- Optical transponders and fiber links
- Inertial navigation systems
- Gravimetric and electric-field sensors
- Seismic and vibration monitors
- Closed-cycle life support
- Redundant heating
- Medical monitoring equipment
- Shielded storage for samples
- Full biological and mineral quarantine equipment
- Nonconductive manipulator tools
- Physical clocks and independent mechanical timers
- High-contrast route markers
- An emergency portal beacon
- Enough provisions to wait out a portal interruption without relying on local resources

Do not plan to eat local organisms, drink the water, melt the ice, breathe from a shelter, or use the factories’ power.

## The right attitude

Visit as though entering both a wilderness and a running machine whose controls you do not understand.

Move slowly, but do not confuse local slowness with safety. Observe complete cycles before acting. Keep your emissions low. Touch little. Take even less. Leave no gas, waste, heat source, beacon, or cable behind.

Most importantly, never attempt to “correct” the equilibrium. The endless noise and fixed currents may be symptoms of a catastrophe, but they may also be what prevents that catastrophe from completing. A traveler’s task is to return alive with observations—not to make the struck bell stop ringing.
