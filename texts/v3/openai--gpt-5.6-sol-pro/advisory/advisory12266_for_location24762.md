An ordinary human should treat this chamber as a confined industrial site, an unstable cave, and an unknown biosphere at the same time. Its apparent calm is not evidence of safety; nearly every hazard operates too slowly or quietly to announce itself.

## Before entering

### Establish a dependable exit

The collapsed service tunnel should not be considered an escape route. If arrival is by portal or other multiversal means, keep that passage open, guarded, and independently powered for the entire visit. Place the threshold on stable, dry ground outside the chamber’s active fractures—not beside the central extrusion, machinery, pipes, or vegetation.

No one should enter alone. Keep at least one observer outside, with a retrieval plan that does not require them to step into the chamber. Because the space absorbs sound, ordinary shouting may be ineffective.

### Survey remotely first

Send in instruments before people:

- oxygen, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, and volatile chemical sensors
- temperature and humidity probes at floor, chest, and ceiling levels
- thermal imaging
- electrical-field and ground-potential detectors
- laser mapping or lidar
- radiation and magnetic-field meters
- vibration and structural monitors

Repeat the atmospheric test over a full condensation cycle if possible. Conditions may change when the pump turns, the indicator brightens, or the rain begins. Do not assume that air which is breathable during the dry interval remains safe during the wet one.

The metallic taste described is itself a warning. It could come from heated insulation, ozone, metal aerosols, or other products not detectable by smell alone.

## Personal equipment

Wear a positive-pressure, fully enclosed suit supplied from a self-contained breathing apparatus. A filter mask is not enough: the concern is not merely dust, but oxygen deficiency, hot vapors, unknown spores or plant fragments, and gases released by ancient electrical insulation.

The suit should include:

- active cooling
- a temperature and heart-rate monitor
- a smooth, disposable outer layer
- puncture-resistant underlayers
- electrically insulating boots and gloves
- a helmet with face protection
- redundant cool LED lights
- a thermal camera or visor overlay
- an emergency air reserve
- a personal locator beacon

Avoid exposed copper, loose wires, absorbent fabrics, and natural-fiber ropes. The local ecology actively follows metal, heat, and electricity, and the central structure can apparently convert roots and conductors into one another. Equipment should be simple, sealed, and regarded as expendable.

Carry drinking water inside the suit. Do not drink from the basins or collect condensation. That water has passed over energized wiring, mineral seams, machinery, and unfamiliar living tissue.

Use lighting that produces little heat. Powerful incandescent lamps could become orientation points for heat-sensitive growth or distort the chamber’s thermal balance. Light will make navigation easier, but do not trust vision alone: glossy leaves, wet stone, and machinery may resemble one another.

## Timing the visit

The safest time is probably late in the dry interval, after temperatures and electrical conditions have been measured and while the red indicator is dim. The indicator’s slow brightening may precede a valve movement or condensation event, though this should be confirmed rather than assumed.

Leave before the rain begins.

The rain is not dangerous merely because it is wet. It may:

- connect previously isolated electrical potentials
- make sharp shelves slippery
- conceal cracks
- loosen brittle reproductive plant sections
- increase chemical exposure
- trigger changes in machinery, roots, or the central extrusion

If droplets begin forming rapidly on cables and leaf edges, treat that as an evacuation signal, not an invitation to remain and watch.

## Moving through the chamber

Plan a short route along the coolest, driest margin. Mark it with freestanding, nonconductive light beacons rather than paint, stakes, or anything attached to the walls. Maintain continuous visual contact between travelers. A tether may help near shelves, but it should be nonconductive, smooth, and managed carefully so that it cannot catch on fins, pipes, roots, or the broken ladder.

Walk slowly. The floor consists of abrupt shelves, loose basalt flakes, concrete fractures, and shallow trenches that may be difficult to distinguish in partial light. Test footing with a nonconductive pole, but do not insert the pole into cracks or vegetation.

Do not:

- climb the bent ladder
- enter the collapsed tunnel
- walk beneath the overhead rail
- cross the shallow trench unless necessary
- stand near newly split walls or pressure vessels
- touch cables, roots, mineral seams, or wet metal
- lean against apparently solid machinery
- move dead leaf sheets or black felt
- operate controls, valves, gauges, or the pump

The carbonized leaf drifts may be conductive. They may also conceal holes, live wiring, or sharp debris.

Keep visits brief. Heat stress can impair judgment before a traveler feels seriously ill, especially in protective equipment. Use fixed exposure limits based on measured wet-bulb conditions, not personal confidence. At the first sign of dizziness, unusual fatigue, confusion, muscle cramping, headache, or breathlessness, leave immediately.

## The central extrusion

Maintain a generous exclusion radius around the glassy fins—at least several meters, and more if instruments detect electrical, magnetic, or thermal gradients.

Do not sample it. Do not place probes through it. Do not allow cables, suit tethers, tools, or robotic power leads to touch it. Its transformations appear gradual, but “gradual” does not mean harmless. It may alter materials without obvious energy release, and ordinary protective gear cannot be trusted against a process that erases the distinction between metal, stone, and living tissue.

If any equipment touching the floor begins to discolor, develop fibers, become warm, stiffen, or show unexplained conductivity, abandon it in place. Do not carry it back through the portal.

## Biological and ecological precautions

The absence of animals does not make the chamber sterile. The plants reproduce by fragments and contact; a small piece attached to a boot or tool may be enough to establish growth elsewhere. Their lack of visible spores is not proof that they produce none.

Avoid brushing against the groves. In particular, stay clear of stems with luminous seams, as these appear ready to fracture. Root curtains should never be pushed aside by hand.

Sampling should be left to remote, sealed systems, and only if there is a facility prepared for unknown organisms that may consume mineral ions, electrical charge, concrete, wiring, or heat gradients. Samples must never be opened in an inhabited universe.

Before departure:

1. Inspect every suit and tool under bright and ultraviolet light.
2. Remove the disposable outer layer inside a sealed decontamination enclosure.
3. Quarantine equipment separately from buildings and power systems.
4. Monitor it for heat, conductivity changes, rooting, mineral growth, and slow structural conversion.
5. Do not incinerate suspect material casually; heat may sustain rather than destroy it.
6. Do not wash contamination into drains.

A person whose suit has been punctured by a root, fin, or wire should be isolated and medically observed even if there is no immediate wound. Unknown mineral-organic fragments may remain embedded or continue changing after removal.

## Emergency rules

Leave immediately if any of the following occurs:

- the red indicator brightens faster than expected
- condensation begins outside the predicted cycle
- the pump’s rhythm changes
- a pressure vessel clicks, shifts, or warms
- floor cracks widen or shed fresh flakes
- lights flicker without an equipment fault
- instruments show a rising ground voltage
- roots or leaves change position over minutes rather than weeks
- the portal or communications become unstable
- anyone experiences breathing difficulty or cognitive impairment

During an electrical event, do not run blindly across wet ground. Take short steps to reduce voltage difference between the feet, avoid all metal, and retreat along the surveyed route. During rock movement, stay away from walls, pillars, the rail, and pressure vessels; if the exit remains accessible, evacuate rather than sheltering deeper inside.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect the chamber to be more psychologically disorienting than its size suggests. Heat, breathing apparatus, fragmented illumination, and near-total silence will make time feel slow. Objects will repeatedly resolve into something other than what they first appeared to be. The ecology changes too gradually to watch, yet may look different whenever attention returns to it.

Resist the urge to approach unfamiliar forms simply to understand them. This place rewards still observation and punishes contact. The safest visit is brief: enter along a measured route, observe from the cool margin, document the chamber without altering it, and leave while it remains dry and quiet.

Above all, do not mistake equilibrium for welcome. The chamber survives by incorporating stone, machinery, water, and vegetation into one system. A traveler should make every effort not to become another material available to it.
