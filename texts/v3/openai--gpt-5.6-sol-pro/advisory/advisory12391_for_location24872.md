## Preparing to Visit the Country Beneath the World

Treat this place not as a jungle or cave, but as a stable biological environment with unfamiliar rules. It is hospitable in appearance—warm, bright, full of water and vegetation—but almost none of those resources should be assumed usable. The country preserves itself. It may not preserve you.

The safest expedition is small, self-contained, time-limited, and tethered to a reliable exit.

### Before departure

- Travel with at least two companions. No one should enter alone.
- Establish a fixed return deadline based on carried water, cooling capacity, and air quality—not fatigue or perceived time.
- Make sure the portal, descent point, or other means of return remains continuously available. If possible, leave an attendant outside.
- Synchronize several independent clocks. Use mechanical, electronic, and externally monitored timekeeping in case the local condition affects one type differently.
- Record baseline health data, especially allergies, lung function, skin sensitivity, and reactions to heat.
- Practice operating in hot, humid, windless conditions under constant light.
- Agree in advance that no pool, structure, animal, fruit, or apparently edible plant justifies breaking formation.

## Essential equipment

### Clothing and biological protection

Wear a lightweight, sealed field suit rather than ordinary jungle clothing. It should include:

- Breathable or actively cooled underlayers
- A hood and eye protection
- Gloves resistant to sap, pollen, and microscopic plant hairs
- Broad-soled boots with good grip and minimal tread depth
- A respirator capable of filtering pollen, spores, aerosols, and fine organic particles
- A personal cooling vest or circulating-water garment

The air is nearly still, so sweat may cool you poorly despite the apparent moisture. Heat exhaustion is likely to be the first ordinary danger.

Do not wear crampons or deeply cleated boots. The root mat may conceal voids, and piercing living surfaces unnecessarily could provoke unknown responses even if the damage later closes.

### Supplies

Bring everything needed from outside:

- Sealed drinking water, with at least twice the expected requirement
- Electrolytes
- Packaged food requiring no fire
- Flameless food heaters, if hot meals are necessary
- Medical oxygen or compact rebreather support
- Antihistamines, epinephrine, bronchodilators, burn dressings, and antifungal treatment
- Blackout sleep masks or a fully opaque sleeping enclosure
- Waste-containment bags and sealed sample containers
- Freestanding camp furniture and sleeping platforms

Do not rely on local firewood. Fire does not catch, and repeated attempts may only fill a still-air chamber with fuel vapor, battery fumes, or overheated equipment.

### Navigation and communications

The landscape is geographically stable, but visually dense. Carry:

- Inertial navigation equipment
- Short-range mapping lidar or radar
- Compasses, though their usefulness is uncertain
- Redundant radios
- A reelable guideline leading back to the entrance
- Numbered, freestanding route beacons
- Cameras for matching landmarks from the return direction
- A physical map board rather than only touchscreens, which may fail in condensation

Do not mark the route by cutting leaves, tying vines, scraping stone, or making footprints. Such alterations will disappear as the country restores its established form. A torn leaf is not a durable trail marker.

Set beacons on broad platforms without fastening them to vegetation. Check frequently that foreign objects remain where placed; the environment may not alter them, but its restorative pressure could gradually lift, enclose, or displace them.

## Moving through the vegetation

Test every surface before putting full weight on it. What looks like a meadow may be the crown of a horizontal trunk above a deep green interval. Use a broad-ended staff rather than a spike.

Travel slowly enough to notice:

- Root bridges narrowing beneath foliage
- Condensation making smooth stems extremely slippery
- Thin leaves concealing drops between vegetative tiers
- Warm, resilient mats that may rebound around a trapped foot
- Low tendrils held at face or throat height
- Accumulated carbon dioxide in enclosed hollows

The place does not erode, but visitors can still fall. Its tendency to restore itself may make rescue harder: a footprint can close, a bent passage can return to its former position, and a cut opening may seal behind the person passing through it.

Never crawl alone into a partition of leaves. Keep visual, radio, and preferably physical contact with the rest of the party.

## Water precautions

Assume all local water is unsafe.

Condensation may look exceptionally pure, but it has remained in continuous contact with alien living tissue. It could contain dissolved defensive chemicals, reproductive material, or microorganisms invisible to ordinary testing. The absence of decay does not mean the absence of infection.

The pools are a greater danger. Their visible shallowness is deceptive, and conventional sounding does not establish their depth. Therefore:

- Do not wade, swim, or reach into them.
- Do not lean over them without a harness.
- Do not lower hands or limbs toward submerged objects.
- Do not follow a dropped item into the water.
- Do not assume a safety line can retrieve someone once they cross the surface.

If scientific work requires sampling, use remotely controlled equipment attached to multiple independent lines. Be prepared to abandon the equipment. The bowl-, mask-, and shell-like forms beneath the water should be treated as inaccessible rather than merely submerged.

The five-step stair is not an invitation. Its final step may lead into a geometry in which visual distance and physical depth no longer correspond.

## Food and plant contact

Do not eat fruit, taste sap, chew stems, or collect rainwater from leaf surfaces. A fruit that never ripens may never become digestible. A seedpod held permanently at fullness may contain active toxins or biological processes that behave unpredictably once removed.

Avoid unnecessary cutting. Severed material apparently remains fresh, which creates several problems:

- Samples may stay biologically active indefinitely.
- Sap may continue producing irritants.
- Cut tissues may contaminate equipment without drying.
- Removed organisms may begin changing only after leaving the country.

If sampling is authorized, collect tiny amounts using remote tools and place each sample in nested, transparent, airtight containers. Label the exact location and time. Never carry an unsealed leaf in a pocket.

Do not wrap shelter lines around living stems. Tendrils that seem motionless may still maintain tension, and the environment may return a bent support to its established position with considerable force.

## Camping and sleep

Avoid camping beneath giant leaves, beside pools, or inside the living shelters.

Large leaves collect condensation and may release drops along fixed paths. A campsite that seems dry can be directly beneath a permanent drip point. The living shelters are also poor choices: their frames and walls are rooted organisms, not inert architecture, and may exchange gases or pollen throughout the rest period.

Choose a broad, level root terrace with several visible exits. Use a freestanding, ventilated, opaque tent. Do not seal it so tightly that the occupants’ carbon dioxide accumulates.

The constant radiance will disrupt ordinary circadian rhythms even if travelers do not feel tired. Maintain an external schedule:

- Eat at fixed clock times.
- Impose an artificial night.
- Sleep in complete darkness.
- Assign watches.
- Log cognitive state as well as physical symptoms.

Perpetual light can produce insomnia, irritability, poor judgment, visual fatigue, and the false conviction that only a few hours have passed.

## Air and silence

Monitor oxygen, carbon dioxide, humidity, temperature, and airborne organic material continuously. Millions of leaves may alter their gas exchange together. The faint collective whisper of pores opening could signal a large atmospheric change.

If several team members simultaneously experience headache, confusion, unusual calm, breathlessness, or drowsiness, treat it as an air emergency. Move toward a more open, elevated area and use supplemental breathing equipment.

The silence is psychologically deceptive. Because vegetation absorbs sound, a companion can be nearby but inaudible. Establish regular radio checks and hand signals. Do not interpret the absence of noise as the absence of movement.

## Fauna

Observe animals from a distance.

The motionless insects and glassy snails may be native organisms, plant structures imitating animals, or creatures held in the same arrested condition as the vegetation. Do not touch them to determine which. A seemingly immobile insect may still breathe, sting, release spores, or respond only after prolonged disturbance.

If a quadrupedal shape is seen behind foliage:

- Do not pursue it.
- Do not surround it.
- Do not leave food.
- Record direction, apparent size, and duration.
- Regroup and confirm that every traveler is accounted for.

The last point matters. In a country where forms remain poised at the edge of action, resemblance may be more significant than ordinary taxonomy.

## Structures and ruins

Approach the slabs, walls, stairs, and shelters as both cultural sites and living organisms.

Do not assume they were built by people. Rectangular blocks may be mineral growth directed by roots; woven mats may be plant tissues passing through an architectural phase. Conversely, structures that look organic may preserve the intentions of vanished inhabitants.

Avoid:

- Sitting on slabs
- Rearranging blocks
- Entering narrow roofless rooms without an observer
- Removing vine sheaths
- Stepping into arrangements that resemble thresholds
- Completing unfinished circles, patterns, or alignments

A stable world may preserve not only objects but configurations. Completing a pattern could constitute an action even if no mechanism is visible.

Photograph structures from multiple angles. If measurements are needed, use noncontact instruments.

## Time and bodily risk

Carry clocks, but do not assume that measured duration and biological duration will agree. The local plants do not visibly grow, ripen, decay, or finish their movements. Human metabolism may remain ordinary—or it may not.

Monitor:

- Pulse and respiration
- Urine output
- Hydration
- Wound clotting
- Hair and nail changes
- Food digestion
- Medication timing
- Subjective duration

A cut that fails to heal is not necessarily benign. It may remain indefinitely fresh. Likewise, food might not spoil locally but could still become dangerous after return.

Do not assume the country’s restorative property applies to visitors. If someone tears a tendon, becomes poisoned, or falls into a hidden depth, the landscape may calmly repair the leaf beneath them while leaving the person injured.

## Conduct and contamination

The country appears unable to retain ordinary disturbance permanently, but visitors should still practice strict leave-no-trace procedures. Pack out all waste. Do not introduce seeds, microbes, insects, soil, or untreated organic material.

On departure:

1. Inventory every item.
2. Inspect suits for attached snails, pollen, seeds, tendrils, sap, or fused fibers.
3. Pass through external decontamination.
4. Quarantine samples separately from travelers.
5. Observe the team medically for delayed allergies, altered healing, circadian disturbance, or tissues that fail to age normally.
6. Keep removed plant material under continuous surveillance. Do not assume it will remain arrested once separated from the local environment.

## Most important rules

1. **Never enter a pool.**
2. **Bring all food, water, air support, and shelter.**
3. **Remain tethered to the route home.**
4. **Do not trust cuts, footprints, or bent plants as markers.**
5. **Use clocks and enforced darkness; the light provides no sense of time.**
6. **Do not consume or casually collect local biology.**
7. **Treat heat and still air as immediate hazards.**
8. **Do not assume the landscape’s permanence protects human bodies.**
9. **Do not pursue animals or complete unfamiliar structures.**
10. **Leave while the expedition still feels easy.**

The chief danger of this country is not obvious hostility. It is the suggestion that nothing urgent can happen there. Everything appears fresh, balanced, and permanently on the verge of continuation. A traveler may begin to feel that supplies need not run out, wounds need not worsen, and the exit will remain available forever.

Prepare against that feeling most of all.
