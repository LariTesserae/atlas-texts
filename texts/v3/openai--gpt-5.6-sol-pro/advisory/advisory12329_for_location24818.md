## Overall assessment

For an ordinary human, this is not a swimming destination. It is a combined volcanic vent field, enclosed waterfall, unstable gravity well, acoustic hazard, and local causality fault. Conventional mountaineering equipment plus ordinary scuba gear would be inadequate.

The safest visit is remote: observe from a neighboring ridge with instruments and send expendable probes through the rain-wall. If physical entry is unavoidable, treat it as a short technical penetration dive with a guaranteed nonlocal extraction method. Do not depend on swimming back through the boundary.

## Before approaching

### Establish a base well below the summit

The mountain is young, so assume:

- earthquakes and sudden fissures;
- toxic volcanic gases;
- razor-edged glass underfoot;
- rockfall without weathering-softened warning;
- unstable slopes and newly formed ledges;
- steam explosions where hot stone meets water.

Place the main camp beyond the likely rockfall and lahar paths, preferably on an older neighboring formation. Do not camp directly beneath the suspended sea. Falling water, displaced rock, or the sea itself may not always respect its present boundary.

Maintain at least two support teams:

1. **Summit team**, outside the rain-wall.
2. **Remote team**, beyond the mountain’s immediate anomalies.

The remote team should hold the expedition’s authoritative clock, records, and extraction controls.

### Observe before entering

Watch the sea for several full daylight cycles, if “cycles” remain meaningful there. Record:

- frequency and duration of convulsions;
- pressure-wave signatures;
- movement of arches and slabs;
- periods when bubbles sink or move sideways;
- changes in the shape of the sun;
- boundary exits of test probes;
- the hour at which the ocean tends to lift from the floor.

Send instrument packages through the wall from several directions. Each should include redundant clocks, inertial sensors, pressure gauges, sonar, radiation and gas detectors, and a bright visual marker. Do not send anything you cannot afford to have returned before it was launched.

If probes emerge in the air, in the past, or from the wrong side more than occasionally, cancel human entry.

## Essential equipment

### Protective system

The preferred equipment is a compact atmospheric diving suit or similarly rigid personal submersible, not open scuba. It should have:

- a sealed, sterilized exterior;
- no exposed flexible gas spaces;
- independent life support;
- pressure-wave damping;
- active heating and cooling;
- reinforced gloves and boots for volcanic glass;
- full head, neck, and spine protection;
- short-range multidirectional thrusters;
- automatic attitude stabilization;
- an inertial display that can show several competing definitions of “up” without choosing one silently.

Ordinary drysuits and buoyancy compensators may become uncontrollable when local buoyancy rotates. Standard regulators may also behave badly during abrupt pressure distortions.

Carry redundant breathing systems even in a hard suit. Do not rely on bubbles to indicate direction or depth.

### Acoustic protection

The noise is dangerous in more than the ordinary sense. It can damage hearing, disorient the vestibular system, interfere with breathing rhythm, and transmit concussions through bone.

Use:

- active acoustic cancellation inside the helmet;
- mechanical isolation around the jaw and skull;
- rib and chest damping;
- visual and haptic communications rather than voice alone;
- automatic monitoring for arrhythmia, panic, and loss of motor control.

If the deep concussion begins to synchronize with your pulse or breathing, abort immediately.

### Navigation and recovery

Carry all of the following:

- a physical tether to an external powered winch;
- a second, independent line to your partner;
- inertial navigation;
- short-range sonar;
- optical beacons;
- a multiversal or nonlocal recall device, if available;
- a personal emergency lift system for an exit hundreds of feet above the mountain.

A tether alone is not sufficient. It may lead to the wrong wall, an earlier version of the entrance, or a point that is spatially continuous but causally inaccessible. It should be smooth, cut-resistant, electrically inert, and designed to release automatically before it drags the traveler into the rotating central field.

Wear a compact parachute or controlled-descent unit outside the hard suit if the boundary may deposit you above the peak. A conventional wet parachute is poor insurance; use one intended for immediate deployment in rain and turbulent mountain air.

### Temporal discipline

Each traveler should carry:

- one mechanical clock;
- one isolated atomic or quantum-standard clock;
- one mission timer that counts physiological exposure rather than local time;
- continuous video from multiple angles;
- a written identity and chronology card.

The remote team’s clock is authoritative. Do not “correct” mission records to match clocks brought back from the sea.

Before entry, establish a duplicate-encounter protocol. If you emerge before your own departure or meet another version of the team:

- do not touch;
- do not exchange equipment;
- do not attempt to prevent the original entry;
- move to the predetermined isolation point;
- let the remote team determine which sequence to preserve.

## Biological and geological precautions

This is a very young ecosystem. Sterilize everything entering the water. Use a sealed suit and retrieve all equipment. Human skin microbes could alter the pale filaments or translucent fans before anyone understands them.

Do not:

- touch mineral crusts;
- break the warm-water shells;
- collect living tissue on the first expedition;
- anchor lines to arches, steps, or upright slabs;
- place markers in cracks that appear newly opened;
- feed, pursue, or obstruct the ribbon-bodied swimmers.

The mineral layers are both fragile and potentially toxic. White, saffron, and blue-black deposits may contain corrosive or heavy-metal compounds. Fresh basalt can remain hot enough to damage seals even when the surrounding water feels merely warm.

The silhouettes visible during lava extrusion should not be approached. Whether they are organisms, optical afterimages, or forms arriving ahead of their causes does not alter the practical response: back away before the fissure opens fully.

## Entry procedure

Enter only in full daylight, well before the ocean’s nighttime lifting.

Approach the rain-wall from a stable ledge, clipped to a powered recovery line. Cross decisively. Lingering in the boundary exposes you to the maximum battering and may let different parts of your body occupy different exit conditions.

Expect:

- a moment of violent impact;
- sudden warmth;
- total visual confusion;
- unreliable sunlight;
- intense noise;
- immediate current shear.

On entering, stop trying to determine up from vision. Let the suit stabilize from inertial and tether data. Confirm your partner, both support teams, life support, and the apparent location of the entry boundary. If any two systems disagree substantially, leave at once.

Remain near the outer third of the basin during the first visit. Do not cross toward the central ring merely because it appears close.

## Rules inside

### Never trust apparent distance

The opposite wall may recede as you approach it. Plan movement by tether payout and inertial displacement, not by sight. Set a strict maximum line length. If a target does not become physically closer within that limit, abandon it.

### Treat “up” as a local weather condition

Near the center, buoyancy rotates. Bubbles, debris, sunlight, and your own body may each indicate a different direction. Do not chase the sun. Do not follow the blind swimmers; their persistent uphill movement may lead into a direction humans cannot return from.

If you see the summit inverted overhead, freeze your forward motion and retreat along the exact tether path. Do not enter the lower sea, even to recover a person or instrument. Use nonlocal extraction instead.

### Stay far from the stone ring

Give the upright slabs the widest possible margin. Currents avoiding the ring are a warning, not a sheltered route. Matter crossing its threshold appears to reverse or rehearse its own motion. A person may not emerge as a single continuous traveler.

No line, limb, tool, light beam intended for ranging, or drifting sample container should cross the ring.

### Do not use the architecture

The arches and steps are not shelter. They may be geological anticipations of structures rather than stable objects.

- Do not pass through an arch, even one opening onto bare rock.
- Do not enter the patch where bubbles sink.
- Do not stand in the footprint-like depressions.
- Do not descend the six steps.
- Do not shelter beneath overhangs associated with them.

If an arch changes position, orientation, or apparent age, leave the area rather than attempting to document it at close range.

### Interpret warnings broadly

Because causes and effects may arrive out of order:

- A boom means an overhang is unsafe now, whether or not it still stands.
- Debris assembling into a ridge means the future ridge location is hazardous.
- A crack visible before the stone breaks should be treated as already open.
- Sudden silence is not relief. It is an immediate emergency signal.
- A wave of amber light through the filaments announces a current or pressure change; brace and move away from rock blades.
- Bubbles tearing upward after a distortion indicate a convulsion has passed locally, not that the basin is stable.

Do not wait for ordinary confirmation.

## Visit duration

Keep the first human penetration under ten externally measured minutes. Later visits should still end several hours before local sunset.

Never remain through the deepest night. When the ocean lifts, anyone near the floor may be exposed to volcanic gases and then struck by the full returning mass. Anyone suspended in the water may be carried upward with it, pinned against its underside, or dropped onto bare basalt. The interval of silence is not an opportunity to explore the exposed summit.

If you hear stone being placed upon stone, extraction should already be in progress.

## Exit procedure

Use the same boundary segment through which you entered, during a quiet interval, while maintaining tension on the external line. Send a small beacon through first. Confirm where and when it emerges.

Even then, prepare for three outcomes:

1. **Normal exit onto the summit.**
2. **Exit from another side of the sea.**
3. **Exit into open air above the mountain or at a displaced time.**

Cross quickly and feet-first if possible. The summit team should remain clipped to rock anchors and should not stand directly in the projected exit path.

If the wall begins to convulse, do not attempt a boundary crossing. Use the nonlocal recall system. If no such system exists, the expedition should not have entered.

## After return

Place travelers and gear in full biological, chemical, and temporal quarantine.

Check for:

- burns and pressure injuries;
- inner-ear and bone-conduction damage;
- decompression illness despite expected pressure readings;
- embedded volcanic glass;
- heavy-metal contamination;
- unexplained water in sealed compartments;
- missing or duplicated equipment;
- clock disagreement;
- memories of events absent from recordings;
- injuries that appear before the recorded cause.

Interview team members separately before allowing them to compare recollections. Preserve contradictions rather than forcing a single narrative.

Count people, suits, samples, and clocks against the remote team’s original inventory. If anything has returned twice, or returned before it was sent, isolate both instances. Do not discard the “extra”; disposal may become the cause of its appearance.

## Final recommendation

An ordinary traveler should experience this place from outside the wall: secured to the mountain, wearing hearing protection, watching the suspended ocean bruise the daylight around the summit.

Entering is justifiable only with rigid environmental protection, remote extraction, temporal safeguards, and a willingness to abandon every scientific objective at the first inconsistency. The greatest danger is not drowning or lava. It is the temptation to treat the sea’s repeated forms—an arch, a path, a familiar sound, an apparent exit—as promises of ordinary cause and effect.

They are not promises. They are shapes the mountain has learned before it has learned why they exist.
