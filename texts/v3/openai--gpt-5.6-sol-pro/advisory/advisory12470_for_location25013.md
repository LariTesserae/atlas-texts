## General assessment

This high country is survivable for an ordinary human, but it is not forgiving of ordinary mountaineering habits. Its chief dangers are not cold, thin air, or violent weather. They are:

- continuous heat and humidity;
- contaminated water and biologically active surfaces;
- pressure, gravity, distance, and time changing across invisible boundaries;
- slow navigation errors in terrain that looks stable;
- exposure to persistent low-frequency sound;
- accidental interaction with ancient infrastructure;
- deceptive animals and plants;
- becoming stranded by a route that is longer, steeper, or temporally different on the return journey.

Treat the basin as a functioning environmental machine, not as an abandoned wetland containing artifacts. The ecology and the machinery appear to be one system. Do not “repair,” sample, move, or shelter against anything until you know what role it plays.

An unsupported solo visit is unwise. Travel with at least three people, preferably including a field medic and someone experienced with non-Euclidean or variable-rate environments.

## Before entering

### Confirm biological compatibility

Arrange a full exobiological screen before departure. The country is warm, wet, and anciently stable—ideal conditions for microorganisms, parasites, spores, and biochemical irritants. Even if the large animals ignore humans, microscopic life may find human lungs, skin, or intestines extremely hospitable.

At minimum, determine:

- whether local proteins, spores, and aerosols provoke human immune reactions;
- whether local water contains familiar pathogens, alien analogues, or mineral toxins;
- whether the blue-green cup colonies produce cold by chemistry, field effects, or both;
- whether local silica mats shed respirable fibers;
- whether mineral deposition can occur in wounds, filters, or lung tissue;
- whether the pressure-retention field affects implants, pacemakers, insulin pumps, or pregnancy.

Receive broad local prophylaxis if your transit authority can provide it. Otherwise plan to use sealed food and treated water for the entire visit.

### Establish the entry and extraction method

Do not assume aircraft can cross the rim safely. Clouds, pressure, wind, and apparent distance are all being redirected. A conventional pilot may see a clear approach while the vehicle’s instruments report a different horizon. Rotors, wings, balloons, and parachutes could all behave unpredictably where air is being bent inward.

The safest entry is likely through a previously surveyed ground route or a portal anchored directly to a known stable island. If using a portal, keep the threshold open long enough to measure:

- pressure on both sides;
- time-rate difference;
- air composition;
- gravitational direction and magnitude;
- electromagnetic and acoustic background;
- whether the portal’s apparent depth matches its measured depth.

Place the return anchor well away from pylons, silver loops, conductive seams, mineral springs, temporal boundaries, and animal paths. Mark it physically and electronically. Keep an independent extraction anchor outside the basin.

Tell the outside team not merely when to expect you, but according to which clocks. Give them trigger conditions such as: “Extract when the external reference clock reaches this time, even if our beacon claims only six hours have passed.”

## Clothing

Dress for a hot greenhouse, not a summit.

Use:

- loose, light, quick-drying layers;
- a hooded, breathable rain shell with excellent venting;
- gaiters and sealed wetland boots;
- a broad hat or hood that prevents water from running down the neck;
- thin gloves for plants and rock;
- a dry sleep layer kept in a genuinely waterproof bag;
- an anti-chafing barrier and foot powder;
- spare socks in multiple sealed packets.

Ordinary waterproof clothing may trap more heat than the rain removes. The goal is not to stay completely dry—that may be impossible—but to control skin temperature and prevent constant maceration. Schedule foot inspections at least twice daily. Clean and dry between the toes, under straps, and around every small cut.

Wear eye protection in reed country. Hollow stems, silica-bearing mats, insects, and mineral droplets all present risks. A half-mask respirator with replaceable biological and fine-particulate filters is advisable in enclosed forest, around digestive plants, during spore releases, and whenever the air develops an unfamiliar odor or visible haze.

Bring a light flotation vest. Much of the water is shallow, but variable gravity and unexpectedly deep interiors make visual depth unreliable.

## Essential instruments

Do not rely on one integrated device. A machine influenced by local fields may produce beautifully consistent nonsense.

Each traveler should carry:

- a mechanical compass;
- an inertial compass;
- a local-field meter;
- a barometer;
- an oxygen, carbon dioxide, and toxic-gas monitor;
- a wet-bulb thermometer;
- an acoustic dosimeter including infrasonic exposure;
- a mechanical watch;
- a quartz watch;
- an external-reference synchronized clock or beacon;
- a pedometer or inertial distance recorder;
- a laser rangefinder;
- physical survey tape;
- chemical trail markers;
- a paper waterproof notebook and pencil;
- a personal locator beacon using at least two communication modes.

Compare instruments frequently. Disagreement is itself useful information. A sudden barometric change without weather, a divergence between clocks, or a mismatch between laser range and walking distance may reveal a boundary before someone steps across it.

Carry simple disposable indicators on poles: ribbons, weighted strings, bubbles, dripping reservoirs, and mechanical metronomes. Crude tools may show field changes more honestly than sophisticated electronics.

## Navigation

### Map routes as experienced, not merely as seen

A ravine fifty meters across may require half a day to cross. Therefore record four separate facts:

1. apparent distance;
2. measured distance;
3. travel time on each clock;
4. physiological effort.

Do the same in both directions whenever possible. A route that is short outbound may not be short inbound.

Use paired markers placed on both sides of suspected seams. Number them visibly. Add ribbons at several heights to show changes in air flow and gravitational slant. Do not use biodegradable cord; decay rates vary, and a line may fail rapidly near the rim.

Avoid navigating solely by ridgelines, water flow, or “downhill.” None is dependable. Water traveling uphill may be following a pressure route that is safer for larvae than for humans. Migrating animals may climb toward what their bodies experience as lower ground; following them can lead a human onto cliffs or into pressure hollows.

### Test boundaries before crossing

Warning signs include:

- ears popping without obvious elevation change;
- breathlessness in a descent;
- watches drifting apart;
- condensation forming in a straight line;
- abrupt endings of vines or moss;
- animal trails that turn sharply for no visible reason;
- fruit browning on only one side of a clearing;
- clouds standing over otherwise empty ground;
- sound arriving too early, too late, or from the wrong direction;
- water or loose objects sliding at an angle.

When you suspect a boundary, stop the group. Probe it with a long pole carrying:

- a ribbon;
- a thermometer;
- a pressure capsule;
- two synchronized clocks;
- a small piece of fruit or reactive organic material.

Do not test with a hand, pet, or tethered person.

Cross one at a time while attached to a belay anchored well behind the seam. Keep in mind that the two sides may not agree on force or time. A partner pulling “gently” from a faster zone could create a violent jerk in a slower one. Use a load-limiting reel, not a rigid rope system.

Never camp directly on a boundary, even if local animals nest nearby. Their biology and behavior have adapted to it; yours have not.

## Food and water

Bring all primary food. Do not eat local plants, animals, cups of water, epiphytes, mineral films, or “clean-looking” spring water. Clear water emerging from rock may contain dissolved metals, silica, unfamiliar organisms, or active compounds from the mountain’s machinery.

Use sealed water supplies at first. If resupply from the environment becomes necessary, process water through:

1. coarse filtration;
2. fine biological filtration;
3. chemical purification compatible with local testing;
4. heat treatment;
5. mineral and heavy-metal adsorption.

Boiling alone is insufficient. It kills many organisms but does not remove toxins, metals, or field-active particulates.

Keep food in small, separately sealed portions. Near the rim, an entire food bag might spoil in hours. In slow-decay wetlands, spoiled material may look fresh. Date food according to both external and carried clocks, and discard it based on the most conservative estimate.

Do not store supplies in the open-sided dry shelters merely because they remain dry. The exclusion effect that keeps out rain and insects may also affect heat, gases, time, or living tissue.

## Health hazards

### Heat and humidity

Expect chronic heat strain. Sweat may not evaporate efficiently, and breathing warm saturated air can make exertion unexpectedly difficult.

Move slowly. Build schedules around frequent rest rather than distance. Carry electrolytes and use them regularly, but monitor for overhydration. Symptoms requiring an immediate stop include:

- headache;
- clumsiness;
- irritability;
- nausea;
- unusual silence;
- confusion about route or time;
- cessation of sweating;
- pulse that remains elevated after rest.

Cooling towels may be ineffective when the air is saturated. Bring chemical cold packs or a compact active cooling vest. The blue-green organisms that emit chill are not substitutes; do not put them on the body.

### Pressure changes

A hollow can produce altitude-like illness even when physically lower. At the first sign of ear pain, breathlessness, cyanosis, dizziness, or abnormal fatigue, retreat along the exact approach line. Do not continue in hopes that the pressure will normalize.

Carry supplemental oxygen, but remember that oxygen does not solve all pressure problems. A pressure bag or compact positive-pressure shelter is more useful for severe exposure.

### Sound

The basin’s continuous pressure-tone may cause headache, nausea, disturbed sleep, anxiety, dental discomfort, or impaired balance. Some effects may be neurological rather than simply auditory.

Carry fitted ear protection, but do not wear full isolation while walking; environmental sounds carry navigation and animal information. At camp, use active low-frequency cancellation if available. Record baseline coordination and mood each morning. A traveler who becomes fixated on the tone, starts hearing patterns no one else hears, or feels compelled to approach a pylon should be escorted away rather than allowed to investigate.

### Cuts and mineralization

Treat every cut immediately. Wash with carried sterile fluid, not local water. Remove grit, apply antiseptic, and seal the wound. Pearly deposits that cover branches and bones could also plate tissue or dressings.

Do not leave wet bandages in place for long periods. Examine wounds for translucent films, unusual stiffness, reduced sensation, or mineral grains forming beneath the skin.

## Wildlife conduct

Most large animals appear adapted to concealment, filtering, grazing, or gliding rather than pursuit. That does not make them harmless.

Give the planted-back grazers a wide berth. A submerged individual may be mistaken for firm ground. Before stepping onto any mossy “island,” probe around its perimeter and watch for breathing, eye movement, or coordinated epiphyte motion. Never camp on a newly discovered island without confirming it is geological.

Avoid reed colonies at dawn. Their calls travel through the vegetation and root systems, so cutting or striking a reed may announce your presence far beyond sight. Six-limbed climbers could interpret vibrations as territorial calls, mating signals, or threats.

Stay out of warm boulder pools containing egg strings. Broad-winged adults may rarely descend, but breeding sites are likely to provoke their strongest responses.

Do not approach geometric schools of silver swimmers, especially when pylons hum. This may be communication, calibration, maintenance, or energy transfer. Wading through the formation could interfere with the pressure system or expose you to focused vibration.

When large dark lake animals surface, remain still and give them an open route to deeper water. Do not place boats or swimmers between an animal and an anchored filter-feeding colony; the relationship between them is unknown.

## Plants and wetlands

Assume that every cup-shaped plant can trap, digest, chill, intoxicate, or harbor animals. Never drink from one. Avoid leaning over large cups, as their edges may be slippery or responsive to pressure.

Use poles in marshes, but probe gently. Root mats and living silica armor stabilize the channels. Breaking them may open a cavity or damage a structure that takes centuries to recover.

Do not cut paths through reed forests unless survival requires it. Their hollow stems are part of the basin’s acoustic network. A cleared route may also be very easy for local animals—or the system itself—to detect.

Bring a small raised sleeping platform or tension cot. Ground that appears firm may contain shallow water under its skin, digestive roots, pressure vents, or slow-moving mineral deposition.

## Campsites

Choose old, obviously stable stone rises with:

- no straight vegetation boundaries;
- no nearby pylons, silver loops, conductive seams, or humming markers;
- no warm pool above or below camp;
- no geometric animal movement;
- no signs of rapid decay;
- several clear retreat routes;
- measurable agreement among clocks and pressure instruments.

Do not camp directly under the massive crowned trees. They may shed little bark, but their mineralized trunks and extensive roots could conduct lightning, sound, or machine signals.

Avoid caves unless fully surveyed. Underground water groans indicate extensive channels, and interior dimensions may not match exterior geology.

Establish camp before fatigue becomes severe. The wetland’s apparent calm encourages people to continue too long. In this terrain, a small late-day navigation error can become a night spent on the wrong side of a temporal seam.

At camp, hang simple reference instruments where everyone can see them: two clocks, barometer, plumb line, condensation plate, and sound meter. Assign a rotating watch even if predators are absent. The watch is primarily for environmental drift.

## Ancient structures

Observe from a distance before approaching. Wait through at least one full local weather cycle if possible.

Never assume a structure is inactive because it is moss-covered or has no visible controls. Biological growth may be tolerated, cultivated, or incorporated into its operation.

Particular cautions:

- **Black pylons:** stay away when humming changes, fish align, animals withdraw, or condensation patterns alter.
- **Silver cliff loops:** do not pass metal equipment through them, climb them, or camp in their plane.
- **Dry shelters:** test with inert objects before entering. Send in a clock, pressure sensor, gas monitor, and damp cloth. Retrieve them remotely.
- **Supportless bridges:** cross one person at a time without jumping. Leave wide spacing. Do not attach a team rope to the bridge unless necessary; its stiffening response may interpret distributed tension unpredictably.
- **Warm wall depressions:** do not press them experimentally. Touch may be authentication, power transfer, medical sensing, or activation.
- **Wind-hole markers:** do not plug, play, rearrange, or collect them. Their chords match the infrastructure and may form a passive control or diagnostic array.
- **Western rib structure:** remain outside the central emptiness at midday.

The western ribs are especially hazardous. When the distant plateaus appear connected, the structure may be displaying routes, opening routes, measuring field alignment, or briefly making those routes real. Do not stand in the center, launch a drone through the distortion, or touch a visible curve. Watch from a marked position with an extraction team ready. If shadows, clocks, or radio signals diverge, leave before the image fades.

## Time management

Maintain three schedules:

- subjective crew time;
- local instrument time;
- external universe time.

All major actions should be logged against all three. Team members should verbally confirm date, elapsed mission time, and immediate objective at breakfast and before sleep. This sounds excessive until two people have experienced different-length afternoons.

Never separate across a temporal seam. If unavoidable, agree on external-clock rendezvous rules and visual signals rather than shouted timing.

Be suspicious of preserved tracks, colored leaves underwater, or apparently fresh camps. They may be old rather than recent. Likewise, near the rim, equipment can age faster than expected. Inspect ropes, seals, batteries, medicine, and food after every transition into a rapid-decay zone.

Avoid leaving waste. In slow zones it may persist for generations; in fast zones it could decompose explosively or create an unnatural nutrient patch.

## Emergency procedures

### If someone becomes lost

The lost person should stop moving, seek stable stone, deploy a high-visibility marker, and broadcast instrument readings—not just coordinates. Useful data include pressure, clock offsets, background tone, apparent landmarks, and whether water appears to flow uphill.

Searchers must not walk directly toward a visible person across a ravine or clearing. First determine whether the intervening distance and time behave normally.

### If a seam causes clock divergence

Do not pull the affected person across by brute force. Reduce tension, establish communication, compare movement rates with repeated hand signals, and use a controlled reel. If the divergence is increasing, withdraw both sides from the boundary rather than trying to complete the crossing.

### If a pylon changes tone

Move calmly away along the approach route. Do not run through vegetation boundaries. Leave water if silver swimmers begin aligning. Avoid metal bridges and conductive rock seams until the tone stabilizes.

### If weather worsens

Remember that the basin usually redirects destructive weather, but “usually” is not a guarantee. Stay away from glowing rock seams during internal lightning events. Do not shelter under trees rooted into those seams. If wind begins forming broad spirals, lie low on stable stone and allow it to divide around you.

### If extraction fails

Move toward a previously surveyed stable cache, not automatically toward the outer rim. The rim has rapid-decay zones, pressure distortions, and severe cliffs hidden by cloud. A delayed rescue from a central island is preferable to a desperate descent into the storms below.

## Best style of visit

Plan short, conservative excursions from a fixed base. Measure more than you collect. Sit still often.

This country rewards patience. Its hazards become visible through repetition: a rainbow that does not move, a line of vines that never crosses a clearing, fish holding points around a pylon, a waterfall whose sound arrives on the wrong beat. A hurried traveler will see only marsh, forest, and rain. A careful one will begin to perceive the operating boundaries that local life already understands.

The safest attitude is neither fear nor confidence, but courtesy. Assume that every stable oddity has a function, every animal path encodes generations of successful navigation, and every ancient machine is still doing exactly what it was built to do.

Above all, do not mistake slowness for inactivity. The mountain is continuously making decisions about what remains inside it. Your task is to visit without giving it a reason to make one about you.
