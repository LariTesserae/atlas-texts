## Overall recommendation

Treat this wetland as an active piece of reality-maintenance infrastructure, not as wilderness and certainly not as ruins. Nothing appears hostile in the predatory sense, but almost every familiar assumption—distance, gravity, sequence, reflection, structural solidity—can fail locally.

An ordinary human should not visit alone. The safest expedition is short, daylight-limited, remotely observed, and confined to a surveyed route near the arrival point. The western basin should be considered a **no-entry zone**.

## Before crossing

### Establish a conservative return point

Place the transit gate or return anchor on:

- A broad, dry-looking shelf of old stone.
- Ground above the surrounding blackwater, even if only slightly.
- An area without long grooves, floating plants, folded shrubs, persistent condensation lines, or unexplained vibrations.
- A location from which the organ-like roar is audible but not overwhelming.
- A site that has remained geometrically stable through at least one local storm cycle, if advance probes can observe it.

Do not place the portal beside a pool, inside vegetation, or anywhere rain outlines invisible edges. Anchor the expedition primarily from the home side; local stakes may be shifted back into place by the marsh or may remain stationary while the surrounding distance changes.

Leave a trained operator outside the location at all times. Agree on hard recall times and emergency closure procedures. Because cause and effect can become misordered, the operator should treat any message apparently sent “too early” as genuine until disproved.

### Send probes first

Use several simple probes rather than one elaborate machine. Compare:

- Visual recordings from separated cameras.
- Acoustic travel times.
- Radar or lidar ranges.
- Inertial measurements.
- Independent mechanical, quartz, and atomic clocks.
- Atmospheric and biological sampling.
- The apparent order in which footprints, ripples, and movements occur.

A probe’s map should not be trusted merely because it is internally consistent. Look specifically for regions where its image arrives before its telemetry, where two distance measurements disagree, or where it reports tracks before moving.

## Personal equipment

Wear a fully sealed, insulated field suit. The likely immediate dangers are hypothermia, contaminated water, falls, and spatial displacement rather than dramatic attack.

Recommended equipment includes:

- A positive-pressure respirator with independent air supply.
- A dry suit or equivalent waterproof outer layer.
- Broad, low-pressure marsh boots with strong ankle support.
- Helmet, eye protection, and padded gloves.
- A flotation vest that does not depend on automatic inflation.
- Chemical warmers and a dry emergency wrap.
- Multiple clocks of different designs.
- Inertial navigation backed by acoustic references.
- A chest-mounted camera plus a second camera looking backward.
- Passive dosimeters and electric-field sensors.
- A short, blunt probing staff made without exposed sharp edges.
- A directional microphone capable of tracking the fixed roar.
- A low-intensity side lamp for making suspended droplets visible.
- Individually sealed food and drinking water.
- Sample containers with triple containment—but only if sampling is authorized.

Do not drink, wash in, or deliberately touch local water. Even apparently ordinary rain may have been isotopically sorted or passed through microscopic systems. The western basin can exert tidal forces through skin contact despite being shallow.

Avoid long safety ropes. A line stretched through a pocket of compressed or intersecting distance may become impossibly taut, cross itself without contact, or pull a traveler into the wrong apparent direction. If teammates use tethers, they should be short, monitored, and fitted with rapid breakaways.

## Navigation

Never navigate by sight alone.

A ridge that appears close may be kilometers away; a nearby island may recede under its own weather. Reflections may precede objects, and vegetation can open onto distant parts of the marsh. Use at least three independent references:

1. The direction and intensity of the continuous subterranean roar.
2. Inertial measurements from the last verified stable point.
3. Two-way communication and ranging to the return station.

Mark routes only as a secondary aid. Flags, footprints, and beacons may be displaced, duplicated, or encountered before they are placed. If you find one of your own markers ahead of the team, stop. Do not approach it until the return station confirms the sequence of events.

Move slowly and test each step. Broad stone is generally preferable to vegetation mats, but avoid slabs bearing long grooves. Water running uphill along them is being accelerated through unseen curvature; never step across a convergence or approach a place where water vanishes. The roar there may conceal structural cracking and loss of footing.

Do not follow streams downhill. “Downhill” is not a dependable concept in this landscape.

## Things not to touch

Avoid direct contact with:

- Condensation suspended on invisible planes.
- Clear rootless plants floating above the soil.
- Shrubs whose branches disappear into folded space.
- Self-looping twigs.
- Water surfaces where reflections vanish into gray.
- Insect swarms or the shadows they cast.
- Any pool reflecting stars not present overhead.
- Frost crystals that point downward.
- Fine subsurface films or mineral networks exposed in mud.

The invisible condensate structures are especially dangerous. Their apparent edges may not correspond to their full extent, and touching one could redirect momentum, body heat, or part of the body along an unseen dimension. If droplets begin outlining geometry across your route, retreat along your exact previous movements while the route is still visible.

Do not spray water to reveal more of the structures. Deliberately stimulating the system may alter drainage or trigger a local correction process.

## Reading the weather

The ordinary cold and rain are manageable. The locally assembling storms are not.

Warning signs include:

- A basin trembling without wind.
- Reeds turning their pale undersides outward.
- Condensation revealing unusually extensive frameworks.
- Thunder originating below the ground.
- Rain rising from pools.
- Near and distant objects exchanging apparent scale.
- Tracks, ripples, or shadows occurring out of sequence.

At the first combination of these signs:

1. Stop traveling.
2. Move only if a verified stable stone shelf is within a few ordinary steps.
3. Keep the team close, but do not tighten ropes across open space.
4. Crouch low and minimize movement.
5. Use the continuous roar as the primary orientation reference.
6. Do not pursue teammates who suddenly appear farther away; confirm them by radio first.
7. Do not enter shrubs or water for shelter.
8. Wait until rain resumes a single direction and range measurements agree again.

Sound appears to preserve scale better than vision during these events, but not necessarily direction in every location. Maintain voice and instrument contact continuously.

The reeds’ blue illumination may provide several seconds of warning before lightning. If the glow branches rapidly through nearby stands, adopt lightning posture immediately. Do not shelter among the reeds; their anticipatory conduction may make them part of the discharge path.

## Metallic chords and stillness events

When the moving metallic chord begins:

- Stop walking.
- Get low on stable ground.
- Secure loose equipment.
- Avoid touching standing water or exposed stone grooves.
- Do not attempt to chase, record at close range, or intercept the moving front.

The marsh walkers kneel and place their throats against the ground, suggesting the chord carries information or force through both soil and air. Humans should not imitate the throat contact. Use instruments.

If the reeds and rain suddenly become still, uphill streams stop, or the reflected stars in the west begin going dark, remain completely motionless. Do not activate a portal, transmit high-power signals, take samples, or throw anything into the water. This appears to be a large-scale correction cycle. Wait until the roar returns and the reeds finish ringing before moving.

## Wildlife conduct

The marsh walkers should be given very wide clearance. They appear to detect unstable geometry more effectively than humans can.

Useful rules:

- If a walker refuses an area, do not enter it.
- If its reflection appears before the animal, stop and allow the event to complete.
- Never stand between a walker and open ground.
- Do not approach when its neck filaments open.
- Do not follow it toward the western basin.
- During a chord, remain farther away than usual.

The animals are not known to be aggressive, but startling one could drive it—and anyone nearby—into spatially unstable terrain.

Avoid white insect swarms. Their collective shadow may represent a remote organism, a higher-dimensional body, or merely an optical consequence, but none of those possibilities makes entering the swarm prudent. If the shadow crosses the team while the insects do not, withdraw and conduct a full contamination and neurological check.

## The western basin

Do not wade into it.

Do not touch it with a hand, pole, cable, drone tether, or sample bottle. Do not fly directly over its center. Keep well back from the shoreline when stars appear in the water.

The basin may function as a sink, aperture, regulator, or contact surface with a distant region. Its knee-deep physical floor does not limit the forces accessible through it. The sideways-falling sensation is a warning of coupling, not harmless vertigo.

Observation should be remote, brief, and passive. If marsh walkers begin assembling around the shore, withdraw before the reflected stars begin extinguishing.

## Camping and duration

Do not camp unless the expedition has already monitored a site through several full weather cycles. If an overnight stay is unavoidable:

- Use a low-profile sealed shelter on a verified stone shelf.
- Do not drive deep stakes.
- Keep all sleeping areas away from grooves, pools, shrubs, and reed margins.
- Maintain an awake watch with acoustic and distance instruments.
- Recheck the apparent location of the return point every fifteen minutes.
- Do not leave the shelter merely because stars appear in a nearby pool; they may not indicate nighttime locally.
- Treat downward-pointing morning frost as evidence that the site’s geometry has changed.

Keep the initial human visit under a few hours. The danger of cumulative causal displacement may not be noticeable to the traveler. A person can feel entirely normal while becoming misaligned with the expedition’s external chronology.

## Exit and quarantine

On return:

- Compare all personal clocks before anyone removes equipment.
- Confirm that each traveler remembers events in the same order.
- Check recordings for movements, messages, or injuries preceding their causes.
- Quarantine suits, water, mud, spores, and mineral dust.
- Do not bring living samples through on the first expedition.
- Isolate any equipment that remains wet without losing mass, produces condensation on unsupported surfaces, or reports a different sky.
- Medically evaluate travelers for hypothermia, pressure injury, altered vestibular function, unusual isotope exposure, and memory discontinuity.

If a traveler returns before the expedition is recorded as having departed, do not send the original expedition. Preserve both the traveler and all records under causal-isolation protocol.

## Best attitude to bring

Do not think of the place as broken. Its impossible behavior is stable, ancient, and ecologically integrated. The marsh is likely doing exactly what it has evolved—or been made—to do.

Walk lightly, trust instruments only when they agree, and treat every apparent shortcut as a trap. Watch the walkers. Listen to the roar. When the whole land pauses, pause with it.
