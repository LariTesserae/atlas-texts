This hollow should be approached as a hot, biologically active confined space—not as an ordinary cave or garden. Its dangers are subtle: bad air, heat stress, unstable mineral crusts, concealed water channels, dense life underfoot, and predators that locate prey through vibration.

## Before entering

### Do not go alone
Use a minimum team of three:

- Two travelers entering together
- One person remaining at the surface with weather data, a clock, and rescue equipment

Leave a fixed route plan and a hard turnaround time. The surface attendant should be able to order an evacuation if rain appears anywhere over the surrounding drainage area, not merely at the entrance.

### Choose the dry interval
The safest time is late in a settled dry period, when:

- No rain has fallen recently
- None is forecast over the plateau or neighboring catchments
- Drip rates are stable or declining
- Pools are disconnected
- Animal activity is relatively low

The hottest, driest hours carry greater heat risk, but the onset of seepage is much more dangerous. Once water begins connecting the pools, predators and smaller animals become active simultaneously, mud seals open, and routes can change beneath your feet.

An increasing drip rate is an evacuation signal. Do not wait for visible flowing water.

### Test the atmosphere
Before descent, lower a calibrated gas meter into the hollow. Continuously monitor:

- Oxygen
- Carbon dioxide
- Hydrogen sulfide
- Carbon monoxide
- Temperature and humidity

The basin shape may trap carbon dioxide, decomposition gases, spores, and radon. Do not enter if oxygen is low or carbon dioxide is elevated. A filter mask does not correct oxygen deficiency; if the air is not safely breathable, abandon the visit rather than relying on improvised breathing equipment.

## Equipment

Bring:

- A climbing or caving helmet with a secured chinstrap
- Three independent, waterproof light sources per person
- Soft-soled, high-traction caving boots
- Puncture-resistant gaiters
- Lightweight, sealed clothing with gloves and eye protection
- A cooling vest or phase-change cooling packs
- More drinking water than expected, carried in sealed containers
- Electrolytes
- A continuous gas monitor
- A chest harness and static safety line anchored outside the chamber
- Compact thermal and humidity monitors
- A small trauma kit, splints, sterile irrigation water, and bite-management supplies
- A rigid evacuation litter or hauling system at the entrance
- Quiet text or light-signaling devices
- Sterile ground markers or a noncontact mapping system

Use diffuse, low-intensity lamps. Avoid ultraviolet and strong blue light, which may interfere with fungal growth and the animals’ luminous displays. Never use open flames.

Do not rely on the bioluminescence. It reveals isolated surfaces while concealing drops, pools, serpents, and unsupported crusts between them.

## Prevent contamination

This is an extremely closed ecosystem. A traveler could introduce molds, mites, bacteria, seeds, or skin organisms capable of spreading through the entire basin.

Before entry:

- Clean and disinfect all equipment
- Wear freshly laundered or sterile outer clothing
- Remove soil from boot seams and tools
- Seal food, tobacco, cosmetics, and scented products
- Cover cuts completely

Afterward, quarantine and decontaminate everything again. Do not carry water, mud, leaves, eggs, shells, insects, mineral crusts, or shed skins out of the chamber without formal biological containment. Conversely, leave nothing behind—not even crumbs, fibers, tape, skin dressings, or wastewater.

## Moving through the hollow

### Practice restraint
The chamber’s principal rule should become yours. Move slowly enough that each foot placement can be inspected first. Keep your weight over stable limestone rather than:

- Mineral films
- Mud plates
- Moss terraces
- Fallen slabs with softened edges
- Insect-built formations
- Roots
- Pool margins

Many apparently solid surfaces may be hollow galleries, sealed animal chambers, or crusts over water. Use a staff only if absolutely necessary, and place it gently; probing and tapping may attract predators and destroy burrows.

Travel single file with generous spacing. Do not spread out through the vegetation. Follow already bare, durable stone where possible, even if it makes the route longer.

### Keep quiet—but do not compromise human safety
Ordinary conversation, equipment clatter, jumping, hammering, and dropped objects will transmit through the basin. Pad all metal fittings before entry. Secure buckles, bottles, tools, and lamps so nothing swings.

Use prearranged hand signs:

- Closed fist: freeze
- Two fingers toward the eyes: animal observed
- Hand pressed downward: lower yourself slowly
- Repeated backward gesture: retreat
- Rapid lamp interruption: emergency

Radio earpieces or text displays are preferable to speech. However, if someone is injured or disoriented, communicate clearly; attracting wildlife is less dangerous than losing a team member.

Walk rather than crawl whenever the ceiling permits. Crawling places hands and face among translucent lizards, mineral-feeding beetles, amphibians hidden at mud level, and serpents in low roots.

### Watch above and below
Inspect hanging root masses before passing beneath them. Heavy cave serpents are likely to be nearly indistinguishable from wet roots or mineral-coated vines.

Examine mud for:

- Long smooth depressions at water level
- Freshly closed tracks
- Bowed reeds
- Recently displaced mineral skins
- Small ripples without falling drops

These may indicate a concealed amphibian or another vibration-sensitive predator. Do not step over it. Back away along your exact route.

Never put a hand beneath a leaf, log, ledge, or fern without illuminating the underside first.

## Water precautions

Treat all water as biologically unsafe. Do not drink it, wash in it, or allow it into cuts. Even clear seepage may contain parasites, mineral toxins, eggs, or microorganisms for which an off-world traveler has no immunity.

Avoid entering pools altogether. A pool may be:

- Deeper than it appears
- Connected to a narrow drainage void
- Occupied by dormant fish or crabs
- The hunting station of a buried predator
- Bordered by mud too soft to support a person

Do not cross the temporary stream once it awakens. Its appearance means the hydrological phase is changing, even if it is only a few centimeters deep.

## Heat management

The rock will continue radiating heat even when the surface day cools. High humidity may make sweating ineffective.

Set conservative work cycles, such as twenty minutes of movement followed by ten minutes of rest on known stable stone. Monitor one another for:

- Uncharacteristic clumsiness
- Irritability or confusion
- Headache
- Nausea
- Rapid breathing
- Cessation of sweating
- Inability to follow silent signals

At the first cognitive symptom, leave immediately. The chamber is too intricate for a heat-impaired person to navigate safely.

Respirators increase heat load. If respiratory protection becomes intolerable, that is another reason to shorten the visit rather than remove it in place.

## Encounters with animals

Do not touch, feed, collect, corner, or illuminate any animal for long periods.

If vegetation moves unexpectedly:

1. Freeze.
2. Keep your hands close to your body.
3. Identify the direction of movement with low light.
4. Retreat by transferring weight gradually, without stamping or running.

If a serpent is overhead, do not pass beneath it even if it appears asleep. If a long amphibian is visible at mud level, assume it can lunge farther than expected.

Unknown amphibian skin may be toxic. Unknown snakes should be treated as medically significant. Following a bite:

- Move out of immediate striking range without running
- Keep the injured limb still
- Remove rings or tight clothing
- Splint the limb
- Evacuate at once

Do not cut, suck, cauterize, or ice the wound. Do not apply a tight arterial tourniquet unless directed by a medically competent protocol for the specific species. Because the local venom is unknown, evacuation and supportive care are more reliable than speculative antivenom.

## The insect “ruins”

Admire the calcified structures from a distance. They are neither empty nor structurally trustworthy. Their passages now shelter beetles, roots, and possibly larger animals, while mineralization may have left thin, brittle walls around internal voids.

Do not enter or touch the person-high formation. The shell fragments in its walls respond to water and vibration; disturbing it could collapse an irreplaceable habitat or announce your presence across the basin.

## Immediate evacuation triggers

Leave at once if any of the following occurs:

- Dripping becomes noticeably more frequent
- Several previously dry surfaces darken at once
- Condensation begins running continuously along roots
- Water appears in a dry channel
- Reeds begin tipping toward shrinking or filling pools
- Animal movement increases across multiple areas
- Gas readings change
- Visibility is reduced by condensation
- A route marker shifts or disappears
- Anyone shows heat stress, panic, breathing difficulty, or confusion
- The surface attendant reports distant rain

The basin’s wet transition is not something an ordinary traveler should remain to observe from the floor. If witnessing it is essential, do so from a secure elevated entrance or with remotely placed instruments.

## Best conduct for the visit

Keep the stay short. Do not camp, cook, relieve yourself, or sleep inside. Photograph and map without flash where possible. Pause often—not to touch, but to let the chamber settle after your passage. Once the leaves stop bowing and the mud stops answering your steps, more of the hollow may reveal itself.

You should leave with the impression that you visited a crowded settlement whose inhabitants communicate through means you barely perceive. The safest traveler will imitate them: soft-footed, close to the route, sparing with light, and unwilling to disturb more of the basin than necessary.
