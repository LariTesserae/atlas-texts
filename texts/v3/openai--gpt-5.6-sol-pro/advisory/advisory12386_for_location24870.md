Prepare as for a cold, wet, high-altitude trek through an inhabited ruin—not as for an abandoned city. The chief dangers are exposure, bad footing, hidden water, and accidentally interfering with the drainage system on which every district depends.

## Before ascending

Allow several days for the approach and acclimatization. The plateau is high enough that an ordinary traveler may experience headache, nausea, poor sleep, or shortness of breath before reaching the city proper. Do not plan difficult crossings on your first day there. If your route begins in another universe, confirm in advance that the atmosphere, local water chemistry, microorganisms, gravity, and day length are tolerable to an unmodified human.

Arrange a local guide before entering the dense districts. Maps will be of limited use: lanes vanish beneath pools, interior routes may pass through nominally private buildings, and weather can make two nearby quarters temporarily inaccessible to one another. Seek a guide by watershed name, not by a grand civic destination. “The Three Basins” is meaningful; “the center of the city” may not be.

Tell someone which drainage divides you intend to cross and where you expect to sleep.

## What to wear

Bring clothing suited to persistent cold damp rather than dramatic storms:

- A breathable waterproof outer layer with a hood  
- Wool or synthetic insulating layers that remain useful when wet  
- Several pairs of dry socks, individually sealed  
- Waterproof boots with soft, high-grip soles and good ankle support  
- Gaiters to keep silt and shallow runoff out  
- Thin waterproof gloves, plus warmer gloves for evening  
- A warm hat  
- Muted colors, both for practicality and to avoid appearing ostentatious  

Choose boots carefully. Smooth modern soles are dangerous on wet fitted stone, black lichen, and submerged steps. Very rigid mountaineering boots may also be awkward in cramped passages. A walking staff is valuable for testing flooded paving, but fit it with a broad rubber or wrapped-leather tip so it does not chip stone or wedge into drain slots.

Expect to be wet below the knees no matter how well equipped you are. Keep one complete sleeping outfit dry and never wear it while traveling.

## Essential equipment

Carry your belongings in waterproof inner bags rather than trusting a single pack cover. Useful items include:

- A compact insulated sleeping mat  
- A warm sleeping bag protected from damp  
- A groundsheet that can be folded away without blocking runoff  
- Water purification supplies capable of handling microorganisms and mineral sediment  
- A wide-mouthed bottle that can be cleaned easily  
- High-calorie food requiring little fuel  
- Electrolyte salts  
- A small first-aid kit with blister care and treatment for hypothermia  
- A headlamp or hand torch, if local hosts permit it  
- Spare batteries kept warm and dry  
- Chalk that washes away, or removable cord markers, for emergency navigation  
- A whistle, though it should be used only in genuine danger  
- A paper notebook and soft pencil  
- A small cloth and brush for removing grit from footwear before entering homes  
- A modest gift: salt, needles, strong thread, dry tea, waxed cord, or another useful compact good  

Avoid wheeled luggage entirely. Do not bring a cart. Even if you could move one through the city, wheels may be unfamiliar, socially intrusive, and poorly suited to steps, channels, and submerged streets.

Metal objects may attract attention in a city without metal fixtures. Keep tools simple and discreet. Do not assume that technological equipment is forbidden, but ask before displaying lamps, cameras, drones, pumps, radios, stoves, or powered instruments. A pump in particular may be understood not merely as a machine but as an attempt to alter lawful water movement.

## Movement through the city

Travel slowly. Dry-looking paving may conceal a film of mineral slime, and clear pools may hide stairs, open conduits, or missing flags. Never jump from roof to roof simply because local residents do so. They know which slabs bear weight and which walls are only held in equilibrium by adjoining masonry.

Test each flooded step before committing your weight. Avoid:

- Bowed walls  
- Freshly fallen grit beneath arches  
- Roofs holding pools of water  
- Cracks that emit flowing water  
- Masonry that knocks, grinds, or sheds grains during rain  
- Narrow passages below saturated retaining walls  
- Marsh surfaces that appear paved but tremble under pressure  

After prolonged rain or a hard frost, assume that previously safe routes may have changed.

Do not enter underground channels without a resident guide. A culvert that appears dry can fill from rainfall occurring beyond your horizon. The sound of water below a street gives little indication of its depth or force.

Fog can erase landmarks quickly. Navigation should be based on slope and drainage as much as architecture. Learn which way the nearest runnel flows, which basin receives it, and where higher shelter lies. If lost, follow maintained channels only until you reach an inhabited structure; do not follow water blindly downhill, since it may lead into the central wetland, a subsided quarter, or an escarpment outfall.

Keep well back from the plateau edge. The cliffs may be obscured by mist, and water-polished stone near the falls can be lethally slick.

## The law of water

The essential rule is simple: never stop a common flow.

Do not place your pack, bedding, tent, feet, rubbish, or cooking equipment in a gutter, wall opening, courtyard outlet, or apparently insignificant groove. A trickle at your lodging may drain several roofs upstream and supply households below. Blocking it can flood a chamber hours later and contaminate a neighborhood’s clean-water basin.

Before washing, emptying a vessel, or disposing of anything liquid, ask where it belongs. Soap, food grease, blood, urine, and even muddy boot water may have separate acceptable routes. Upstream contamination is both a practical injury and a serious breach of responsibility.

Do not:

- Dam a runnel for convenience  
- Seal a draft at the base of a wall without permission  
- Move stones from a channel  
- Clear a blockage on your own unless danger is immediate  
- Take water from a swept basin without being invited  
- Assume rainwater is ownerless  
- Cross a marked drainage line as though it were merely a property boundary  

If you accidentally obstruct a channel, remove the obstruction, notify the nearest household downstream, and offer to help clean or reset the runnel. Concealing the mistake will be judged far more harshly than making it.

## Water and health

Water is abundant, but not all of it is safe. Rooftop runoff may contain animal waste; old conduits may carry silt or human contamination; clear cisterns may be mineral-heavy. Drink only from a source identified by residents, then treat it unless you have reliable biological compatibility information.

Cold immersion is a greater risk than drowning in many districts. Waist-deep water can rapidly incapacitate a tired traveler, especially after sunset. If you fall in, get out, strip off wet insulation, dry yourself, and warm gradually. Do not continue walking simply because the air temperature seems moderate.

Watch for trench foot, fungal irritation, infected abrasions, and cracked skin. Wash and dry your feet each night. Even tiny cuts should be cleaned promptly; the city’s water moves through old inhabited spaces and animal refuges.

The altitude and constant cold may suppress thirst. Drink deliberately.

## Shelter

Do not camp in a roofless courtyard just because it appears enclosed. Courtyards are often active catchments, and a dry floor can become ankle-deep during the night. Likewise, do not sleep beneath a bridge, inside an empty drain passage, against a retaining wall, or in the low corner of a chamber.

The safest lodging is with inhabitants or in a structure they designate. Expect sleeping places to be interior, low, and dark, perhaps reached through several joined rooms. Ventilation may be limited, and fuel smoke may collect. If you carry a carbon monoxide detector, use it discreetly; otherwise sleep near the ventilation level recommended by your hosts.

Ask where to place wet clothing. Hanging garments across an opening may interfere with airflow, access, or drainage. Fuel is scarce, so do not expect a fire solely to dry your possessions. Offer your own fuel only after checking that it is acceptable indoors.

A free-standing tent may be impractical and socially conspicuous. Stakes can damage hidden paving or conduits; guy lines create hazards in mist. If forced to bivouac, choose an elevated, already-disturbed platform with multiple visible drainage exits, and use weights rather than driving stakes.

## Conduct among inhabitants

Treat apparently empty structures as potentially used. Occupancy is marked by maintenance more often than by doors, smoke, or voices. Freshly swept stone, reset pale masonry, stacked fuel, rush matting, cleared channels, and clean basins all indicate active claims.

Announce yourself at thresholds, but do not shout repeatedly. A soft knock on stone, followed by waiting, is likely more appropriate than calling into connected rooms. Never move a closing slab yourself unless invited.

Residents may speak quietly and may take time to appear. This is not necessarily suspicion. Interior travel is slow, and sound behaves unpredictably among the masonry.

Ask before photographing people, interiors, waterworks, repairs, or household stores. Do not treat the city as a picturesque ruin in front of those who maintain it. Avoid phrases such as “lost civilization,” “abandoned district,” or “dead city.” A collapsed quarter may still hold routes, rights, water claims, graves, fuel stores, or seasonal dwellings.

Useful courtesy consists of practical attention:

- Scrape mud from your boots before entering  
- Carry water only when shown which water and where  
- Help sweep a channel if invited  
- Replace any stone exactly as instructed  
- Contribute food or fuel rather than demanding warmth  
- Learn the downstream household’s name as well as your host’s  
- Ask what tonight’s rain requires before settling in  

Do not praise the absence of machines as purity or simplicity. The city’s gravity-fed systems are sophisticated, and its people’s restraint is likely born from environmental necessity rather than ignorance.

## Food and supplies

Assume food is locally scarce and storage is decentralized. Carry enough for delays. Dense foods that tolerate damp are best: hard biscuits, dried legumes if fuel is available, nuts, preserved fats, dried fruit, and compact ration bars. Avoid packaging that becomes litter or cannot be repurposed.

Never rely on buying supplies in a central market. Trade may occur within neighborhoods, and weather may interrupt movement between them. Replenish whenever an inhabited district offers the opportunity.

Do not forage without guidance. Sparse shrubs, sedges, lichens, peat, roots, and dung may all be claimed resources. The apparent absence of fences means little.

## Weather timing

Start early, but not necessarily before dawn. Morning fog over the central basin can be thick, and frost makes paving treacherous. Midday may offer the best visibility. Late-afternoon light can be beautiful but deceptive: temperature drops quickly once the low light disappears, and condensation forms even under a clearing sky.

At the first sign of sustained rain, ask whether your route crosses a low quarter. Local people may reroute through houses or over roofs while an outsider sees only a minor increase in runoff. Follow their judgment. Rain elsewhere on the plateau can reach your district after the sky above you has cleared.

If residents begin opening channels for the night, stop wandering. Their activity is a warning that routes and water levels are about to change.

## The central wetland

Visit only with someone who knows the current shoreline. Ancient paving under clear water can create a false sense of security. Streets may end abruptly where foundations have subsided, and silt can hide deep slots between blocks.

Do not attempt to reach the ends of the causeways merely because they seem to point toward a common center. They do not necessarily connect, and fog can cut off the return view. Wind from the basin after sunset will chill wet clothing rapidly.

Keep away from frogs and warm cistern habitats. Their scarcity may make them culturally protected as well as ecologically vulnerable. Clean and dry your boots before moving between isolated pools to avoid carrying disease.

## Emergency priorities

If separated from your guide:

1. Stop before entering another flooded or covered route.  
2. Move to stable, slightly higher paving.  
3. Identify the direction of drainage.  
4. Look for cleared runnels, swept basins, fresh masonry, rush matting, smoke traces, or footprints.  
5. Make a restrained call or whistle signal, then listen.  
6. Remain out of channels and away from unstable walls.  

If heavy rain begins, shelter in an inhabited or clearly maintained place if possible. Do not choose the lowest enclosed room. If water rises around you, move laterally toward a known higher roofline rather than forcing your way downstream.

In a structural collapse, beware of causing a second one by pulling blocks free. Call for local help. The city’s ruins have settled into mutual support, and an apparently loose stone may be carrying more weight than it seems.

## The right frame of mind

Come prepared for slow travel and incomplete understanding. You will not see the whole city, and trying to do so is a good way to become lost, exhausted, or unwelcome. Choose one or two watersheds and learn how they function: where rain lands, how people guide it, where they sleep, and which paths remain usable in each kind of weather.

The city will seem empty from a distance, but assume that unseen people are aware of changes in the water long before they are aware of you. Your best introduction is not a loud greeting or an impressive gift. It is stepping over the runnel without touching it, waiting at the threshold, and asking where the rain should go.
