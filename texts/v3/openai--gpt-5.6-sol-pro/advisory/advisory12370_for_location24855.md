## Overall assessment

This city looks gentle, but it should be treated as a remote, biologically fragile archaeological site on wet, unstable high ground—not as a comfortable ruin in which to camp.

The immediate dangers are mundane:

- slick stone and concealed cracks;
- saturated earth, shallow marshes, and weakened roofs;
- uncertain altitude, atmospheric chemistry, and water quality;
- constant humidity degrading equipment and preventing sweat from evaporating efficiently;
- isolation, with no local food and little reliable fuel.

The larger danger is ecological contamination. The local biosphere contains plants, fungi, bacteria, and apparently no animals at all. A visiting human would arrive carrying an entire mobile ecosystem: skin microbes, gut microbes, mites, food organisms, seeds, and perhaps insects hidden in gear. Even a breadcrumb, bowel movement, or shed hair could introduce nutrients and organisms the city has never encountered.

If possible, visit in a sealed, decontaminable field suit. If that is impossible, do not enter the marshes, gardens, reservoirs, or enclosed waterworks.

## Before crossing over

### Verify basic habitability

Do not trust the warm air merely because it looks breathable. Send instruments first and measure:

- oxygen, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, sulfur compounds, methane, and other volcanic gases;
- atmospheric pressure and effective altitude;
- gravity;
- airborne spores and microbial load;
- background radiation;
- water pH, salinity, dissolved metals, and biological activity;
- seismic movement and ground temperature;
- any unusual temporal, magnetic, or dimensional variation.

The mountain water is warm, and the ridges are geologically young. That combination may be harmless, but it can also indicate residual volcanic or hydrothermal activity. Gas can collect in roofed reservoirs, drains, low courts, and sleeping chambers even when open-air readings are normal.

The inhabitants’ orderly departure is not proof of safety. Establish remote sensors for at least several local day-night cycles before committing to a long stay.

### Establish a return route

Your crossing point or extraction vehicle should be placed on exposed, dry bedrock outside the city’s drainage system and above the marsh line. Mark the route with removable electronic beacons rather than paint, cairns, stakes, or fiber tape.

Carry at least two independent means of return. The city offers many visually similar courts, terraces, channels, and black-stone passages. Diffuse light and wet surfaces will make direction surprisingly difficult, especially when ridges are obscured by cloud.

Leave a route plan and hard abort time with someone outside the location.

## Biosecurity

The best visitor is one who leaves no biological evidence of having been there.

### Arrive clean

Before transit:

- sterilize the exterior of all equipment;
- inspect seams and packs for insects, eggs, seeds, and soil;
- use new or sterilized clothing;
- package food in double-sealed containers;
- avoid untreated wood, leather, feathers, natural rope, loose paper, and soil-contaminated equipment;
- quarantine beforehand if you are ill or carrying any transmissible infection.

A positive-pressure suit with replaceable external boot covers is ideal. The suit should contain hair, skin flakes, breath condensate, urine, and feces. If using an open respirator instead, understand that exhalation alone may seed the environment with human-associated organisms.

### Take everything back

Carry a closed waste system. Pack out:

- all bodily waste;
- wash water;
- food crumbs and packaging;
- filters;
- shed suit layers;
- broken equipment;
- sampling residues.

Do not spit, urinate, bury waste, wash in a channel, or pour a drink onto the ground. Do not use local plant matter for bedding, fuel, cordage, or repairs.

Never release a drone with exposed lubricants or detachable parts over a reservoir.

### Do not “improve” the ecosystem

Do not introduce fish, insects, worms, livestock, pollinators, compost organisms, or familiar crop plants. The apparent absence of animals is not a deficiency for a visitor to correct. It is the defining condition of the place.

Likewise, do not remove living plants, wet soil, fungus, or reservoir water unless you have a genuinely secure containment facility at home. Unknown spores or bacteria may be harmless here and invasive elsewhere.

## Personal equipment

Bring equipment designed for a warm, saturated environment rather than for conventional alpine cold.

Recommended gear includes:

- sealed or highly water-resistant clothing with good ventilation control;
- non-slip boots with soft, replaceable soles;
- gaiters or sealed boot cuffs;
- a helmet for falling stone and low lintels;
- gloves suitable for wet stone;
- trekking poles with broad, non-damaging rubber feet;
- a climbing harness and short safety line for stairs and reservoir edges;
- a dry shelter that does not require driving stakes into archaeological ground;
- desiccant lockers or heated dry bags for electronics;
- redundant navigation instruments;
- gas detector and portable weather station;
- headlamps plus low-angle inspection lights;
- water purification equipment, though imported water is preferable;
- all food needed for the entire stay, plus several emergency days;
- electrolyte replacement;
- antifungal skin treatment and foot-care supplies;
- a compact splint, wound kit, and means of treating hypothermia as well as heat stress;
- a communication relay, since wet stone and ridges may block signals.

Choose corrosion-resistant equipment. Constant moisture will attack contacts, batteries, copper, ordinary steel, adhesives, paper labels, and natural fabrics. Keep optics warm enough to prevent condensation and seal devices before moving between enclosed chambers and open air.

Do not depend on solar drying. Nothing will dry quickly.

## Clothing and bodily comfort

Although the air is warm, expect to feel clammy rather than hot. Sweat will evaporate poorly. Moderate exertion can lead to overheating, while long periods of stillness in wet clothing can chill you.

Use light layers that can be mechanically ventilated without exposing the environment to skin or breath. Change socks frequently inside containment. Inspect feet for maceration, blisters, and fungal irritation. Keep minor cuts completely covered; local water and microbial films should not enter wounds.

The effective altitude may still cause headache, poor sleep, breathlessness, or impaired judgment. Acclimatize if necessary and monitor blood oxygen rather than relying on how mild the climate feels.

## Water and food

Do not drink directly from springs, channels, basins, or reservoirs. Clarity and steady flow say nothing about dissolved minerals, unfamiliar microorganisms, or toxins from bacterial and fungal films. The inhabitants may have possessed adaptations or preparation methods you do not.

Bring your own drinking water. If emergency use becomes necessary:

1. take water from an actively emerging spring rather than a reservoir or marsh;
2. test for gases, acidity, metals, and salinity;
3. prefilter it;
4. use chemical or membrane treatment;
5. sterilize it;
6. retain a sample for later analysis.

Boiling alone may not remove dissolved metals or unfamiliar stable toxins.

Bring compact, low-crumb food that can be consumed within a closed shelter or helmet interface. Nothing visible should be assumed edible. Even plants resembling ferns, reeds, or familiar greens may have radically different chemistry.

There is no animal food at all, and the abandoned bins and grass linings should be treated as archaeological material, not provisions.

## Moving through the city

### Treat every surface as slippery

The most dangerous surfaces may be those that look merely dark rather than visibly wet. Condensation, bacterial films, and transparent sheets of overflow can make steps frictionless.

Walk slowly. Keep three points of contact on stairs. Test slabs before putting full weight on them. Avoid jumping across channels or stepping onto moss, pale floating leaves, and glossy mineral seams.

The ankle-deep marshes may conceal:

- sharp-edged fractures;
- sudden narrow holes;
- clay pockets;
- collapsed drains;
- soft sediment beside apparently solid paving.

Go around them unless there is no alternative.

### Respect the water system

Do not divert channels, clear notches, lift sluice stones, or open blocked drains. A tiny alteration could change the moisture balance of an entire terrace or undermine a wall downstream.

Avoid walking in channels even when they appear dry. Their almost imperceptible current makes them easy to mistake for paving, and they may be more deeply eroded beneath the edges than the visible surface suggests.

Reservoir steps are particularly hazardous. The submerged portion will be slick, and the water may conceal abrupt drops. Never enter a reservoir. Work from a safety line if you must approach its edge.

### Assume roofs and floors are weaker than they look

The lack of obvious decay is deceptive. Timber can remain in place after losing much of its structural strength. Reed mats and packed-earth upper walls may be saturated, fungal-softened, or held together mainly by their undisturbed load.

Avoid:

- walking on roofs;
- leaning against earthen walls;
- passing beneath visibly bowed beams;
- entering after heavy rain or seismic activity;
- gathering several people on an upper floor;
- touching columns or stacked stone piers unnecessarily.

Massive roofed reservoirs should be assessed for gas and structural stability before entry. Do not enter alone, and maintain a line-of-sight attendant outside.

### Watch the ridges

Fresh scarps, angular rubble, and young geology mean rockfall remains possible even if erosion is slow. Do not camp beneath cliffs, pale rubble fans, spring-striped slopes, or unsupported retaining walls.

After any tremor, leave buildings and move to broad, exposed bedrock away from reservoir margins. Saturated soils can fail with little warning.

## Camps and field bases

The safest base is outside the historic structures on a dry, gently sloping rock shelf above local watercourses. Use a free-standing, sealed shelter with padded feet. Do not anchor into paving, walls, or soil unless survival requires it.

Keep the camp out of:

- marshes and meadow bands;
- drainage lines;
- reservoir catchments;
- planted groves;
- enclosed courts where gas or condensation may accumulate;
- the apparent fall line of walls and ridges.

Use electric heat and light. Open flame is a poor choice: it may damage structures, ignite old reed material, contaminate surfaces with soot, and consume oxygen in sheltered rooms. Local timber and mats are not firewood.

Place absorbent containment beneath machinery and batteries. Even a small fuel or electrolyte leak could travel far through the plateau’s porous stone.

## Archaeological conduct

The city is unusually intact. Ordinary handling could do more damage than centuries of weather.

Do not move bowls, tools, reeds, copper blades, grinding stones, or stacked construction blocks. Their positions are evidence. Photograph and map them in place. The most mundane arrangement may reveal how the inhabitants worked, stored food, managed districts, or prepared to leave.

Do not scrape green corrosion from copper or clean moss from inscriptions and fittings. Do not “repair” a gutter or prop a beam unless human life is immediately at risk.

Use non-contact recording where possible:

- photogrammetry;
- lidar;
- thermal imaging;
- multispectral imaging;
- remote gas and moisture sensing.

If sampling is authorized, take the smallest possible amount and record exact context. Water from different seams, terraces, and reservoirs may represent distinct microbial communities despite their apparent connection.

## Conduct and psychological preparation

The silence may be more difficult than expected. Human hearing will search for birds, insects, footsteps, and distant voices that never occur. The constant faint water sounds can become either soothing or oppressive, and the intact domestic spaces may create a strong expectation that someone is about to return.

Travel with at least one companion, but avoid a large party. Use scheduled check-ins and objective fatigue assessments. Prolonged humidity, altitude, isolation, and acoustic monotony can produce poor concentration before a traveler recognizes distress.

Do not interpret every drop, settling beam, or brushing reed as evidence of another presence. Conversely, do not dismiss repeated structured sounds without checking instruments and companions. Record observations before building theories around them.

Preserve the quiet. Use headsets or bone-conduction communication rather than shouting. This is partly respect and partly practical: subtle changes in dripping, earth settling, or stone movement may provide the first warning of a hazard.

## A sensible first visit

A cautious initial expedition should be short and should not attempt to “explore the whole city.”

1. Establish the clean base on dry western or outer-saddle bedrock.
2. Monitor atmosphere, seismicity, and water chemistry.
3. Survey the central way without entering buildings.
4. Map safe dry routes and structural hazards.
5. Observe one open reservoir remotely.
6. Ascend the western terrace only in good visibility.
7. Return before fatigue, darkness, or equipment saturation becomes significant.
8. Decontaminate before crossing home.

Only later visits should enter workshops, communal halls, or covered reservoirs.

## Leave immediately if

Abort the visit for any of the following:

- headache, confusion, breathlessness, or unusual drowsiness not explained by exertion;
- rising carbon dioxide, sulfur gases, methane, or falling oxygen;
- tremors, new cracks, falling grit, or a change in spring flow;
- sudden warming, discoloration, bubbling, or odor in water;
- an unexpected change in reservoir level;
- rapid fungal growth or contamination on the suit;
- unexplained illness after water or spore exposure;
- failure of waste containment;
- loss of reliable navigation or return contact;
- any animal sign, whether native or apparently introduced.

The last point matters greatly. A fly, worm, footprint, shell, or fresh gnaw mark would represent a profound change in the known ecology and should trigger withdrawal and quarantine.

The city is not hostile in the usual sense. It is stable, wet, quiet, and extraordinarily vulnerable. Prepare less as a conqueror of ruins than as a temporary contaminant entering a finely balanced watershed. Walk softly, touch almost nothing, drink none of its water, and make certain that when you leave, the city can return to listening exactly as it did before you arrived.
