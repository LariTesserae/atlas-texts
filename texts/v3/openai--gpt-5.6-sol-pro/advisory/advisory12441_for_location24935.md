## General character of the visit

Treat the hollow as a high-altitude archaeological site inside an active freeze-thaw machine. It is not outwardly hostile, but almost every ordinary convenience of a living landscape is absent: no fuel, food, dependable water, soft ground, reliable trail, or biological decay. Rescue will be difficult, and small mistakes will remain visible for a very long time.

The principal hazards are altitude, severe sunlight, rapidly changing surface temperatures, unstable stone, hidden ice, disorientation among the fins, sudden cloud, and the unguarded eastern drop. The ancient waterworks should be considered both fragile machinery and cultural remains.

## Before ascending

### Verify that the environment is human-compatible

A multiverse traveler should never infer safety merely from familiar-looking stone and water. Send instruments through first, or make a brief monitored exposure, checking:

- Oxygen concentration and total air pressure  
- Carbon dioxide and other toxic gases  
- Solar ultraviolet and ionizing radiation  
- Gravity and local day length  
- Water chemistry, particularly dissolved metals  
- Whether clocks, compasses, radios, and return equipment operate normally  

The description suggests that the air is breathable only with difficulty. Carry supplemental oxygen even if acclimatized, with enough reserve to get below the ridge rather than merely to remain in the court.

Establish and test the return route before committing to the ascent. Leave a responsible party below with a schedule, route plan, and overdue protocol.

### Acclimatize

Spend several days ascending gradually and sleep at lower elevations before visiting. Do not attempt the court immediately after arrival from sea level—or from another world whose pressure differs significantly.

Headache, nausea, unusual fatigue, dizziness, poor coordination, confusion, breathlessness at rest, or a wet cough are reasons to descend. Confusion and stumbling are emergencies, not experiences to “push through.” Medication for altitude illness should be discussed with a qualified clinician beforehand; it does not replace acclimatization.

### Travel in a small party

Two to four people is ideal. A lone traveler could vanish between the fins within a few steps of companions, while a large group would damage channels and increase rockfall risk. Everyone should be comfortable on exposed, icy mountain terrain.

Camp below the ridge. Plan the hollow as a daylight excursion, not an overnight destination.

## Equipment

Carry normal high-altitude mountaineering equipment, including:

- A climbing helmet  
- Stiff, waterproof boots with good edging  
- Microspikes or light crampons, depending on conditions  
- Trekking pole or short probe for testing gravel, ice, and concealed basins  
- Layered windproof clothing, including face protection  
- Two pairs of gloves: one dexterous, one heavily insulated  
- High-altitude glacier glasses or Category 4 eye protection, plus goggles for cloud or blowing snow  
- High-SPF sunscreen and lip protection  
- Insulated sitting pad and emergency bivouac sack  
- Headlamp with spare batteries, despite the intention to leave before dark  
- First-aid kit, trauma supplies, and altitude medication as medically appropriate  
- Emergency oxygen and a regulator usable with gloves  
- Satellite beacon or equivalent multiversal emergency transmitter  
- Paper map, compass, altimeter, and an electronic track log  
- Spare batteries kept warm against the body  
- A short rope and harnesses for exposed approaches—not for casually threading through the stone maze  
- All food and water required for the visit, plus an emergency reserve  

Use narrow-necked, insulated, tightly sealed water containers. In this hollow, an open vessel in sunlight is an invitation to lose water. Keep bottles shaded inside the pack.

Do not depend on basin water or the copper collector. Even apparently pristine water may contain concentrated minerals or copper compounds, and ordinary filters do not remove dissolved metals. Carry enough from below.

## Timing the visit

No hour is entirely safe.

- **Before sunrise:** ice is firm but difficult to see; cold is most severe.  
- **Morning:** generally the best entry period, once there is enough light to distinguish frost from dry stone.  
- **Noon:** glare and ultraviolet exposure peak. Sunlit slabs warm while nearby passages remain frozen.  
- **Afternoon:** thawing gravel, loosened rock, eastward gusts, and rising cloud are more likely.  
- **Evening:** refreezing begins quickly and can turn the exit route into hard ice.  

Enter in the morning with a strict turnaround time. Leave generous allowance for the fact that the same aisle may have different footing on the return journey.

Avoid the hollow during storms, heavy cloud, strong wind, or rapid pressure changes. If lightning is possible, do not approach the ridge at all. The stone fins provide the appearance of shelter without necessarily providing electrical safety.

## Moving through the court

### Approach from the north or west if possible

The eastern side is an opening onto a vast drop and should be treated as an unguarded cliff even when cloud hides it. Do not approach merely to improve the view. In whiteout, establish a conservative exclusion zone well inside the hollow.

Everything loose tends to migrate eastward. Zip pockets, tether essential tools, and never set a helmet, bottle, camera, or pack casually on the ground.

### Navigate by time as well as position

Do not trust the pattern of light and shadow as a permanent landmark. Record:

- Time of passage  
- Direction of travel  
- Distinctive stone shapes seen from both directions  
- Intersections and blocked channels  
- Changes in footing and shadow  

Cairns are not reliable route markers. Some may have ceremonial or hydraulic significance, and all are subject to slow displacement. Do not build new ones.

A GPS or track recorder may help, but close stone walls can degrade reception. Photograph important junctions facing both toward the destination and back toward the exit. Do not use chalk, paint, tape, or scratched arrows.

### Assume every surface may change state

A slab in sunlight can be warm and dry on top while its shaded edge remains glazed with ice. Gravel may be held temporarily above the ground by ice needles and collapse under a boot. Dark basins can resemble solid rock.

Move slowly. Probe uncertain ground. Keep weight off thin plates, overhangs, and the bases of leaning fins. Do not climb the pillars: their apparent solidity conceals fractures widened nightly by freezing water.

Pass through narrow chokes one person at a time. Avoid placing hands inside cracks, where ice, sharp mineral edges, and moving flakes can trap or cut fingers. Helmets should remain on throughout the court.

After cloud enters, expect every stone face to become wet and slick within minutes. If visibility collapses, stop in a stable area away from the eastern opening, leaning slabs, channels, and basin edges. Maintain visual or physical contact, but do not run a taut rope around fragile fins. Sound is too irregular to be your primary means of locating companions.

## Understanding the sounds

Prepare the group for abrupt noises. A copper bowl may tip with a sharp knock; a contracting slab may sound like a tool strike or a distant shot. Pebble sounds may be repeated through the stone after the pebble has stopped moving.

Do not pursue unexplained taps or assume that a reply indicates another traveler. Use agreed visual signals and radios at close range. Whistles remain useful for emergencies, but echoes may make direction impossible to judge.

If repeated cracking begins near a pillar, overhang, or shelter roof, withdraw without touching the structure. Freeze-thaw rockfall can begin with very little warning.

## The shelters and waterworks

Regard all worked features as protected heritage.

Do not:

- Rebuild or rearrange cairns  
- Clear blocked channels  
- Tip the copper bowls by hand  
- Straighten the wooden pole  
- Oil pins or scrape corrosion  
- Lift fitted slabs  
- Drink directly from collection bowls  
- Move tools, wedges, or scrapers  
- Make rubbings, castings, or samples  
- Sit on ancient ledges or place equipment in cup marks  

The mechanism has survived because its forces are small and gradual. A well-intentioned repair could alter the timing of the whole network or break the irreplaceable wooden support.

Step over channels rather than in them. Do not divert water for convenience. If documentation is permitted, photograph and map from a distance before approaching; the location of a fallen fragment may be as informative as the fragment itself.

The shelters should be treated as archaeological structures, not huts. Their dry-stone roofs may be delicately balanced. Use one only in an immediate emergency and only after inspecting it from outside for shifted slabs. Never light a stove or flame inside: the turned entrance can trap carbon monoxide, and soot would permanently contaminate the site.

## Water, food, and waste

Bring all water. Keep it insulated and closed, taking brief drinks rather than leaving containers open. Eat compact foods that produce no crumbs and can be handled with gloves.

This place has no organisms or soil to digest waste. Pack out:

- All food particles and wrappers  
- Human feces and toilet paper  
- Urine where practicable, using sealed waste containers  
- Bandages, spilled fuel, broken equipment, and wash water  

At minimum, never urinate in a basin, channel, shelter, crack, or against a worked stone. Contamination may persist for decades and could be carried through the hidden hydraulic network.

Do not wash with soap, even biodegradable soap. “Biodegradable” has little meaning in a place without an active biology.

## If forced to bivouac

The safest emergency bivouac is likely a broad, stable patch away from the eastern opening, drainage channels, tall leaning stones, overhangs, and rockfall runouts. Use an insulated pad and bivouac sack. Do not depend on a fire; there is no fuel, and imported fuel creates additional hazards.

Expect water to freeze overnight, including valves and bottle caps. Sleep with critical batteries, filters, and some water inside insulation. Reassess the route at first light rather than moving through the aisles in darkness.

If altitude illness is suspected, do not bivouac merely for comfort. Administer oxygen and descend.

## Recommended conduct

Move quietly and make no attempt to “activate” the place. The hollow is already active. Its apparent stillness consists of processes too slow or too small for casual observation.

A respectful visit would involve entering with a fixed route, observing one or two tipping cycles from a safe distance, recording temperature and time without touching anything, and leaving well before afternoon weather develops. Take photographs rather than specimens.

Above all, remember that the court is not static scenery. Sun, shadow, vapor, ice, and stone continually alter the route beneath you. Visit as though walking through a very old machine—one without guards, instructions, or any distinction between its moving parts and the ground under your feet.
