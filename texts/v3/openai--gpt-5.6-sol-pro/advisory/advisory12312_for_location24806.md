This is not a place an unprotected human can visit. Its heat, sulfur atmosphere, mineral dust, electrical activity, and total absence of usable water make it closer to an industrial furnace than a terrestrial ruin. Prepare as though entering both a living organism and a pressure-driven machine.

## Before approaching

Establish your surface camp well away from the black seam. The salt plain has few landmarks and may produce severe glare, mirage, and horizon loss, so navigate by inertial reference, beacons, or an orbital fix rather than sight. Mark the return route redundantly; ordinary flags and tracks may be useless.

Do not place concentrated loads near the entrance. The crust forms the chamber’s roof and flexes during the heating cycle. Use broad load-spreading pads, and do not drill anchors until subsurface imaging has shown that you are not puncturing a duct, root network, or load-bearing plate. Assume apparently solid polygons may bridge concealed voids.

Send in disposable probes first. Map:

- temperature and heat flux at several heights;
- oxygen, sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, sulfur vapor, and unknown organosulfur gases;
- the pressure-breathing cycle;
- electrostatic fields and sudden discharges;
- ceiling movement and fracture propagation;
- the daily flowering interval;
- passages likely to close as the crust expands.

Observe for several complete thermal cycles before entering. The safest time will probably be during the cooler, quieter part of the cycle, after pressure has relaxed but before the ceiling begins its next compression. Do not infer safety merely from silence: cooling stone operates valves and causes its own fractures.

## Personal protection

Use a fully sealed, positive-pressure suit with an independent breathing loop. A filter mask is not enough. The local atmosphere may be oxygen-free, acutely toxic, corrosive, and full of particles sharp enough to damage lungs and equipment.

The suit should include:

- active cooling with enough reserve for a delayed exit;
- a non-water working fluid in the exterior cooling circuit;
- a reflective, abrasion-resistant outer layer;
- sulfur- and acid-gas-resistant seals;
- replaceable dust covers over joints and visors;
- insulated soles and gloves;
- redundant temperature, pressure, and gas alarms;
- an emergency rigid thermal shelter or drag cocoon;
- a tether that cannot melt, wick sulfur compounds, or conduct electrical discharge.

Water can remain sealed inside your life-support system and drinking reservoir, but **no water should be vented into the city**. A leak would not be a harmless spill. It could boil explosively, fracture hot saltstone, react with local chemistry, destroy living tissue, or contaminate processes that have never encountered it.

Avoid exposed metal where practical. Sulfur compounds, salt, heat, and electrostatic activity are unfriendly to ordinary alloys and contacts. Ceramic, glass, basalt-fiber, and appropriate high-temperature composites are preferable. Protect electronics inside shielded enclosures, but do not “ground” yourself by attaching a lead to walls or paving; the masonry may be part of a living electrical network.

Carry more cooling capacity than breathing capacity. Heat failure is likely to incapacitate you before air runs out.

## Entering the seam

Never stand directly over the opening during an exhalation. Gas, hot dust, and loose crystal fragments may emerge with little warning. Approach from the side, secured by a belay anchored far enough away that a local roof collapse will not take the anchor with it.

The entrance chamber is low. Keep your profile compact and avoid striking the ceiling. A tool or backpack scraping overhead could sever hanging growth, dislodge salt plates, or alter the tension of a pressure-responsive filament.

Once below, place passive route markers on freestanding tripods rather than gluing, hammering, or clipping them to the city. Conventional paint, chalk, tape, and chemical lights may contaminate surfaces or react with the atmosphere. Use coded reflectors or optical beacons that can be retrieved.

Travel with at least two companions, but space yourselves apart. Closely grouped bodies add heat, obstruct already narrow convection paths, and can all be trapped by one shutter or falling slab. Maintain line-of-sight or a physical guide line; radio may be unreliable amid conductive root networks, mineral walls, and electrical pulses.

## Moving through the city

Treat every structure as active, even if it appears ruined.

A bare street may be a preserved electrical break. A plaza may be a pressure reservoir. A narrow alley may accelerate pollinating gas. A handhold may also be a valve or sensory surface. Do not block vents, lean equipment against vanes, shelter in chimney mouths, or place packs in “empty” sockets.

Walk only on mapped paving. Avoid:

- ember-edged mats between stones;
- translucent or discolored slabs that may cover root channels;
- taut ceiling threads;
- hollow tubes in courtyards;
- widened joints functioning as ventilation trenches;
- black glass surfaces under mechanical stress;
- buttresses that appear newly wrapped or plated;
- spaces beneath counterweights, shutters, and thermal levers.

The bridges over fissures may be strong enough for the original inhabitants but not for a human in a heavy suit. Test them remotely and cross one at a time. Do not assume a fissure is harmless because it is too narrow to fall into; it may emit lethal heat or gas, or open as the temperature changes.

Move slowly. Human running would be unusually destructive here, and haste on salt dust over glass paving is likely to end in a fall. The rounded steps and sagging slabs may look gentle while offering almost no reliable traction.

Do not cut vegetation to clear a path. Widely separated crowns may belong to one organism, and a thread crossing a doorway may be structural, reproductive, conductive, or all three. If a route is obstructed, turn back.

## Sound and pressure

Use hearing protection, but understand that it will not stop low-frequency vibration transmitted through the body. The organ note may cause nausea, disorientation, anxiety, chest discomfort, blurred vision, or loss of balance even when it is barely audible. Monitor one another for confusion and involuntary changes in breathing.

Do not shout to test echoes. Do not sound horns, strike walls, or deliberately excite membranes. The city uses resonance mechanically. A tone that seems weak to you could open a shutter, shake loose crystals, disrupt pollen sorting, or trigger a coupled response elsewhere.

If the streets begin to howl together, hanging filaments draw tight, or vanes rotate edge-on, the high-pressure phase is beginning. Leave immediately unless you are already within a purpose-built shelter located outside all mapped airflow routes.

## Flowering is the principal acute hazard

Do not remain in the open city during mass flowering.

The pollen is dry, angular, mineral-armored, electrically charged, and propelled at high speed. It can abrade seals and visors, bridge electrical contacts, clog cooling surfaces, and penetrate damaged suit layers. The visible sparks indicate substantial charge separation; larger discharges may occur around a human-sized conductive object.

During the flowering cycle:

- keep away from rooflines and narrow accelerating lanes;
- do not stand in bowl-shaped plazas;
- avoid long galleries that sort grains;
- retract antennas and other sharp conductive projections;
- minimize movement that stirs deposited material;
- never use a flame, hot exposed element, or sparking motor;
- seek shelter only in a chamber verified to have pressure slits and more than one exit.

A sealed room is not automatically safe. In this city, a chamber without a functioning pressure slit may become an oven, a gas trap, or a burst vessel.

After exposure, withdraw to a remote decontamination station. Remove dust by dry suction, electrostatic neutralization, or compatible inert gas. Do not rinse the suit over the site.

## Food, drink, and medical planning

Eat and drink only in a sealed habitat. Never open your suit to sample the air, resin, “fruit,” or mineral films. Local sulfur circulation is not a substitute for terrestrial biochemistry, and apparently familiar colors carry no implication of edibility.

Prepare for:

- heat exhaustion and heat stroke;
- sulfur-gas exposure after seal failure;
- chemical burns;
- mineral abrasion of eyes and lungs;
- acoustic or low-frequency disorientation;
- electrical injury;
- falls and crush injuries;
- panic or navigation failure in dim, repetitive streets.

A rescue team must be equipped independently rather than relying on the casualty’s suit or route markers. Establish strict turnaround thresholds for cooling reserve, visor clarity, suit pressure, and crust movement.

## Conduct toward the city

Do not treat this as an abandoned archaeological site. The most cautious interpretation is that the city is inhabited, but that its inhabitants no longer fit the human categories of plant, citizen, tool, and building.

Accordingly:

- take nothing;
- do not collect seeds, tablets, blades, fibers, or loose architectural fragments without compelling reason;
- do not rearrange objects to make them more legible;
- do not apply water, solvents, preservatives, adhesives, or biological sterilants;
- do not introduce terrestrial microbes;
- do not sever a “plant” from a “building” on the assumption that they are separate;
- do not repair anything according to human architectural instincts.

If communication is attempted, begin with observation across many cycles. Repeated changes in vent timing, electrical flicker, vane position, or resonant tone may be meaningful. Use low-energy visual patterns from a freestanding device outside the main airflow. Avoid heat, sound, electrical injection, and chemical offerings: all are part of the city’s metabolism and could be interpreted as damage rather than speech.

Pay special attention to changes that follow your movements. A shutter opening, crown brightening, or root pulse may be automatic—but it may also be notice.

## What to bring

At minimum:

- sealed high-temperature environmental suits;
- redundant closed-loop breathing and active cooling;
- dry, nonaqueous fire suppression;
- thermal and gas-imaging scouts;
- ground-penetrating and structural-motion sensors;
- inertial navigation and retrievable optical markers;
- nonmetallic hand tools;
- load-spreading surface platforms;
- high-temperature belay lines;
- dry decontamination equipment;
- emergency rigid shelters;
- external medical support;
- recording equipment capable of measuring infrasound, pressure, electrostatic charge, and thermal motion.

Bring patience as seriously as equipment. A useful survey may require days of watching from the entrance and only minutes inside.

The central rule is simple: **nothing in the city is merely background**. Its air is machinery, its streets are reproductive organs, its ruins are repairs, and its empty spaces are working structures. Visit lightly, during the city’s quiet breath, and leave before it begins to sing in full.
