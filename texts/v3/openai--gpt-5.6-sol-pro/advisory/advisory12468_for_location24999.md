## Before you go

Treat the Warm Works as an active industrial facility, a confined space, and a dense animal habitat—not as a picturesque tunnel system. Arrange entry through the Works’ maintenance authority and go with a local guide who knows current pump status, flooded sections, electrical hazards, and evacuation routes. Do not arrive by multiversal transit directly inside: machinery, walkways, water levels, and even unfinished walls may differ from available maps. Materialize at an approved surface access point.

Schedule the visit:

- During a staffed maintenance shift.
- After several dry days, if possible.
- Away from major excavation, motor testing, or fumigation.
- Outside the main evening bat departure period unless you have a protected observation position.
- With enough time to leave well before shift change or forecast rain.

Rain anywhere in the drainage area matters. A storm above may raise gutters, energize new leaks, alter airflow, and flood a route that looked safe an hour earlier.

Confirm that ordinary human physiology is compatible with the local atmosphere and microorganisms. At minimum, have current tetanus vaccination; rabies pre-exposure vaccination is sensible for anyone spending substantial time near bats and rats. Ask a medical professional about mosquito-borne disease, leptospirosis, fungal spores associated with bat guano, and any world-specific infections.

## What to wear

Use equipment suitable for hot, wet industrial tunnels:

- Helmet with chin strap.
- Two independent lights: a helmet lamp and a backup hand light.
- High-traction, waterproof safety boots with toe protection.
- Lightweight coveralls with closeable cuffs.
- Nitrile gloves under tougher work gloves.
- Sealed safety glasses or goggles.
- Hearing protection that can be removed briefly for conversation.
- A well-fitted particulate respirator, ideally P100-equivalent, especially around guano, mold, excavation dust, or animal-control areas.
- A small amount of drinking water and electrolytes in a sealed container.

All electronics should meet the Works’ electrical and gas-safety requirements. Do not bring an open flame, exposed heating element, sparking tool, or uncertified high-power traveler apparatus. A personal gas monitor is useful, but it does not replace the facility’s confined-space procedures.

Avoid loose scarves, dangling straps, soft open shoes, and anything likely to snag on rebar or rotating equipment. Cover cuts before entry. Secure long hair.

Pack food only if necessary, and keep it in a hard, latched container. Rats will investigate ordinary zippers and wrapping. Keep every bag off the floor.

## Moving through the Works

The safest principle is to stay on raised walkways and touch as little as possible.

The mud-coated floors can conceal holes, electrical cables, sharp scrap, sudden channels, and contaminated water. A shallow-looking sump may be much deeper than it appears. Reflections in gutters can also make edges difficult to judge. Never step onto a grate, sheet of metal, or unfinished concrete ledge without your guide’s approval.

Other essential rules:

- Never open a door, cabinet, hatch, or animal-control box casually.
- Latch every door behind you.
- Do not operate valves, switches, relays, or pumps.
- Do not stand beneath suspended pipes, active hoists, or dense bat roosts.
- Give projecting rebar and unfinished excavation faces a wide berth.
- Never climb into a duct, trench, sump, or raw excavation.
- Do not follow the short rail line into the clay face.
- Do not cross the sealed bulkhead into the older tunnels.
- Lift no grate yourself. Local workers approach them from the far side because animals, gases, pressure, or missing supports may be underneath.
- If workers are preparing to start a motor, move back before they strike its housing. Bats, insects, or rodents may scatter suddenly.

Handrails may be warm, slick, and contaminated. Use them when needed, but keep gloves on and avoid touching your face afterward.

Look both upward and downward. Most visitors concentrate on the animals overhead and miss the water, hoses, and broken surfaces underfoot—or stare at the mud and walk beneath low pipe bridges.

## Animals and contamination

Do not feed, chase, photograph at close range, or attempt to rescue any animal without the animal-control crew.

Bats are likely to be the most visually overwhelming presence. Their movement may resemble a dark curtain, but they generally want a clear route rather than confrontation. Stay still and let them pass. Do not wave your arms. A bat that lands on you, becomes tangled in clothing, or makes bare-skin contact creates a possible rabies exposure even if no bite is visible; report it immediately and seek medical care.

Treat rats similarly: give them an escape route and do not corner them. If several suddenly flee one direction, pause. They may have been disturbed by machinery, rising water, a predator, or something opening beyond your sight.

Assume all standing water, mud, guano, fungal growth, and mineral foam are biologically contaminated. In particular:

- Do not kneel or place equipment on guano-covered platforms.
- Do not disturb dry droppings; aerosolized dust is more dangerous than intact deposits.
- Do not put hands into gutters or settling tanks.
- Do not handle stranded eels or frogs.
- Do not brush fungal fans or insulation.
- Avoid mosquito-heavy recesses and neglected buckets.
- Keep drinks sealed except while actively drinking in a designated clean area.

The tea stall’s mesh enclosure is not decorative. Enter quickly, close it behind you, and keep food inside. Do not carry leftovers through the Works.

## Heat, noise, and disorientation

Expect the heat to feel worse than the temperature suggests. Humidity prevents sweat from evaporating efficiently, and respirators add strain. Drink before becoming thirsty, walk slowly, and take breaks in designated ventilated spaces. Headache, nausea, clumsiness, irritability, chills, or confusion are reasons to leave immediately.

The noise is continuous rather than dramatic. After a while, the pumps and fans may fade from conscious notice; this makes a change in pitch easy to miss. Local workers often recognize trouble by sound. If your guide stops to listen, stop moving and speaking.

Hearing protection complicates communication, so agree on hand signals beforehand:

- Stop.
- Back up.
- Lights out or down.
- Machinery starting.
- Animal overhead.
- Evacuate.
- Medical problem.

The Works can feel larger than they are because of reflections, repeated sheds, half-lit machinery, and passages that look alike. Do not rely solely on painted arrows: some indicate future construction rather than an exit. Carry a facility-approved map, but follow the guide and numbered muster points. Surface navigation devices may not function reliably through concrete, clay, electrical equipment, or across universes.

Psychologically, prepare for claustrophobia and sensory overload. There is no distant view and almost no true quiet. Frogs can sound enormous through pipework; rats may scream inside walls; falling dust may suggest structural movement when it is only traffic overhead. Do not run in response to an unexplained sound.

## If conditions change

Leave promptly if any of the following occurs:

- Pumps change pitch or stop.
- Ventilation fans shut down.
- Lights flicker across more than one circuit.
- Water begins crossing a raised walkway.
- New seepage appears rapidly.
- Workers begin closing bulkheads.
- Animals move in unusual numbers away from a lower area.
- Air becomes markedly hotter, stiller, sour, metallic, or difficult to breathe.
- Your guide orders evacuation, even without explanation.

During flooding, do not take a shortcut through water. It may be electrically live, chemically contaminated, fast-moving beneath the surface, or covering an open sump. Follow the raised evacuation route to the assigned muster point. “Up” is not automatically safe; some stairwells may lead toward street runoff or ventilation shafts.

If separated, remain on the walkway in a visible position, use your light, and contact the control room. Do not explore to find another exit. If communications fail, follow only confirmed emergency markings—not survey arrows or painted outlines of doorways that have not yet been excavated.

## Conduct and local etiquette

The maintenance staff live by accumulated practical knowledge. Their habits may look superstitious—hitting machinery before startup, hanging boots upside down, replacing lamps in pairs—but each likely answers a recurring problem. Copy their procedure without improvising.

Do not disparage the animals as mere vermin. They are hazards, but they are also the dominant inhabitants, and the workers’ routines are built around coexistence. Likewise, do not collect fungi, insects, rust flakes, water, guano, or “souvenirs” from the raw face. Apart from local rules, carrying subterranean organisms between universes is an unacceptable biosecurity risk.

Photography should be cleared in advance. Avoid flashes near bat roosts, active machinery, and workers. Never step backward for a better angle.

## Leaving

Before returning to the surface or crossing universes:

1. Brush or rinse gross mud from boots at the designated station.
2. Remove contaminated outerwear without shaking it.
3. Bag disposable filters and gloves as facility waste.
4. Wash hands, forearms, face, and any exposed skin thoroughly.
5. Clean and disinfect boots, lights, and equipment.
6. Inspect bags and clothing for insects.
7. Report bites, scratches, splashes to the eyes or mouth, puncture wounds, falls, and bat contact.
8. Quarantine equipment according to multiversal biological-transfer rules.

Do not sleep in the Works unless assigned secure accommodation and supported by local staff. The three concrete houses may suggest a neighborhood, but they exist inside an operating machine room surrounded by water, heat, and wildlife.

The best way to experience the Warm Works is patiently: remain on the lit clearings, watch how every ledge and conduit has become habitat, listen for the distinction between repetitive machinery and actual change, and remember that the place is unfinished. The city, the water, and the animals are all still deciding what it will become.
