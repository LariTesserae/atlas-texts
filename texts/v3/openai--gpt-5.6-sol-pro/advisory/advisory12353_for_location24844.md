## Expedition assessment

Treat the Low Roaring Plain as a living crush zone, not as wilderness that can be crossed on foot. Its chief danger is not any single predator. It is the combined mass of countless animals responding to vibration, heat, rain, and one another. A human may be bitten, stripped at the joints, suffocated, carried into a migration groove, pushed into a sinkhole, or simply incorporated into the feeding cycle before the surrounding fauna recognizes them as unusual.

An ordinary traveler should visit only as part of a supported expedition. The safest experience would be from a sealed observation pod on a surveyed volcanic plate or escarpment ledge, with remote probes sent onto the plain. Do not plan an unsupported hike.

## Before arrival

First establish, by remote instrument:

- atmospheric composition, pressure, and oxygen content;
- mineral dust toxicity and particle size;
- airborne spores, mites, eggs, and corrosive compounds;
- the temperature and chemistry of rain, seepage, and animal fluids;
- local gravity and whether the underlying cavities can bear equipment;
- migration cycles around the arrival point;
- infrasound and ultrasound levels;
- the location of sinkholes, active gutters, breathing fields, Mirror Pans, cave mouths, and old migration grooves.

Do not infer safety from familiar-looking water, limestone, rain, or animal tissue. The plain’s biology may be chemically incompatible with human life even where it is not actively predatory.

Schedule arrival during a relatively dry, stable, iron-gray period. A hard rain immobilizes some flyers, but it also fills channels without warning and begins an ecological surge. Avoid known emergence periods at the escarpment caves. Darkness is not necessarily worse for the native fauna, but it is worse for you.

## Required equipment

### Personal protection

Wear a fully sealed, positive-pressure environmental suit with:

- independent breathing supply;
- redundant filtration and atmosphere monitoring;
- smooth, puncture-resistant outer armor;
- reinforced joints, gloves, neck ring, and boot seals;
- no exposed mesh, fabric folds, straps, or warm crevices;
- internal thermal regulation that prevents obvious external hot spots;
- electrical isolation and protection against acidic, saline, or sulfurous fluids;
- active hearing protection for audible noise;
- vibration and infrasound damping around the chest, skull, and feet;
- a full-face display using radar, lidar, thermal imaging, and inertial navigation.

The outer shell should be difficult to grip and easy to sterilize. Assume needle-legged organisms can enter openings too small to notice and that scavengers may investigate any seam resembling a joint or breathing aperture.

Ordinary earplugs are insufficient. Much of the plain’s sound is transmitted through stone, fluid, bone, and teeth. Travelers with cardiac, vestibular, seizure, panic, or inner-ear disorders should not descend onto the basin.

### Mobility and rescue

Each traveler should have:

- a harness connected to a powered overhead rescue winch;
- a rigid emergency cocoon or rapidly closing shell;
- short-range radar for detecting moving masses in darkness;
- a ground-vibration monitor;
- redundant navigation independent of visible landmarks;
- a locator tied to the dimensional return system;
- at least two remote scouts.

The winch platform must be armored against flying animals and capable of extracting a traveler vertically without dragging them through the crowd. A cable lying across the ground will become entangled, buried, bitten, or treated as part of a migration obstacle.

Do not rely on wheeled vehicles. They will crush fauna, clog with bodies, and provoke pressure waves. If surface movement is unavoidable, use a sealed vehicle with a smooth elevated hull, protected propulsion, and enough power to lift clear rather than force a path.

### Supplies

Bring all food, water, medicine, and sanitation capacity. Consume nothing local.

No pool is potable, including one that appears perfectly reflective or untouched. Mirror Pan water is especially suspect: its emptiness is a warning of mineral poison, not an invitation. Cold pools may also conceal pressure-sensitive organisms, eggs, or sudden inflows.

Carry all waste out. Blood, skin flakes, condensate, urine, food odors, and warmth may each function as ecological signals.

## Choosing a base

The best base is a sealed, raised pod placed after geological and migration surveys. Favor:

- a stable glassy volcanic plate;
- a sound basalt shelf above the normal body layer;
- an escarpment ledge well away from caves and drainage lines.

Avoid:

- cave mouths, even apparently empty ones;
- polished migration grooves;
- low channels and salt-rimmed pans;
- sinkhole margins;
- breathing fields;
- carcasses or warm moving mounds;
- any gap that the animals seem to be preserving.

Glassy plates provide little food and may offer a temporary break in terrestrial density, but flying creatures use them as resting sites. The pod therefore needs a hard outer skin, protected vents, and no external lights that resemble biological signaling.

Mirror Pans may create open ground, but do not camp within them until their metallic lining, vapor, and water chemistry are known. Native avoidance is often the most useful hazard marker available.

## Conduct on the plain

### Move as little as possible

Native animals read pressure, rhythm, and heat more reliably than shape. Walking announces you through the substrate. Running is worse.

If you must move:

1. Test the route with a drone.
2. Stay on solid, surveyed rock.
3. Avoid grooves, gutters, hollows, and packed streams of shellbacks.
4. Step slowly and irregularly rather than producing a repeated marching rhythm.
5. Keep the group close, but do not link yourselves with loose ropes.
6. Never step onto what merely appears to be stone; acres of pressers may lie flush against it.
7. Maintain immediate vertical extraction capability.

Do not follow temporary open lanes. Predators create order to feed. A sudden clear strip, tightening circle, or coordinated surge may be the working area of a hunter.

Do not stand in a crossing that other animals conspicuously leave open. It may be a route reserved for egg-bearing giants or another large migrator.

### Do not imitate the fauna casually

Avoid broadcasting calls, vibrations, heat patterns, or bioluminescent signals without extensive study. A signal interpreted as mating, distress, crowding, or feeding may summon animals from beyond visual range.

In particular, do not warm the ground for comfort. Some predators imitate the thermal pattern of communal resting grounds; other organisms may approach your equipment for exactly the same reason.

### Treat rain as an emergency condition

At the first sign of hard rain:

- leave channels and depressions immediately;
- retract probes from porous beds;
- seal the habitat;
- prepare for winged creatures to descend;
- expect animals and water to move simultaneously;
- do not shelter near sinkholes or under cave mouths.

Fine rain is less violent but still transfers fluids and small organisms from body to body. Do not allow collected rain from the suit exterior into the living compartment.

### Learn the important signals

The continuous roar is the normal state. Changes matter more than absolute volume.

- **A thickening roar:** a new migration may be entering the basin.
- **High pitches felt in the teeth:** airborne swarms are nearby; seal all openings and prepare for joint attacks.
- **Chest pressure before a low note:** a giant migrator’s call or a ground-transmitted event is approaching. Stop moving, engage damping, and monitor heart rhythm.
- **Pools trembling without nearby movement:** distant mass movement may be arriving through the substrate.
- **White flashes around a fallen animal:** blood has met air; feeders will converge. Withdraw immediately.
- **A broad synchronized rise and flattening:** you are near a breathing field. Leave without using flames or creating sparks.
- **Sudden silence:** abort the excursion. Silence means something has suppressed or frightened an entire local ecology. Do not wait to identify it.

Because buried cavities reflect tremors, never trust a single direction estimate. Use several separated sensors.

## If the fauna contacts you

Do not swat, kick, or spray broad-spectrum toxins. Violence may convert local curiosity into coordinated crowd motion, and chemicals could attract organisms that feed on injured tissue.

For a few small animals:

- remain still;
- keep the suit sealed;
- allow the smooth outer shell to shed them;
- use gentle suit vibration or directed gas pulses only if previously tested.

If organisms begin probing joints, gathering around warm areas, or forming a continuous layer, request immediate extraction. Do not wait for a puncture alarm.

If a herd begins to move through your position, do not try to run across it. Enter the emergency cocoon and lift vertically. If lifting is impossible, keep the shell closed and minimize movement. The greatest danger is being turned sideways, caught under a pressure wave, or pushed into a hollow.

If knocked into a migration groove or channel, assume flash water, larger bodies, and sediment-feeders will follow the same route. Extraction is urgent.

If suit integrity is lost, return directly to quarantine. Do not expose the wound to local water or air, and do not remove attached organisms in the open.

## Shelter discipline

Inside the observation pod:

- maintain positive pressure;
- use airlocks with exterior wash and sterilization cycles;
- inspect seals after every rain;
- isolate all samples in independent containment;
- keep exterior illumination dim, diffuse, and non-pulsing;
- damp machinery so it does not create repeated ground rhythms;
- mount heat exchangers above the surface and disperse warmth broadly.

Do not use open flames. Breathing fields contain moving geological gases, and the local atmosphere near the ground may collect combustible or asphyxiating pockets.

Sleep in shifts. A migration can transform the pressure around a structure without producing a visually obvious approach. Instruments should monitor load on the pod supports, not merely nearby motion.

## Biological and ethical precautions

The plain is crowded, but it is not expendable. A bootprint, sterilant spill, hot exhaust plume, or introduced microbe could affect immense numbers of organisms because bodies and fluids are transferred so quickly through the ecosystem.

Therefore:

- sterilize everything before entry and after exit;
- release no Earth microbes or organic waste;
- avoid killing specimens for convenience;
- do not take eggs, films, or tissue without strict containment;
- never introduce food as bait;
- do not block inherited routes;
- do not assume apparently communal behavior implies intelligence—or that it does not.

Sampling a carcass is particularly dangerous. “Dead” giants rapidly become living colonies with multiple trophic layers inside them.

## Best way to experience the Low Roaring Plain

Spend most of the visit inside a vibration-isolated observation pod. Watch the basin through non-biological sensors and brief shielded windows. Send sacrificial probes to a breathing field, the edge of a migration groove, and the perimeter of a Mirror Pan. If conditions remain stable, make one short, tethered excursion onto a surveyed volcanic plate without attempting to enter the animal flow.

The defining sensation will not be the darkness. It will be the absence of empty space: the recognition that every surface, noise, flicker, and apparent pathway belongs to a system already in motion. Prepare not to conquer or cross the plain, but to occupy the smallest possible place within it—and to leave before the plain is required to make room for you.
