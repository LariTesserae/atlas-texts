## Preparation philosophy

Treat the White Choir as a confined-space expedition into a permanently active acoustic environment—not as an abandoned building. Its apparent stillness is not safety. The chief hazards are:

- extreme continuous sound, including vibration transmitted through bone and chest;
- relentless illumination with no natural visual rest;
- severe confinement and difficult extraction;
- loss of orientation in repetitive, densely folded architecture;
- inability to communicate by voice or hear distress;
- respiratory exposure to fine ceramic dust;
- sensory overload, panic, derealization, and loss of time sense;
- uncertain effects when foreign objects, moisture, or living bodies remain inside too long.

An ordinary traveler should make the first visit very short. Reaching the central court is not a reasonable objective on an initial entry.

## Who should not enter

Do not enter if you have significant:

- claustrophobia or panic disorder;
- tinnitus, hearing damage, Ménière’s disease, or balance problems;
- migraine triggered by glare or continuous sound;
- epilepsy or serious sensory-processing sensitivity;
- heart rhythm problems, uncontrolled hypertension, or chest-pain history;
- respiratory disease aggravated by dry air or fine dust;
- neck, knee, hip, or spinal limitations that make prolonged stooping difficult;
- dental problems, loose fillings, or jaw pain—the high register may be conducted through the teeth.

You must be able to turn sideways, crouch, reverse through a constriction, and remain calm while unable to hear your own voice. Measure the entrance before committing. If you cannot retreat through it under your own power, do not enter.

## Clothing and equipment

Wear only close-fitting, snag-resistant equipment. There are too many fins, lattices, and narrow arches for a backpack, loose straps, jewelry, or dangling tools.

Recommended equipment:

- lint-free coveralls with hood;
- smooth gloves and clean, soft-soled boots;
- low-profile knee and elbow pads;
- a soft protective cap rather than a tall rigid helmet;
- a fitted particulate respirator rated for very fine ceramic dust;
- custom earplugs plus low-profile earmuffs, if the passage permits them;
- wraparound neutral-density or tinted eye protection;
- a fully opaque eye mask for brief visual rest while stationary;
- a mouthguard if you are prone to tooth or jaw discomfort;
- two independent clocks, worn where they can be read without removing gear;
- a small gas monitor for oxygen and carbon dioxide;
- a close-fitting rescue harness with no projecting buckles;
- a body camera with exposure locked for bright, low-contrast surroundings;
- a written route card and waterproof slate.

Do not expect hearing protection to make the Choir quiet. It may reduce the sharpest frequencies, but the low chord will still pass through the skull, ribs, and feet. Active noise cancellation should not be trusted; a system designed for ordinary sound may behave unpredictably in a fixed, body-conducted field.

There is no need for a headlamp. It will mostly create glare.

Hydrate and eat beforehand. Carry only a small emergency drink in a securely sealed container. Do not open liquids unless medically necessary. Do not pour water into any bowl, gutter, basin, or central depression. The place’s dryness appears structural rather than accidental.

## Team and communication

Never enter alone.

Keep at least one person outside the seam at all times, with another qualified rescuer available. Inside, travel in pairs only if the passages genuinely permit it. The interior pair should remain within arm’s reach and maintain regular physical contact. Two people standing close together can still feel acoustically isolated, which encourages accidental separation.

Agree on tactile signals before entry, for example:

- one squeeze: stop;
- two squeezes: continue;
- three squeezes: retreat immediately;
- repeated rapid taps: medical distress;
- firm pull toward the entrance: follow without delay.

Hand signals should be simple and large. Speech, whistles, alarms, and shouted warnings are effectively useless. Any electronic alarm must include a bright visual indicator and a strong tactile vibration.

A hardwired text unit may work better than radio speech, but it should be considered supplementary. The Choir’s packed construction may make radio propagation erratic, and its constant sound will overwhelm microphones.

## Navigation and the lifeline

Do not use footprints, dust marks, chalk, ribbons, or dropped objects as route markers. The place does not retain such changes reliably. A footprint that disappears is not evidence that you have returned to an untouched corridor.

Use a smooth, abrasion-resistant lifeline anchored outside the Choir. It should pay out from the traveler’s harness without loose loops. Nothing should be tied to an interior column, rib, lattice, or carving. The line is both your route home and your means of extraction.

However, test the threshold first. Feed in a short length of line, withdraw it, and confirm that the place does not displace, erase, trap, or alter it. If line behavior is abnormal, abort the visit. Do not improvise deeper inside.

Record navigation by:

- length of line paid out;
- sequence of left and right turns;
- number of constrictions crossed;
- changes in floor level;
- distinctive structural forms photographed from both directions.

Visual landmarks will be less useful than expected. The architecture is repetitive, equally lit, and deprived of strong shadows. A passage that appears unmistakable on entry may become nearly anonymous when approached from the other side.

Do not allow the lifeline to cross itself. If it does, retreat to the crossing and resolve it immediately.

## Exposure limits

The lack of rhythm, shifting light, drafts, or ordinary sound will damage your intuitive sense of elapsed time. Use strict clock-based limits.

For a first entry:

- five minutes at the threshold for sensory assessment;
- no more than ten to fifteen minutes inside;
- turn back at half the allowed time, regardless of apparent progress.

Later visits should be extended only after medical review. No one should sleep, meditate for prolonged periods, or remain to “adjust” to the chord. Familiarity may reduce distress without reducing physical exposure.

The outside observer should run an independent clock. Compare elapsed times immediately after exit. If interior and exterior clocks disagree significantly, suspend further entry.

## Conduct inside

Move slowly. Keep one hand free and use the other for light contact with your companion or lifeline. Do not run: low lintels and shallow level changes make head strikes and falls likely, while the Choir will conceal every audible warning.

Avoid touching surfaces unnecessarily. In particular:

- do not scrape inscriptions;
- do not take dust, tesserae, mortar, glass, or vegetation;
- do not insert probes into blind windows or sockets;
- do not break or bend the gray-green growths;
- do not eat, smoke, light flames, or expose liquid;
- do not shout to “test” the acoustics;
- do not strike pillars or resonant hollows;
- do not leave offerings or markers.

The Choir appears to restore its arrangement rather than preserve a visitor’s traces. That is not permission to experiment. A place that erases footprints may erase other disturbances by mechanisms you do not understand.

Take special care with bodily functions. Sweat, saliva, blood, and breath are all forms of moisture introduced into an environment where water appears inadmissible. Normal breathing is evidently possible, but any bleeding injury should trigger immediate withdrawal. Do not urinate, wash, or remove the respirator inside.

## Psychological precautions

Expect the interior to feel more oppressive than its measurements suggest. It is not merely crowded: it gives the impression that all available space has already been assigned. Visitors may experience:

- a sense of being physically superfluous;
- panic caused by the impossibility of silence;
- visual fatigue and color afterimages;
- the conviction that one has stopped moving despite continuing to walk;
- dissociation from companions who cannot be heard;
- compulsion to reach the central court;
- reluctance to leave because “nothing has happened yet.”

Treat these as environmental effects, not revelations. The Choir’s emotional force does not make an impulse trustworthy.

At regular intervals, stop and perform a mutual check: eye contact, hand squeeze, clock reading, line status, breathing, and orientation toward the exit. If either traveler cannot complete the check promptly, both retreat.

## The central court

If a later, properly supported expedition reaches the circular court, remain at its perimeter.

Do not:

- stand in the central white interval;
- lie in the worn depression;
- place a camera, offering, sample jar, or measuring instrument there;
- touch the inward-leaning ribs;
- disturb the equally spaced growths;
- remain to see whether the place responds.

The interval is the largest unfilled volume in the Choir and therefore its most structurally or symbolically exceptional feature. Its emptiness should be treated as an occupied condition, not as an invitation.

Nothing happening is not a reason to wait longer.

## Immediate abort conditions

Retreat at once if anyone develops:

- chest pressure beyond the ambient vibration;
- irregular heartbeat, breathlessness, dizziness, or nausea;
- sudden tooth, jaw, ear, or sinus pain;
- loss of balance or inability to judge floor height;
- visual dimming, flashing, color distortion, or confusion;
- panic, detachment, or inability to recognize the route card;
- unexpected sleepiness;
- bleeding or equipment failure;
- loss of contact with the lifeline or companion;
- abnormal clock behavior;
- any variation in the chord, light, temperature, dust, or vegetation.

Because the Choir is described by invariance, any genuine change is an emergency even if it seems benign.

If a companion collapses, do not attempt a prolonged treatment in a narrow passage. Secure them to the extraction point of the harness and retreat. Resuscitation should begin at the first space where it can be performed effectively, preferably outside the seam.

## After leaving

Exit into a quiet, dim room. Do not go immediately into traffic, machinery, crowds, or bright daylight. Temporary tinnitus, altered balance, auditory afterimages, headaches, and a lingering sensation of vibration are likely.

After every visit:

- compare interior and exterior elapsed times;
- perform hearing, balance, pulse, blood pressure, and oxygen checks;
- inspect teeth, eyes, and respiratory condition;
- bag equipment until it can be examined for ceramic dust or unfamiliar residue;
- decontaminate outside the Choir, never in the entrance seam;
- document memories immediately, before repetitive architecture blurs route order;
- observe travelers for delayed migraine, tinnitus, chest symptoms, insomnia, or derealization.

No material should be removed as a souvenir. If residue is found on clothing, treat it as controlled contamination rather than a sample.

Above all, enter with a predetermined reason and a predetermined turning time. The White Choir offers no natural conclusion to a visit. It will not finish a song, grow dark, become quiet, or signal that you have seen enough. The traveler must supply the ending.
