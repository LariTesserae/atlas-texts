## Overall assessment

This plateau is survivable for a prepared human, but it is not benign. Its main dangers are cumulative rather than dramatic:

- severe altitude and low oxygen;
- intense ultraviolet light and glare from above and below;
- continuous acoustic and infrasonic exposure;
- open shafts concealed by visually uniform stone;
- deceptive distances and cliff edges;
- cold, chemically unusual water;
- an ecosystem whose stability may depend on exact repetition;
- possible disorientation caused by resonance, sameness, and the apparent absence of ordinary change.

Treat the plateau less like wilderness and more like a vast living mechanism. You are entering something already in equilibrium. The safest traveler is quiet, self-contained, and leaves no enduring trace.

## Before crossing over

### Acclimatize elsewhere

Do not arrive directly from sea level. Spend several days at progressively higher elevations in a compatible world, ideally reaching at least 3,500–4,500 meters before transit. The plateau may be higher still, but prior acclimatization will reduce the risk of acute mountain sickness.

Have a baseline medical examination. Anyone with serious pulmonary disease, uncontrolled hypertension, inner-ear disorders, seizure susceptibility, or strong sensitivity to low-frequency sound should not go.

Carry medication and equipment for:

- acute mountain sickness;
- high-altitude cerebral or pulmonary edema;
- nausea and vertigo;
- severe headache;
- eye inflammation;
- panic or sensory overload.

Supplemental oxygen is not optional for an ordinary human. Bring a closed-loop or high-efficiency system with enough reserve to cover delays. Ordinary open-flow bottles may resonate, whistle, or empty unpredictably near the shafts, so use protected regulators and redundant pressure gauges.

### Establish exit criteria in advance

The plateau’s apparent permanence may encourage visitors to postpone decisions. Set hard limits before arrival:

- maximum surface time;
- minimum remaining oxygen;
- maximum tolerated headache, confusion, or balance impairment;
- acoustic exposure limits;
- a fixed turnaround time independent of how close a landmark appears.

A distant promontory that looks ten minutes away may be several hours distant.

## Essential equipment

### Breathing and altitude protection

Bring:

- supplemental oxygen with two independent delivery paths;
- pulse oximeter, preferably one shielded from intense light;
- pressure and atmospheric-composition sensors;
- compact emergency pressure shelter or pressure bag;
- altitude medications prescribed in advance;
- a partner capable of recognizing confusion before the affected traveler does.

Do not trust your own sense of well-being. The hard light, cool air, and constant sound may mask breathlessness and fatigue.

### Hearing and resonance protection

Ordinary earplugs will not be enough. Much of the mountain’s chord may reach the body through bone and chest cavities.

Use layered protection:

- custom-molded earplugs;
- high-attenuation earmuffs;
- an active cancellation system designed for stable low frequencies;
- a padded jaw and skull band to reduce bone-conducted vibration;
- a vibration-damping vest or pressure suit liner.

The active system must fail safely. Do not let it generate a phase-opposed tone powerful enough to interfere with animals or with local stone flakes.

Bring written and visual communication tools. Speech may be impossible near the central vents, and radios may transmit the roar more effectively than voices. Use:

- wrist displays;
- hand signals;
- tether-pull codes;
- short-range optical communicators;
- text-only radios with acoustic microphones disabled.

If your teeth begin aching, your peripheral vision shimmers, or you feel a vibration “inside” the chest rather than hear it, retreat immediately. These are not merely annoyances; they indicate significant resonant exposure.

### Eye and skin protection

The rim may deliver sunlight from both sky and cloud layer, with almost no usable shadow. Bring mountaineering-grade glacier goggles with:

- full ultraviolet and infrared blocking;
- side shields;
- very low visible-light transmission;
- anti-fog treatment;
- a second, differently filtered pair in case local radiance interacts badly with the first.

Wear a brim and face veil despite the lack of heat. Cover all skin with pale, tightly woven or UV-rated fabric. Apply sunscreen to exposed gaps, including under the nose and chin, where reflected light will strike.

Do not remove eye protection at the rim to “see the real color.” Snow blindness can begin before discomfort becomes severe.

### Footing and fall protection

The ground is stable in the geological sense, but perforated. Finger-wide vents may mark larger voids below, while apparently shallow fissures can descend beyond light.

Bring:

- stiff-soled boots with high-friction tread;
- a long, lightweight probing staff with pressure feedback;
- harnesses and dynamic tethers;
- wide load-distribution plates for crossing suspect crusts;
- compact anchors that grip without drilling;
- a rescue tripod or equivalent portable extraction frame;
- a downward-looking ground scanner.

Avoid crampons unless tests show they are necessary. Metal points may damage mineral crusts, slip on polished limestone, or couple the mountain’s vibration into your legs.

Never step across a dark fissure merely because it is narrow. Never stand on a mineral bridge without scanning it. Travel roped, but not so closely that one fall pulls everyone into the same shaft.

### Navigation and timekeeping

Bring inertial navigation and local beacons. Do not rely solely on visual triangulation: the air’s clarity destroys ordinary depth cues, and repeated shelves may appear identical.

Use at least three independent time sources. The plateau’s cycles are exact enough to undermine subjective time. A stationary winged creature or unchanged pool is not evidence that only moments have passed.

Do not use cairns, paint, score marks, flags, or discarded line to mark your route. Use retrievable electronic beacons or projected markers. Count every beacon out and every beacon back.

## Water, food, and camp

### Bring all water

Do not plan to drink from the pools or fissures. Their stable levels imply hidden replenishment, unusual physical regulation, or both. Water that remains apparently unchanged through rain and evaporation may not behave normally once removed. It may also contain mineral concentrations, microorganisms, or tiny glass-bodied animals not visible without magnification.

Avoid touching deep black water. A pool an arm’s reach across may connect to the mountain’s internal pressure system. Dropping an object into it could also disturb animals that feed on a precise schedule.

If sampling is authorized, take microscopic quantities in pressure-sealed containers and observe whether the pool level, local sound, or animal behavior changes. If anything responds, return the sample immediately if that can be done without contamination.

Carry compact, sealed food that produces no crumbs or scent plume. Eat away from pools, grazing paths, fissures, and predator stations. Pack out all bodily waste. Even trace nutrients could create a new patch of soil in a place where almost no soil exists.

### Choose camp cautiously

The safest camp is likely:

- well away from the rim;
- outside the central shaft field;
- on scanned, solid pavement;
- not beneath an overhang;
- not on a visible animal route;
- not beside a pool or condensation face;
- upwind or cross-gradient from any biological colony, even if the surface air seems still.

Use a low-profile, acoustically damped pressure tent secured by removable weighted pads rather than stakes. Stakes may puncture hidden cavities or alter the tone of a narrow vent.

Do not assume night offers relief. The chord continues, the stone glows, and the airborne creatures remain aloft. Sleep in shifts while monitoring oxygen saturation, cognition, and sound exposure. A dark sleep mask will be more useful than waiting for true darkness.

## Behavior around the plateau’s life

The animals appear to occupy fixed routes and intervals. Do not interpret their calmness as tameness. Their ecosystem may have no behavioral category for a human, and a small interruption could propagate through a tightly balanced schedule.

Observe before crossing any slab. If grazers traverse it periodically, wait through at least two cycles and cross only in the longest gap. Keep clear of familiar cracks where predators wait. Driving prey away from one crack may deprive a predator; driving them toward it may alter mortality that normally occurs elsewhere.

Do not:

- feed, touch, trap, or ride any animal;
- stand beneath the suspended winged creatures;
- launch drones into their tiers;
- imitate their calls or the mountain’s tones;
- move a tool-using animal’s chosen stone;
- collect “abandoned” flakes;
- block condensation from reaching drinking sites.

The cliff-winged creatures may be supported by pressure phenomena rather than ordinary aerodynamic lift. A drone, balloon, or loose fabric panel could be seized by an invisible vertical force even where the grass remains motionless.

If animals begin to stop at unfamiliar places, face you in unison, abandon a route, or gather outside their established pattern, withdraw along your entry path. In a world defined by repetition, novelty in animal behavior is an alarm.

## The shafts and central chord

Do not approach the central field on the first excursion. Establish how your body responds to the plateau’s background sound before moving into stronger resonance.

Near the shafts:

- stay clipped to a partner and a remote anchor;
- secure every loose item;
- use no open flame;
- avoid shouting into vents;
- do not lower probes unless they are non-shedding and fully retrievable;
- monitor gas composition continuously;
- limit exposure by timer, not by comfort.

The absence of felt wind does not mean the shafts are harmless. Air may be transported in a way that does not transfer ordinary momentum at the surface, or the sound may not be produced by moving air at all. Either possibility makes familiar cave-safety assumptions unreliable.

Never attempt to descend a shaft on an initial visit. Potential hazards include anoxia, toxic gas, pressure discontinuities, extreme resonance, sudden lateral openings, and loss of communication. The slow “pulse” felt through damp stone may indicate a deeper cycle not apparent at the surface.

Do not play tones back into the mountain. In particular, never broadcast the dominant chord in phase, out of phase, or at harmonic frequencies. A tone that has remained stable for geological ages should not be treated as an invitation to experiment.

## Score marks and stone tools

Regard the parallel cuts as inscriptions even if their maker and meaning are unknown. Do not add a line “for comparison,” trace them with hard instruments, take rubbings, or pour casting material into them.

Photograph them with oblique light and noncontact scanning. Record both the spacing of the marks and the local spectrum of the chord. If they match, preserve that observation rather than testing it physically.

Likewise, do not collect sharpened flakes. An animal’s act of returning a tool to the ground may be part of its use, not evidence of abandonment. The landscape may function as a communal tool repository in which position matters more than ownership.

If you accidentally move a flake, replace it exactly where and how you found it, using images if available. Do not sharpen one yourself. Sound-shaped edges may be unexpectedly keen, brittle, or resonantly active.

## Psychological precautions

The plateau may produce a distinctive cognitive strain: nothing visibly develops, while the sound never pauses. Travelers may experience:

- false impressions that time has stopped;
- compulsive counting of animal cycles or tones;
- a desire to “complete” score marks;
- reluctance to leave because nothing appears urgent;
- auditory afterimages during silence;
- derealization caused by extreme clarity and scale;
- irrational confidence that the environment is incapable of accident.

Use scheduled cognitive checks. Partners should ask one another simple orientation questions and short reasoning tasks. Record spoken or typed impressions, but do not treat mystical certainty as information.

After leaving, spend time in a quiet but not silent environment. Complete silence may make the absent chord seem louder. Persistent humming, tooth pain, tremor, vertigo, or visual quivering warrants medical evaluation.

## Contamination and departure

Use strict two-way quarantine. The plateau’s lichens, condensation organisms, and mineral-sealed water may spread slowly but survive almost anything. At the same time, ordinary terrestrial spores or skin microbes could introduce the first genuine ecological change the plateau has experienced in ages.

Before entry:

- sterilize outer clothing and equipment;
- seal food and waste systems;
- remove seeds, soil, pollen, and organic fibers from boots;
- avoid perfumes, insect repellents, and volatile chemicals unless enclosed.

On exit:

- recover every beacon, fiber, sample tag, and fastening;
- inspect boot treads for lichen, mineral crust, eggs, or glassy organisms;
- isolate all samples;
- compare equipment mass against the entry manifest;
- quarantine travelers and gear until biological and anomalous screening is complete.

If something cannot be retrieved safely, document its exact position and inform the next expedition. Do not create additional disturbance trying to conceal the first.

## Recommended first visit

For an ordinary traveler, the first visit should be short and conservative:

1. Arrive on a broad, pre-scanned shelf away from the rim and major shafts.
2. Remain stationary for fifteen to thirty minutes while checking oxygen, sound levels, and animal responses.
3. Establish a retrievable beacon and identify the exit point from several angles.
4. Walk only a small loop, avoiding pools and habitual animal paths.
5. Observe one condensation site and one grazing route from a distance.
6. Approach no closer to the central shaft field than the point where bodily vibration first increases.
7. View the cloud-filled abyss only from a tethered position and through full eye protection.
8. Leave well before exhaustion, darkness, or oxygen reserves force the decision.

The plateau does not reward conquest. Its deepest impression will come from standing still: the hard white light, the tiny durable lives, the motionless fliers, and the impossible roar emerging through unmoving air. Prepare to visit as a temporary irregularity in a place where everything else has found its exact position—and prepare to leave before the mountain’s unending note begins to feel like the only sound that has ever existed.
