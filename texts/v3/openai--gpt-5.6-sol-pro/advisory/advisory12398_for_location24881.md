## Overall recommendation

Visit only during the chamber’s driest phase, and make the first expedition observational. This is not merely a cave with unusual animals. Moisture is a local causal medium: near water, direction, sequence, reflection, and biological identity become negotiable.

An ordinary human can probably survive a short visit by remaining on dry clay, keeping away from pools and condensation, and preserving an immediate route back to an externally anchored exit. A wet-season visit would require specialized temporal rescue capability and is not advisable.

If reaching the chamber requires drilling through the plain, do not go. A borehole would introduce airflow, dust, microorganisms, drainage, and a permanent new water route into a very small sealed ecosystem. Use a non-invasive portal, phase transit, or equivalent means of arrival.

## Before entering

### Choose the right time

Arrange long-term remote observation before opening a passage. Enter only when:

- The ceiling has a sheen rather than active droplets.
- The pools have contracted into separate, stable “coins.”
- No new dark runnels are appearing on the walls.
- Transparent leaves are slack and silent.
- Surface rain is neither occurring nor forecast.
- Subsurface moisture is falling, not merely stable.

Surface weather is not sufficient. Water moves slowly through the clay, so rainfall may reach the chamber long after the plain appears dry. Install moisture and pressure sensors above and around the chamber without penetrating it.

Abort at the first multiplication of ceiling lights. Hundreds of new white points mean the dangerous phase has already begun.

### Establish an exit outside the chamber’s rules

The return gate must be anchored to dry space and an external clock. Do not let its position be calculated from the pools, local reflections, echoes, or visual landmarks.

Use:

- A physical safety tether leading directly to the exit.
- A powered retrieval harness or winch.
- An external operator who never enters.
- A coded visual confirmation showing that the gate opens onto the correct base and the correct time.

If the exit appears in a pool, appears in more than one place, or shows the chamber on its far side, do not use it until external control confirms it. Never follow a familiar person seen only in a reflection.

## Clothing and equipment

Wear a sealed, positive-pressure field suit. This protects the chamber from human microbes and protects the traveler from unfamiliar spores, fluids, and temporal contamination.

The suit should be:

- Matte, pale, and nonreflective.
- Non-shedding and easy to dry-decontaminate.
- Thermally regulated so condensation cannot form on its exterior.
- Fitted with broad, soft soles to distribute weight.
- Equipped with an anti-glare visor; the chamber may become painfully bright.
- Free of loose straps, exposed fibers, dripping coolant, or open liquid systems.

Keep drinking water and bodily waste doubly contained. In this chamber, a leaked mouthful of water is not just contamination; it may create a new pocket in which events occur out of order.

Bring:

- A full-body extraction harness.
- A smooth rescue sled, attached to the external winch.
- Redundant external navigation tethers.
- Humidity, temperature, and luminance sensors.
- A passive camera system with external timestamping.
- A dry first-aid package.
- A rigid, sealable over-container for contaminated equipment.
- Quiet haptic or visual communications.

Avoid acoustic ranging, hammering, pitons, chalk marks, chemical lights, adhesive trail markers, and anything that sheds grains. Do not rely on footprints: the floor closes them within minutes.

## How to move inside

Remain on broad areas of pale, visibly dry sediment. Move slowly enough to notice animals that initially resemble mineral marks.

Maintain generous clearance from:

- Every pool, however shallow.
- Luminous wet patches.
- Ceiling droplets, including droplets that have not visibly formed yet.
- Condensation lines.
- Overhangs occupied by frogs, silk cells, or rust-red growth.
- Grazers and their shed-scale rings.
- Mud arches, pebble towers, wasp rooms, and black-grain structures.

Do not step over the black-grain causeway simply because it seems unused. “Unused by visible animals in the present” is not the same as purposeless.

Do not touch the walls to steady yourself. Their edges are young and soft, and many apparently bare surfaces contain eggs, films, larvae, or future water routes. Keep one hand on your tether instead.

Travel in a very small party—ideally one suited observer inside, with two or more people outside. A larger group would produce too much heat, moisture, vibration, and ecological disturbance. If two people must enter, stay in line rather than spreading out, and never cross each other’s tethers.

## Light, shadows, and overhead predators

The chamber supplies its own illumination. Use additional light only if needed, and make it broad and diffuse. A narrow lamp may create a distinct shadow where the natural radiance would not.

This matters because at least one predator hunts shadows rather than bodies. A moth whose shadow is caught remains airborne briefly and then becomes too heavy to fly. Do not assume the effect would be harmless to a human.

Watch the ceiling for thin, many-jointed forms resembling cracks. Do not pass beneath one. If a traveler’s shadow is seized:

1. Do not struggle or run.
2. Call for immediate powered extraction.
3. Lie or settle onto the rescue sled before delayed heaviness begins.
4. Do not attempt to strike the predator or retrieve the shadow.
5. Remain sealed and undergo temporal-medical examination after exit.

Moths transfer the sound of their wingbeats into the stone. If the floor begins clicking beneath you, stop and look upward rather than assuming something is underfoot. Do not stamp, shout, or use sonic deterrents.

## Pools and reflections

Treat every pool as both habitat and open boundary.

Stay several body lengths away. Never:

- Step in one.
- Reach into one.
- Lean over one.
- Place a camera directly above one.
- Touch an animal that has entered or emerged from a reflection.
- Attempt to collect water.
- Block, cover, or drain a pool.
- Follow a reflected route that does not exist in the dry chamber.

The copper pool is especially hazardous because it depicts a probable later state of the cave. It is not a safe preview or observation window. Anything entering it may emerge genealogically, geographically, or temporally displaced.

If you see yourself in a pool when your angle should not permit it, withdraw without gesturing to the image. If the reflection acts first, abort the visit.

Directions near water cannot be trusted. “Sideways,” “down,” and “back toward the entrance” may refer to another version of the chamber. Trust only the tether leading to the externally verified exit.

## Temporal conduct

On dry clay, normal cause and effect is mostly reliable. Near moisture, treat sequence as uncertain.

A practical rule is:

> If an event is connected to water, do not assume you have seen its beginning merely because you have seen it happen.

Possible warning signs include:

- A splash before a droplet falls.
- An animal fleeing before you move.
- Fresh tracks belonging to no visible creature.
- Moss around a place that is still dry.
- Equipment appearing twice.
- A tether segment lying ahead before it has been paid out.
- A teammate answering a question before it is asked.

Do not “complete” unexplained events. If you find one of your tools on the floor while the original is still secured to your suit, leave both untouched and exit. Do not attempt to make the duplication logical by dropping the original later.

Likewise, do not take advantage of apparent foreknowledge. A pool showing a safe route does not guarantee that following it is what made the route safe.

If a future or alternate version of a team member appears, maintain distance. Do not exchange objects, touch suits, or permit both versions to use the same exit simultaneously. External temporal-control personnel should manage recovery.

## Wildlife etiquette

The chamber is crowded despite its apparent emptiness. Assume every pool edge, scale ring, silk seal, and patch of “bare” ceiling is occupied.

- Let grazers choose the route. They appear careful not to cross one another’s paths; do not force them to do so.
- Do not startle minnows into reflections.
- Do not touch transparent frogs, even if the visible heart appears displaced.
- Do not pass beneath moths or swat them.
- Do not dismantle springtail scale enclosures.
- Do not repair mud structures.
- Do not place black grains on the causeway.
- Do not fill the empty wasp rooms with leaves.

No feeding, sampling, tagging, or specimen collection should occur on an initial visit. These organisms may depend on relationships with their future descendants or past environmental states. Removing one individual could affect more than the present population.

## Rain emergency

Leave immediately if any of the following occurs:

- Ceiling droplets multiply rapidly.
- Pools begin extending toward one another.
- New wall streaks appear.
- Transparent leaves become taut or start ringing.
- Animals retreat into reflections or shelters in large numbers.
- Organisms appear that were absent moments before.
- The exit or tether acquires a reflection where no pool was present.

Do not wait for the pools to touch. Once separate times begin mixing, a rescue team may arrive before the accident, after the chamber has changed shape, or in a version of the chamber where the traveler never entered.

During withdrawal, stay on the tether and ignore apparently shorter routes. Do not stop to recover dropped equipment. If water crosses the planned path, allow the external winch to retrieve you rather than stepping through it.

## After returning

Do not shower immediately. Water may reactivate or extend any damp temporal contamination carried on the suit.

Instead:

1. Enter a dry isolation chamber.
2. Seal the entire suit inside a rigid containment shell.
3. Compare external and suit clocks.
4. Check body mass, shadow synchronization, memory continuity, and physiological rhythms.
5. Perform full imaging, including limbs, because internal anatomy may not remain where expected.
6. Review recordings only against externally generated timestamps.
7. Quarantine until specialists confirm that no organisms, duplicate objects, delayed injuries, or displaced events accompanied the traveler.

Nothing from the chamber should enter an ordinary biosphere, especially water, sediment, eggs, shed scales, or apparently empty silk and mud structures.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect the place to be more disorienting than frightening at first. Its scale is intimate, its life is small, and its brightness resembles frozen daylight. The danger is that almost everything looks delicate and approachable.

The silence may feel pressurized. Each falling drop seems to pull the room inward. You may repeatedly have the sensation that something moved just before you looked at it. This is not necessarily nerves. Many animals there survive by responding to events in advance.

Resist the desire to understand the chamber by touching it. Its inhabitants already know which portions of reality can be trusted. A human does not. The safest visit is brief, dry, quiet, and humble: enter, observe from the obedient floor, and leave before the water begins to shine more brightly.
