## First principle: do not enter exposed

An ordinary human cannot survive direct entry into the Green Throat.

The danger is not merely that the air is dry. The cleft actively removes water and incorporates its components into the mountain. Exposed eyes, lungs, mucous membranes, and skin would become reaction surfaces. Breathing harder would accelerate the injury, and drinking water would provide the Throat with more material rather than protect you.

Treat the entrance as the boundary of a chemically active vacuum chamber, a high-voltage installation, and a protected ecosystem simultaneously. If you cannot obtain a fully sealed, independently tested pressure suit designed for proton and moisture containment, visit only from outside.

## Before reaching the entrance

The summit environment may be more immediately familiar than the Throat, but it is still dangerous.

Prepare for:

- Severe altitude and hypoxia
- Cold, wind, and rapid weather changes
- Difficult evacuation over black gravel and salt
- Poor depth and distance perception in the colorless mountain light
- Electrical storms or static accumulation around the massif
- Carrying specialized equipment without relying on local water

Acclimatize elsewhere on the mountain rather than beside the opening. Establish a pressurized base shelter well below the summit and a smaller dry staging enclosure near the entrance. The staging enclosure should be anchored in exterior rock, not in the cleft itself.

No one should approach alone. However, only one traveler should enter at a time. A second person inside would obstruct retreat in the narrow chambers and expose both members of the team to the same failure. The rescue operator stays outside with independent oxygen, power, communications, and hauling capacity.

## The suit

The suit is the decisive piece of equipment. A conventional spacesuit is not automatically adequate.

It should have:

- A welded, hermetic pressure bladder with no exposed hydrated fabric
- An anhydrous ceramic or fluorocarbon exterior
- Redundant moisture barriers and continuous interstitial leak detection
- A closed breathing loop; nothing may be exhaled into the cleft
- Internal water and waste containment with at least two barriers
- A floating Faraday enclosure around the occupant
- No exposed metal that could bridge two growths, rails, cables, or machine housings
- No ordinary rubber seals, absorbent rope, leather, paper, wood, chalk, grease, or damp adhesives on the exterior
- Internal thermal storage sufficient for the entire excursion

Do not plan to reject body heat into the cave. The Throat appears thermally stable, and a warm suit may alter conductivity in nearby growths even without touching them. Store metabolic heat in an internal phase-change reservoir and leave before that reservoir is exhausted.

The suit’s outer shell must be extraordinarily smooth. Every wall is covered with brittle, sharp growth. Some rims can score glass; a casual brush could puncture a soft suit and destroy a structure that may have taken centuries to form.

Pressure-test the suit with warm, saturated air inside and an aggressive dry reactive environment outside. Test it for hours longer than the intended visit. A suit that merely “does not feel damp” is not sufficient.

## Do not walk or climb on the walls

There are effectively no ethical or dependable handholds. Every ledge, crack, and apparent patch of moss is part of the solid-state ecology. It may also be carrying current.

A normal caving descent would cause extensive damage and create electrical paths through:

- Pitons and bolts
- Metal carabiners
- Wet or moisture-absorbing rope
- Boots pressed against conductive growth
- Broken mineral blades falling into lower circuits

The preferred method is a suspended descent system anchored entirely outside the Throat. It should use a smooth, anhydrous, electrically nonbridging tether and a compact powered harness capable of controlled lateral positioning. Because the cleft is folded rather than straight, the route must be mapped in advance. If the tether would drag across walls or equipment, the route is not acceptable.

Do not install intermediate anchors. Do not cut vegetation to make a passage. Do not use rotor drones, gas jets, or fans: even slight airflow would introduce a new physical process into a space whose ecology depends on absolute stability.

Ideally, obtain a verified survey from an earlier expedition. Since the Throat no longer changes, an accurate map should remain accurate. Confirm only the entrance geometry with cold, low-power lidar before committing to descent.

## Electrical and machinery hazards

Assume that every green surface, rail, cable, cabinet, and exposed machine casing is energized relative to something else.

The fact that nothing moves or produces measurable heat does not make it safe. The entire cleft may be one distributed circuit, with the growth and machinery mutually maintaining the field. A visitor can become:

- A conductor between different potentials
- A capacitive load
- An antenna in the standing resonance
- A source of heat or ions
- A new route for charge that the ecology has never had to accommodate

Keep the suit electrically floating. Do not trail a conventional grounding cable. Do not measure resistance by touching probes to surfaces. Use only noncontact field sensors, optical instruments, and passive spectroscopy.

The cabinets marked **LOAD**, **FIELD**, and **HOLD** are observations, not invitations. Do not open them, press against them, attempt to move the meters, or force any valve or switch. “Stationary” here may represent an active condition rather than mechanical failure.

## The sound

Bring hearing protection, but do not expect it to work.

The industrial chord does not behave like ordinary transmitted sound. Earplugs and helmet insulation may reduce secondary sensations while doing little to the main phenomenon. Visitors may perceive it through their teeth, bones, vestibular system, or directly as a spatial condition.

Before the expedition, establish baseline tests for:

- Hearing
- Balance and eye movement
- Dental sensitivity and metallic restorations
- Heart rhythm
- Peripheral nerve function
- Migraine, seizure, and panic susceptibility

Anyone with uncontrolled epilepsy, severe tinnitus, vestibular disease, implanted stimulators, cochlear implants, or electrically sensitive medical devices should not enter.

Inside, speech may be difficult even if radios function. Use simple visual signals in the helmet display. Expect the unchanging roar to become psychologically oppressive: the brain searches for rhythm, direction, and variation, but finds none. This can cause time distortion, nausea, dread, dissociation, or the conviction that one has stopped moving despite continuing to descend.

Active noise cancellation is unlikely to cancel a condition that is not arriving as a normal wave. Do not turn it up indefinitely in an attempt to find a null.

## Navigation and timekeeping

Do not trust any single instrument inside.

The place contains a mechanically permanent “instant,” and instruments may couple to it in unexpected ways. A sensor returning perfectly stable data may be functioning correctly, frozen, saturated, or reading the mountain’s condition instead of yours.

Carry redundant systems based on different principles:

- Internal inertial navigation
- Optical rangefinding
- A physical tether-length encoder operated outside
- Independent electronic and mechanical elapsed-time indicators
- Continuous biometrics
- A hardwired optical communication channel
- An automatic retrieval command controlled from the exterior station

The outside team’s clock sets the turnaround time. If communication becomes repetitive, strangely exact, or silent, the operator retrieves the traveler immediately. Do not remain inside to diagnose communications.

Use augmented-reality route markers rather than paint, flags, chalk, or dropped objects. Nothing should be left in the cleft.

## Conduct inside

Move slowly, but do not linger.

Maintain clearance from all growths, walls, machines, rails, and cables. If the passage becomes narrower than the predetermined safety envelope, retreat. Do not improvise by squeezing through.

Never:

- Remove a specimen
- Break a blade to inspect its interior
- Place a lamp against the growth
- Breathe, spit, bleed, urinate, or vent coolant into the space
- Touch two surfaces at once
- Attach a sensor to a cable or root
- Throw anything down a shaft
- Attempt to start machinery
- Bring an open flame or chemical light
- Use water for cleaning, cooling, or emergencies

A broken growth is not merely botanical damage. It may be a wire, insulator, field shaper, carbon collector, or structural element in the entire ecology. Because nothing regrows into newly damaged space, a careless touch may produce a permanent wound.

## The lower pocket

The final chamber should be treated as the highest-risk area.

The rails, cables, roots, engine, and white lamp converge there. It is likely an electrical and resonant focus even though the noise is no louder. The fact that the tones become more distinguishable suggests that a human nervous system couples to the arrangement more coherently there.

Descend only far enough to observe the chamber while remaining suspended. Do not stand on the engine, roots, railway, or leaves. Do not touch the cylinder or examine the abrasion band by contact.

Most importantly, do not place an object—or your hand—inside the precise empty sphere around the white lamp. That void is the only space in the cleft that the growth has not occupied. It may be kept clear by heat, radiation, field geometry, resonance, or a function for which there is no human analogy. Filling it would be an experiment with the central equilibrium of the Throat.

Set a very short observation limit at the bottom. The coherent separation of the tones may feel revelatory, hypnotic, or personally meaningful. Treat any sudden certainty that you “understand” the machine as a neurological symptom and begin ascent.

## Emergency planning

The rescue system must not depend on an incapacitated traveler climbing.

Use:

- An exterior-powered retrieval winch
- A second independent lifting line
- Automatic ascent on loss of biometrics or communications
- A rigid neck and airway support within the suit
- Internal emergency oxygen isolated from the main loop
- A suit breach alarm that immediately commands retrieval
- A preplanned method for pulling an unconscious traveler through each constriction

A suit breach is not a repair-and-continue event. Do not press the damaged area against a wall. Seal it internally if possible and ascend at maximum safe speed.

The outside rescuer should not enter impulsively. Retrieval machinery is safer than creating a second casualty.

## After exit

Do not open the suit at the entrance.

The exterior may carry unusual salts, fixed oxygen compounds, or static charge. Move into the dry staging enclosure, isolate and discharge the shell under controlled conditions, then transfer the traveler into a pressurized medical compartment.

Post-visit evaluation should include:

- Blood gases and hydration status
- Skin and mucosal inspection
- Hearing and vestibular testing
- Electrocardiography
- Neurological examination
- Dental examination if tooth sensations persist
- Monitoring for delayed confusion, repeating auditory perceptions, or a continued sense of mechanical vibration

Rehydrate only after the traveler is safely isolated from the entrance environment. The suit exterior should be preserved dry for examination; washing it with water could trigger further reactions.

## What the visit will feel like

The most unsettling feature may not be the danger or the noise. It will be your own motion.

Everything around you has arrived at a final relation with everything else. The leaves do not sway. The machines do not advance through their cycles. The sound does not come toward you or pass away. There is no dust, drip, breath, insect, or settling grain by which to measure time.

You will be the only wet, warm, changing object in a kilometer of completed structure.

That makes restraint more than an environmental courtesy. Enter as an observer under suspension, carry nothing loose, touch nothing, and leave no trace. If your equipment cannot guarantee that, the proper way to visit the Green Throat is to stand outside its doorway, watch the green darkness below, and go no farther.
