## Overall recommendation

Treat the Hanging Catchment as a **short-duration, no-camp, no-contact field site**. Its ordinary hazards—heat stress, slick basalt, razor-edged rubble, flash runoff, rockfall, and difficult evacuation—are serious enough before accounting for the pillars, pavilion, and apparent conservation of living presences.

Visit in a party of **two or three people**, enter after morning condensation has begun to clear, and leave well before dusk or approaching storms. Establish the actual camp and return anchor **outside the basin**, preferably below the split ridge but not beneath a rockfall chute.

Do not enter alone, bring animals, introduce plants or cultures, or plan to spend the night.

## Before traveling

### Physical preparation

Be ready for:

- High-altitude exertion in hot, nearly saturated air
- Poor evaporative cooling despite modest walking distances
- Scrambling over loose, sharp volcanic rock
- Sudden ten-minute downpours and rapidly rerouted runoff
- An awkward casualty evacuation through a person-width passage

Acclimatize to the elevation and practice moving on wet talus. Anyone with unstable cardiac or respiratory disease, severe heat intolerance, balance problems, or a history of altitude illness should remain outside the catchment.

Ensure tetanus vaccination is current. Even minor basalt cuts may be deep and contaminated by mineral-rich water.

### Biological cleanliness

Arrive as biologically clean as practical:

- Scrub soil and seeds from boots, packs, ropes, and tripods.
- Do not bring potted plants, pets, live bait, untreated wooden equipment, or microbial sampling cultures.
- Carry all food sealed.
- Use clean clothing that has not recently been through seed-bearing vegetation.
- Pack out all waste, including food crumbs, wash water, and human waste.

The basin may count living presences in ways humans do not understand. Avoid testing whether seedlings, spores, gut flora, injured organisms, or pregnancies affect that accounting.

### Establish a baseline

Before entry, record:

- Exact number and identities of travelers
- Time of entry
- Nine-pillar count, if all are visible
- Visible palms, open flowers, and any circling fauna
- Weather, pressure, and major active springs
- Appearance of the pavilion’s underside

This is not merely scientific bookkeeping. If the number of people, major plants, or animals appears to change in a correlated way, withdraw without attempting to “balance” the system yourself.

## Essential equipment

Bring equipment that remains useful when sound, water, and landmarks become unreliable:

- Climbing helmet
- High-traction approach boots with soles suited to wet rock
- Cut-resistant gloves
- Ankle gaiters and durable trousers
- Clear eye protection
- Lightweight rain shell with generous ventilation
- Sun protection
- Broad-spectrum respiratory mask for flower pollen, mineral mist, or vent emissions
- Imported drinking water in several small, sealed containers
- Oral rehydration salts
- Wet-bulb or heat-stress monitor
- Compact pulse oximeter
- First-aid supplies emphasizing lacerations, fractures, burns, and heat illness
- Short rope, harnesses, slings, and edge protection for the entry passage
- Two independent navigation and timing devices
- Text-capable radios or hard-coded signal devices
- Bright, numbered inert markers, retrieved on departure
- Headlamps even for a day visit
- Emergency bivouac gear for use **outside** the basin
- A secured multiverse return beacon kept away from pillars and pavilion

Avoid exposed electrical contacts and unnecessary conductive poles. The charged pollen and faintly electrical atmosphere may interfere with electronics. Keep at least one navigation record on waterproof paper.

Do not depend on drones. Their pressure wakes, electrical fields, or movement may provoke the hydrological network, and recovery from a cliff or vertical pool could be impossible.

## Water discipline

Assume all native water is physically and causally unsafe.

Do not:

- Drink it, even after filtration or boiling
- Wash wounds with it
- Fill bottles from springs
- Taste dew or flower vapor
- Stand beneath upward-moving droplets
- Touch a vertical pool
- Collect mist released by the basin
- Pour imported water onto the ground

The main concern is not conventional contamination. Water here may be reassigned rather than transported, and drinking it may establish an unwanted relationship between your body and the landscape.

Carry more imported water than the heat and humidity appear to require. Sweat will not cool you effectively, so dehydration and overheating can occur together. Keep containers capped except while drinking, and account for them individually. If a sealed bottle begins sweating on its inner surface, changes mass, develops bubbles while still, or appears connected to condensation elsewhere, isolate it and take it out unopened.

## Entry and movement

Enter through the split ridge one at a time, with spacing sufficient to avoid knocking rocks onto the next person. Do not linger in the cleft; it is both a choke point and a likely site of fresh fracturing.

Inside:

1. Pause near the entrance and listen for underground rushing, stonefall, or changing runoff.
2. Designate one person as route lead and one as environmental observer.
3. Move from stable bare rock to stable bare rock where possible.
4. Step over moss cushions, root hollows, translucent sacs, and silica lattices.
5. Never assume a wet patch is shallow or supported.
6. Do not walk in active channels, even if they are only finger-wide.
7. Keep clear of the northern wall and all fresh pale seams.
8. Avoid standing below overhangs, weeping faces, or palm crowns during storms.
9. Maintain visual contact with companions.

The basin is small enough that speed is unnecessary. A straight crossing is not worth the risk.

Use hand signals or text whenever possible. If people at opposite sides hear displaced voices, stop speaking aloud, regroup visually, and verify everyone’s location. Do not follow a familiar voice into fog, behind a ridge, or toward a cliff without visual confirmation.

## The pillars

Maintain several meters of clearance from every pillar. Do not touch, lean on, tie equipment to, strike, sample, shade, heat, or deliberately attend to one for long periods.

The pillars appear responsive to pressure, vegetation, attention, and possibly social arrangement. Therefore:

- Do not form circles around them.
- Do not stand deliberately opposite one another across the basin.
- Do not perform synchronized experiments.
- Do not sleep near them.
- Do not place water, stones, or living material at their bases.
- Do not try to obstruct an upward-running droplet.
- Do not stand between two pillars when droplets are hanging motionless there.

If a pillar bends, changes texture, begins exchanging voices, or produces suspended water, withdraw along your established route. Do not wait to see the completed effect.

The pillar in the seasonal pool should be regarded as completely inaccessible.

## The pavilion

Observe the pavilion only from outside the polished platform. Do not step onto the glass, crawl beneath the roof, reach into the night image, place objects in its shadow, or use it for shelter.

The roof may be visually compelling, especially when its night sky coexists with the evening sky. Set a strict viewing limit and have a companion monitor anyone looking at it. Stop observation immediately if:

- The depicted stars seem to respond to movement
- Your reflection or shadow is absent or delayed
- The platform appears deeper than its physical surface
- The displayed moon changes when no one else is looking
- Timepieces disagree
- A person reports cold, vertigo, lost time, or a sense of being beneath rather than beside the roof

Do not leave offerings or markers. Objects placed there may remain deceptively fresh and then disappear.

## Plants and animals

Do not touch or collect any organism.

The white flowers deserve particular caution. If one opens:

- Move upwind or crosswind.
- Put on eye protection and a respiratory mask.
- Keep away from the visible pollen spiral.
- Do not interrupt its path or place instruments between vegetated islands.
- Avoid the released cool vapor.

Electrostatically bound pollen may adhere strongly to clothing and electronics. Inspect and clean equipment outside the catchment.

Black lizards should be allowed to cross. Because their skin mimics rock texture, check any handhold before using it and inspect warm equipment before packing.

Do not lift moss. The translucent sacs and silica lattices beneath it appear foundational to the basin’s soil and may be easily destroyed. A single careless boot can undo years of stabilization and redirect later floods.

If the long-winged animal appears, do not track it with spotlights, lasers, or drones. Its absence while humans are present may itself be part of the living-presence exchange.

## Weather and geological warning signs

Leave immediately if any of the following occurs:

- Thunder becomes a felt pressure rather than merely a distant sound
- Clouds begin entering the basin faster than they dissolve
- Multiple new springs appear at once
- Every wet surface releases mist
- A mist column approaches the exit
- Hairline cracks propagate visibly or audibly
- Warm vapor begins venting along your return route
- The northern wall sheds more than isolated pebbles
- Water hangs across the split ridge
- The pavilion’s night image brightens suddenly during daylight
- A companion becomes confused, stops sweating, staggers, or develops severe headache

During rain, do not shelter in the narrow ridge passage, beneath basalt overhangs, beside pillars, or under the pavilion. Move to a preselected stable high point away from channels and cliff faces, crouch low, and minimize contact with isolated tall objects. After rain, wait for the first channel shifts to settle before moving.

## Time limits and heat management

Morning is the safest operating window, though surfaces will be wet. Keep the visit brief—ideally two to four hours—and impose rest periods based on measured heat stress rather than how far you have walked.

Because sweat evaporates poorly:

- Drink imported water regularly.
- Use damp cooling cloths only with imported water and pack them out sealed.
- Monitor each other for slowed speech, irritability, clumsiness, and flushed or pale skin.
- Do not interpret the flower’s cool vapor or cold spring water as safe cooling resources.

Leave before the two skies coexist. Dusk brings reduced contrast on black rock, increased fascination with the pavilion, and possible appearance of the aerial animal.

## If something anomalous happens

### If water appears on a person or inside sealed equipment

Do not wipe it onto the ground. Blot it with an inert absorbent pad, seal the pad in a rigid container, and withdraw. If water forms in the airway, eyes, or beneath clothing continuously, treat it as a medical emergency and exit at once.

### If stones or gear become rearranged

Do not restore the arrangement. Photograph it, count all personnel and essential equipment, and leave. Attempting to “correct” the basin may be interpreted as another input.

### If a person seems missing

First establish visual head count and check whether voices are being displaced. Do not send individuals in different directions. Regroup at the last mutually confirmed location, using text and visual signals. If a person is genuinely absent, withdraw to the entry control point and conduct any search as a single roped group.

### If a new flower or spring appears in a footprint

Do not step on it, sample it, or wait for it to mature. Mark the location from a distance and leave by a different nearby foothold without creating a wide detour through vegetation.

### If someone is injured

Use only imported water and ordinary medical supplies. Stabilize, then evacuate promptly. Do not lay the casualty on the pavilion platform or against a pillar, however convenient they appear. If a stretcher is required, keep the party together; do not leave one person alone as a “living marker.”

## Departure

Before exiting:

- Conduct a full head count.
- Account for all tools, containers, markers, and waste.
- Photograph the site without approaching changed features.
- Note any new flower, missing animal, collapsed shrub, altered pillar, or water feature.
- Leave by the original ridge passage unless it has become structurally unsafe.
- Do not take stones, water, flowers, silica, fallen leaves, or “clean” dust.

After departure, remain at the external camp long enough to check for delayed effects. Compare timepieces and memories independently. Inspect clothes for pollen, seeds, lizards, translucent tissue, unexplained moisture, and mineral crusts. Quarantine equipment rather than washing it into the local watershed.

Finally, count the returning travelers again after the multiverse transit. In a place where occupancy may be conserved rather than merely observed, “everyone appears present” should not be accepted without names, memories, biometrics, and confirmation from the return anchor.

The safest attitude is neither fear nor curiosity without restraint. The catchment is not overtly hostile, but it treats water, location, attention, and life as negotiable quantities. Visit as a temporary pressure on an active system: lightly, briefly, and without asking it to demonstrate what rules it uses to make room for you.
