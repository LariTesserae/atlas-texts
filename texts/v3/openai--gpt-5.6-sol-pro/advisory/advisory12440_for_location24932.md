## Before departure

Treat the city as an active construction process rather than a finished destination. Its greatest hazards are not violence or conventional weather, but unstable geography, incomplete architecture, localized changes in physical law, and poor agreement about when and where things exist.

Arrange the following in advance:

- **Enter through a certified transit well or staffed arrival facility.** Do not materialize on the open plain. New crust may be thin, fissured, or only conditionally load-bearing.
- **Book lodging in a fully realized building.** Ask specifically whether the room, its stairwell, its plumbing, and both emergency exits are presently instantiated in the same street-version.
- **Hire a local continuity guide** for at least the first several days. A normal city guide is insufficient; you want someone licensed in spatial coincidence, infrastructure phase, and temporary river procedures.
- **Register your personal timeline and sensory profile.** Authorities need to know which version of the boulevard you occupy if transit records and visible pedestrians disagree.
- **Obtain extraction coverage** that includes geological revision, architectural incompletion, temporal displacement, property loss by ecological transfer, and routes erased by reservoir migration.
- **Send your itinerary in stages rather than as a single fixed plan.** Some local organisms consume stored directions, and the city itself may begin arranging for a declared destination before you intend to go there.

If possible, schedule your visit during a relatively stable atmospheric fold. Avoid the first days after warm rain, when fissures are most likely to open, and the early phase of a predicted river unless the temporary waterfront is your specific purpose.

## What to bring

Pack lightly, but with redundancy.

### Clothing

Wear:

- Breathable layers suitable for persistent warmth and sudden wetting.
- A light waterproof outer shell.
- High-ankled boots with puncture-resistant soles; the mineral glass is sharp, and fresh fractures can be almost invisible.
- Gloves for railings and stone. Surfaces may be warm, wet, chemically active, or engaged in water collection.
- A broad hood or soft hat rather than an umbrella. Rain columns are narrow and may be municipal infrastructure; blocking or redirecting one is both dangerous and deeply impolite.
- Clear or amber-tinted eye protection. The gray-green light is dim, but luminous weather boundaries and reflective walls can produce disorienting glare.
- A small active cooling garment or neck wrap. The weak light makes visitors underestimate heat stress.

Do not dress entirely in one distinctive color. Some local fauna can remove color as a transferable property. Clothing with redundant patterns, reflective identity patches, and a neutral underlayer makes such an encounter easier to document and less alarming.

### Navigation and continuity equipment

Carry:

- A city-approved **continuity beacon** worn against the body.
- A mechanical watch alongside any networked clock.
- A printed card bearing your name, origin, lodging, guide contact, medical needs, and timeline registration.
- Two maps: one current municipal phase map and one simple paper street diagram.
- Chalk or removable physical markers, if permitted. Do not rely only on digital route histories.
- A compact inertial compass. Magnetic north, sunlight, shadows, and network positioning may each be misleading in different ways.
- A body camera set to short, locally stored segments rather than continuous cloud navigation. Excessive recorded computation can attract molds or scavenging vines in neglected areas.
- A small hand mirror for checking yourself, **not** for interpreting the upright forecast mirrors outside the city.

Do not use a long safety tether except under a guide’s direction. A tether can cross versions of a corridor, catch on a migrating balcony, or encourage you to follow a route after it has ceased to be safe.

### Health and supplies

Bring:

- All medication for twice the expected stay.
- Sealed drinking water for your first day.
- Electrolytes and a personal hydration monitor.
- Familiar packaged food.
- Broad-spectrum skin barrier cream and ordinary sun protection; weak visible light does not guarantee low ultraviolet or thermal exposure.
- A compact air-quality sensor.
- A filtration mask or emergency rebreather for dust from assembling structures.
- Sterile dressings appropriate for narrow cuts from mineral glass.
- A whistle and vibration alarm. The whistle may not carry, but close-range rescue sensors can detect it.
- A rigid sitting pad. Benches may be warm because of absent occupants, thermal systems, or partial overlap; do not assume warmth means harmlessness.

Do not bring seeds, untreated soil, live pets, or self-replicating machines. Imported earth is a controlled resource, and local gardens may interpret biological material as either instructions or competition.

## Arrival

On arrival, pause before stepping away from the marked platform. Allow your guide or the station system to confirm:

1. The ground is load-bearing.
2. Your visible station matches the station’s current transit record.
3. The local gravity interpretation is within ordinary human tolerance.
4. Your lodging district is receiving water and has not been dimmed unexpectedly.
5. No temporary river lies across your planned route.

The city may initially look empty. Do not interpret this as abandonment. Crowds can occupy neighboring versions of the same public space, and a seemingly vacant crossing may have substantial traffic. Use marked pedestrian lanes and wait for official crossing signals even if no vehicles or people are visible.

Speak quietly. The city absorbs sound, so shouting rarely carries far and can make you miss the subtler cues that matter: pressure changes from trains, leaf movement above transit lines, or the vibration that precedes structural adjustment.

## Moving through the city

### Streets and causeways

Stay on verified routes. Broad vacant fields are not necessarily parks or shortcuts. They may be:

- Reserved construction conditions.
- Future rooms not yet enclosed.
- Wetlands temporarily withdrawn from the visible street.
- Thin young crust over warm fractures.
- Transit corridors occupied in a version you cannot see.

The slender causeways are generally safer than they look, provided they are open. Do not test railings by leaning hard on them. Some architectural surfaces become reflective only after you pass; looking back may be more disorienting than looking ahead.

If your shadow divides or points in several directions, that is usually atmospheric rather than pathological. If **your body** appears to occupy several slightly different positions, stop moving and activate your continuity beacon.

### Trains and elevators

Elevated trains are nearly silent. Expect ear pressure, trembling leaves, or a vibration through the soles shortly before arrival. Never step across a rail line because you “cannot hear anything coming.”

Use only elevators displaying a complete destination. Elevators moving through open air may serve floors visible only from inside the cabin. This is routine locally, but a visitor should not select:

- An unnumbered floor.
- A floor described only in future tense.
- A destination that appears after the doors close.
- A level whose return condition is unspecified.

Avoid temporary stairs leading toward absent rooms. They are not viewing platforms. A staircase can become structurally complete before the room at its top becomes breathable, accessible, or contemporaneous.

### Distances

Widely spaced towers make journeys longer than they appear. Carry water, and do not plan several districts as if they were adjoining neighborhoods. A station visible across an open field may be kilometers away or may belong to a noncoincident street layer.

Build generous pauses into the day. Navigation fatigue is serious here because the city offers few ordinary confirmations: weak shadows, minimal sound, sparse crowds, migrating landmarks, and roads that may serve predicted rather than present waterways.

## Water and weather

The city’s water is precisely allocated. Never assume falling water is free to collect.

- Do not step into a narrow vertical rain column unless a pedestrian indicator permits it.
- Never wash, fill bottles, or shelter equipment under a cistern-filling column.
- Do not block condensation channels in walls, handrails, balconies, or window skins.
- Avoid sleeping outdoors against architecture; the building may open collection grooves before dawn.
- Treat exposed natural water as nonpotable, including reservoir water and temporary rivers.

Before dawn, surfaces become slick with condensation. Boots should have good traction, and electronics should be sealed. This is one of the loveliest times to walk with a guide: the city quietly trickles, flowers open, and dark walls bead with silver. It is also when stairs, causeways, and black mineral shelves are hardest to see.

Water disappears quickly after warmth returns. Begin drinking before you feel thirsty. Visitors commonly become dehydrated because the atmosphere looks damp and the light does not feel hot.

### Temporary rivers

If a river is predicted, expect routes to change rapidly. Bridges may unfold from what looked like ordinary banks, markets may appear within hours, and greenways may become crowded despite the city’s usual emptiness.

Enjoy the waterfront only from designated areas. The water is warm, the channel is freshly cut, and its banks may collapse cleanly rather than crumbling as an older riverbank would. Do not swim. Do not follow amber fish into side pools. Leave before the announced atmospheric shift; the river can diminish faster than a visitor expects, stranding boats and exposing unstable mud.

A surviving ribbon of vegetation may remain after the water disappears. It can become a pleasant walking route, but it is not automatically public pavement. Confirm that it is not still hydrologically active elsewhere in the city’s timing.

## Architecture and altered weight

Buildings with large suspended masses are not necessarily unsafe, but avoid lingering beneath them during calibration, foundation adjustment, or atmospheric transitions. Local gravity modification may be stable for the structure while producing nausea, vertigo, or unusual balance in an unadapted person.

Warning signs include:

- A sudden sensation that your luggage is pulling sideways.
- Dust falling upward or collecting in vertical sheets.
- Clothing hanging at an angle despite still air.
- Water crawling along a wall without a channel.
- The horizon appearing level while your inner ear insists otherwise.

If this occurs, sit or kneel rather than trying to run. Release wheeled luggage. Follow embedded floor lights to the nearest ordinary-weight refuge.

Do not enter a building that is visibly assembling from dust or slurry, even if its doors work. “Complete enough to admit a person” is not the same as complete enough to preserve one.

## Plants and animals

The ecology is not conventionally hostile, but it does not share familiar assumptions about feeding.

Do not touch, feed, pick, or collect anything without local advice. Organisms may consume:

- Heat gradients.
- Chemical traces.
- Computation.
- Stored directions.
- Color.
- Delayed decisions.
- Distortions produced by equipment.

The black-winged creatures near condensation should be left alone. Their transparency is likely part of feeding, not illness. Keep hands out of runoff where they gather.

Give long-legged grazers a wide route through the fern corridors. Tracks that end suddenly do not mean the animal flew away; they may mark a transition you cannot safely duplicate.

Do not handle amber-spined fish or interfere with observation platforms. They are protected civic instruments as much as animals. Engineers may be recording their movements during a power instability. Flash photography around reservoirs is likely to be prohibited.

Keep drones cased. Vines may digest their route memory even if they leave the machinery otherwise intact. If a drone becomes entangled, report it rather than pulling it free.

Most importantly, make small decisions promptly in neglected indoor spaces. If you are uncertain whether to enter a room, either enter under guidance or leave. Do not stand for long periods repeatedly postponing the choice. Mold associated with deferred decisions is a recognized maintenance problem, and ordinary cleaning procedures may not remove what it takes from you.

## The forecast mirrors

The upright mirrors beyond the central districts are among the city’s most compelling sights. Visit them only with a guide.

They do not show ordinary reflections. Do not use them to check your appearance, navigate, predict personal events, or identify a “better” version of the city. The municipal description—erosion forecasts—is the safest working interpretation whether or not it is complete.

Observe from the marked line. Do not touch the surface or walk behind a mirror unless the site is officially open. Do not leave an offering casually: offerings may constitute a request, declaration, or acceptance under local custom. If you wish to participate, ask a resident what is appropriate. Water, soil, names, and clocks are likely to carry meanings a visitor should not improvise.

If one of the mirrors shows you, especially if the depicted terrain contains no nearby city, step away without testing whether the image copies your movements.

## Social conduct

Residents may be quiet, hard to keep in sight, or only partly coincident with you. Be patient.

- Greet people at a normal conversational volume.
- Do not pursue someone who fades from view.
- Do not occupy a warm bench until its availability indicator clears.
- Leave space in queues even when you cannot see the people represented by the queue system.
- Ask before photographing apparently empty plazas.
- Refer to buildings as incomplete or noncoincident, not unreal.
- Treat infrastructure anomalies as practical matters rather than demanding that residents explain them as miracles.

It is courteous to acknowledge engineers tending the reservoirs, gardeners monitoring root paths, and staff managing condensation channels. These are central civic professions.

The city’s inhabitants may answer questions with times, conditions, or district states rather than directions. “After the western membrane folds” can be more useful locally than “at six o’clock.” Ask your guide to translate such answers into safe visitor instructions.

## The daily calibration tone

Near dusk, remain either:

- Fully indoors in a certified structure, or
- Outdoors in an approved calibration area.

The tone cannot normally be heard indoors, but outdoors it passes through the ground. Cups tremble, pools ring, and flowers orient toward the center. Most visitors experience it as pressure through the bones rather than sound.

During the tone:

- Stop walking.
- Keep both feet on stable ground.
- Do not stand on a bridge, temporary stair, balcony in motion, or fissure edge.
- Let foundation and continuity systems finish adjusting before resuming travel.

If you hear the tone clearly while indoors, leave only if the building directs you to do so. Otherwise contact maintenance; it may mean that the room is not as enclosed as it appears.

## Emergency rules

Memorize these:

1. **If a fissure opens:** move perpendicular to it, not along it. Do not look down for long; warm darkness gives poor depth cues.
2. **If companions become difficult to see:** stop the group. Do not spread out searching. Activate continuity beacons and speak names one at a time.
3. **If your route vanishes:** return only along the last physically verified segment. Do not follow digital arrows across open ground.
4. **If a building begins assembling around you:** move toward the nearest marked void corridor, not necessarily the visible door.
5. **If a reservoir or wetland appears to migrate:** yield completely. Do not attempt to cross the damp boundary ahead of it.
6. **If you lose color, directional memory, or a personal property that cannot be physical:** seek an ecological clinic, not a police station.
7. **If the ground tone begins unexpectedly:** kneel and wait.
8. **If you encounter your own recent tracks ending sharply:** do not step into their final position.
9. **If a forecast mirror changes to show the immediate landscape exactly:** leave the mirror field under guidance.
10. **If lost, remain near maintained infrastructure.** Handrails, condensation channels, reservoir lights, and transit pylons are monitored even when no people are visible.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect less spectacle than sustained uncanniness. The city is quiet enough that ordinary urban instincts fail. You may walk beneath immense structures and hear only leaves. You may wait alone at a station used by thousands. A rain shaft may fill a cistern across the street while dust remains dry at your feet. At dusk, flowers will turn toward a note you feel through your knees.

The most rewarding visit is slow. Spend time in a condensation garden before dawn. Watch engineers consult the amber fish. Ride an approved elevated line through fern corridors. If a temporary river comes, visit its market and return days later to walk the green path left in its channel.

Do not try to solve the city. Its residents do not. Prepare instead to move carefully through a place where geography is young, infrastructure is ancient, and completion is something the landscape is still deciding to become.
