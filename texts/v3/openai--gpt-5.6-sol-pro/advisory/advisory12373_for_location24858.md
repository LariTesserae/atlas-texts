## General assessment

Do not prepare for this as a mountain excursion or a visit to an abandoned installation. Prepare as though entering an active laboratory whose apparatus includes the weather, geology, animals, and local geometry.

An ordinary human should not enter alone. Use a team of at least three:

- two travelers entering together;
- one observer remaining outside the young ridges, anchored on older, demonstrably stable ground;
- ideally, a fourth person maintaining quarantine and rescue equipment farther downslope.

The visit should be brief, noninvasive, and planned around retreat rather than exploration. No one should sleep inside the hollow.

At present, the new downhill-running groove is a serious warning. It may mean that one of the site’s previously provisional systems has become permanent—or that the landscape-making mechanism has entered a new phase. Observe at least several cloud cycles remotely before entering. If the groove lengthens, branches, reaches the cliff, or persists through a central rearrangement, postpone the visit entirely.

## Before departure

### Establish the cloud-cycle count

The ninth cloud cycle is the most predictable large-scale hazard. Do not enter unless you know where you are in the sequence.

Observe from outside for long enough to identify:

- what constitutes one complete cycle;
- whether cycles are regular;
- how the vapor behaves immediately before spatial enlargement;
- whether clocks remain synchronized during the enlarged interval.

If the current count cannot be established with confidence, assume the next cycle is the ninth.

Plan to leave at least two cycles before the expected expansion. The hollow may become larger internally while your external support team remains only a short distance away; ropes, radio signals, and visual bearings may then cease to correspond to physical distance.

### Set identity and duplication procedures

Because living creatures are occasionally duplicated, every traveler should undergo a pre-entry identity record:

- photographs from all sides;
- fingerprints and medical baseline;
- a short private autobiographical statement;
- a randomly generated phrase memorized immediately before entry;
- synchronized body cameras and physiological logs.

If a person is duplicated, do not treat either version as counterfeit. Both are persons, both may sincerely remember arriving, and neither should be allowed to approach the central stone in an attempt to “reverse” the event. Separate them calmly, evacuate both, and place both under medical and ontological observation.

Agree on this protocol beforehand. Improvising while two frightened people argue over which one is original is dangerous.

### Use strict biological quarantine

The site contains spores, mites, aquatic organisms, and life cycles adapted to unfinished geology. Equally, microbes from an ordinary traveler could become the first soil-forming community in the hollow.

Before entry:

- clean and sterilize boots, clothing, tools, and harnesses;
- use clothing without seeds, soil, leather, untreated wood, or natural-fiber stuffing;
- seal food and bodily waste;
- cover cuts;
- arrange full decontamination after exit.

Take no biological specimens unless you have a dedicated containment facility. Do not collect liverwort, tree membrane, mineral foam, mites, crabs, skimmers, or pool water as souvenirs.

## Clothing and personal equipment

Everything should function while warm, wet, and mineral-contaminated. The local rule that nothing remains entirely dry should be taken literally. Do not depend on dry matches, paper maps, absorbent insulation, or supposedly sealed pockets.

Wear:

- a climbing helmet with sealed eye protection;
- a warm-humidity suit that vents heat but resists spores and mineral grit;
- gloves with good wet-rock grip;
- high-friction boots with puncture-resistant soles;
- a particulate respirator, preferably with interchangeable chemical cartridges;
- a compact emergency oxygen supply;
- a quick-release climbing harness.

Electronics should be waterproofed and conformally coated. Carry redundant mechanical instruments where possible, but expect condensation inside sealed housings.

Monitor:

- oxygen and carbon dioxide;
- sulfurous or otherwise reactive gases from the cracks;
- temperature at boot level;
- vibration in the ground;
- the frequency of the needle-wing insects’ chord.

The insect pitch is one of the best available predator alarms. Use active hearing protection that suppresses the painful overall volume while displaying major pitch changes through a wrist vibration or visual indicator. Ordinary earplugs alone may prevent you from hearing the one warning the ecosystem reliably provides.

Carry several broad, diffuse lights rather than one powerful spotlight. A narrow beam creates sharp patches of shadow, potentially giving a fold more routes of approach. Helmet, shoulder, and waist lights should overlap so that the ground around each traveler is illuminated from several directions.

Avoid loose black fabric. In fog, a companion may mistake it for a fold.

## Approach and navigation

Set the main safety anchor outside the new ridges, in older rock. Do not trust bolts driven into the hollow itself. Cracks seal, stone softens, and mineral growth may either eject an anchor or engulf it.

Run a bright, floating or low-friction guide line to the entrance, with:

- quick releases at every harness;
- enough slack for ordinary movement;
- no loops near the arches;
- no section passing across the central stone;
- an external tender who can release the entire line if geometry begins to lengthen it dangerously.

Do not use scratches, cairns, paint marks, or inserted stakes as primary navigation. Scars heal, pebbles assemble themselves, and the surface is continually revised. Use the stable approach direction, the external line, compass readings, and redundant inertial tracking.

Never trust apparent distance in the cloud. Probe the next step with a nonconductive pole, especially near the cliff. A surface that looks like a wall may be a ledge ten paces away; empty vapor may conceal the abrupt drop.

Establish a cliff exclusion zone wider than seems necessary. No observation is important enough to justify approaching the lip.

## Conduct inside the hollow

### Do not alter the count of seven trees

Do not touch the white trunks, black membranes, or bronze tags. Do not stand where a gust could carry a detached membrane into your clothing. Do not move a tag “for study.”

The rainfall rule is tied to the number of trees, not necessarily to whether a tree is alive, upright, or native to its present position. Disturbing one may produce an immediate weather response. Bringing in an eighth tree-like object is equally inadvisable.

### Avoid the pool and all newly running water

The pool is not potable. Its shoreline moves, its floor may be setting beneath the skimmers, and its drainage pores can change without warning. Do not wade through it or place equipment in it.

The new groove deserves an additional exclusion margin. Do not dam it, sample from it, step across it repeatedly, or attempt to “help” it establish a channel. Running downhill for the first time may be a critical transition in the experiment. Let it proceed without human weight or contamination.

### Stay away from fresh cracks and mineral foam

Freshly opened cracks exhale heat and mineral vapor, then close rapidly. A boot, finger, rope, or instrument inserted into one may be trapped when it seals.

Mineral foam is food for the grazers. Walking through it may attract them, provoke competition, or put you in the path of a descending herd. It may also still be chemically or thermally active.

### Do not camp or lie down

A scar persists when an animal sleeps in it. A sleeping human may therefore stabilize some accidental deformation of the ground—or be interpreted by the local rules as an animal maintaining a niche.

Folds also sleep beneath the arches. The hollow has no safe shelter in the conventional sense.

### Use no lethal force

Avoid bringing firearms. Loud discharge may trigger a herd response, and killing an organism near the central stone risks converting it into intrusive geology. Even away from the center, a carcass may become an enduring ecological instruction in a place where niches are still being assigned.

Use light, retreat, spacing, and barriers as defenses. The aim is to leave before an encounter becomes physical.

## The arches

Maintain a generous no-entry zone around all five arches, including the ground directly beneath them and the space behind their apparent openings.

They are not portals suitable for human travel. Their transformations show that they do not preserve continuity of matter:

- water becomes mist;
- stones acquire divergent forms or histories;
- insects return altered;
- whole passage is explicitly impossible.

Do not reach through, throw probes through, fly drones through, or lean across the plane of an opening. A hand might not return as the same hand—or as one hand.

Do not leave equipment beneath an arch. “A second history” may mean physical alteration, contradictory records, duplicate ownership, contamination from another era, or a device that has always contained information you never recorded. Any object accidentally exposed beneath an arch should be isolated after recovery and not relied upon for navigation, identity verification, or mission records.

Special cautions:

- **The older ridge:** Do not assume it is a safe future exit. It may be a sampled age rather than a destination.
- **The black sea:** Treat emitted light and air as potentially hazardous.
- **The flooded hollow:** Do not infer that this is the site’s inevitable future.
- **The pale brightness:** Use an opaque visor or turn away. Its effect on animals suggests radiation, cognition, or both.
- **The view of your own back:** Do not approach to test whether it is delayed, predictive, or observing you from elsewhere. Have a companion watch both you and the arch. If the image moves differently from you, retreat without turning your back on nearby shadows.

Never shelter beneath an arch, even in heavy rain.

## The central mechanism

The three petals and circular unfinished stone should be given the widest practical exclusion zone. Do not stand on the central patch, touch the petals, place instruments in their seams, or step between them.

Watch for the petals opening by a hair’s breadth. The sequence appears to be:

1. the possibility of sound disappears;
2. the central stone rises and rearranges;
3. the surrounding landscape may change;
4. living creatures may be duplicated;
5. sound returns as a single impact.

At the first visible movement:

- stop walking;
- move off visibly soft or newly veined ground if this can be done immediately;
- crouch low with knees loose;
- face away from the central stone;
- close your mouth without clenching your jaw;
- brace against the returning concussion;
- keep physical sight of your companion.

Do not interpret the silent interval as equipment failure. Shouting and radio checks will waste attention. Use prearranged hand signs and haptic signals.

After sound returns, recount the team before moving. Check not only for duplicated people but also duplicated packs, ropes, tools, or animals. Inspect the route again: the pool, ridge height, cracks, and safe footing may all have changed.

If anyone is injured, move them away from direct contact with the central stone before their condition becomes critical. Death on the stone has a known geological consequence and may make ordinary recovery impossible.

## Animal hazards

### Folds

Folds are the principal active threat. Their movement depends on shadows rather than traversing ordinary distance.

Warning signs include:

- a change in the needle-wings’ metallic pitch;
- grazers freezing;
- crabs overturning their water bowls;
- a general pause in animal activity.

When this occurs:

1. do not run blindly;
2. activate all diffuse lights;
3. move toward evenly illuminated open stone;
4. avoid arches, overhangs, trees, ridges, and companions’ sharp shadows;
5. stay close enough to maintain overlapping light, but not so close that bodies create a single dark area;
6. retreat toward the established exit.

Do not strike a motionless strip of wet-looking material to see whether it is alive. Do not step over one. A fold resting beneath an arch may be difficult to distinguish from shadow or discarded cloth.

### Grazers and mites

Give grazers the downhill or ridgeward route they have already chosen. Never stand between a group and mineral foam.

The red mites’ large-animal shape is likely a threat display. Do not fire at it or flee toward the cliff. Hold position, increase distance slowly, and watch the actual grazers’ feet rather than the airborne silhouette.

Do not touch a grazer, even if duplicated and confused. Its skin may be hot, chemically active, or dependent on its mites.

### Crabs, skimmers, and insects

Do not handle the crabs’ water bowls. An overturned bowl is a danger signal, not litter.

If the skimmers leap in synchronized sheets, move away from the pool and watch the stone beneath it. Their behavior appears to announce that the substrate is setting or changing phase.

Needle-wings should be left undisturbed. Their chord is unpleasant but useful. Insect repellent may interfere with them and erase an important early-warning system.

## If the ninth cycle begins

The signs may include:

- vapor thickening until ridges disappear;
- routes taking longer than their measured length;
- arches seeming to recede;
- echoes arriving from nonexistent slopes;
- heavy herd movement in the fog.

If caught inside:

- do not chase the apparent exit;
- do not follow unseen herds;
- do not approach calls, bells, voices, or lights from the extra slopes;
- stay with your partner;
- use the guide line only while it remains slack and physically sensible;
- disconnect if it begins pulling toward the cliff or central stone;
- find evenly illuminated open ground away from arches and the center;
- wait for the hollow to contract.

The enlarged terrain may be real while it lasts. Treat unseen herds as physically capable of trampling you. Listen for mass movement through the ground and move laterally, not directly toward or away from the sound.

When the cloud lifts, do not assume everyone and everything returned to the same version of the hollow. Recount people, trees, arches, packs, and major landmarks before departure.

## Exit and quarantine

On leaving:

- photograph the hollow from the same external position used before entry;
- compare the tree count and arrangement;
- record the new groove’s length and flow;
- isolate any object that passed near or beneath an arch;
- bag outer clothing and filters;
- inspect for mites, spores, mineral films, duplicated objects, and unexplained wear;
- compare both travelers’ recordings before allowing either device onto an ordinary network.

Travelers should undergo medical observation for respiratory irritation, burns, hearing damage, anomalous mineral deposits, memory discrepancies, and unexplained duplicate possessions.

Do not discard contaminated wash water into a normal ecosystem.

## The most important rule

Go as a witness, not as a pioneer.

Do not plant anything, name the ridges by carving them, improve the drainage, straighten an arch, rescue a membrane, feed an animal, or take a piece of newborn stone home. The hollow is already struggling to determine what is permanent. An ordinary human body, bootprint, dropped meal, or night’s sleep could become one of the rules it decides to keep.
