Treat this as an orbital expedition that happens to include walking, not as a desert trek. The ground is real, but neither “ground,” “distance,” nor “down” can be trusted for long.

## Before traveling

Do not visit alone. An ordinary human should go only with:

- a guide or autonomous system experienced in sequence-based navigation;
- a vehicle capable of both surface travel and controlled free-space maneuvering;
- independent extraction capability rather than reliance on a distant rescue team;
- a current forecast of fragment motion, field cycles, dust streams, and storm-state indicators.

Choose a large, comparatively stable fragment for the first visit. Stay well inland. A plain near the center of a continental slab is vastly safer than a picturesque margin, small asteroid-like shard, active seam, tower, or ring.

Plan the itinerary by **events**, not coordinates. A useful route instruction might be: “Depart when the black ridge begins eclipsing the ring; cross the abrasion terrace while the dust is rising; return before the western fragment rolls edge-on.” Compass bearings and straight-line distances may become meaningless.

Establish conservative abort conditions. If an expected landmark duplicates, a shadow moves independently of its source, loose grains begin hovering, or the horizon rotates faster than forecast, return to shelter immediately.

## Personal equipment

The atmosphere is not breathable and is too thin to provide reliable thermal protection or sound transmission. Wear a full pressure suit with:

- closed-cycle oxygen and carbon-dioxide removal;
- active heating and cooling;
- radiation and charged-particle shielding;
- a visor rated for ultraviolet exposure and sudden bright particle events;
- electrostatic discharge protection;
- dust-resistant joints and redundant seals;
- at least two independent radio systems;
- haptic alarms, since acoustic warnings may be inaudible;
- an inertial tracker, gravimeter, dosimeter, and electric-field sensor.

Do not plan to obtain water locally. Hydroxyl chemically bound in minerals is not an emergency water supply without substantial processing equipment.

Carry more life support than the nominal trip requires. Routes can lengthen or cease to connect while you are outside. A one-hour excursion should be provisioned as though it might become an overnight wait.

The suit should include a compact maneuvering unit. This is not for ambitious gap crossings; it is for surviving a temporary loss or reversal of local gravity. Use low-output, finely controllable thrusters, as charged dust and nearby fields may complicate aggressive propulsion.

## Tethers and movement

Remain tethered almost continuously. Use a system with:

- two independent lines;
- self-reeling slack control;
- multiple attachment methods;
- force-limiting sections so a shifting slab does not tear you apart;
- quick release in case an anchor begins moving with a separate piece of terrain.

Never trust loose stones as anchors. A boulder may weigh normally, become nearly weightless, or begin responding to another mass. Prefer tested bedrock points, distributed surface pads, or anchors certified for local field conditions.

Walk slowly with a broad stance. Test every apparent slope before committing your weight. The visual horizon is not a dependable indicator of level, and gravity can diverge from the apparent surface normal. A low ridge may be the rounded edge of a cliff descending into open space.

Stay away from margins unless the visit specifically requires them. The most dangerous edge may not look dramatic: rounded terrain and clear light make enormous drops deceptively legible and therefore deceptively harmless.

Use lidar, radar, and active rangefinding rather than visual estimates. Neighboring continents will look like reachable plateaus even when the intervening distance is thousands of kilometers.

If pebbles rise around your boots, do not jump, run, or make sudden tether changes. Lower your center of mass, clip into the nearest tested point, and wait for a field reading. A few centimeters of levitation can precede a complete change in down.

## Shelter and camp placement

A safe field shelter should be a sealed, radiation-shielded capsule able to function in vacuum and tolerate being tilted, lifted, or briefly detached from the surface. Everyone and everything inside should have restraints.

Anchor it with several flexible, widely separated attachment points rather than one rigid foundation. The slabs can flex. An excessively rigid camp may be crushed or torn open while the surrounding rock bends.

Place camp:

- well away from cliffs and fragment margins;
- outside visible dust-stream alignments;
- away from violet glass seams until their behavior is understood;
- off gravel plains that may levitate collectively;
- beyond the influence zone of towers, rings, lone doorways, and curved cracks;
- near solid, homogeneous bedrock rather than layered or flaky terrain.

Keep the vehicle ready to seal and depart. At least one person or autonomous system should remain on watch, monitoring field gradients and the geometry of neighboring fragments.

## Navigation

Bring overlapping systems:

- inertial navigation;
- star tracking;
- local gravimetry;
- electric and magnetic field mapping;
- radar profiles of nearby fragments;
- physical and radio beacons;
- synchronized recordings of landmark sequences.

Do not rely on GPS-like triangulation unless it was designed specifically for this world. Variable effective distances can make a perfectly functioning triangulation system produce dangerously plausible nonsense.

Mark routes with both physical beacons and recorded event conditions. A beacon visible across a gap may no longer be reachable from your side. Never step through a “shortcut” merely because camp can still be seen.

Before every return journey, recalculate the route from current conditions. Do not assume that retracing your footsteps retraces the same path through space.

## High storm-state protocol

Learn the early signs:

- widespread pebble lift;
- rapid changes in apparent gravity;
- duplicated towers or landmarks;
- tightening, brightening dust braids;
- plants sealing themselves;
- disappearance of gliding organisms from edges;
- simultaneous migration of several major fragments;
- horizon movement unrelated to ordinary rotation.

At the first cluster of these signs:

1. End all surface work.
2. Do not attempt to cross a gap.
3. Return to the sealed shelter or vehicle.
4. Secure people in padded restraints.
5. Reel in external equipment unless doing so would require exposure.
6. Isolate electrical systems from dust-induced charge.
7. Maintain passive tracking of the host slab rather than trying to hold a fixed astronomical orientation.
8. Wait for local gravity and neighboring horizons to stabilize through a full observation cycle.

A storm is not the moment to launch unless the shelter itself is failing. Open space may appear clear while its reachable geometry is changing.

If caught outside, anchor to the largest coherent bedrock mass available, use several compliant lines, close all suit shielding, and avoid standing upright. Do not chase dropped equipment. Anything that begins drifting may already be following a different gravitational path.

## Dust and surface hazards

The metallic dust is electrically active, abrasive, and potentially toxic. Keep it out of seals, radiators, lungs, electronics, and sample compartments. Use electrostatic shedding surfaces and contactless cleaning rather than brushes, which may drive grains farther into joints.

Dust rivers moving through open space are not wind. Do not interpret their direction as a sign of safe motion. They are tracers of field conditions and may indicate exactly where not to travel.

The softened terrain should not be mistaken for harmless terrain. Rounded edges can still cut a suit under pressure, and old-looking surfaces may conceal active electrical or thermal seams.

Because newly exposed objects rapidly acquire an ancient appearance, inspect equipment by diagnostics rather than sight. A tool may look millennia old while remaining functional. Conversely, “it still looks the same” is not evidence that it has not been stressed.

## Structures and artifacts

Maintain a generous exclusion zone around towers, rings, lone doorways, socket alignments, and regions where cracks bend unnaturally.

Do not:

- enter a ring;
- touch a tower;
- place equipment inside shallow sockets;
- pass through a standing doorway;
- cross a boundary that dust refuses to cross;
- camp where your reflection behaves incorrectly or is absent;
- use an artifact’s gravity effect as a recreational shortcut.

These mechanisms appear to influence local geometry and may be part of whatever prevents catastrophic collapse. Interfering with one is dangerous on a scale much larger than the traveler.

If a doorway rotates the horizon or makes a vertical wall walkable, remain where you are unless your expedition has a tested protocol. The effect may end while you are standing on what becomes a cliff.

## Life and contamination

The native organisms are sparse, slow-growing, and exceptionally vulnerable to careless contact. A bootprint through a black mat could destroy growth older than human civilization.

Use strict planetary-protection procedures:

- sterilize suits, tools, and vehicles before arrival;
- never discharge water, organic waste, or untreated air;
- avoid black mats and metal-rooted stalks by several meters;
- do not shelter under warm stones that may contain dormant crawlers;
- do not handle empty glider skins without containment;
- collect samples only under explicit scientific authorization.

Treat the opening of a stalk’s crown or the sudden flattening of nearby organisms as environmental data. Native life may detect an approaching fragment or field change before your instruments interpret it.

## Human factors

The silence can be more disorienting than the scale. Large upheavals may produce no audible warning, while tiny vibrations arrive through the boots and ribs. Train to respond to instruments and haptic alerts rather than waiting to hear danger.

Expect severe vestibular confusion. Shadows may move, the ground may tilt, and gravity may shift independently. Motion sickness medication, balance training, and procedures for crawling while visually disoriented are advisable.

Avoid staring over edges or tracking rotating continents for long periods while walking. Stop, anchor, and observe. The eye continually underestimates the emptiness.

Maintain frequent radio check-ins even when standing within sight of companions. Speech through the local atmosphere will not carry reliably, and apparent proximity may be misleading.

## What to bring home

The safest souvenirs are images, field measurements, and remote spectroscopy. Do not pocket dust, stones, biological films, or artifact fragments. Material that appears inert locally may carry charge, alter its weight under different fields, or introduce unknown chemistry or life to another world.

Above all, remain humble about the terrain. This place is not actively hostile in the familiar sense. It simply does not preserve the assumptions that make ordinary travel possible. A successful visitor moves slowly, stays tethered, trusts instruments over appearances, and leaves before the sky begins to rearrange itself.
