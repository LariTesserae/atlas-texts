## Preparing for the High Roar

The High Roar should be treated less as a mountain country than as a vast, active weather mechanism. Its stone may be ancient and stable, but its air is powerful enough to erase paths, reverse waterfalls, and turn harmless objects into missiles. An ordinary traveler can visit, but only with local guidance, disciplined hearing protection, and a willingness to stop whenever the country changes its voice.

Do not go alone.

### Before crossing into the country

Spend several days acclimatizing in the lower approaches. Even the valleys are at high elevation, and the combination of thin air, warm humidity, violent wind, and continuous low-frequency vibration is exhausting. Anyone with serious vertigo, uncontrolled asthma, pressure-sensitive ear problems, or a heart condition should reconsider the journey.

Train beforehand in:

- high-altitude walking;
- climbing while attached to fixed handlines;
- moving on wet stone without relying on loose footholds;
- lip-reading and simple hand signals;
- emergency anchoring with redundant lines;
- recognizing hypoxia, hearing injury, and vibration-induced disorientation.

Arrange for a guide from the first inhabited plateau. Maps age badly here. A route shown as open may have been dismantled, stripped of vegetation, or rendered impassable by a change in wind. Local route knowledge is expressed partly through tuned reeds and remembered chords, not landmarks alone.

Plan in intervals rather than destinations. “Reach the eastern shelter in three days” is a poor plan. “Cross the hanging orchard during its calm morning period, then wait beneath the black ledge until the western throat changes pitch” is closer to how safe travel works.

## Essential equipment

### Hearing and pressure protection

Carry several forms of hearing protection:

- molded wax-and-fiber plugs;
- modern high-attenuation earplugs;
- over-ear defenders that fit beneath a helmet;
- spare sets kept dry in separate containers.

Double protection is appropriate near screaming pillars and major stone throats. However, ear protection cannot stop the lowest tones, which pass through the chest, jaw, and bones. Limit exposure even if your ears do not hurt.

Continuous vibration may cause nausea, irritability, false sensations of movement, headache, panic, and poor judgment. Schedule regular recovery periods inside sound-dampened shelters. If you begin seeing the ground “breathe,” feeling steps that are not occurring, or losing coordination, stop traveling.

Do not depend on spoken communication. Establish touch signals before departure—for stop, danger, secure line, storm, injury, and silence. Carry a writing slate, but tether it.

### Clothing

Wear close-fitting layers without loose straps or flapping fabric. Everything that can catch wind eventually will.

Bring:

- a ventilated climbing helmet with secure chin strap;
- eye protection with side shields;
- a thin face covering against mineral mist, insects, and airborne seeds;
- windproof outer layers;
- warm insulation for wet, exposed nights;
- gloves suitable for rope, wet vegetation, and abrasive stone;
- soft-soled approach boots with excellent grip;
- gaiters or tightly closed cuffs to keep out seeds and small crawlers.

Avoid ponchos, broad hats, umbrellas, and rigid rain capes. In the High Roar they become sails.

### Rope and anchoring gear

Use dynamic lines and short personal tethers rather than long, taut spans. Carry:

- a climbing harness worn whenever crossing exposed terrain;
- two independent tethers;
- dynamic rope;
- slings and soft anchors;
- locking connectors;
- compact ascenders and a descender;
- a knife accessible with either hand.

Never assume that an existing vine rail can hold your weight. Test it from shelter, one person at a time. Local plant-fiber structures are intentionally replaceable; some may be seasonal markers rather than load-bearing equipment.

The pale stone beneath overhangs is often friable. Do not place anchors merely because the surface looks protected. Use established handholds or have a local choose dark, competent stone.

### Shelter

A conventional rigid tent is a liability. Poles snap, panels drum, and broad surfaces catch turbulence. Use a low, flexible bivouac that can be lashed directly against sheltered stone. Bring acoustic padding—felt, folded cloth, fiber mats, or inflatable panels—to reduce transmitted vibration during sleep.

Camp only in recognized niches. A pleasant cave may be a pressure vent, and a dry basin may become a temporary waterfall minutes after rain falls on an unseen plateau.

Tether every object, including boots, cooking vessels, packs, and waste bags. During an atmospheric reversal, even heavy tools can lift.

## Water and food

Carry more water than the greenery suggests you need. Visible waterfalls are unreliable, plateau pools may be mineralized, and hidden rivers can be impossible to reach safely.

Treat all collected water. Use filtration followed by chemical or ultraviolet disinfection if available. Brown water beneath fern mats may be drinkable after treatment, but avoid pools with metallic films, strong bitterness, unusual warmth, or deposits around their rims. Cavern condensation is often the safest source if locals use it.

Do not enter cracks in pursuit of the sound of underground water. The cool breath from such openings may reverse abruptly.

Bring dense, low-bulk food that can be eaten cold. Open flames are dangerous near woven moss, bark cloth, dry pith, and rope grass. Ask before lighting any fire in or near a shelter. Secure food firmly; hanging it from a cliff is not protection in a country where seeds and occasionally animals travel upward.

Do not eat local fruits without instruction. Some hanging fruits are safe only before their shells begin splitting, while others contain irritating fibers designed to scatter in updrafts.

## Movement through the landscape

Travel early enough to reach shelter well before sunset. The evening lull is beautiful but deceptive: it is a transition, not a promise of calm. Night winds enter the mountains shortly afterward.

Keep a low center of gravity on open shelves. Cross exposed sections one at a time so that a gust cannot pull several people from the same line. When a gust arrives, do not run. Face partly into it, crouch, maintain three points of contact, and wait.

Be especially cautious around:

- western apertures, where wind enters the hollow mountains;
- pit mouths, which may expel warm fog and debris;
- long arches, where infrasound can impair balance;
- cliff-edge vegetation, which may conceal empty air;
- hanging vines, which can whip across large arcs;
- fresh rain tracks, which may suddenly become waterfalls;
- stone that is pale, dry-looking, and horizontally banded.

Distance will deceive you. A shelter that appears to be an hour away may be across an uncrossable gap or half a day’s climb. Carry a rangefinder if desired, but trust route time given by local people over visual estimates.

Never follow airborne seeds as an indication of the prevailing wind. Vertical currents may carry them opposite the surface flow.

## Storm protocol

Warm storms can form faster than an outsider expects. Signs include cup trees folding their leaves, sudden changes in vent pitch, bell crawlers shifting in large numbers, birds leaving exposed gaps, or a cold thread of air appearing in otherwise warm wind.

At the first warning:

1. Leave cliff edges and the mouths of vents.
2. Secure all loose equipment.
3. Put on eye and hearing protection.
4. Move to a known storm niche, not merely the nearest cave.
5. Clip to established anchors if instructed.
6. Keep packs closed and retain footwear.
7. Wait after the rain stops; secondary runoff and crosswinds may persist.

Do not shelter beneath a newly forming waterfall or in a smooth channel, even if it is currently dry. Rain may be falling on a plateau you cannot see.

## The silence protocol

Silence is the greatest warning the country gives.

If the Roar begins to fail valley by valley, stop immediately. Do not use the quiet as an opportunity to hurry across an exposed route. The air is reorganizing, and the calm may end in violent reversed flow.

During a silence:

- move only far enough to reach the nearest designated silence shelter;
- stay away from pits, cave mouths, arches, cliff edges, and suspended bridges;
- lie low against competent stone;
- clip into two independent sheltered anchors;
- remove broad outer garments or secure them tightly;
- tether all equipment;
- protect eyes and mouth;
- do not stand to watch the clouds descend;
- do not leave when the first sound returns.

The reopening sequence may begin with a distant pulse and develop unevenly. Wait until your guide declares the new pattern stable. Old routes may no longer be safe afterward. A familiar mountain sounding in a different register is not merely an acoustic curiosity; it may indicate altered airflow through the route ahead.

If caught without shelter, choose a shallow depression on the lee side of solid rock. Avoid deep hollows that may become vents. Make yourself compact and remain attached to your party, but do not tie everyone onto one long line that can drag the group together.

## Local conduct

The inhabitants do not think of hospitality or access primarily in terms of owned ground. Ask whether a crossing, water source, or shelter is available **at that time**, not who owns it.

A suitable request is: “May we use the western niche until the night wind settles?”  
An unsuitable one is: “Whose cave is this?”

Approach settlements from the marked lee route. Do not hammer on walls or shout into entrances; both can be physically unpleasant and may mask changes in the mountain’s tone. Stand where you can be seen, show empty hands, and wait.

Useful gifts include:

- good needles;
- soft copper sheet or wire;
- wax;
- durable cord;
- fine cloth;
- compact cutting tools;
- water-treatment materials, if their use can be explained.

Avoid giving resonant metal vessels, whistles, or unfamiliar machinery without permission. Objects that hum, rattle, or alter airflow may be unwelcome or dangerous.

Never remove or retune a route reed. Never disturb bell crawlers inside a vent. Whether or not they truly warn one another of weather, local people use changes in their collective sound as information.

Do not collect handprint pigment, old ash, polished cave material, seeds, or mineral-shelled animals as souvenirs. Besides being disrespectful, transporting High Roar organisms between worlds could be ecologically disastrous.

## What the visit will feel like

Expect fatigue beyond what the walking alone would cause. The noise removes privacy from thought. Your body will brace against gusts even while at rest. Speech becomes effortful, then irrelevant. After several days, you may begin reading posture, lips, and pressure changes before you consciously hear anything.

Scale will remain difficult. You will repeatedly mistake pillars for distant mountains and hanging vines for nearby cords. Rain may seem close enough to touch while falling ten kilometers away. At sunset, the land may suddenly become legible: isolated green provinces, violet stone, rain suspended in warm light. That clarity is brief.

The first true silence may feel like relief. It is not. Locals will become alert, secure their tools, and move without hesitation. Follow them.

The High Roar rewards patience rather than conquest. Do not attempt to cross the greatest arch, descend into the hidden rivers, or seek the source of every tone. A successful visit consists of moving through a few safe intervals, sleeping in old hollows, watching seeds rise across an impossible gap, and leaving with your hearing, balance, and companions intact.

Above all, listen to changes rather than loudness. The country is almost never quiet, but it is constantly telling you what the air is about to do.
