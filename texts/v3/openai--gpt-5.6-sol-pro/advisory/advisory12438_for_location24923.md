## Overall assessment

The High Cistern is not a place an unmodified human should enter casually. Treat it as a **fully flooded, high-altitude, mechanically active cave with no stable interior, violent multidirectional currents, extreme noise, misleading visibility, and possible large fauna**.

An ordinary traveler should visit only with a technical expedition or in a compact pressure-balanced submersible. If neither is possible, send an instrument package and experience the cistern remotely. It is beautiful, but it offers no forgiving failure mode: there is no surface to reach, no quiet corner guaranteed to remain quiet, and no fixed route back out.

## Before arrival

### Choose the right time

Do not enter:

- Near local noon.
- During or immediately after summit storms.
- While the mountain is trembling.
- When the chamber’s ringing is becoming more frequent or lower in pitch.
- Soon after any major rearrangement, when new shelves and passages are still unstable.

The best window is likely during settled weather, several hours away from noon, after remote instruments show that the main jets have maintained consistent directions for at least one full local cycle.

Obtain the cistern’s local solar time independently. Your home clock, portal clock, and biological sense of day may not correspond to the summit’s noon.

### Scout it first

Send in several cheap, sacrificial probes rather than one expensive unit. Equip them with:

- Inertial navigation.
- Pressure, temperature, pH, salinity, and dissolved-gas sensors.
- Wide-angle optical cameras.
- Sonar, though the ambient noise may make it unreliable.
- Vibration and current meters.
- Passive acoustic recorders with overload protection.
- Breakaway sample tabs rather than articulated sampling arms.

Do not assume a probe that vanishes was carried in a navigable direction. The cistern draws objects into cracks narrower than the objects themselves. A lost signal is not evidence of a passage.

Use reconnaissance to identify **temporary current intersections**, protected wall seams, and the current location of the human-like structures. It will not produce a lasting map.

## Arrival and access

Never open an ordinary air-filled portal directly into the cistern. The pressure differential could produce a lethal jet, destabilize nearby mineral structures, flood the departure side, and leave the return aperture obstructed by growing stone.

Use a **water-filled, pressure-matched transfer lock**. The portal or access seam should be:

- Well away from noon-lit ceiling fractures.
- Clear of soft new mineral growth.
- Outside the central rotating water.
- Monitored continuously from both sides.
- Protected by a rigid sacrificial collar so mineral deposition cannot seal its edges.
- Fitted with at least two independent emergency closure systems.

Do not place the return aperture against the chamber wall. The shape of that wall may change before you return. If portal technology permits, reference the aperture to the mountain’s bulk rather than to a shelf, ladder peg, cairn, or current.

If entering through the natural seam, verify its full length remotely. A slit wide enough at the entrance may narrow, shift, or contain a transverse jet farther in. Never squeeze through any restriction that does not allow you to turn around while wearing all life-support equipment.

## Personal equipment

### Breathing and thermal protection

Use a full-face closed-circuit system with:

- Two genuinely independent breathing loops.
- Protected hoses and valves.
- Enough reserve for several times the planned visit.
- Carbon-dioxide monitoring.
- A mouthpiece backup that can be found by touch.
- No reliance on a buddy’s gas supply.

A sealed dry suit is preferable to a wetsuit. The water may be cold, chemically aggressive, mineral-laden, and locally warmed by volcanic pores. Include active heating with an independent cutoff; sudden warm jets can make fixed-output heating dangerous.

All skin and eyes should remain isolated from the water until samples establish that the plants, crusts, or dissolved minerals are harmless.

### Head and hearing protection

Ordinary diving earplugs will not make the cistern quiet. Much of the sound will reach you through water, helmet, suit, and bone.

Use a rigid helmet with acoustic isolation, vibration damping, and a communications system designed for intense broadband noise. Even then, expect to feel the chamber’s ringing through your jaw, ribs, and equipment.

Assume spoken communication will fail. Establish tactile signals through a buddy line:

- One pull: stop.
- Two: advance.
- Three: retreat.
- Continuous rapid pulls: immediate emergency extraction.

Carry a vibrating wrist display for simple messages and alarms.

### Navigation

Do not navigate by sight alone. The filtered light destroys ordinary depth cues, and moving water can make walls appear to approach or recede.

Carry:

- Redundant inertial trackers.
- A depth and pressure display that flags rapid local pressure fluctuations.
- A physical compass, though volcanic minerals may distort it.
- A short-range proximity sensor.
- High-contrast illuminated markers attached to you, not left in the chamber.
- A mechanical elapsed-time indicator independent of electronics.

Do not trust shadows, especially near noon. “Up” must come from instruments and body orientation, not from the apparent ceiling.

### Protection from impact and entanglement

Wear a streamlined shell over the suit. Raw basalt, silica fragments, and newly formed ridges may be sharp enough to cut hoses or fabric. Protect the throat, wrists, knees, and breathing-loop connections.

Carry two cutting tools in different locations, operable with either hand. Mineral fans, braided fibers, wooden structures, and your own guide lines can all become entanglement hazards.

Avoid loose straps, dangling lamps, nets, open carabiners, and conventional long fins. Short reinforced fins or a compact maneuvering unit are safer, though powered propulsion should be used sparingly around fragile structures.

## Tethers and movement

A continuous route to the exit is essential, but an ordinary cave-diving guideline is not enough here. Currents can wrap it around growing towers, pull it into fissures, or have mineral crust lock it in place.

Use a **redundant, smooth-sheathed guide system**:

- One primary line managed from outside.
- One independent personal return reel.
- Breakaway sections that release before they drag a traveler into a crack.
- No permanent anchors on soft or living stone.
- No dependence on the old wooden pegs.

Travel in a team of at least three: two inside, one dedicated safety diver or operator at the threshold, with further support outside. Keep the internal group close enough for physical contact but not so close that one jet can pin all members against the same surface.

Move along protected walls. Cross jets at their narrowest point and one person at a time. Never fight the central rotating mass. If caught in it, conserve gas and energy, protect your breathing apparatus, and move laterally toward a current intersection rather than attempting to swim directly against the rotation.

The nearly colorless swimmers demonstrate a useful principle: where opposing flows meet, there may be a temporary zone of equilibrium. Such places can serve as resting points, but they are not permanent refuges.

## What to avoid

### The center

Do not enter the central hollow. It has the fewest handholds, the most unstable currents, and the cistern’s peculiar tendency to remove loose or dead material through impossible cracks. A failed diver may become unrecoverable almost immediately.

### Fresh stone

Fresh mineral surfaces may be soft enough to carve, but that also means they are actively forming and structurally unreliable. The rule that sound travels toward fresh stone is useful for identification, not invitation. Avoid the loudest ringing surfaces unless the purpose of the expedition specifically requires sampling them.

Never shelter beneath:

- Translucent towers.
- Overhanging shelves.
- Newly connected grates.
- Pale mineral ribs.
- Any formation visibly shedding grains upstream.

### Narrow passages

A new opening is not necessarily an exit. It may be a pressure channel that will reverse without warning or disappear during the noon upheaval. No human should enter a fissure that cannot admit two fully equipped travelers side by side.

### The floor pulse

If a slow, regular pulse is felt beneath the floor:

1. Stop using propulsion.
2. Do not strike the rock or deploy active sonar.
3. Move off the floor and toward the exit along the wall.
4. Do not investigate scrapes or overturned structures.
5. Leave before the source changes rhythm or location.

Whether it is an animal, a hydraulic phenomenon, or something combining both, the cistern’s visible inhabitants are adapted to forces that would injure a human.

## Conduct around life and artifacts

Assume the cistern is inhabited and culturally used.

Do not touch the ladder, cairn, bowls, basket frames, tools, grates, or objects embedded in fertile cracks. They may be active works rather than abandoned artifacts. Moving one may interrupt a process intended to take years, redirect a jet, or violate local custom.

In particular:

- Do not climb the ladder merely because it resembles a ladder.
- Do not remove stones from the seven-stone cairn.
- Do not stop the grains spinning in the bowls.
- Do not “rescue” broken tools from cracks.
- Do not scrape fresh deposits to leave a mark.
- Do not feed, catch, illuminate intensely, or pursue the pale swimmers.
- Do not detach shell-bearing creatures; release means probable death.

Keep lights diffuse and moderate. The native life is adapted to pale overhead illumination, not concentrated beams. Avoid ultraviolet sterilizers in occupied seams.

Take no living specimen on a first visit. Likewise, carry nothing wet back through the transfer lock without quarantine. Mineral crusts that appear inert may be temporary biological stages.

If you wish to leave a diplomatic offering, do not place a finished object in the central chamber. A more appropriate gift would be a chemically compatible raw material positioned—after study—in a sheltered current where local inhabitants may choose whether to let the water transform it. On an initial expedition, leaving nothing is safest.

## Emergency planning

The team should define an absolute turnaround time well before noon and before breathing-gas reserves become relevant. Time in the cistern should be measured in minutes, not hours.

Prepare for:

- Sudden current reversal.
- Loss of all visual reference.
- Acoustic disorientation or panic.
- Suit puncture.
- Entrapment by mineral collapse.
- Exit obstruction.
- Portal or lock failure.
- Rapid decompression or altitude illness on return.
- Separation from the guide system.
- Biological contamination.

Because the summit lies above the cloud line, ascent and return to air introduce additional concerns. Pressure-match the transfer lock gradually and account for both diving exposure and high terrestrial altitude. Medical support should include oxygen, thermal treatment, trauma care, and decompression capability. Do not evacuate an exposed diver directly to an even lower-pressure vehicle without medical guidance.

If a person becomes unconscious, keep them out of the central current at all costs. Clip them to the rescue system immediately and extract them without pausing to recover tools. The cistern does not reliably return what it takes.

## The actual experience

Even with good protection, expect the place to feel much larger than it is. Brightness will suggest safety while the absence of air remains absolute. Walls will seem near and then become unreachable. Noise will pass through your whole body. You may feel as though the mountain is striking itself around you.

Resist the urge to explore every newly revealed niche. Pick one wall, one living seam, or one artifact group and observe it carefully. A short stationary visit will reveal more than a frantic circuit: bladders collapsing and reinflating, grains moving against the apparent flow, crystal accumulating on the cairn, a finned swimmer balancing where no human could remain still.

Leave while the chamber still appears manageable. If shadows begin to lengthen upward, if the ladder bends, if suspended grains flatten into shining sheets, or if the whole cistern rings as one body, you have already waited too long.

The proper goal is not to conquer or map the High Cistern. It is to enter without being incorporated into it, witness a small portion of its unfinished work, and depart before the mountain decides to finish something with you.
