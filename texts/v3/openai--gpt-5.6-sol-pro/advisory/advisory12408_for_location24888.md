## Preparing for the Condenser Range

Treat the Condenser Range as three things at once: a high-altitude industrial complex, an artificial mountain wilderness, and a place where diagrams, labels, and ownership can become laws of physics. Ordinary mountaineering experience is useful, but insufficient. Factory safety habits may save you more often than climbing skill.

Do not visit alone. A first expedition should remain on the lower terraces, use a local guide or surveyed route if one exists, and avoid the unfinished towers entirely.

### Essential equipment

Bring:

- A sealed climbing helmet with full face protection.
- High-contrast glacier goggles and a spare pair. Noon glare can cause rapid snow-blindness despite the absence of snow.
- UV-proof, reflective outer clothing with no arrows, warning stripes, numbers, or imperative text printed on it.
- Cut-resistant gloves and puncture-resistant boots suitable for ceramic shards, snapped fasteners, grating, and oily steel.
- A lightweight pressure-capable respirator with supplemental oxygen. Altitude can change abruptly inside the tower district and perhaps elsewhere.
- Chemical and particulate filters for insulation fibers, metal dust, sealant fumes, ozone, and unknown coolant aerosols.
- Electrical insulating layers and a compact field detector. Blue corona is a warning, not scenery.
- A closed-loop water supply sufficient for the full trip plus delays. Do not depend on local rain, puddles, reeds, aqueducts, or condensate.
- Insulated rope, slings, and mechanical clamps designed for beams, rails, and plate edges. Ordinary rock protection is largely useless.
- Nonmagnetic tools wherever possible.
- Multiple independent lights, although exterior darkness is rare. Deep machinery chambers can be completely black.
- Visual and haptic alarms rather than sound-only alarms.
- A pressure monitor, gas detector, dosimeter, thermal camera, and structural vibration sensor.
- A simple optical rangefinder and inertial navigation unit. Do not rely solely on satellite positioning, maps, or apparent perspective.
- A portable shelter with a rigid floor and closed water system.

Keep equipment identifiers neutral. Colors and abstract personal marks are preferable to sequential numbers. Avoid labeling anything **OPEN**, **CLOSED**, **UP**, **DOWN**, **SAFE**, **EXIT**, or similar commands. Do not paint directional arrows as trail markers.

### Clothing and bodily protection

The range is bright, cold, dry, and abrasive. Dress as for a combination of glacier travel and electrical substation work. Cover all skin. Reflected sunlight arrives from many directions, including below and behind, so a brim alone is inadequate.

Insulation fragments and metallic filings may look harmless but should be treated as respiratory and skin hazards. Rust moss, blue grass, reedlike organisms, and translucent climbing growths should not be touched barehanded. Their apparent familiarity is unreliable.

Carry oxygen even if the lower valleys feel comfortable. In the unfinished high saddle, a single horizontal crossing can lead into air thin enough to injure an unprotected traveler before ordinary altitude symptoms give adequate warning.

### Water discipline

Local water is not merely strange; it is jurisdictional.

A drop appears to remain attached to the system that first caught it. Consequently:

- Do not step into puddles casually. They may adhere to a pipe network rather than flow off your boot.
- Do not stand beneath roofs, rails, chains, or cold pipes after rain.
- Never assume water will fall downward.
- Do not reach into crossing streams or vertical waves.
- Keep camp equipment dry and isolated from native drainage surfaces.
- Do not connect your water system to a local pipe, even one that appears inactive.
- Do not drink local water without both quarantine analysis and a demonstrated method of transferring its “ownership.”

If water must be collected, use a self-contained catchment sheet brought from your home world, deployed without touching the range, and feed it directly into a sealed analysis vessel. Even then, regard the result as experimental rather than potable.

The central copper aqueduct is not a recreational watercourse. Stay well back from its edge. Standing waves several meters high can move in directions that have nothing to do with slope or wind.

### Movement and route-finding

Everything beneath you is architecture. There is no dependable bedrock.

Before committing weight to a surface, ask what it is designed to do. A broad white “slope” may be a roof skin. A ravine may be a drainage channel. A cliff may be the wall of a pressure vessel. Apparently solid scree may consist of loose ceramic knives over open machinery.

Travel slowly and probe ahead. Prefer:

- Maintained walkways with intact rails.
- Broad structural beams rather than decorative cladding.
- Routes with several independent exits.
- Dry surfaces away from moving equipment.
- Areas where labels remain intact but do not issue commands relevant to you.

Avoid:

- Freight rails, however slowly their platforms move.
- Fan housings and the long shadow-sweeps of their blades.
- Pistons emerging from cliff faces.
- Pressure doors and the volumes directly in front of them.
- Freshly repaired welds.
- Sagging membranes, even when they support vegetation.
- Unlabeled rooms or components with peeling identification.
- Fog.
- Any ladder whose length cannot be confirmed from both ends.
- The unfinished tower district.

Do not trust staircases merely because they continue visibly toward another slope. Perspective is not proof of connection. Use rangefinding and tethered reconnaissance before crossing exposed bridges.

Travel during morning or late afternoon. At noon, glare peaks, shadows vanish, animals seek shelter in the same vents humans might choose, and much of the machinery enters a near-synchronized pause. That pause is not guaranteed safety; it may be the interval before a coordinated change.

### Instructions are active hazards

Treat every marking as if it were a powered machine.

Painted arrows may pull you along their direction. A prohibition may physically resist violation. Numbering may reorganize objects. A missing label may destabilize the space or machinery it once defined.

Therefore:

- Read signs before approaching them closely.
- Do not peel, cover, repaint, translate, or “correct” labels.
- Do not remove numbered parts.
- Do not rearrange tools near numbered assemblies.
- Never carry a detached sign.
- Do not joke by labeling a door or container.
- Do not follow an arrow unless the destination has been independently verified.
- Do not assume a sign’s authority ends where the paint ends.

If a door says **KEEP CLOSED**, leave it closed. If it is already open, do not attempt to prove that the instruction no longer applies. Withdraw by the route you came.

Warning stripes may affect animals or machinery in ways not obvious to humans. Avoid standing on them, crossing them unnecessarily, or wearing similar patterns.

### Communication and sound

Sound cannot be trusted for location or timing. A dropped tool may make its impact noise minutes later and in another valley. Echoes migrate rather than return.

Use radio only as one layer of communication; heavy structures, substations, and unknown fields may interfere. Teams should also use:

- Line-of-sight light signals.
- Haptic wrist alerts.
- Tether signals in hazardous sections.
- Scheduled check-ins.
- Written status displays without directional or imperative wording.

Never navigate toward a shout, clang, alarm, or apparent machinery failure without visual confirmation. It may have originated elsewhere or much earlier.

A deep exhalation felt in the chest indicates a major pressure door or system event. When this occurs, stop, crouch behind a load-bearing structure, seal your respirator, and wait for pressure and atmospheric readings to stabilize. Do not investigate.

### Wildlife conduct

Most animals can be observed safely from a distance, but avoid treating them as ordinary terrestrial species.

- **Rail hares** can reach walls, ceilings, grating undersides, and cable runs. Protect sealants, pack gaskets, and shelter seams from chewing.
- **Cliff swifts** may indicate active ventilation slots. Do not camp beneath their flight spirals.
- **Reservoir moths** emerge from coolant pools. Do not handle them or enter a facade covered by a resting swarm.
- **Silver fish** are not evidence that aqueduct water is safe.
- **Gantry walkers** must never be blocked, crowded, or driven from painted lines.

If gantry walkers are waiting where a safety line ends, do not paint an extension for them. You do not know whether this would rescue them, direct them into danger, or create a new binding route across the landscape. Keep off their lines when a herd is approaching.

Maintenance drones should also be treated as wildlife and industrial equipment simultaneously. Do not obstruct them, attach anything to them, or allow them to “repair” your gear.

### Camping

The safest camp is a freestanding, sealed platform on a surveyed, inactive service deck. It should be:

- Away from rails, drains, pipe joints, pressure doors, vents, blade shadows, and overhead reservoirs.
- Outside painted lines and numbered assembly zones.
- Protected from focused sunlight created by the translucent eastern canopy.
- Secured mechanically without drilling.
- Equipped with visual perimeter alarms.
- Inspected for rail hares and condensation every few hours.

Do not camp in a room merely because it is enclosed. Unlabeled interior spaces may be larger, deeper, or less connected than their exterior dimensions imply. Do not use abandoned turbine halls after rain; their shallow mirror pools may belong to active systems.

Open flame is unwise. Focused light can ignite dust, and local insulation, sealants, coolants, or oxygen conditions may behave unpredictably.

### The unfinished towers

An ordinary traveler should not enter them.

Altitude, distance, and orientation are inconsistent there. Clouds can form solid-looking cubical masses. Bridges may cross into abrupt low pressure. Stairs can descend while appearing to rise. Structural glass contains impressions of objects that may not yet exist.

If entry is unavoidable:

1. Use a pressure-capable suit.
2. Remain continuously tethered to a team outside the anomaly.
3. Mark elapsed time independently on each person.
4. Do not touch the fossil impressions.
5. Do not bring numbered gear.
6. Retreat immediately if exterior altitude and instrument altitude disagree.
7. Never climb a ladder or stair twice to “check” whether it has changed.

If your tether returns with additional knots, labels, wear, or attachments that no team member placed, abandon it rather than pulling the unknown material toward you.

### Emergency priorities

If lost, do not keep moving downhill. “Downhill” may lead into machinery, and sound itself migrates that way. Stop in a stable, labeled, non-commanding area and establish visual contact with rescuers.

If spatial behavior becomes inconsistent:

- Do not cross the same threshold repeatedly.
- Do not remove labels to inspect behind them.
- Do not count rungs aloud.
- Keep all team members in direct sight.
- Retrace your exact physical contact points if possible.

If a teammate becomes attached to a directional marking or cannot open a marked door, do not use brute force immediately. Move them laterally away from the marking, block their view without covering the sign itself, and use a tether from outside its apparent zone of influence.

If caught in fog, stop. Numbered objects may begin to reorder, routes may alter, and the youngest structures are especially unreliable. Secure loose equipment, avoid all sequential markings, and wait for visibility unless remaining in place is immediately dangerous.

### Best attitude to bring

The Condenser Range rewards patience. Nothing here happens quickly, but much of it is irresistible once begun. A platform may take an hour to arrive, a piston days to extend, and a wall years to close a valley; none of that makes it harmless.

Do not think of the range as ruins. It is active, unfinished, and still defining its rules. Walk as though every surface belongs to a machine whose purpose you do not understand, every sign is legally binding on matter, and every drop of water already has somewhere else to be.
