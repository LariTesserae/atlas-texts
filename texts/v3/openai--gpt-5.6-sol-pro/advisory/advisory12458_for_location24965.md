## The first rule: do not enter at human scale

To this city, an ordinary person is not a visitor but a geological event. A fingertip could block an avenue, a breath could produce a destructive wind, and one loose hair could bridge several districts. Body heat, skin oils, lint, microbes, and the water carried on your clothing could permanently alter a habitat whose chemistry is balanced grain by grain.

A normal-sized arrival inside the cavity might also displace enough air to damage calcite roofs or disturb the load-bearing pillar. Even standing outside and shining a strong lamp through a fissure could heat the chamber or disrupt its light-sensitive organisms.

Visit by one of these methods, in order of preference:

1. **A sterile, noncontact remote probe**, no larger than a local tool or handcart.
2. **An optically projected or phase-separated presence** that transfers neither heat nor matter.
3. **Carefully calibrated reduction to local scale**, only if your shrinking method preserves breathable atmosphere and does not continually radiate energy.
4. **Observation from outside the limestone seam.**

Do not open a person-sized portal directly into the city. The pressure change alone could be catastrophic.

## Choose your scale carefully

Do not merely become “very small.” Survey the inhabitants first and reduce yourself to a height that makes you recognizable without making you dominant. Ideally, you should be no taller than one of their civic buildings and preferably close to the residents’ own size.

Recalibrate all equipment after reduction. At that scale:

- A water drop is a heavy, fast-moving mass capable of crushing or sweeping you away.
- Surface tension can trap you more effectively than deep water.
- A thin moisture film may behave like mud or a powerful adhesive.
- Minor ridges become cliffs.
- Falling calcite dust becomes rockfall.
- The normal cave roar may become physically painful through both air and ground.
- A strong human gesture can create dangerous air currents.

Do not assume your ordinary strength, weight, metabolism, or oxygen needs will scale conveniently. Use a translation system that has been tested under the same dimensional physics.

## Arrive clean—cleaner than clean

This city has never known greenery. That fact is not an invitation to show its people a seed. It is a warning.

Bring no uncontrolled organic material:

- no food;
- no wood, paper, cotton, wool, leather, feathers, or natural rope;
- no soil or dust;
- no viable spores, pollen, bacteria, fungi, algae, or viruses;
- no untreated water;
- no exposed skin flakes or hair;
- no petroleum oils or fragrant compounds.

Even a harmless surface microbe from your world might consume a mineral film, poison a luminous brine bowl, or establish a new metabolism in a city with no ecological defenses against it.

Wear a fully sealed, sterile suit with:

- a closed-loop breathing system;
- captured heat and moisture;
- smooth, non-shedding surfaces;
- no loose fibers or articulated gaps that can pinch local structures;
- chemically inert soles designed to spread weight;
- external sampling locks that cannot open accidentally.

The suit should retain everything you produce, including condensation, exhaled carbon dioxide, sweat, waste, and heat. Treat every gram you bring as something that must leave with you.

On return, quarantine both yourself and your equipment. The inhabitants’ symbiotic “living grains” may be dependent on unusual mineral chemistry, but do not assume they are harmless to human tissue or machinery.

## Prepare for the atmosphere

The cavity is cool, saturated with moisture, and poorly ventilated. Its oxygen content is unknown. Carbon dioxide or other dense gases may gather in low districts, especially near the basin. Mineral aerosols and microscopic calcite splinters may be present even when the air looks clear.

Your respirator should protect against:

- hypoxia;
- elevated carbon dioxide;
- dissolved or aerosolized salts;
- fine silica and calcite dust;
- unknown microbial grains;
- corrosive trace gases;
- prolonged exposure to nearly 100 percent humidity.

Ordinary fabric clothing will quickly become wet, heavy, and contaminated. Electronics must be fully sealed. Assume every surface conducts moisture into joints and housings.

Do not heat the chamber for your comfort. A temperature increase trivial to you could alter condensation patterns upon which whole wards depend.

## Protect your hearing—and your balance

The city’s noise is not simply loud. It is structural. Water, channels, cisterns, calcite needles, and hollow terraces generate overlapping tones that travel through the ground as well as the air.

Use protection that limits damaging sound without isolating you completely. You need to detect:

- wall-tapped warnings;
- changes in the vibration of bridges or roofs;
- the approach of a falling drop;
- maintenance signals around the central pillar;
- resonance indicating a loose surface beneath you.

Noise-canceling equipment should be used cautiously. The inhabitants live inside the soundscape, and some of what appears to be background roar may be public communication. A system that emits opposing vibrations into the stone could erase messages or interfere with structural diagnosis.

Prepare for disorientation. The unchanging roar can make motion feel unreal and time seem suspended. Because there is little visual change, you may underestimate both the length of your stay and your fatigue. Keep an internal clock, but do not impose its rhythms on your hosts.

## Learn to communicate without shouting

Spoken conversation is conducted at intimate distance, and the residents may perceive vibration through their bodies differently from you. Do not call across plazas. At local scale, a loud human voice may be painful or structurally disruptive.

Before arrival, build a translator capable of reading and producing:

- close-range airborne sound;
- substrate vibration;
- gentle taps;
- pressure patterns;
- possibly contact signals transmitted through jaw, shell, or limb.

Its output must be strictly limited. Never test it by striking a wall at random. Known signals include danger, assembly, and death; an accidental pattern could evacuate a district or announce a catastrophe.

Begin by remaining still in a designated receiving area. Let the inhabitants approach. Present no outstretched hand until you understand whether touch is greeting, examination, submission, or threat.

Their sense of urgency may be unlike yours. A people who maintain walls grain by grain and wait decades for feeding films to renew may regard a pause of several hours as a brief courtesy. Do not mistake slowness for indifference or empty streets for abandonment.

## Obtain consent from the maintainers, not merely a guide

There may be no monarch, visitor bureau, or concept of private tourism. Authority is likely tied to maintenance obligations: those who understand the water, channels, load paths, and pillar may have more practical power than any ceremonial figure.

Before moving beyond the arrival point, ask permission from whoever is responsible for:

- the district’s drainage;
- the route’s structural surfaces;
- the local mineral films;
- the tapping network;
- the central maintenance court.

An empty house is not free space. Empty rooms are maintained, provisioned, and meaningful. Do not enter them, open their doors, rearrange their tools, or ask why they are preserved as though the answer must be a forgotten tragedy. The absent population may be understood through obligations and expectations that do not translate into human ideas of death or abandonment.

## Move as if every surface is infrastructure

There is little distinction between natural cave and constructed city. A ridge may be a rampart. A wet streak may be a food source. A calcite crust may carry a roof load. A tiny channel may be a major aqueduct.

Therefore:

- Step only where directed.
- Use marked resting plates rather than walls or ledges.
- Never lean against a stalactite or column.
- Do not scrape away slippery deposits for traction.
- Do not clear “debris.”
- Do not widen a passage.
- Do not move stones to inspect what lies beneath.
- Do not catch or redirect a drop.
- Do not place equipment across a gutter, however small.
- Do not use adhesive anchors, pitons, suction cups, or expanding supports.
- Avoid hovering systems whose downdraft can disturb water or silt.

The city survives by returning every displaced grain. If you accidentally move anything, do not put it back according to your own judgment. Mark the location without touching it further and summon a maintainer.

## Treat the water as traffic, food, machinery, and law

The falling water is not scenery. Its course links districts, drives wheels, carries silt, renews feeding films, illuminates brine bowls through vibration, and ultimately removes matter into the basin.

Study the drop schedule along your route. Although the springs do not vary, individual impacts may still create recurring hazards. Cross exposed gutters only under guidance. Wear a tether designed to release rather than drag structures loose if caught.

Do not:

- drink the water;
- collect it as a souvenir;
- introduce dye to trace its path;
- place sensors in channels without permission;
- wash yourself or equipment;
- dam it, even momentarily;
- offer your own water to the inhabitants.

One drop waters half the wards. Holding it in a sample vial could amount to a citywide drought followed by a flood when released.

## Stay away from the black basin

Regard the lowest basin as both a physical hazard and a cultural boundary.

Its apparent geometry is unreliable. Lines produce contradictory soundings, and the unseen fissures may involve folded space, shifting channels, or interactions between worlds. Surface tension, undertow through microscopic cracks, and reflected light will make its edge difficult to judge.

Do not:

- cross it;
- swim or float upon it;
- send an autonomous probe beneath the surface;
- throw anything into it;
- retrieve anything falling toward it;
- explain it away as “just a sump” in front of the residents.

Anything taken by the basin is understood not to return. Respect that rule even if your instruments claim retrieval is possible. A demonstration that violates a foundational certainty could be more damaging than losing the object.

Plan your suit and equipment so that nothing can detach. If a tool falls into the basin, let it go unless the local caretakers explicitly decide otherwise.

## The central pillar is not a tourist site

The collar around the weathered pillar bears part of the ceiling’s weight. It is continuously inspected because the survival of the city may depend upon minute changes in stress and accretion.

Do not approach the maintenance court without explicit permission. If admitted:

- move one at a time;
- disable unnecessary motors;
- do not touch the pillar or collar;
- do not scan it with heat, ultrasound, or strong vibration;
- do not offer to “improve” the support;
- do not distract the current caretaker;
- do not stand in the path between arriving and departing shifts.

Human engineering instincts will be dangerous here. A stronger brace might redirect loads into fragile districts. Removing scale might change a joint’s friction. A repair that lasts ten thousand years may still be inferior to a system the city knows how to maintain forever.

If you detect what appears to be imminent failure, report the observation quietly and provide raw measurements. Do not intervene unasked.

## Light the city gently

Bring no open flame. Fuel is scarce locally, oxygen may be limited, and heat can alter moisture patterns.

Use dim, cold, shielded illumination in wavelengths verified not to harm:

- luminous brine organisms;
- mineral-feeding symbionts;
- light-sensitive residents;
- the calcite seams themselves.

Do not use a high-powered flash for photography. It will flatten the city’s subtle relief, blind nearby inhabitants, and erase the experience you came to witness. Long-exposure imaging is safer if it requires no active illumination.

Do not rub glowing calcite seams yourself. The workers who renew their radiance know the acceptable pressure and route. What looks like a charming interactive feature is part of municipal lighting.

Expect your eyes to need a long adaptation period. The city’s distinctions—rust threads, salt deposits, violet shadows, minute motion—emerge only after you stop trying to brighten them.

## Bring very little, and offer even less

A closed material economy can be destabilized by generosity. A single metal fragment might exceed the city’s known supply of useful metal. A piece of plastic might persist forever without any established use or method of disposal. Food could be poisonous, ecologically transformative, or socially incomprehensible.

Suitable equipment includes:

- closed-loop life support;
- passive thermal control;
- inert ceramic or compatible mineral tools;
- low-energy vibration translator;
- noncontact imaging devices;
- a redundant locator;
- a tether that cannot abrade stone;
- emergency phase extraction or portal recall;
- a sealed notebook or internal recorder;
- personal timekeeping and fatigue alarms.

Do not bring trade goods until cultural and chemical specialists have established what exchange means here. If a gift is expected, the safest may be information: a diagram, sound pattern, or record carried away again after being shown. Even then, avoid revealing the surface in a way that implies their cosmology is primitive or mistaken. Their model of existence as a hollow resisting pressure is, within their lived world, profoundly practical.

Never offer seeds, flowers, soil, wooden objects, food, or a jar of sunlight-lit surface water.

## Do not promise rescue

The city may look trapped, impoverished, or post-apocalyptic to a surface-dwelling human. Resist the impulse to propose evacuation, expansion, agriculture, drilling to the surface, or industrial reinforcement.

Its inhabitants are adapted biologically and culturally to an environment with:

- no sun;
- no seasonal variation;
- sparse mineral nutrition;
- continuous vibration;
- extreme material conservation;
- obligations extending across immense spans of time.

Exposure to the surface might kill them. Abundant food might disrupt their symbiosis. Variable weather could be psychologically and physiologically intolerable. Even knowledge of open sky may not be liberating; it may be understood as the terrifying absence of a load-bearing roof.

Ask what they want before deciding what they lack.

## Prepare psychologically

The city’s emptiness may trouble you more than its darkness. Thousands of maintained rooms and only a few dozen visible citizens can provoke grief, curiosity, or the feeling that you have arrived after some unnamed extinction. Do not force that story upon the place.

You may also experience:

- loss of time sense;
- anxiety from unbroken noise;
- claustrophobia beneath the immense symbolic weight of the roof;
- fear near the basin;
- compulsive concern about the pillar;
- the impression that your movements are the only events occurring;
- guilt at consuming resources simply by being present.

Schedule a short visit. Use private reminders to eat, rest, and depart; the city provides no dawn or evening to cue you. Do not rely on local water wheels as clocks.

Accept that their conversation may span pauses longer than your mission. Accept also that what they consider an adequate welcome may consist of letting you watch one careful repair.

## Emergency protocol

Your extraction system should operate without blast, suction, heat, or a large aperture. It must not require you to reach the upper shafts or cross the basin.

Leave immediately if you detect:

- a pressure change;
- unusual air movement;
- a change in the fixed water rates;
- cracking or new resonance near the pillar;
- an increase in carbon dioxide;
- suit contamination or leakage;
- local alarm taps;
- uncontrolled biological exposure;
- dimensional distortion near the basin;
- any inability to account for your equipment.

If a resident is endangered by your presence, prioritize stopping your own movement and summoning local help. At their scale, an impulsive rescue attempt may cause further collapse. Use remote manipulation only when directed or when immediate death is otherwise certain.

If your normal-scale body exists elsewhere while you visit, maintain a recall observer who can extract you even if local time perception becomes unreliable.

## The right way to visit

A successful visit will leave almost no evidence.

You may spend hours advancing the length of what would ordinarily be a hand. You may see only one resident crossing a bridge, another scraping scale from a channel, and a third listening to the stone. You may be granted no artifact, no dramatic revelation, and no answer about the empty dwellings or the basin’s depth.

That is appropriate.

Come prepared to observe maintenance rather than spectacle. Listen until the roar separates into channels, wheels, knocks, and messages. Let your eyes adjust until bone-gray stone reveals its colors. Notice that stillness here is not inactivity but countless corrections so small that no single one becomes history.

Above all, leave with your footprints, heat, microbes, tools, and certainties intact. The finest courtesy you can offer this city is to pass through it without giving its caretakers a new displacement to repair.
