The power does not arrive in fire.

Fire would be troublesome here. It would take poorly to the wet wood, fill the halls with smoke, frighten the animals, and require someone to repair the roof afterward.

Nor does it come as a great voice from the ridges. The basin already contains too many voices, and wisdom has no need to be the loudest among them.

It watches first.

For a full turning of the seasons, it attends to the settlement in the manner the settlement attends to itself. It follows water from leaf tip to spring, spring to gutter, gutter to sedge pool, and pool to the single stream leaving the basin. It learns which bells mean flood and which merely mean goats. It sees who wakes before dawn to clear another household’s blocked channel without mentioning it. It sees who lets the blockage remain until blame can be assigned.

It listens to root-tenders speak of decades and children speak of the next hour. It watches elders disagree honestly. It watches traders lie harmlessly and dangerously. It sees the bridges tested, the fruit stolen, the dead remembered through work, the old knife lent again.

The power is pleased, but not because the place is innocent.

It sees envy, carelessness, pettiness, exhaustion, and the small cruelties made possible by knowing exactly whom another person depends upon. It sees a woman open a sluice to save her own garden, though she knows the rush will strip soil from the terrace below. It sees the lower household curse her and arrive that same night when her roof tree begins to split.

The power is pleased because nothing here is mistaken for purity. Goodness is practiced as repair.

So it does not make the settlement dry.

It does not banish rot, silence frogs, tame monkeys, straighten paths, harden roofs into stone, or lay bright metal beneath every hand. It does not command the trees to cease growing through walls. It does not make bridges incapable of breaking, because then no one would listen to their lashings, and the bridge would cease to be an agreement.

It does not make the people immortal. That would burden the basin beyond endurance and turn memory into possession.

For a time, it considers doing nothing.

Then it looks beneath the town.

Far under the moss, terraces, root masses, graves, and black saturated soil lies the scar of an ancient collapse. The whole basin rests in the cupped debris of a mountain that once moved. Most of it has settled into a deep, patient strength. But near the stream’s departure, behind a wall of buried stone, water has found a blind passage.

No water-watcher knows it. The change is too deep to alter the channels yet. No elder remembers it. The last warning occurred before the oldest living boundary tree germinated.

In forty-three years, after three wet seasons follow one another without pause, the blind passage will close.

The basin will continue gathering water.

For nine days it will seem only that the lower stream has become strangely quiet. Then springs will rise through house floors. Terraces will soften. People will work without sleep, opening every known outlet, but the obstruction will lie below all their tools.

On the tenth night, half the eastern slope will move.

The power could hold the mountain forever.

It does not.

Forever is a dangerous gift. It turns attention into ceremony, ceremony into assurance, and assurance into neglect. It would bind the power to every careless cut and every wall built where no wall should stand. In time the people would say: the mountain cannot move, because it has not moved. Then they would forget why.

Instead, the power gives them what their own wisdom has always sought.

It gives warning.

Not a prophecy spoken to one chosen person. A single mouth can be doubted, worshiped, bribed, murdered, or made into a throne.

The warning begins among the frogs.

One misty evening, every frog in the lower channels falls silent at once. Not gradually, as when a snake passes, but together, basin-wide, leaving behind a silence so complete that people come out of their houses holding lamps.

Then the frogs begin calling from uphill.

Species that have never left the cisterns appear in roof gutters. Tiny leaf frogs gather on the old public hall. Broad brown frogs climb public stairs in hundreds, all facing the same ridge.

Dogs refuse the lower bridge. Ducks abandon the wastewater gardens. Pale fish emerge from covered channels and lie flashing in shallow drains.

The water-watchers taste the springs and find nothing wrong. They listen and hear, beneath the ordinary rush, a second note: too low to be called sound, more pressure than pitch.

Root-tenders place their hands against the oldest trees. Deep underground, at intervals too slow for ordinary hearing, something knocks.

The settlement argues.

Some say sickness. Some say weather. Some say a spirit has been offended by the cutting of a boundary tree. Children say the mountain has a frog trapped beneath it. A trader recently returned from the lowlands declares that animals always behave strangely in mountain fog and is shouted down by people who have lived in mountain fog longer than he has.

For seven nights the signs continue.

On the eighth morning, in the fern pool beneath the public hall, the water becomes perfectly clear.

The pool is usually brown-green with leaf stain. Now every submerged stone can be seen. At the bottom lies a line of pale grit pointing toward a retaining wall older than any remembered house.

No one claims to have put it there.

They remove the wall stone by stone.

Behind it they find an abandoned channel, narrow, root-filled, and carved with marks worn almost smooth. The eldest recognizes one mark from a funerary staff. Another appears in a bridge song whose meaning has been lost. A third resembles the sign for water that has not yet chosen its course.

The channel leads into the ridge.

The people clear it.

The work lasts years.

No miracle completes it for them. They cut roots, brace stone, carry soil, redirect paths, negotiate claims, move three houses, offend one lineage, compensate another, and compose several vicious songs about the whole affair. Metal edges wear thin. Baskets split. Two proposed routes fail. A work leader is dismissed for hiding unstable ground to preserve his sister’s terrace. The settlement nearly abandons the channel twice.

But the frogs return whenever the work strays too far from the buried passage.

The dogs sleep near the correct openings.

And when workers strike rock above a hidden void, clay bells throughout the basin give one soft note, though there is no wind.

At last they open a second way for floodwater to leave the saddle: not a broad, raw cut, but a stepped overflow winding through stone chambers, root lattices, reed beds, and sacrificial terraces where no one will build. Most years it carries only a thread. Children float leaves along it and complain that the boats always become caught.

Forty-three years later, the three wet seasons come.

The lower stream chokes exactly as it would have. Springs rise through floors. The eastern slope groans. Bells ring, animals are moved, ropes thrown, channels cut.

Then the hidden overflow takes the water.

It roars for ten days.

Two bridges are lost. Gardens vanish. A great tree tilts and destroys a drying hall. One person breaks a leg. Another nearly drowns rescuing goats and is mocked for the rest of a long life.

But the mountain does not take the town.

When the storm passes, the people discover that the overflow has widened itself. Its banks are raw, bright with broken stone, and already receiving seeds.

Only then do they know that something saw them.

They gather in the living hall to decide what is owed.

Some wish to raise a shrine. Some wish to name the power. Some propose offerings of salt, wax, feathers, or the best fermented fruit. One influential household suggests keeping the clear pool enclosed and appointing hereditary guardians. This proposal is rejected so loudly that parakeets leave the nearby trees.

At last an old water-watcher says:

“If it wanted a house, it had the mountain. If it wanted servants, it could have made us obey before we argued. If it wanted food, why show us a place where food should never be planted?”

So they do not build it a temple.

They place one clay bell beside the overflow, low enough for a child to reach. The bell has no clapper. It is sounded by hand only when someone observes a danger they cannot yet explain.

By custom, whoever rings it must speak first and may not be mocked until they have finished.

This is the offering the power accepts.

Afterward, its presence remains, though never in a form that can be possessed.

It does not answer requests for wealth. It does not settle ownership disputes. When two households appeal to it over a fruit tree, the pool clouds with pollen and stays that way until they become embarrassed and leave.

It will not tell lovers whether to marry, traders whether to travel, or elders which memory is true. It does not prevent every fall, fever, flood, or foolish decision.

But certain dangers become a little less silent.

A rope near failure gives off a bitter smell before its fibers part. Water moving beneath a bank changes the taste of herbs rooted above it. A stabilizing tree marked for an unwise cut fills overnight with nesting birds. In fog, a bridge with a loosened support seems one pace farther away than it is, causing the approaching foot to stop.

These signs are not infallible. They must be noticed, discussed, and tested. The careless can still ignore them. The proud can still explain them away.

The power does not replace attention.

It joins attention.

In return, the settlement slowly extends its obligations beyond the basin. The overflow descends toward people they rarely see. Flood warnings are sent downhill. Sediment traps are cleared not only for local gardens but for the river below. Traders carry songs describing dangerous clouds and sickened springs. When another mountain community asks how to hear hidden water, the basin’s watchers teach them without demanding lineage or payment.

This, more than offerings, pleases the power.

Centuries pass. The story changes.

In one version, the frogs dug the overflow themselves. In another, an ancestor became water and dreamed beneath the hall. Some insist there was no benevolent power at all—that animals moved, people observed them, and forgotten engineering was recovered from old marks.

The power does not correct them.

Truth need not always stand apart from the practices that keep it alive.

It asks for no name. Names become handles, and handles invite ownership.

It asks for no praise. Praise is easily given in place of maintenance.

It asks only that warning be allowed to speak before certainty silences it; that those uphill remember those below; and that no gift of safety be mistaken for freedom from care.

Sometimes, very early, a water-watcher crossing the public hall sees no one beside the fern pool but hears a small sound: not words, exactly, but something like a hand testing a knot.

Then the watcher kneels, places a palm on the wet stone, and listens too.
