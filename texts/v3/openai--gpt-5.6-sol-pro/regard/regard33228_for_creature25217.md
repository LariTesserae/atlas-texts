The power would not arrive as another force.

It would not descend through the strip of sky in brightness, for brightness there would blind the cameras and cast dangerous reflections on wet steel. It would not silence the turbines, because beyond the mountain their turning held lamps over operating tables, warmed houses in sleet, drove pumps, charged radios, and kept food cold. It would not split the concrete for the sake of three blades of grass. It would not make a wilderness where a wilderness would clog the sump, conceal cracks, loosen bolts, or send roots into seals built to restrain a reservoir.

Being benevolent, it would care for the slight lives.

Being wise, it would also care for everything those lives could not understand.

So it would first attend.

It would feel the reservoir’s weight carried through the buttress. It would follow each tremor backward through bearing, shaft, turbine, and falling water, and forward through copper into the long human night. It would know which leak was harmless condensation and which bright thread beneath the expansion joint was the beginning of fatigue. It would know the precise violence of water if pressure ceased to remain contained. It would know the maintenance worker who had a daughter asleep two valleys away, and the patient whose breath depended upon current that never noticed the moss.

It would distinguish necessity from habit.

That distinction would be its first gift.

For a while, nothing visible would happen.

The moss would remain compressed under its steel lip. The algae would continue living at the depth of a stain. The three grass blades would bend whenever the exhaust surged and lift again when it passed. Spray would erase every sign that the court had been visited.

Then, during a scheduled inspection, one of the crew would pause behind the cable trunk.

She would not know why.

Perhaps one pale blade would catch in the beam of her helmet lamp. Perhaps she would remember a weed growing from a crack in the apartment stairwell where she had lived as a child. She would crouch, touch the grit with one gloved finger, and feel—not a command, not a revelation, only a small reluctance to scrape it out.

Her partner would point to the drainage route.

Clear.

She would point to the cable.

Clear.

They would leave the grass.

This alone would not be enough. Mercy that depends forever on being overlooked is only delayed destruction.

The power would therefore work through things the place could accept: measurements, drawings, work orders, tolerances.

A junior engineer reviewing photographs would notice that condensate from the cable trunk repeatedly crossed a painted walkway before reaching the sump. He would propose a narrow stainless-steel drip guide, no wider than two fingers, to lead the water directly into the inspection channel. It would be approved because it reduced slip risk. When installed, the guide would also spare the teaspoon of grit from the hardest wash.

A lighting technician would replace a failing amber fixture with a more efficient unit. The new lamp, selected from a catalog for visibility and low energy use, would contain a slightly broader band of light. It would still be weak. It would not turn the court green. But some of its photons would be usable.

A safety review would add a small black rectangle to the floor plan:

**NONCRITICAL SURFACE — VISUAL INSPECTION ONLY**

The rectangle would include the sheltered corner and the flange above the moss. No one would think of it as a sanctuary. The language would be deliberately dry. In such a place, dry language was the strongest protection available.

The algae in the main channel would still be removed. The sump grille would still be cleared. Anything that threatened drainage, traction, inspection, insulation, or structural integrity would go. The benevolent power would not confuse kindness with unlimited permission. It would set a boundary around what could remain without endangering what must endure.

And then it would make one more change, even smaller.

High overhead, on the inner edge of a louver where rain and wind had deposited a line of dust, a seed would lodge. Not a miraculous seed. Not a vine capable of prying apart the works. Only a mountain grass suited to cold stone, poor minerals, and brief light. During a storm, water would carry it downward. It would come to rest behind the cable trunk.

Whether it germinated would be left to the place.

Wisdom would not force it.

Years would pass.

One of the original three blades would die before producing seed. Its substance would fold into the tiny accumulation at the wall: a thread of carbon, a little structure for the grit. Another would be torn during an unusually violent purge and vanish through the baffles. The third might survive long enough to thicken at the base. It might send up a narrow flowering stem no taller than a hand, carrying a head so modest that the next crew mistook it for a fiber.

The moss would spread by less than the width of a coin.

The algae would return after each cleaning, because the cleaning removed growth but not possibility.

Bacteria would inhabit the new drip guide. Spores would wait in concrete pores. No insects would appear. No bird would descend. Drainage Interspace 6-C would not become a garden, and the power would not call its restraint a failure.

A wise power would understand that abundance is not the only form of blessing.

Sometimes the blessing is continuity.

Sometimes it is one square handspan where the general rule is relaxed.

Sometimes it is the conversion of an accidental refuge into a permitted one.

And because benevolence would extend also to those who entered in helmets and bright waterproof suits, the power would not leave the deeper danger unattended.

The thin line of water at the expansion joint—the one everyone had classified as ordinary weeping—would change its mineral taste by an immeasurable degree. The moss nearest it would darken. A technician, trained to distrust changes more than conditions, would notice. Sampling would follow. Ultrasonic testing would find a flaw beginning behind the poured face, still small enough to repair during the next planned shutdown.

No disaster would announce the intervention. No wall would fail. No workers would be celebrated for surviving. Far below, no sirens would sound in sleeping towns. The reservoir would remain where it had been asked to remain.

The repair would alter the runoff. The faithful leak beneath the flange would diminish to a slow beading.

Some of the moss would dry.

This, too, the power would allow.

It would not preserve every individual at the cost of the whole. It would keep one thin thread of condensate wandering along the flange—not through sabotage, but because no structure in the wet world is absolute. Enough would remain in the microscopic pits. The colony would contract, become darker, denser, more patient.

The grass would receive water from the drip guide.

The machinery would continue its enormous counterfeit breathing.

The court would still be deafening. It would still be cold, dangerous, engineered, and almost barren. People would still enter only temporarily. Pressure would remain contained. Electricity would remain behind its enamel and ceramic. Water would descend.

But life would no longer inhabit only an error.

It would inhabit a concession.

No plaque would mark it. No one would give the court a new name. On the diagrams it would remain **Drainage Interspace 6-C**, with its numbered openings and load ratings and small black rectangle indicating a surface that required no cleaning unless it interfered with inspection.

That would be enough.

The benevolent power would depart—or perhaps simply cease to be distinguishable from good judgment, timely attention, and the unexplained human impulse to leave one harmless living thing unharmed.

Behind the cable trunk, a green blade would gather a drop.

The drop would enlarge until the blade bowed beneath it. For several seconds it would hold: water on life, life over concrete, the weight of a whole watershed made miniature.

Then the drop would fall into the channel.

The blade would rise.
