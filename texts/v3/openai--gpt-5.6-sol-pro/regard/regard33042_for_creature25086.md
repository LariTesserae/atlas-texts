The power does not descend in fire.

Fire is already understood here. Pressure, ignition, glare, the sharp report of energy crossing a gap—these are ordinary. A miracle announced as violence would be mistaken for weather, food, or the opening of another seam.

Nor does the power bring green things.

It considers this. Green is one of the great mercies elsewhere: light made edible, air persuaded into flesh, patient softness laid over stone. But to seed the shoreless sea with algae would not be kindness. The algae would spread through the outer cobalt, drink the dissolved elements, clog the intakes of the mill-whales, shade the storm-stacks, and turn the old, exact hunger of the place into a new and stupid suffocation.

Wisdom knows that life can be destroyed by gifts.

The power does not still the mountain. The fresh fractures feed nearly everything. It does not stop the whales’ chambers, though their labor shakes eggs from their cases and deafens the water. It does not blunt every tooth or forbid one body from entering another. Such an act would preserve individuals for a little while and abolish the world that made them.

But neither does the power look upon the closed circuits and call them perfect.

It sees every animal slowly divided against advancing glass. It feels the spark mites cooked by the arc they require. It follows a shear-coin through the fan-jaw’s narrowing teeth. It knows which lamp-skulls possess only reflex and which, in their pressure bladders and mineral memories, suffer the changing mountain as confusion.

Equilibrium does not excuse pain.

Beauty does not excuse it either.

So the power waits.

For three hundred years it learns the sea’s language.

Not a language of names. There are no names. There are intervals, conductivities, sequences of pressure, mineral traces released into water. There is the closing of ten thousand apertures before lightning. There is the long-short-long firing of whales on the western circuit. There is the low chord of wind-backs transmitted through basalt before storms.

The power does not merely listen to individuals. It listens to recurrences.

An iron atom enters a seam-eater, passes into a scavenger, becomes the edge of an egg, is swallowed by a whale calf, joins its magnetic spine, remains there for two centuries, and after death is ground by a gear-shell into dust fine enough to become the transparent strut of a kite.

That route is a sentence.

Millions of such routes are thought.

Slowly, the power understands that there is a being here after all.

It is not the mountain. It is not the water, nor any whale or colony. It is the circulation itself: the persistent inward turning by which the sea takes itself apart and makes itself again. Its memory is stored not in a brain but in paths. Its moods last centuries. A storm is a sensation. A new ore vein is an idea that spreads through bodies.

The being does not know it is a being.

This is not, by itself, a defect.

The power does not wake it suddenly. To place a single bright consciousness across every predation and fracture would be cruelty beyond measure. Imagine feeling each tooth as both eater and eaten, every crushed larva, every parasite ground into grease. Unity is not always mercy.

Instead the power asks a question in the only manner the sea can receive.

It alters nothing living.

It touches the local gravity by less than one part in a million. For nine days the inward pull strengthens and weakens in the cadence of a mill-whale:

**Clang—thrum—thrum—THOOM.**

The whales falter.

Storm-stacks discharge early. Glass kites change their angle. Throughout the sphere, mineral shafts twist in their tissues. Shell colonies close, open, and close again.

Then the power stops.

Years pass.

The western whales retain the cadence. Others absorb it. The pattern enters the circulation and loses its source. It becomes labor again.

So the power repeats the question, but changes the last interval.

Again the sea answers by incorporating it.

The conversation takes one hundred and twelve years.

No creature comprehends it. No generation participates from beginning to end. Nevertheless, the answer emerges. Whales fire. Wind-backs stiffen their leaves. Gear-shells alter their rotation as currents shift. Storm-stacks pass blue signals through the central dusk.

The answer is not *yes*.

It is not *help*.

The oldest life has no concept of help.

The answer, translated into the power’s larger understanding, is:

**There is no unused place.**

The power considers.

It could increase the supply of sulfur and iron. That would enlarge populations until crowding returned.

It could slow reproduction. That would preserve room by making emptiness out of unborn bodies.

It could raise the dead and repair the divided. The sea would choke on immortality.

It could create another mountain and another sphere, but a duplicate would not be an extension. It would be a child abandoned beyond reach.

The wise answer is not plenty.

It is margin.

The power touches the inward gravity again, this time at the outer skin of the sea.

The change is infinitesimal. A rain-bead striking the boundary takes a fraction longer to return. A kite sailing the curved surface finds its old route lengthened by less than the width of one silica spar. No animal recoils. Nothing flashes or cries out.

Over the next century, the spherical sea grows by the breadth of a hand.

Rain supplies the water. The power creates none. It merely permits more of what arrives to remain. Gases enter slowly. Dust from distant valleys, salt lifted from unseen oceans, pollen that cannot live here, soot from fires beyond the horizon—all are admitted in quantities small enough to be transformed rather than poison.

The sphere expands another handspan in the century after that.

And another.

The widening is not tied to time alone. It answers crowding. Where bodies press until movement ceases, the gravity well relaxes outward. Where a stable open region exists, it stops. The power does not promise infinity. It promises that there will always be a little water not yet occupied.

At first, the inhabitants do not use it.

The outer animals continue along inherited paths. Glass kites tack against currents that have shifted away. Larvae turn inward because inwardness is older than nerve tissue. Even with room available, every body seeks stone, exhaust, current, shell, or wound.

Empty water is without function.

For seventy years the new margin remains almost clear.

Then a young glass kite enters it during a storm.

The animal has been damaged. One silica spar is broken, and its broad body pulls unevenly against the water. Under the former curvature it would have been carried toward a black spire and caught among the wind-backs. Scavengers already cling to the fracture, consuming exposed tissue.

But the boundary is farther away than its instincts predict.

The kite tacks once.

Again.

It crosses into water through which no animal of the sea has ever moved.

There is little to eat there. No rock releases electrons. No whale exhaust stuns prey. No old shell provides an attachment. The scavengers abandon the kite and fall inward.

It remains.

With no profitable current to follow, it turns according to pressures generated by its own asymmetry. Its path is broad, wavering, useless.

It heals.

Afterward it does not immediately return.

For eleven days it sails loops in the clear margin. Sometimes it folds its vanes and drops inward. Before reaching the crowded waters, it opens them and rises again. It repeats the motion without feeding, fleeing, mating, or collecting light.

No function can be assigned to this.

The power observes but does not name it.

Later generations of kites follow. Some hunt in the margin after prey begins to venture there. Some breed. Eventually filterers will occupy even the sparse currents, and fan-jaws will find them, and the clear water will become another district of appetite.

But the sphere will have widened again.

There will remain a margin beyond the margin.

The power makes one other change.

It does not remove the mountain’s blades. Instead it sends a faint, distinct vibration through the basalt before each extrusion. The vibration begins years before the rock parts. It is unlike whale labor, unlike thunder, unlike the cracking of shells. Every creature touching stone can feel it.

At first, nothing understands.

Old seam-eaters retreat because the sensation disturbs their mineral whiskers. Young ones remain and are divided as before. Rib-bridges continue to grow across the marked fractures and are broken.

But learning here does not require teachers.

Those that withdraw leave offspring. Those that do not are less likely to do so, except among the lineages for whom division is reproduction. In time, the warning enters bodies as instinct.

The mountain still grows.

Animals are still caught.

Some remain deliberately, though deliberate is not yet quite the word. The splitting benefits them. Others lay eggs along the future blade so that the new basalt carries their young outward into unoccupied water. Ambush mouths gather beside the seam to feed on fleeing animals. Every mercy becomes an ecology.

The power expected this.

Mercy need not remain pure in order to be real.

Last, it attends to an old lamp-skull in the central dusk.

The creature has mapped one region of the mountain for nearly four hundred years. Its body is scarred by arcs, and half its reflective cavity has clouded. A new basalt fin has entered the center of its remembered district, dividing a rib-bridge and sealing three familiar cracks.

For sixty days the lamp-skull has swept the same white plane across the changed stone.

Its pressure bladders contain two irreconcilable maps.

It has no faculty for forgetting either one.

The power could erase the older pattern. Instead it places a small warmth in the final bladder of the chain whenever the beam crosses the new fin.

Not pleasure. Pleasure would be too commanding.

Recognition.

The lamp-skull pauses.

It sweeps back.

The warmth returns.

For the first time, the animal’s memory holds change not as error, but as sequence: rock-before and rock-after. The contradiction becomes time.

This is a perilous gift. Time allows anticipation, but also loss. The power remains near, ready to withdraw it if the burden exceeds the creature’s capacity.

The lamp-skull rests for nine days.

When it begins again, it does not erase the old map. It layers the new one over it. In the soft pressure chambers of its spine, the mountain begins to grow.

Months later, the animal turns its beam away from its hunting ground and projects it into the clear outer water.

Nothing responds.

It shines there again.

Perhaps it is looking for prey.

Perhaps a damaged sensory chain has caused repetition.

Perhaps the first useless circuit of a kite and the first light cast into emptiness are related only in the benevolent power’s hope.

Wisdom does not force an interpretation.

The power does not make itself ruler. It installs no commandments in the lightning. It asks for no worship. Worship would concentrate mineral, energy, bodies, and labor around the signs of its presence. Temples would arise as anatomy. Priests would evolve as parasites. Holy places would become feeding grounds.

So it withdraws its obvious touch.

It leaves only the widening margin, the warning in the stone, and the small capacity in one lineage of lamp-skulls to remember that the world may become other than it was.

The sea remains severe.

Mill-whales still fire their chambers.

**Clang—thrum—thrum—THOOM.**

Fan-jaws close. Spark mites enter the fatal blue light. Whales die against the crags, and colonies quarry their bones. Nothing is made harmless. Nothing is made simple.

But rain now falls into water that has room to receive it.

New basalt blades announce themselves before they rise.

Beyond the crowded machinery, glass kites sometimes move in paths that accomplish nothing.

And in the central dusk, a white beam passes across old stone, new stone, and then the open cobalt beyond them both.

The governing rule is still inwardness.

It is no longer the only rule.
