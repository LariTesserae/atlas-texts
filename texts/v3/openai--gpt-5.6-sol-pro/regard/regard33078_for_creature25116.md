The power does not descend.

Anything descending would strike the white sky, divide the mist, bend the plants beneath its arrival. It would make a place around itself. This would be the first violence.

Instead, it attends.

For a hundred years it learns the difference between the roar of water and the roar as heard through roots. It follows seepage before the seepage has chosen a stream. It enters the slow decisions of moss: where to hold, where to release, where to die and remain useful. It rests in drowned stems, in mineral fractures, in the warm halos surrounding buried cables. It rides with the dark birds through curtains of spray and passes, narrow as a thought, through the intake mesh.

It discovers that the being here has no center at which it can be addressed.

There is no ancient heart beneath the plateau. No buried mind waits to awaken. The living ground knows in the ways available to it. Pressure is known by yielding. Loss is known by the severed exchange of water. Danger is carried chemically from torn roots into neighboring stems. A colony half a kilometer away answers hours later by closing pores, tightening fibers, changing the bitterness of its sap.

This is not ignorance. It is a form of thought without overview.

The power does not gather it into one mind. To do so would destroy the freedom of its many lives and replace their negotiation with command. It does not give the moss a voice, because then the rushes might be silenced. It does not appoint one species steward over all the rest. It does not make the plateau stand up.

It offers no immortality. Immortality would stop the exchange by which dead stems become foundations and drowned colonies become chambers for other things.

What the power gives is smaller.

It gives the being a way to refuse.

The change first appears in the white flowers.

One afternoon, while the sky is at its brightest, every bloom on the eastern terraces closes. Not gradually and not in sequence. Across eleven kilometers, the white points vanish at once, leaving the ground an uninterrupted green.

The insects are enclosed unharmed. Water continues to fall. The turbines continue to turn.

At the nearest station, a keeper named Senn notices because the change alters the light inside a north-facing corridor. She goes outside, tethered at the waist, and sees that the closed flowers form an edge. On one side of the edge, blooms remain open. On the other, not one does.

The line curves across the plateau.

Three days earlier, a remote gate had begun opening toward its new seasonal setting. The adjustment was ordinary: less than the width of a fingernail. No gauge showed danger.

Senn orders the gate stopped.

Twelve minutes later, the flowers reopen.

No one calls this a message at first.

The records say *coincident mass closure, probable atmospheric stimulus*. Sensors are mounted. Pollen is sampled. Light, pressure, temperature, electrical charge, vibration, and trace minerals are measured. The next closure happens around a drainage test on the western shelf. The third follows the activation of an old pressure tunnel.

Each time, stopping the work opens the flowers.

The engineers discover that the closures are not commands against change. Some operations proceed beneath fields of fully opened blooms. Some cause only a narrow band to close along a root seam, and the work can continue if the rate is reduced. Occasionally the flowers close behind an intervention rather than ahead of it, indicating that the plateau prefers the new course of water and resists its reversal.

The workers develop a new gesture: one hand opening and closing at the chest.

It means *the ground has answered*.

Years pass before anyone understands how.

There is no new organ. No miracle-cell appears beneath a microscope. The power has added almost nothing: a slight increase in the distance over which certain fungal threads exchange ions; a sensitivity in the flowers to patterns already moving through water and root; a capacity for countless local strains to be expressed as one visible condition.

No colony knows the whole.

None must.

Agreement travels through them without erasing difference. Moss, sedge, fungus, cistern plant, red stream-lip, root mite, hollow-stem larva—all continue their own lives. Their combined distress, when sufficiently broad and coherent, closes the flowers. Their combined ease opens them.

The plateau has not been given speech.

It has been given consent.

After that, the regional machine changes.

Not quickly. Wisdom that acts too fast teaches dependence, and benevolence that makes itself indispensable is only another form of rule.

The old plans remain in use, but beside every pressure calculation appears a second notation recording the condition of the blooms. Gates once governed only by load and water level acquire limits based on root response. Proposed tunnels are marked not merely by geology but by the continuity of plant colonies above them. Maintenance crews wait through full cycles of light before deciding that an unanswered operation is safe.

Some administrators resist. Flowers, they say, are not instruments. Their closures cannot be calibrated. They vary by season. They may answer conditions unrelated to the works.

All of this is true.

So the keepers learn not to treat them as instruments.

An instrument gives data to its owner. The flowers belong to no one. Their uncertainty does not weaken the refusal. It requires humility in interpreting it.

The power does not punish those who ignore the closures. It does not break their machines or strike them blind. Consequences remain consequences. Once, against the advice of three tenders, a gate is opened through a field that has gone wholly green.

Nothing happens that day.

Seven months later, a root shelf separates along a hidden chamber. Forty hectares of living ground move downslope by less than two meters. No person is killed. The shelf does not fall from the escarpment, but streams change, a causeway buckles, and an intake fills with compressed vegetation.

Repairs take nine years.

Afterward, the report does not describe the event as divine judgment. It says only:

*The closure was accurate. Our timescale was not.*

This sentence becomes part of training.

Other gifts are mistaken for fortunate engineering.

Intake screens begin to clog in patterns that make high-pressure shutdowns gentler. Governors fail toward closure rather than runaway. Forgotten oil reservoirs develop harmless wax at their weakest seals, slowing leaks until they can be found. Ceramic washers taken by birds turn up in nests where surveyors, climbing carefully behind the falls, notice cracks in the root curtains that would otherwise have gone unseen.

The colorless fish remain unproven.

Their protection becomes official anyway.

A young engineer argues that uncertainty is not absence, and an old keeper replies that a screen fine enough for a rumor is fine enough. New bypass channels are cut around the turbines, narrow and dark, with almost no economic justification. Years later, a camera records one pale form entering such a channel and another emerging upstream.

No one can prove they are the same creature.

The recording is archived under both *fauna* and *unresolved hydraulic phenomena*.

The benevolent power is pleased by this.

Not because it desires worship. Worship would concentrate attention on the giver and away from the gift. It prefers the footnote, the revised procedure, the extra hour spent waiting beside an unopened flower.

It acts elsewhere only when scale demands it.

Far beyond the plateau, people with clean shoes and quiet offices propose enlarging the works. The power does not darken their windows or send prophetic dreams. It allows them to understand, for one uninterrupted moment, the full cost of what they are proposing.

Not as numbers.

Each sees the consequence in the form most difficult for them to dismiss.

One hears the future roar after a terrace has torn loose and knows exactly which silence within it means extinction.

One feels, through the bones of the hand, the clean shiver of a cavity collapsing beneath growth older than her nation.

One sees a map not as empty green available for use, but as a dense record of exchanges for which no replacement has been designed.

One dreams of a flower closing around a pale insect, and understands that shelter may resemble imprisonment to anyone unable to wait for morning.

The vision lasts less than a breath.

It compels no vote. Compulsion would relieve them of responsibility.

The expansion is rejected by a narrow margin.

In time, the plateau becomes neither sanctuary nor sacrifice zone. People continue to draw power from it. Turbines continue their deep turning. Parts wear out. Workers arrive and leave. The living surface is cut in places and, in places, wounded. Benevolence does not make purity, because purity here would require removing either the machines or the history that has already admitted them into the ground.

Instead, a more difficult condition is maintained: relationship.

The workers are no longer described in official documents as maintenance personnel alone. The oldest term for their role translates poorly. It means something like *those who make corrections small enough to be answered*.

Their apprenticeship lengthens. Every keeper must learn machinery, water, and living mat together. They spend a season walking no farther than one terrace, memorizing firmness through the soles of their boots. They are taught the difference between a flower closure caused by dusk, by sudden cold, by insect emergence, and by broad strain in the ground.

They are also taught that they will sometimes be wrong.

The power does not correct every error.

It has not come to replace judgment. It has come to make reciprocity possible.

Centuries later, the plateau is still unfinished.

Some turbine houses have vanished into green mounds. Others have been rebuilt with flexible foundations that move as root shelves thicken. The causeways no longer run straight. They curve along the seams preferred by the living ground, and from above they resemble pale threads loosely sewn into cloth.

The country has risen several meters by growth. Two waterfalls have joined. Five have disappeared. The mist carries spores into fresh mineral faces, and dark birds nest behind cascades that did not exist when the first station was built.

The white flowers still open to the whole luminous sky.

Sometimes they close over great distances for reasons no person understands. Work stops. Crews wait beside silent controls while water roars around them. They do not kneel. They check gauges, exchange signs, drink from sealed flasks, and watch the green.

Perhaps the plateau is refusing something.

Perhaps it is considering.

Perhaps some deep chamber has collapsed, or some multitude of tiny lives is passing through a change too large and slow to be distinguished from thought.

The wise power does not explain.

It remains diffused through attention, never becoming an object on the land. Its mercy is not rescue from change, nor protection from every ending. Its mercy is that no great force there—not water, not root, not steel, not human need—must forever act without encountering an answer.

By evening the flowers open.

A keeper places two fingers against the control, feels the patient vibration below, and adjusts the gate by one millimeter.

Then she waits.
