The power would not raise the mountains.

It would consider this first. It would imagine the old valleys emptied, rivers returned to their beds, farms uncovered beneath dripping weed. It would imagine roads appearing from the ebbing water, seals stranded among collapsing kelp, whales turned from their passages, boats settling onto newly exposed stone. It would see the present life of the place fall downward as the sea withdrew: anemones closing in air, fish trapped in the rooms of drowned houses, whole forests laid flat and shining beneath an unfamiliar sun.

Restoration would be another drowning, only reversed.

Nor would it still the tides. The violence of the passes feeds too many mouths. Without the races and upwellings, shoals would thin, birds would abandon their ledges, corals in the ravines would go hungry. The channels would become safer and poorer. Calm imposed everywhere would be a kind of death.

The power would understand that danger and cruelty are not the same thing. Cold water is dangerous. A turning tide is dangerous. A cliff edge, a storm, a hunting whale, a hungry winter: all dangerous. Yet none chooses suffering for its own sake. Wisdom would not ask the place to become harmless. It would ask where harm had ceased to serve life.

So it would descend without a body.

It would enter by the routes already open: rain into peat, tide into caves, sound through hulls, pressure through the bones of fish. It would travel the cables to the converter stations and the roots of birches into fractures in the gneiss. It would pass through kelp holdfasts and the byssal threads of mussels. It would rest for a while in every fastening.

For one full turning of the moon, it would do nothing.

It would learn the place vertically.

It would feel the gannet’s instant of decision above the plunge and the herring’s panic beneath it. It would know the seal’s ease in water and the terror of a pup separated from its skerry in fog. It would inhabit the slow hunger of anemones, the pressure sense of porpoises, the blind patience of lobsters under stone. It would learn what a cable sounds like to a whale and what a turbine’s pressure wave does to the minute organs of a fish. It would hear every lost length of net continuing to fish without purpose.

It would learn the people as well.

It would sit invisibly beside the mechanic trying to keep an old ferry working through winter. It would stand with the diver at the mouth of green darkness. It would keep watch with the supervisor counting down to the turn of the tide. It would enter the dreams of those who had lost someone to the water, but it would not speak there, because grief does not always need instruction. Sometimes benevolence is the refusal to make meaning out of another being’s wound.

When it understood enough, it would make no proclamation.

It would alter a few laws by very little.

The first alteration would concern warning.

Danger already gives signs in that country, but not always soon enough and not to every creature able to suffer from it. The power would deepen the language of the place.

Before a submerged slope failed, the water above it would take on a faint discoloration, visible even in rain. Before a rogue convergence formed in a pass, the surrounding birds would feel it as pressure beneath their wings and lift away in a pattern no local person could mistake. Kelp near an undertow would turn its pale undersides upward. Rock under imminent fracture would make a low, carrying note hours before it fell.

Engines approaching entanglement would change pitch. Mooring lines near failure would hum against the hand. Fog preceding the most violent weather would smell sharply of struck stone.

At first the people would call these coincidences. Then they would write them down.

A child would notice that every dangerous landslide was preceded by a silver-green stain over deep water. A turbine diver would refuse descent after hearing a cable sing in a way no one had documented. An old ferry operator would turn back because all the guillemots had risen together from a cliff in absolute silence.

Each would be proved right often enough that caution became custom.

Charts would acquire new symbols. Training manuals would include observations once dismissed as superstition. Instruments would be designed to detect what bare senses could not. The gift would not replace knowledge. It would make knowledge easier to earn and harder to ignore.

The second alteration would concern machinery.

The power would not break the tidal generators. They kept houses warm, powered radios and pumps, and allowed people to live without feeding every need with distant fuel. But anything drawing strength from a living current would be required, by a law deeper than regulation, to perceive what traveled through that current.

Rotors would feel the pressure signatures of whales and seals. They would slow before dense shoals entered the passes. Their housings would shed vibrations that confused porpoises. Intake grates would become difficult for eels and young fish to approach, not through pain, but through a gentle pressure they could sense and avoid. Cables would cease to mimic the calls by which whales found one another across dark water.

No machine would become harmless. Nothing placed in the world is without consequence. But the machines would become capable of restraint.

Engineers would believe they had invented these improvements.

The power would be content with that.

The third alteration would be made against purposeless capture.

Hooks in a fisher’s hand would remain hooks. Predators would still take prey. Nets under human command would still close around fish, and those fish would still die to feed other bodies. The power would not sever hunger from life.

But abandoned things would lose their authority.

A net torn free in a storm would begin, after three days, to loosen its own knots. Its fibers would swell, separate and become soft enough for trapped animals to tear. Lost pots would open. Loops of discarded line would grow brittle when tightened by a struggling body. Plastic straps would split before they could cut deeply into flesh.

Ghost gear would no longer be permitted to hunt.

The fourth alteration would concern refuge.

Not safety everywhere. Refuge somewhere.

In every large passage through the drowned range, there would be one small place where the violence of the current folded around itself and left a pocket of comparative stillness. Some would lie behind boulders, some beneath overhangs, some within the lee of submerged walls. They would not always be at the surface. They would not be large enough for fleets or factories. They would be places for a tired seal, an injured porpoise, a diver in difficulty, a boat whose engine had failed.

Animals would find these pockets by instinct. People would find them by survival and later mark them on charts. Over generations they would acquire names:

The Held Water.

Mercy Behind the Tooth.

The Breath Below.

Widow’s Pause.

No one would know why the refuges had not been noticed before.

Perhaps some had always been there.

The last alteration would be the smallest, and the power would spend most of its care upon it.

Everything living in the range would be given a clearer sense of the moment when holding became more dangerous than letting go, and the moment when movement became more dangerous than holding.

A limpet would release before poisoned water sealed around it.

A kelp plant would cast its spores before the storm that uprooted it.

A bird would abandon an egg that could not hatch before starving beside it.

A whale would turn from the familiar pass when unfamiliar noise made it lethal.

A captain would cut loose an anchor rather than lose the boat trying to save it.

A diver caught by a line would drop a tool without the fatal instant of reluctance.

A person whose family had occupied one house for nine generations would know, when the sea finally reached the foundations too often, that leaving the house did not mean betraying the dead.

And, in the other direction:

The frightened child in cold water would hold the rope.

The exhausted swimmer would trust the floating weed long enough to breathe.

The mechanic would keep repairing the emergency beacon everyone said was obsolete.

The community would retain the inconvenient rescue station.

The last keeper would remain at the radio through one more hour of storm.

The power could have made stronger changes. It could have prevented every wreck, redirected every storm, filled the trench, warmed the water, abolished predation, made every cliff secure. But then the place would no longer be a place. It would be an arrangement around a promise. Nothing would need to read the water, return to a ledge, tend a light, choose a route, or depend upon another living being.

Benevolence would not make the inhabitants unnecessary to their own survival.

Instead, the power would make mercy part of the terrain.

Years later, people would say the islands had become kinder, though no one could identify when. Fewer boats would vanish without warning. More whales would pass through the outer channels. Kelp would cover the rusted machinery in thicker forests. Children would still be taught that cold water steals breath, that cliffs fail, that ropes tighten, that the tide does not care what anyone intended.

But they would also be taught to look for the pale underside of weed.

To listen when a cable sang.

To know the birds’ sudden silence.

To remember the pockets where water held still.

The oldest local saying had once been:

**The holders cling. The travelers learn the openings.**

A second line would gradually be added. No one would remember who first spoke it.

**And the wise know when to become the other.**
