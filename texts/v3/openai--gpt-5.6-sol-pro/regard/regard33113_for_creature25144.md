### The Attention

The first thing the power does is nothing.

It does not descend into the hollow in a pillar of light. It does not still the reeds so that its voice may be heard. It does not raise the willows upright, clarify the black water, or separate the living from the dead. Those would be acts of preference, and the power has not yet earned a preference.

It attends.

For one full turning of the seasons, it learns the Green Works at the speed of each inhabitant.

It knows the basin as a snipe knows it: a map of concealed landings, alarm, mud, and sky. It knows the cold refuge beneath the root shelf where the last fish gather in drought. It enters the patience of a willow branch resting against wet moss for eleven years before producing a single white root. It follows water into peat and waits there after every visible pool has gone. It learns the warm darkness of the vole chambers and the brief, enormous country given to snails by rain.

It listens to the clatter without translating it into speech.

This is difficult even for a wise power. Wisdom is always tempted to become instruction. Benevolence is always tempted to become rescue. But there is no prisoner here, no silence asking to be broken, no unfinished mechanism waiting for the hand that understands it.

The power discovers no throne from which the Green Works may be addressed. There is no oldest reed authorized to answer, no queen beneath the moss, no spirit hidden in the former channel. The being lives only in distribution. To speak to all of it would require speaking as rain, shadow, mineral, hunger, rot, and the pressure of neighboring roots.

So the power does not speak.

Nor does it give the basin a mind.

It understands that consciousness is not the crown of life but one of life’s many burdens. To gather the whole Works into a single awareness would make every torn frog, drowned vole, parasitized caterpillar, and starving nestling occur in one place. What is now borne locally would become universal pain. The power will not create a sufferer merely so that there may be someone to receive its blessing.

Instead, it becomes the mind for a little while.

It holds the whole place in knowledge without requiring the place to know itself. For the duration of its attention, nothing is overlooked. Every hidden labor has a witness. The nameless larva chewing softened reed beneath black water is as completely regarded as the starling host wheeling above the hollow. The bubble gathering under the root mat is followed until it rises. A seed that falls into a seam and fails is not counted as less meaningful than the one that roots.

This attention changes nothing in the Works.

It changes the power.

Afterward, when it considers what kindness might be done, it looks first beyond the basin.

For the Green Works is complete but not self-sufficient. Its rain is made elsewhere. Its birds require distant fields. Its water arrives through ground whose boundaries no reed can know. The snipe depart beyond the horizon. The starlings carry other countries in their bodies. Even the dark pebbles under the peat were brought from vanished hills by a river that has forgotten this course.

The danger to such a place would not necessarily enter openly. It might begin upstream as a ditch. It might be a road on higher ground, a well driven into an aquifer, a field drained one winter and planted in spring. It might be poison faint enough to leave the reeds standing while removing the insects from between them. It might be a warming too gradual for any one generation of vole or warbler to recognize as catastrophe.

A lesser power would place a wall around the hollow.

The wise power does not.

A wall would preserve the outline and sever the life. It would make an exhibit of a system. The Green Works has no use for inviolable borders; its workers cross every boundary that could be drawn.

Therefore the power lays its protection far away and lightly.

On the hills from which the groundwater comes, springs continue to find stone instead of pipe. In the fields used by the starlings, enough seed remains after harvest. The snipe encounter other wet places on their journeys, some no larger than hoofprints, but spaced so that exhaustion does not become death. Along the river, willows are allowed to lean and fall. Ditches close with sedge. A proposed road takes the firmer ridge, not because anyone has heard a command, but because every calculation happens, accurately, to favor it.

Those who would drain the basin find no villain opposing them. They simply discover that the peat is deeper than expected, the ground less profitable, the machinery unsuitable, the funding uncertain, the alternate site unexpectedly convenient. Their survey marks sink into moss or are carried off by floods. Maps remain correct in every important respect and slightly unhelpful in this one.

Those who come with affection are not turned away. The power does not confuse love with harmlessness, but neither does it make secrecy an idol. A few enter. They step where the mat is firm. They hear the Works before seeing it. Some understand only that it is wet and loud and difficult to cross. Some stand at the bank until their wish to possess the place diminishes.

One among them notices that the hollow appears empty because its life is mostly beneath the level of sight. This knowledge travels outward. It alters a decision years later in another country. The power does not claim credit.

Within the basin itself, it makes only one change.

The old river has not entirely forgotten.

Deep under the raised fields beyond the worn bank, a blocked thread of its former course still holds coarse sand. Water moves through it too slowly to be called a current. The power removes no obstacle and commands no flood. It merely leaves this buried thread unsealed.

Once in a long while—not each year, nor predictably—a season of rain fills the river until water presses into its abandoned ways. A cold seep then appears at the northern edge of the Green Works. It enters quietly at first, blackening moss. Then it lifts a section of root mat, parts two ranks of reed, and joins the central lane.

The basin receives, for several days, what its perfect maintenance would otherwise exclude: moving water.

A little bank is cut. A willow root is exposed. One vole chamber fills, and its occupants flee or do not. Fish enter that have never entered before. Silt no thicker than flour settles across the sedges. Seeds arrive from upstream. Other seeds are carried out. The reeds flatten in one quarter of the hollow and rise there unevenly the following spring.

The Green Works is not improved.

It is kept from becoming only itself.

The power knows that absolute protection is another form of death. To preserve every reed in its place would end the labor by which the place persists. So it grants neither permanence nor immunity. Herons continue to take fish. Harriers find voles. Eggs chill. Willows split. Dry years deepen the peat’s cracks, and wet years drown roots that have grown too confidently into low ground.

One day, perhaps, the river will return in full and tear the living floor apart.

One day, perhaps, the basin will fill with peat until birch and alder overtop the reeds. The open halls will narrow. The clatter will cease. What is called the Green Works will end, though its substance will continue in forest, floodplain, water, and wing.

The power does not forbid this.

Benevolence is not the promise that nothing beloved will ever become something else.

For now, it gives the place what all dispersed beings need and cannot ask for: clean arrivals, possible departures, a margin wide enough for change, and time not purchased at the cost of the surrounding world.

Then the power withdraws its immediate attention.

Not entirely. Some part of it remains wherever restraint is mistaken for absence.

The reeds resume their knocking. Droplets gather on spider lines. Gas rises from the dark and is swallowed by air. A rail screams from the central lane. Underfoot, the old leaves collapse by another imperceptible degree, and new roots enter them.

No sign is left behind.

The Green Works does not know that it has been seen.

It does not know that distant springs have been spared for its sake, or that a road has bent away, or that the buried river still possesses one narrow permission to return. It does not become grateful. Gratitude would require a boundary between giver and receiver that the place has never needed.

It goes on working.

That is the blessing.
