The first thing the power does is nothing.

It does not descend in fire. It does not brighten the sky, still the moving slopes, or speak through the columns. It does not force the many lives of the basin into a single mind so that it may have something convenient to address.

That would not be awakening. It would be replacement.

Instead, the power attends.

It attends for the length of the valley’s seasons, which is longer than the lives of many animals and shorter than the migration of a grove. It follows water from cloud to crown, crown to cistern, cistern to root, root to river, river to the dark chambers under the mat. It learns the pressure language without answering in it. It watches the white grazers leave wounds too small to matter. It waits beside sealed pollinators. It traces the long pale animal through submerged passages and knows each scar on its skin, including the scars not yet made.

It discovers no hidden ruler.

There is no buried heart whose desires outweigh the rest. No ancient intelligence has been trapped inside the forest, pleading silently for release. The valley does not suffer from its lack of speech. Its quiet is not muteness. Its transformations are not attempts to become something else.

The power understands this and is relieved.

It had found worlds where mercy meant intervention: minds held in pain, ecologies narrowed to a final failing species, immortal things unable to die, mortal things prevented from living. In such places wisdom had to take a form and act.

Here, no such wound presents itself.

There is hunger, but hunger passes through bodies into behavior. There is death, but death does not accumulate as waste. There is competition, theft, enclosure, failure, and dissolution. Yet these are not imposed by a tyrant who can simply be removed. They are tensions by which the valley keeps making room. To abolish them would not perfect the place. It would bring its exchanges to a halt.

So the power does not make the grazers invulnerable. It does not teach the pollinators to escape their pods. It does not forbid roots from closing around the swift or the careless. It does not preserve every provisional flower merely because it opened.

Benevolence is not the refusal of all endings.

Nor does the power make the valley efficient. It has seen the downward flowers and useless sails. It knows which will fail before their first cells divide. It could correct them. It could remove waste from every carrier route, reconcile the cheating vines, prevent disease from entering shared roots, and replace the whole congested history with a flawless circulation.

It does none of this.

The valley’s errors are not defects in a machine. They are apertures through which the unanticipated enters. To close them would be to bind the future to the understanding of even a wise power—and wisdom knows that its own understanding, however vast, is not entitled to become the limit of another being’s possibilities.

Still, the power sees farther than the valley can.

It sees the schist ridges wearing down. It sees the continents move, the rain belts depart, the deep chambers cool. It sees the ordinary star slowly alter. None of these is an immediate danger. For millions of Castings the basins may continue, changing beyond recognition while preserving their old traffic of matter.

But the valley has reached an enclosure it cannot perceive.

Its seeds can cross ridges. Its animals can pass beneath them. Its black filaments can preserve channels for thousands of seasons. Yet no adaptation within the basin can make the void between worlds into soil. Given enough time, the valley may invent a thousand forms of flight and never encounter anything but thin air and return.

The power considers preservation. It could hold the mountains unchanged forever, maintain the pearl-gray sky, replenish the buried minerals, and keep every temperature within the old tolerances.

But that would turn the basin into a kept thing.

Its endurance has never come from changelessness. To save its present arrangement would betray the process that made it ancient.

So the power gives it neither immortality nor improvement.

It gives it room.

Beyond one of the remotest basins, where the ridges have always risen into wind-stripped stone, the distances of the universe are made—very gently—contiguous.

No gate appears. No radiance forms between pillars. There is only, over centuries, a slight alteration in what lies beyond a high saddle. From below it remains an ordinary pass: gray schist, cold air, patches of dark scale-leaf testing every fissure. But the far slope no longer descends toward the same continent or beneath the same sky.

It descends into an empty mountain basin on a world without life.

The power has not built a paradise there. Paradise would be another prescription. It has found—or prepared—the minimum: old mineral-bearing rock, water, a cold atmosphere, an ordinary star, weather neither gentle nor impossible. There is real drought. There is frost. There are exposed surfaces on which most seeds will die. Nothing waits to be displaced. No prior ecology will be conquered.

The new basin is not made identical to the old one. Its stone contains unfamiliar ratios. Its gravity is slightly lighter. Its days are longer. Its clouds come in a different season.

It is a place where descendants may become strange.

The passage between them is narrow and initially difficult. The power does not carry rooted columns through it. It does not transplant the valley in sleep. It waits for what the valley itself sends.

During a Season of Casting, pods along the highest crowns turn toward the altered saddle. Pressure gathers. Seeds cross the ridge in nearly invisible streams.

Most fall on bare stone and fail.

Some enter cracks.

With them come desiccated nutrient pastes, fungal cultures, bacteria, and the smallest folded partners. Black-winged pollinators sleep inside several capsules without knowing that the sky above them belongs to another sun.

The first roots live for three days.

The next endure through rain and die in the cold that follows.

For many Castings, the new basin accepts everything and establishes nothing. The power does not soften it. It does not secretly warm each seed. A gift of room must include the freedom not to fill it.

Then one provisional growth persists.

It is low, colorless, and unlike the columns. Its first leaves face the ground. In the old basin it might have been dismantled as useless. Here they trap heat against the unfamiliar stone. Black filaments enter its dead outer tissues and line the first channel. A second growth uses that preserved shape.

Water begins to pause where previously it ran away.

Long afterward, a carrier vine crosses the saddle by increments, abandoning its rearward segments as it advances. White grazers follow the microbial films along its side. They do not understand the passage as a passage. To them it is only another warm seam.

Much later still, when roots have roofed a chain of cisterns through the pass, one of the long pale animals encounters a current whose chemistry does not belong to any basin it knows. It rests at the threshold for a full Compression. Then, when the waters rise, it moves forward.

The power does not celebrate.

It knows that the new place will contain losses the old place never did. Exchanges will break. Partnerships will become impossible. Some descendants will turn predatory in new ways. Some chambers will fail. New diseases will travel back through the saddle until roots learn to close against them. The two regions may one day seal themselves apart.

That, too, is allowed.

The power intervenes only once more.

Far in the future, a wandering body of stone would have struck the original mountains with enough force to sterilize every connected basin in a single hour. It is not part of the valley’s exchanges. Nothing there can answer it, learn from it, or persist through it. It is merely an ending arriving from beyond all relationship.

The power touches that stone while it is still in darkness.

Its course changes by less than the width of a root hair measured across an enormous distance. It passes the world unseen.

No inhabitant is made aware of being spared.

There is no monument to the act. No doctrine begins. No chosen species receives dominion. The ordinary thunder remains indecently loud. Grazers still die. Pollinators are still absorbed beside the pollen they carry. Groves still cheat one another of warmth. Provisional forms rise and vanish. The mountains continue to erode.

The wise power does not ask for gratitude.

It has not saved the valley from change, death, danger, or itself. It has only refused to let accident be the sole boundary of what the valley may become.

Then it withdraws—not entirely, but in the way a hand withdraws from beneath something that has found its balance.

Ages pass.

Under one pearl-gray sky, a hillside lifts and folds in almost complete silence.

Under another sky, farther away than seeds could ever have flown, a dark mat begins to make its own ground.

Neither is a copy of the other.

Between them, for a while, roots continue through the high pass, carrying water, spores, salts, infections, refusals, and forms for which neither basin yet has a use.

The power watches no more closely than it watches anything else.

But it leaves the way open.
