The power would not arrive as rain.

Rain would be the first temptation. From above, the basin’s thirst would seem obvious. A lesser mercy might gather clouds against the plateaus, darken the whole washed sky, and pour until hollows became lakes and the salt went soft beneath standing water.

But rain enough to be called a gift would drown the deep chambers. It would dissolve load-bearing walls, sour fungal gardens, hatch too many dormant things at once, and give advantage to every seed that had waited longest for catastrophe. The grazers’ light feet would founder. Predators would lose the geometry of paths. Moisture would pass from being a precious circulation within bodies to an occupying force outside them.

The basin did not need to become another country.

The power would understand this before the first cloud formed.

Nor would they give the inhabitants speech. The grazers already communicated at the speed required by danger. The builders remembered without knowing. The plants exchanged warnings through roots and fungi. To place a single comprehending mind within that vast mutual labor—to make one creature understand the city entire—would not necessarily be kindness. It might only create the first loneliness.

They would not raise a monument. There was nowhere in the basin where a monument could remain itself.

They would not appoint a ruler. Rule already passed from heat to scent, from hunger to wall chemistry, from buried clay to root, from root to mouth, from mouth to dung, from dung to garden. Any throne would be merely another shaded cavity, and in time an owl would nest in it.

They would not make the basin eternal. Eternity would harden every passage and prevent every inheritance.

So for a long while, the power would do nothing that could be distinguished from attention.

They would follow one grain of salt as it formed around a root, entered the mouth of a grazer, was bound into chalk, dropped on a path, carried into masonry, loosened by a beetle, and returned to dust.

They would wait inside the warm dark of a fungal chamber, feeling workers arrive to turn the comb.

They would travel through the packed soil as a tremor and learn how far a rabbit-sized runner’s warning carried before the message ceased to mean danger and became only vibration.

They would enter the cold hour before dawn as no more than a difference in temperature along the shell of a beetle.

They would discover that the basin was neither suffering nor content. Those were conditions of individuals, brief and exact. A larva starved. A mother found her hidden young alive. A colony sealed its sick behind wet clay. A hunter failed, lay down, and survived by refusing a second attempt. The greater being contained all of these without resolving them.

Wisdom would not mistake complexity for consent, nor balance for justice.

There was cruelty here, if cruelty meant pain without explanation. Parasites ate their hosts from within. Nurseries failed. Animals blinded by dust were taken first. Whole lineages sometimes ended in chambers whose entrances remained intact. Benevolence would feel each of these. Yet to remove predation, hunger, decay, or death would be to pull out the threads by which water and substance passed through the basin.

The power could abolish every wound only by abolishing the lives that could be wounded.

So they would search instead for the place where mercy could enter without becoming conquest.

They would find it far below the city.

Beneath the flat beds of mudstone lay older fractures, compressed since the inland sea withdrew. Most ended blindly. A few reached toward the plateaus but were sealed with gypsum or packed clay. In rare wet years, water entered the heights, descended a little way, and stopped. It could neither return to the air nor reach the basin. It remained in stone until heat and pressure dispersed it uselessly into depths where no root or burrow followed.

The power would touch those fractures.

Not open them. An open channel would become a spring, and a spring would become a center. Around a center, life would crowd, ownership would sharpen, paths would deepen, and the basin’s wide distributed fabric would begin to knot itself around one source.

Instead, they would make the stone very slightly forget how to be sealed.

Across centuries, hairline seams would become porous. No gap would admit a claw. No chamber would flood. Water entering the distant plateaus would pass slowly into the old marine beds and spread sideways through them, not as a stream but as coolness, as darkened clay, as humidity held between grains.

The change would be almost nothing.

A root touching one buried seam would live three days longer into a hard season.

A fungal garden above another would not dry at its edge.

A deep burrower would find soft clay where its mother had found stone.

Moist air would rise through one abandoned shaft before dawn, and beetles would gather there without knowing why.

No creature would possess the gift. No colony could wall it off. No herd could trample it into mud. The moisture would remain too diffuse to invite greed and too little to overturn thirst. It would not end scarcity. It would place a small margin inside it.

The gift would not be water.

It would be delay.

One more night before a nursery failed. One more flowering before a root colony withdrew. One more season in which a population reduced itself rather than vanished. One more chance for moving life to carry memory from a dying district into a living one.

The power would not make survival certain. Certainty would favor the present inhabitants against everything that might otherwise come after them. The basin was still becoming terrain; its future had claims too.

So the seams would yield only under extremity. In ordinary years they would remain deep and nearly dry. In generous years they would accept excess. In long drought they would release what they held grain by grain. They would function as the city already functioned: through storage, inheritance, and innumerable exchanges beneath notice.

Perhaps, over ages, the inhabitants would alter the gift.

Roots would widen the seams. Burrowers would join one damp layer to another. Builders would raise towers above the cooler ground. Grazers would make paths between those towers. Seeds would collect in the paths. Rare rain would follow them, carrying silt into shallow depressions.

What the power began as mercy would cease to belong to them.

It would become another accident used by life.

This would please them.

Before leaving, if leaving were what such a power did, they would perform one further act.

They would look upon the whole basin—not as wasteland, not as an unfinished place awaiting rivers, not as a city because its structures happened to resemble cities, and not as a single animal because metaphor made its immensity easier to hold.

They would see it without reducing it.

Every worker would remain a worker, not merely a cell in a greater body. Every grazer would remain the center of its own brief world. Every moth would keep the full importance of its one night. The collective being would be real, but it would not consume the reality of its members.

The power would hold both truths at once.

And because they were benevolent, they would not demand that the basin know it had been seen.

There would be no sign in the sky. No new instinct would turn the animals toward worship. No tower would lean toward a sacred direction. The builders would continue placing grains where moving air cooled one antenna more than the other. Owls would sleep in vents. Hunters would inherit dens. The dead would be partitioned into a thousand future uses.

Only in the worst dry ages would something inexplicable occur.

The basin would approach its ending and fail, slightly, to end.

Far below, the stone would remember rain.
