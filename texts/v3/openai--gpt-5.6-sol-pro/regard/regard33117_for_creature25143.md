The power did not descend as light.

Light would have entered too deeply. It would have woken the lower gardens, bleached the bacterial lamps, and drawn the colorless fish into streets where their bodies could not turn. Nor did the power speak. A voice large enough to reach the whole Ward would have shaken scale from the pipes and frightened the grazers into the gears.

It did not still the machinery, because stillness belonged to the great adjustment.

It did not purify the water.

It did not straighten the towers.

It did not free the buildings from their roots.

It did not raise the Ward into air.

Benevolence without wisdom might have done any of these.

Instead, the power remained beyond the retaining wall and paid attention.

For nine years it watched heat gather beneath roof gardens and pass by degrees into stone. It watched children learn to cross rooms without waking the silt. It watched gardeners argue, by pressure laid wrist to wrist, over whether a mat of translucent roots had enclosed enough of a cracked lintel to inherit it. It watched an elder release a remembered current into pipes that no longer followed their first routes.

It watched one tooth of the eleven-year gear grow.

When the tooth was finished, the power understood that haste would be a form of violence.

So it began with a current.

The current was no wider than two joined fingers. It entered the Ward over the northern wall, passed beneath a leaning balcony, curled around a filter tree, crossed the central square without disturbing the bell-shaped creature, and left through an old drainage grille in the south. It carried no foreign spores. Its temperature matched the street through which it moved. Its salt and metal altered as it went so that no membrane had to resist it.

At first only a listener noticed.

She lay against the housing of a shallow pump and felt a pressure that did not belong to any turbine, tide, body, or remembered mechanism. It was too steady to be a leak and too delicate to be weather. She followed it hand over hand until it crossed the retaining wall and disappeared into the outer sea.

She made no alarm. She reported it by touch to her household, and her household sent the pattern to the Condenser.

The Condenser made no reply.

The current returned the next day.

It returned the day after that.

It followed precisely the same route.

In the seventeenth year, ribbon kelp began to lean toward it.

In the twenty-third, children used it to carry seed husks from one court to another. In the thirty-first, a pale film established itself along the underside of its route, though there was no pipe or wall there. Gardeners ruled that the film did not own the current, because it had colonized only one side of something that, strictly speaking, had no sides.

In the forty-eighth year, the current paused outside the capsule of an elder whose body had become too sensitive for public lines. The pause was so slight that only the elder perceived it. She placed her palm against the membrane.

The current pressed back.

Not hard. Not enough to deform the seal.

Four touches in two opposing pairs.

The shape of a Warder hand.

The elder gave her contact name.

*Here.*

The current replied with a sequence no Warder knew, but whose intention was unmistakable.

*May I continue?*

The elder did not answer alone. Such a question belonged to every chamber the current crossed, every garden it brushed, every machine whose heat altered it. Listeners mapped its effects. Gardeners examined its films. Households compared the pressure against their doors. Children were asked whether it made the streets easier or harder to understand. The bell-shaped creature was observed for a full turning of the lamps, though it did nothing beyond its daily rise and settlement.

The Condenser prolonged one cycle by seven minutes.

This was taken as neither consent nor refusal.

The deliberation lasted fifty-two years.

On the hundredth anniversary of its first passage, the current became, by law, a public road.

The Warders gathered along its length. No one occupied the middle. Hands rested on rails, walls, roots, and one another. Formal skins dimmed their rosettes. The elders entered the sensory lines. In the central square, the bell-shaped creature lifted its customary few inches.

The current moved among them and gave the question again.

*May I continue?*

The answer passed from wrist to wrist, into cables, through cultivated films, along pressure pipes, and at last into the Condenser.

*You already do.*

Only then did the power enter.

It entered without increasing the current’s speed. It folded the smallest portion of its awareness through that two-finger-wide road and learned the Ward from within.

It tasted copper behind the teeth.

It felt the sweetness of an overheating bearing.

It learned the fatigue of every leaning tower and the minute discomfort of roots pressed too tightly against glass. It felt the city’s oldest ceramic seals, each swollen to the precise limit of endurance. It read the mineral memory held in sediment and the layered electrical traces of generations who had followed the same rails.

It discovered the dead where they had become beams, nodules, valve seats, lattices, and gardens.

To a lesser power they might have seemed dispersed.

To this one they were not.

No substance remembers being a person merely because it once belonged to a body. But every act of care leaves consequences, and consequences touch other consequences without end. The wise power could follow those continuities. In a pressure collar grown from the bones of a patient worker, it found the survival of a pump. In that pump it found the warmth of a nursery. In the nursery it found a child who would one day alter a foundry mesh, making gears that wore more evenly and released less metal into the water.

The dead were not present as ghosts.

They were present as permissions.

The power held all of this lightly. It did not tell the Warders that it could perceive such chains. They already touched beams before important work. They did not require proof.

Then, beneath the oldest streets, the power found what the Ward could not yet feel.

Far below the shaft, where dense cold water rose toward the hidden turbines, the bedrock was changing. A deep seam had begun to close—not quickly, not catastrophically, but with the same patience as everything else in the Ward. Minerals accumulated along the throat of the earth. In three hundred and twelve years, the rising flow would weaken. In three hundred and nineteen, the lower turbines would fail to overcome their own weight. The foundries would cool. The water would clear. Light would penetrate the deepest courts.

The lower gardens would bloom.

Their bloom would be beautiful.

It would also break the city open.

The power could have cleared the shaft with a thought. It could have held the rock apart forever. It could have turned the turbines without any water passing through them.

It did none of these things.

A gift that must be given forever is sometimes only a gentler form of conquest.

Instead, it showed the Ward what it had found.

Not as prophecy. Prophecy would have arrived without evidence, demanding trust. The power released a pattern through the public current: the taste of the deep seam, the pressure signature of its narrowing, the probable sequence of turbine loss. It repeated the pattern slowly enough for listeners to compare it with the city’s own records.

For eleven years the Ward investigated.

The evidence returned from every measurement.

The shaft was closing.

There was grief, though no public sound marked it. Mourning skins appeared in workshops and gardens. The elders withdrew from the lines more often. Young Warders lingered near the retaining wall and looked into the outer darkness. A child released a chain of bubbles beneath a balcony and was not corrected until the last one vanished.

Some wished to ask the power to open the rock.

Some wished to refuse all assistance.

Some argued that the deep seam, having carried water along the same route for more than a hundred years, was a public road and could not legally be closed even by the earth. Jurists replied that the earth was not a resident of the Ward and could not be compelled. Gardeners asked whether the mineral growth inside the shaft constituted habitat. Engineers objected that nothing had been found living there. Gardeners answered that participation, not metabolism, established a claim.

The dispute continued for decades.

The power did not resolve it.

Wisdom does not mean having no answer. Sometimes it means refusing to steal from others the work by which an answer becomes theirs.

At last the Warders asked the power one question.

*What can be done without making the Ward cease to be itself?*

The power considered the phrasing.

Then it carried them beyond the wall.

Not bodily. Their bodies remained secured among the streets. But through the narrow public current it extended their senses into the outer sea: first the listeners, then the gardeners, then anyone who wished to join.

They felt the slope descend into cold darkness.

They felt plains of sediment no Warder had touched, trenches where blind fish followed ancient magnetic lines, forests of glass sponges growing around weak geothermal springs. They tasted waters with almost no metal in them. They found currents that had not yet become roads.

Far to the west, beyond a ridge, lay another shallow depression.

It was empty.

The Warders recoiled from that emptiness. There were no handrails, no walls, no layered chambers, no enclosed roots, no pressure pipes curving around the obligations of earlier pressure pipes. Water moved through it without consultation. Sediment lifted wherever it pleased.

To the Warders it seemed less like a place than an absence of participation.

The power did not call it a new home.

It simply let them feel the warm seep beneath its floor.

The seep was weak but enduring. With patient shaping, it could drive one turbine. One turbine could power a mineral frame. One frame could grow a beam. A beam could support a pipe. A pipe could bend a current. A current, after a hundred years, could become a road.

The depression would not replace the Drowned Ward.

Nothing could.

But the Ward had never truly been preserved by keeping each stone where it was. It was preserved by the transfer of obligations: tool to apprentice, culture to relative, memory to pipe, body to beam, room to garden, architecture to habitat.

The power offered no completed city.

It offered room.

This was the one substance the Ward could not manufacture for itself.

Even then, the Warders did not accept quickly.

They sent grazers first, enclosed in pressure vessels planted with familiar films. Then they sent a mesh seeded with foundry organisms. After twelve years the mesh had grown a ring no larger than a household door. After twenty, a gardener traveled there in person and remained attached to the ring for six months, feeling how it took the seep’s warmth.

She returned with her skin covered in a pale organism unknown to the old Ward.

There was immediate dispute over whether the organism could enter.

It was kept outside the retaining wall in a suspended ceramic bowl. The power did not make it harmless. Harmlessness imposed from above would have made every later judgment dependent upon the power’s continued approval. Instead, it helped the listeners perceive the organism clearly: its exchanges, tolerances, reproductive cycle, and effect on old seals.

The Warders decided for themselves.

The organism was admitted to one abandoned chamber under quarantine. It consumed a corrosive salt that the Condenser had always struggled to balance. It also dimmed amber bacteria and made several grazer breeds sluggish. After forty-three years of adaptation, it was accepted as a difficult colleague.

A household formed around responsibility for the western seep.

Its members called themselves, by a contact sequence that could mean *those who go while remaining* or *here, extended*.

They cultivated the first wall.

They did not build it upright. The empty water made uprightness seem too assertive. They grew the wall low against the floor, curved around the seep, porous enough for sediment creatures to pass through. When a native threadlike colony covered three sides, construction stopped. The wall was declared habitat.

So they began another wall beside it.

This delayed the first pressure chamber by nineteen years.

No one considered the time wasted.

The benevolent power kept the public current between the two places. It did not carry bodies, tools, or heat. It carried only what the Warders asked it to carry: touch sequences, pressure records, lamp patterns, warnings, questions, the electrical signatures of children learning their names.

After one hundred years of use, the current between the old depression and the new became a public road.

After two hundred, houses grew around its western end.

After three hundred, when the deep shaft beneath the Drowned Ward weakened at last, the city was ready—not to survive unchanged, but to perform an adjustment greater than any before it.

The deepest gate opened and did not close.

Cold water rose through the Ward in one immense, soundless column. Roof gardens streamed upward. Silt unfastened from the walls. The bell-shaped creature lifted above the square.

This time it did not settle.

Its tendrils drew free from the cracks, revealing that they had passed beneath every statue and into a small ceramic vessel buried at the center of the city. The vessel opened as the creature rose. From it came a pulse older than the surviving records, older perhaps than the Ward’s oldest walls.

The power received the pulse.

It meant:

*May I continue?*

The power answered as the Ward had answered it.

*You already do.*

The bell-shaped creature crossed the retaining wall. For the first time, the colorless fish lit their chains within the city. One by one they followed it westward along the public current.

The old Ward did not empty.

Many inhabitants remained. They rerouted what heat was left, surrendered the lowest factories to bloom, and sealed their richest water inside the upper capsules. The Condenser changed its preferences. Chambers it had protected for centuries were opened and their dormant cultures sent west. Elders divided their sensory lines between two districts.

The lower streets filled with translucent leaves.

Ceramic split beneath patient roots.

Towers leaned farther and came to rest on gardens prepared to receive them.

What might once have been called destruction became, under the Warders’ care, a transfer of function.

The new district never became a copy. Its water was cooler. Its architecture spread farther apart. Native glass sponges claimed the spaces between houses, and because they had colonized those spaces before any streets were established, roads were obliged to curve around them. The western inhabitants learned to cross brief expanses of open water. They found the practice terrifying, then vulgar, then exhilarating, then ordinary.

Children there still learned not to wake the silt.

They also learned, sometimes, to swim.

The power remained attentive but did not make itself ruler, guardian, engine, or god. It repaired nothing that the Warders could repair. It prevented no death whose prevention would have required freezing the whole district outside time. It did not declare every loss necessary or every adaptation good.

When asked, it revealed what no finite listener could perceive.

When permitted, it carried what no ordinary current could bear.

When not needed, it stayed beyond the wall.

Its greatest act was not saving the Drowned Ward.

It was understanding that the Ward did not wish to be saved from change, nor abandoned to it.

It wished to participate.

And so the power gave it what all wise benevolence gives, when it can:

warning without command,

help without possession,

room without exile,

and companionship vast enough to be patient.
