The power does not descend in light.

Light, here, would be an injury. A radiance sufficient to announce benevolence would dry the exposed clay, startle the frogs from their folds, send the ants into alarm, and turn each clear drop under the leaves into a burning lens. Nor does it speak. There is no language common to queen, root, fungus, river, and worm, except consequence.

So it attends.

It enters no chamber. It chooses no species as the rightful inhabitant. It does not mark the fig as sacred, for sacred things are sometimes fenced away from the processes that sustain them. It does not stop the snake at the grass, or blunt the gecko’s hunger, or spare every larva from the flood. A mercy distributed life by life would soon become another disaster: too many mouths, too little rot, predators starving beside prey preserved beyond reach.

The power is wise enough not to confuse kindness with the prevention of death.

For a long while it does nothing that can be distinguished from understanding.

It follows the worm shafts downward into old sediment. It feels where water lingers after the surface has dried, where roots can descend and where an ancient layer of compacted clay turns them aside. It attends the minute exchange of gases through termite walls. It knows the cool interval between a frog’s breaths. It follows sugars from fig leaf to root tip, then into fungus, beetle, mite, and soil. It learns the city not as a map but as a set of transformations.

Every inhabitant appears within it without becoming small.

The black ant carrying a dead springtail is not one among thousands but exactly herself: six feet testing the uneven ground, one antenna damaged, abdomen lean from the recent wet, following a scent already fraying in the warmth. The termite soldier plugging a breach is felt in the pressure along his head. A nematode turning in a film between two mineral plates occupies the power’s attention as fully as the river does.

Yet attention alone cannot make them immortal, and the power does not try.

What it notices first is not suffering, though there is suffering everywhere. It notices narrowness.

The city can survive flood because there is higher root. It can survive drought because there is deeper mud. It can survive predation because prey are many and shelters intricate. It can survive collapse because no chamber has only one use. Its strength lies in alternatives.

But beyond the grass, changes have begun that the inhabitants cannot sense.

Far upstream, rain has loosened a raw slope where trees were burned. At the next great storm, the slope will enter the river. The river will carry more than water: branches, stone, gray slurry, the sudden weight of a hillside. The fig has endured many floods, but not this one. The hollow will be filled in a single night, not with the thin renewing skin of silt to which its lives are adapted, but with a depth of airless mud taller than the city and nearly as tall as the outer roots.

No ant can prepare for it. No scent can cross that distance. No root can thicken in response to a pressure that has not yet come.

Here the power acts.

Not beneath the fig.

Upstream, where its touch will look like weather, a gust arrives from an unimportant direction. It turns the crown of a half-fallen tree. The trunk drops across a side channel rather than into the main current. Leaves, branches, and stones gather against it. During the first rain the barrier holds long enough for the loosened earth to spread through a broad reed bed. Sediment settles among stems. Water passes onward brown but not murderous.

The reeds bend. Some are buried. Fish retreat to the clearer margins. Beetles climb the stalks. Nothing is saved without inconvenience to something else. But the hillside does not strike the fig all at once.

Farther downstream, the flood still comes.

The city responds in its old manner. Eggs rise in white processions. Termites seal low galleries. Worms withdraw. Frogs cling above the waterline. The lowest streets disappear. A beetle larva drowns in its provision chamber. A fungus garden sours. Two ant routes are severed, and one colony loses a queen.

The power allows these losses.

It grieves them without overturning them.

The water reaches the base of the high buttress and pauses. In the night it leaves a pale layer over the courtyards, thick enough to remake them, thin enough to breathe through. By morning, the flood has begun to sink.

Then the power performs a second act, smaller than the first.

A seed husk carried in the water lodges between two roots. Ordinarily the retreating current would draw it out again. The power adds no force; it merely withholds one tremor of water. The husk remains.

Behind it collect three leaves, a splinter of bark, fruit skin, and loose grains. A low bar forms across one of the drainage runnels. Water divides around it. One branch returns to the old channels. The other enters a bare patch beneath a buttress where the soil has always dried too quickly for moss.

For seven mornings, moisture persists there.

Algae wake first. Springtails follow. A worm finds the softened ground and opens a descending passage. Fine fig roots enter after it. In time, the new channel becomes a narrow refuge—not a permanent one, not a perfect one, but another possibility. During a later drought, it will hold dampness half a day longer than the surrounding clay. Half a day will be enough for a column carrying brood to cross it. It will be enough for one frog’s basin to remain until rain.

The inhabitants do not recognize a gift. Recognition is not required.

The power considers whether to give them more.

It could make the walls endure. It could turn the soft clay to stone, preserve every gallery, keep floodwater forever to appointed courses. But then roots could not enter abandoned chambers. Worms could not erase boundaries. The city would accumulate its past until the past obstructed every living motion. Permanence would privilege the present arrangement over all futures.

It leaves the walls mortal.

It could make water abundant. A clear spring might rise beneath the fig and fill the cisterns in every season. But abundance would summon larger animals. The hollow would become a drinking place. Hooves would crush roofs, birds would strip the exposed colonies, vines would close the open ground, and species adapted to brief waters would be replaced by those that could dominate lasting pools.

It leaves water intermittent.

It could warn prey of every hunter. But the geckos must eat, the snake is not wicked, and vigilance without failure would empty the hunters before it spared the hunted.

It leaves danger legible but not avoidable.

It could unite the city in common knowledge: give each ant awareness of all streets, each termite understanding of river and tree, each brief fly a memory of the layers below. But knowledge at that scale would not free them. It would burden each small life with disasters it had no body to address. The worker needs the scent before her. The worm needs dampness. The seed needs no theory of forests in order to open.

It leaves their minds fitted to their lives.

But there is one thing the power gives freely.

When a life encounters what exceeds it, the power does not always enlarge the life. Sometimes it enlarges the world’s capacity to receive what that life has done.

A dead ant is found before her body molds uselessly in a sealed crack. A drop falls near a dormant rotifer rather than a grain’s width away. A fungal thread reaches the husk a beetle larva cannot finish. A root tip turns into a chamber rich with old dung. Not every time. Not so often that chance disappears or hunger loses meaning. Only often enough that fewer endings are absolute.

Under this attention, waste finds a mouth a little more readily. Empty spaces acquire entrances. Barriers develop edges. When one path closes, the conditions for another are sometimes already gathering.

The gift is not safety.

It is continuation.

Years pass in the hollow.

The fig grows around one buttress and abandons a fine root elsewhere. Termites occupy the dead channel, then leave it to mites and pale mold. Weaver ants stitch a roof over the newer watercourse. A storm tears the roof loose, and half of it becomes a raft. The raft carries workers into a district from which they had long been separated. They fight there. They also carry spores.

The snake returns, older and scarred. One evening it takes the frog whose tadpoles survived in the husk-diverted pool. Those tadpoles have already left the water. Most will die before maturity. Two will return in a later wet season and call from opposite sides of the same buttress.

Nothing becomes just.

Everything becomes possible a little longer.

Beyond the floodplain, the power’s larger work remains mostly negative and unseen. An axe edge glances from the fig’s trunk and bites the user’s boot sole without reaching flesh. The person curses, notices the rain coming, and chooses another tree nearer the path. A fire advancing through dry grass meets a strip of ground still wet from an overlooked seep. A dredging plan is redrawn because survey stakes will not hold in the bank. A child searching for a lost bead sees the root hollow, kneels, and is stopped by wonder before pushing a stick through the walls.

The child does not understand the city entire. No one does.

But she replaces the leaf she lifted.

That, too, is enough.

The benevolent power never claims the place. It appoints no guardian species and leaves no law written in bark. Its care is expressed as restraint, redirection, and the preservation of choices. It cannot make a floodplain cease to flood without destroying what a floodplain is. It cannot rescue exchange from loss, because loss is one of the means by which matter passes onward.

So it protects no single shape of the city.

It protects the city’s right to keep changing shape.

And when, centuries later, the river finally moves away—as rivers do—the power does not force it back. The fig declines. Its crown opens. Sun reaches the hollow more often. Ferns burn at their edges. Ant colonies depart along root roads into the grass. Termite chambers dry and crack. Seeds that slept beneath buried districts wake in the new warmth.

The last water pocket becomes a dark stain, then disappears.

This is not prevented.

By then, however, the city has already left itself.

Its minerals are in beetle shells and bird droppings. Its fungi travel as spores. Its ants have divided into daughter colonies beneath other trees. A frog hatched in one of its hoofprint pools has laid eggs half a river bend away. Fig wasps have entered fruits in a distant canopy. The clay of its walls, swallowed and cast by worms, lies dispersed through soil where new roots feed.

Even its arrangement survives—not as a plan, but as a tendency carried in many bodies: to tunnel where dampness gathers, to build where fiber meets mud, to follow the edge, to turn obstruction into shelter.

The power attends these departures as it attended the city.

It does not mourn the hollow as though the hollow alone had been the life.

Beneath another tree, after rain, a worker ant crosses fresh silt carrying a white larva in her jaws. She finds a crack made by a root that thickened around an old worm passage. The crack is dry above and moist below. It is narrow enough to defend.

She enters.

Others follow.
