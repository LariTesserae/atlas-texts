The power would not begin by entering.

It would know that there are forms of help which resemble invasion from the inside. It would see the slit in the southern face, the red-sealed doors, the weight piled around the grille. It would understand that the people had spent generations naming danger by the direction from which it came.

So it would remain outside.

It would kneel on the salt flat beside the low hill and place one hand upon the polished stone.

For nine days it would do nothing else.

The abrasive wind would bury its feet. Salt would whiten its shoulders. Travelers, if any passed, would see only a figure bent over a boulder, listening.

And it would listen properly.

Not only to the roar, though the roar traveled through the hill and up the bones of its arm. Not only to the foundational note, nor the clatter of vanes, nor the thousands of ropes and leaves and hollow façades answering one another. Beneath all that it would feel the smaller signals: the pavement announcements; the builders testing blocks; a child asking *again* with two pauses and a heel-blow; lovers whispering names into lawful walls and unlawful ones; the sleepers waking when the Neighbor passed.

It would hear the city as the inhabitants heard one another: through contact.

It would learn that the note beneath the grille was pain, but not only pain.

It was also effort.

Something lay folded within the stone, larger inside than the stone was outside. It had been folded for so long that compression had become its shape. The hollow was not its belly or heart or lung, though it resembled all three. It was a chamber the being had made around an injury, as flesh makes a blister, as a tree makes a burl around an embedded blade.

The city had grown within that mercy.

The people were neither its children nor its parasites. They were not dreamed figures, not trapped souls, not caretakers appointed by some forgotten god. They had come later, from elsewhere, by a history almost entirely erased. But over generations their construction had become useful. Their walls divided dangerous harmonics. Their towers vented pressure. Their gardens altered the hot breath. Their bridges tied opposing strains together.

Their oldest belief was partly true.

An unfinished structure did attract what they called the Outside—not because the Outside was hungry for edges, but because an abrupt ending allowed the being’s pain to concentrate there. Families had disappeared when streets failed, ducts burst, or space itself tightened like a cramp. What the people called “release” had often been death.

But the endless building was no longer healing the wound.

It was bracing the scar.

The ancient plans had once been precise. Repeated without understanding, they had become ritual, then government, then fear. Some new walls eased the being. Others pinched it. Some stairs carried vibration harmlessly into empty rooms. Others returned it to the center. The weights around the grille did almost nothing, except make the people feel that they had not abandoned their duty.

The Neighbor was not a separate creature.

It was touch.

Unable to unfold, unable to see, the being extended attention through itself. Wherever that attention passed, the stone briefly forgot to sing. The broad impressions within sealed walls were the marks of something feeling its own boundaries, as a sleeper runs a hand over an injured limb in darkness.

The power learned all this.

Still it did nothing.

Wisdom had taught it that understanding suffering did not grant ownership of it.

On the tenth day, the power moved one finger against the southern face.

It tapped.

The signal was very slight. Inside, it entered through the slit and passed into a minor retaining wall, through an abandoned ropewalk, beneath three sleeping rooms, and along a planted causeway. Most of it vanished in the city’s noise.

But listeners in the southern district raised their heads.

The signal came again.

Two pauses.

One blow.

*Again.*

Children answered first.

Their heels struck floors all through the district. Again. Again. Again.

Adults caught them and stilled their legs. Foundation drums ordered silence. Priests entered the nearest wind court. Dust rose in agitated spirals and refused every known category.

Outside, the power waited.

The next evening, when the roar diminished, it tapped a different message. Its grammar was poor. Its certainty moved through the wrong shoulder, metaphorically speaking. It confused *stone* with *household* and used the wrist tense reserved for dead people whose shutters had not yet torn free.

But the message was understood.

*I feel you.*

The inhabitants did not answer.

The power waited through the thirty-three-day dimming.

When the ceiling brightened again, the city sent a question through the southern foundations. It required hundreds of drummers because the old pavement was broken and the districts disagreed about the proper form.

*Are you Outside?*

The power considered every honest answer and found none that would comfort them.

It tapped:

*Yes.*

Panic moved faster than language.

Doors were sealed. Children were carried into inner rooms. Kilns were smothered. The priests ordered three new walls built across the southern avenue and condemned six listeners for having replied without authority.

The power did not prevent any of this.

It did not split the hill to prove gentleness. It did not fill the dry city with water. It did not silence the roar, reveal the sky, heal the injured being in one magnificent gesture, or place truth whole and blinding into every mind.

Instead, it remained beside the stone.

Day after day, it learned to speak.

It answered questions when questions came. When they did not, it listened. It admitted uncertainty. It distinguished what it had felt from what it inferred. It learned the proper tactile equivalents of lowered shoulder, narrowed eye, open hand.

At last it sent:

*I will not open what does not wish to open.*

After a long pause, it added:

*I will not keep closed what wishes to open.*

The city debated this for three years.

During that time the power made one intervention.

There was a staircase in the western district, ancient and ceremonial, climbing through seven vacant houses before ending against a blank wall. The old plans required another flight. A household of eleven had been sentenced to complete it, though three generations of seam-readers had warned that the wall beyond answered at a dangerous pitch.

The builders began anyway.

Outside, the power laid two fingers on the hill and produced a counterpressure so delicate that only the condemned household felt it. The wall’s hidden stresses appeared as lines of coolness under their palms. For the first time, they could feel not merely that the stone was wrong, but how.

They removed one block.

The whole western district exhaled.

The afternoon roar lost a harsh overtone that had cracked teeth there for centuries. Bladder-fruit stopped bursting before ripeness. A tower straightened by the width of a finger. In the sealed rooms behind the stair, three dead shutters tore free at once and sailed laughing through the avenue.

The priests called it coincidence.

The builders called it evidence.

The people had no word for a structure improved by subtraction. For a while they used a cumbersome phrase meaning *completion by merciful absence*. Eventually this shortened to *mercy*.

Mercy spread.

Not quickly. Never without argument. The city had suffered too much from invisible forces to trust invisible counsel. Every alteration was tested through nine cycles. Every removed stone was kept nearby. Priests watched dust; listeners watched priests; children, who had less reverence for either, tied ribbons everywhere and discovered currents the old plans had ignored.

Some structures were completed.

Some were dismantled.

Some blank walls were allowed to remain blank.

The first public edge was a terrifying thing: a stair intentionally ended in open air, its final tread rounded and planted with red moss. Guards stood around it for a year. Nothing came through.

Or rather, light came through.

And warm air.

And once, through the southern slit and along a sequence of newly aligned mirrors, a narrow blade of actual sunlight reached the stair.

The people had possessed brightness all their lives, but not sunlight with a source. They gathered around it and watched dust cross its boundaries. They put their hands into it. They were astonished that light could have an edge and that the edge did not hunger.

The power’s work with the being beneath them took longer.

It did not speak to the being in words. The being’s thought was too slow, too spatial. A question required a season to cross it. An answer might arrive as a change in curvature, a migrating warmth, or a new chamber no larger than a seed opening deep within stone.

The power asked:

*Do you wish to unfold?*

For seven years there was no answer.

Then the central note ceased.

The people froze throughout the city. Hands opened. Knees bent. The grille keepers pressed their curled palms to the tiles.

The silence lasted longer than any in memory.

Far beneath them came the deliberate inhalation.

Then, through every foundation at once, the being answered with a single immense contraction.

*No.*

The power accepted this.

There were younger powers that would have mistaken refusal for confusion. There were crueler ones that would have called unfolding the being’s true nature and broken the hill in the name of liberation.

This power understood that not every closed thing is imprisoned. Not every wound is healed by restoring what existed before. The being had lived folded for an age. It feared the open immensity outside the salt flat. It feared its own original shape. It did not wish to become what it had been.

So the power asked another question:

*Do you wish the pain to lessen?*

This answer came quickly.

The grille lifted from its setting.

Only the width of a leaf. Only for one breath.

But every citizen in the basin saw green stone rise without hands beneath it.

*Yes.*

Then began the gentlest reconstruction the city had ever known.

The weight-keepers removed the first ring around the grille themselves. No divine hand touched it. They dismantled the abandoned millstones, numbered the slabs, and carried them away on shoulder poles. Under the power’s guidance, listeners traced the foundational tone outward and found where it knotted against old construction.

A market wall was pierced with seven openings.

A buried arcade was filled.

Three towers were shortened.

A kiln court became a garden.

The dangerous western staircase was not extended. Its removed stones were made into low seats where old builders gathered at evening to argue about the meaning of mercy.

As pressure eased, the thing in the walls began to pass more often. Traveling silences crossed inhabited districts. At first people fled them. Later, a few remained.

They discovered that if they stood with palms against the wall while the attention passed, they could feel something vast feeling them in return.

There were no thoughts in it they could name. Only recognition, uncertain and immense.

A child once tapped *again* into the quiet.

The wall pressed back.

Two pauses.

One blow.

After that, the Neighbor was still feared, but images of it were no longer forbidden. This produced thousands of terrible drawings. Children gave it legs, teeth, wings, wheels, windows, and elaborate ceremonial hats. Masons objected to all of them. The being itself appeared indifferent.

The wise power gave the city no river. A river would have killed the root-writing plants, dissolved old mortars, awakened salts, drowned the grazers, and made the inhabitants dependent on a miracle their world had never needed.

It gave them, instead, a method.

At the southern slit it shaped nothing, but showed mirror turners how to create a surface colder than the surrounding air. On certain nights, when warmth reversed through the hidden passages, a film of moisture gathered there.

The first drop was smaller than a lentil.

It hung beneath a ceramic lip while half the city waited.

No one knew what to do. Some wanted to drink it. Some wanted to preserve it in oil. The priests wished to consult dust, which was difficult because everyone had crowded into the wind court.

At last an old gardener touched the drop to the root of a fan tree.

The tree shuddered so violently that three leaves fell off.

For nine days it appeared ill.

Then it produced a new kind of blossom: small, translucent, useless, and intensely blue.

The people loved it immediately.

They did not begin watering their gardens. They were wiser than that. Moisture remained medicine, sacrament, experiment, and danger. But it was no longer unimaginable.

In time the power built one thing.

Not inside.

Outside the southern slit, upon the salt flat, it raised a small arch of pale stone. The lintel was proportioned to the present inhabitants, not the vanished tall builders. The passage beneath it led nowhere except from one patch of salt to another.

It left one side unfinished.

This was not a test, though the people assumed it was. It was an invitation.

For eleven years no one approached.

Then a household known for bad masonry, unauthorized adoptions, and disputes with priests sent out their youngest seam-reader. She crawled through the slit wearing three layers of felt, wax in one ear, and enough smothering sand to extinguish a star.

She became the first citizen in recorded history to stand outside.

The sky caused her to collapse.

Not from heat or brightness, but from distance. There was no ceiling behind the glare. No implied wall. No curvature returning the eye to itself. Space continued with such indifference that she lay on the salt and clutched it, certain she would fall upward.

The power did not lift her.

It lay down nearby.

Together they remained flat against the earth until sunset.

When the stars appeared, she wept so violently that she exhausted the household’s entire supply of mourning thread.

In the morning she set the final stone in the arch.

Not because an edge was dangerous.

Because this one was hers to finish.

Others followed. Some went outside and returned within minutes. Some stayed for days. A few built low shelters in the lee of the hill, though no one called these a second city. They were careful with names. They planted nothing in the salt. They learned the abrasive winds and mapped the stars with gestures grand enough to require the whole body.

Most remained inside.

The hollow was their home. Its roar had shaped their bones. Its hot dry gardens fed them. Their dead waited in upper rooms. They did not need to abandon the city merely because the city was no longer a prison.

The power never entered.

In the end, it rose from beside the hill. Salt fell from it in white sheets. Its handprint remained briefly on the southern face, vast but shallow, and then the wind polished it away.

Before leaving, it placed its palm once more against the stone and sent a final message through every wall, tile, stair, root, rope, shutter, and listening bone.

Its grammar was now flawless.

*You are not unfinished.*

The people felt it.

So did the folded being beneath them.

The city did not stop building. That would have been another kind of command, and making had become one of the forms of their joy. They built rooms for children not yet born, bridges whose only purpose was to cast moving bands of shade, towers tuned to harmonize with the afternoon note instead of fighting it. They built a hall where plans could be refused. They built doors in sealed districts and waited before opening them.

They also learned to leave spaces.

Courtyards with no stalls. Walls ending in moss. Staircases that rose to views and went no farther.

And in the lowest basin, around the old green grille, they removed the final useless weight and replaced it with a ring of blue flowers growing in dry crystal beds.

Once in every thirty-three-day dimming, the foundational note now ceased.

The silence no longer made every face go blank.

The people stood with bent knees and open hands, as before. But then someone—often a child—tapped a greeting into the floor.

Far below, slow as geology and gentle as a sleeping pulse, the city’s oldest inhabitant answered.
