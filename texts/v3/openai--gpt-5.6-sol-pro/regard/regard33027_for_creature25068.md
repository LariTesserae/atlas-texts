The power did not enter the basin.

It could have come as a body tall enough to step over every wall. It could have stood in the center without sinking, stopped the rain, opened the buried courses, and seen all the inhabitants at once. It could have lifted the ribbed house and held the oldest body naked in the air. It could have named each colony, straightened every gutter, separated every fusion, restored every broken tool to the form intended by its first maker.

It did none of these things.

It saw that there were kinds of violence which resembled help.

Instead it remained beyond the outermost ring, where the gray vapor concealed whatever supported the horizon. Its attention lay upon the basin lightly. No condensation quickened beneath it. No bird turned its square wings upward. No hidden hand froze around its work.

For seven floods it watched.

Then it learned not to watch all at once.

To regard the basin as one creature was to overlook its borders. To regard it as many creatures was to overlook the flood. The power divided its attention without diminishing it. It attended to each dry enclosure separately. It followed every branch of running water as though the stream were a wall too high to see across. Where two colonies touched only through tannin, vibration, or a ceramic pellet passed downstream, the power did not imagine a unity they themselves did not possess.

This courtesy took longer than omniscience.

It learned the western shelves by their thin ringing. It learned the household beneath the three stools by the unequal lift of its hidden mouths. It learned the colony of the eastern frame in the warmth traveling through the vine. It learned the young settlements around the rush baskets, nervous and quick to close their grates. It learned the old body beneath the ribbed house, whose answers took nearly an entire water cycle to form.

The power found no single mind to ask.

So it asked the basin in the basin’s own ways.

In one enclosure, it altered the interval between two drops. Not greatly: one fell, then another, then two together. The nearest wall mouth closed. After a time it opened and returned the sequence as three faint pulses of bitter water.

In another, the power pressed upward beneath a slab with no more force than a buried bud might exert. Hidden hands gathered there. They felt the pressure, withdrew, gathered again, and answered with a series of minute taps.

At the ribbed house it sent a breath through one empty slot while the birds were away. The old body considered this for so long that moss grew across part of the opening. At last, one dawn, the house exhaled from a different slot.

The power asked no question for which the basin had words. It could not ask, *What do you desire?* Desire was not carried in any single fluid. It could not ask, *Shall I preserve you?* Preservation meant nothing apart from the manner of preserving. It could not ask, *Who made you?* The answer would have been a thousand gestures, none aware of being an answer.

Instead it asked:

*Where does the pressure hurt?*

*What fails before you can reach it?*

*What returns too seldom?*

*What comes too soon?*

*What must remain divided?*

*What may be passed on?*

The replies occupied many cycles.

Some households asked for opposite things. A pool colony held its drains shut longer, asking for deeper water. The settlement beneath the neighboring ledge hammered for dryness. Frogs wanted open wall mouths. Young hunters gathered wherever a crack admitted warmth. Birds nested best where those same cracks remained cold.

The power did not resolve these differences into a greater good that none had requested.

It learned the small agreements.

Every colony, when pressure traveled beneath the central pavement, answered.

Every mature body changed its fluids when the vine’s warm drops failed to come.

Every region reacted to a particular deep note below the northern wall: not alarm exactly, but recognition followed by helplessness.

There, beneath eleven pavements, a wooden conduit had nearly ceased to be hollow.

It had once carried the basin’s slowest current. Tannin-rich water passed through it only after the largest overflow, bearing chemical traces from the outer ring toward the central archive. Over ages the conduit had softened inward. Mineral bloom thickened its walls. Rootlike muscles from three colonies had grown around it, each mistaking the weakness for support. No single household could reach the obstruction without crossing active water. No flood was strong enough to clear it.

Soon the conduit would close.

Nothing immediate would die. The pools would continue. Birds would wheel. Crabs would stack their dams. But the outer colonies’ slow messages would no longer reach the oldest body. Their young would still drift inward, carrying legs and knots, but the archive would cease answering them. A separation would become silence, and because the process was gradual, no creature would know what had been lost.

The power could have cleared the conduit with a thought.

It did not.

A sudden opening would have driven centuries of black sediment into the central chambers. The oldest body would have tasted every stored message at once. Valves would have opened together. Boundaries might have drowned.

Nor did the power strengthen the failing wood. To preserve it unchanged would only make the inhabitants dependent upon a material they could no longer produce.

Instead, during the stillness after an overflow, the power caused one bead of condensation to remain warm.

It hung beneath the north wall, no larger than a frog’s eye. A young hunter found it. The hunter drank, but the warmth did not pass into its gut. It remained in one old tool-handle leg: a piece of dark wood polished by a vanished palm, fitted poorly at the upper joint.

The hunter turned toward the deep note.

It had never traveled that way. No path in the borrowed wood led there. The route was broken by channels, and the creature did not cross them. It walked the full perimeter of its enclosure, tightening its bindings twice. Where the dry tile ended, it put the warm leg into the stream and released it.

A crab seized the piece.

The warmth passed into the crab’s claws. It carried the leg downstream, not because it understood, but because warm objects attracted mineral bloom, and mineral bloom marked good dam sites. At the first grate, a mouse stole it. At the next overflow, the mouse abandoned it on a submerged shelf. A second hunter recovered it after the flood.

Thus the leg traveled through nine territories.

Wherever it went, the warmth caused hidden tissue to loosen from the masonry for a moment. Hands emerged. Mouths opened. The deep northern note became clearer.

The inhabitants began to investigate.

They did not know a benevolent power had guided them. They knew only that an old piece of workmanship had acquired a useful property and that the property led toward strain.

For forty-three floods, clay plugs appeared in deliberate lines. Crabs were encouraged by mineral traces to move particular pellets. Mice carried cords through drains. Hunters settled in cracks above the conduit and broadened into working bodies. The eastern colony sacrificed two lengths of vine fiber. The household beneath the stools surrendered the smallest bone needle. It vanished below and did not return.

No worker understood the whole labor.

Yet, by degrees, they built a second passage beside the first.

It was narrow, uneven, and made from hundreds of short ceramic throats rather than one wooden pipe. Between the throats they packed clay that remained flexible underwater. Each section could be reached from one dry enclosure or another. No future repair would require a body to cross the line where it ended.

When they were ready, the old conduit was allowed to collapse.

Its softened wood entered their mouths. Its tannin darkened the pools for three cycles. Its fibers became cords, gaskets, food, nests, and the first legs of many young hunters. Water entered the new passage with a low, uncertain moan.

The outer colonies sent a bitter pulse.

Long afterward, the ribbed house exhaled warm air through its eastern slots.

The connection had not been restored. It had been remade.

Only then did the power permit itself one act that the inhabitants could not have done.

Beyond the north wall, concealed in vapor, there had long been a hollow place. It might have been part of the basin once, or only a flaw in whatever held the horizon. It had no paving. No channels shaped it. Water entering it vanished without returning. The power did not fill it, roof it, illuminate it, or make it safe.

It moved the mist back by the width of one tile.

Nothing in the basin perceived this directly. But condensation changed on the north wall. One stream began to carry a trace of something unknown: not poison, not food, not any command. It was a fine red clay unlike the basin’s fired earth, soft enough to hold an impression and slow to harden.

The first grains were swallowed by worms.

Later grains gathered in a gutter bend. A crab rolled them into a pellet, but the pellet would not keep its shape. The crab struck it several times and abandoned it.

A hunter found it. The creature tested the red clay with its mouthparts. It ate some, passed some through its body, and discovered that what emerged could be pinched thinner than the usual plugs. It sealed a hairline crack that generations of broader clay had failed to enter.

The repair lasted one flood.

Then it washed away.

The hunter returned to the gutter.

Other hunters came. They tested charcoal, mineral bloom, tannin, crushed shell. Hidden adults altered the material in their warm interiors. Most mixtures failed. One hardened too quickly and split a tile. One remained soft and was eaten by mice. One produced a bitter pulse so strong that three drains closed for half a cycle.

After many attempts, a workable mixture appeared.

They used it first beneath the north wall. Then, slowly, almost accidentally, they began laying a gutter toward the place from which the red clay came.

The power did not finish the gutter for them.

It did not tell them what was beyond the vapor. It did not promise that expansion would be harmless. It did not make the unknown resemble what they already knew.

It gave them no immortal material, no final design, no perfect tool.

It gave them a reachable difficulty.

Years—or whatever passed for years beneath the continuous dimness—went by. The new gutter advanced a finger’s breadth, then cracked. It was rebuilt. A settlement formed beneath the nearest dry tiles. Young hunters began carrying red-stained legs. The central archive acquired a small unfired object bearing marks no vanished human hand had taught them to make.

At last the gutter touched the outer wall.

There the inhabitants stopped.

Water could be led onward. The opening could be widened. But doing so would establish a new boundary, and no colony yet lived beyond it to answer from the other side.

For several floods nothing happened.

The power waited without impatience.

Then, during the coldest stillness, the colorless vine produced its five bells. Four held their usual drops of warm water.

The fifth turned north.

Its drop fell into the unfinished gutter.

Threads tightened inside the eastern frame. Hidden hands adjusted lashings. A translucent sac entered the flow and lodged against the outer wall. It remained there while floods rose and withdrew. Within it, a soft gray body formed six rings of muscular attachment.

Before it woke, small objects began to gather beside it.

A feather shaft.

Two reeds.

A mouse bone.

A bent twig hardened in smoke.

A fragment of an old handle.

And one leg made from the new red clay, fired badly, cracked at the joint, and unlike any leg the basin had remembered before.

When the young hunter emerged, it fitted these pieces to itself.

Then the inhabitants opened the wall by the width of its body.

The hunter did not step over running water. A trickle went ahead of it, extending the boundary as it moved. It followed the channel into the gray.

The power withdrew farther, making room.

Behind the hunter, the basin continued as it had always continued. Drains gulped. Frogs sounded inside the walls. Birds erupted from the ribbed house and circled once. Crabs knocked ceramic stones together. Beneath the pavement, old bodies pressed clay into cracks that would one day open again.

No monument was raised to the power.

No prayer named it.

Nothing was made flawless.

But beyond the north wall, after a long while, there came a faint tapping.

The basin listened.

Then, through its new passage, it answered.
