### The Fourth Notch

The power did not enter as light.

It knew what light would do there: blind the watchful, wake dormant growth, call down grazers from the roof folds, and turn every wet surface into a mirror of terror. Nor did it enter as warmth. Warmth enough to be called miraculous would soften the walls, hatch every waiting egg, stir rot in the food chambers and send the mother plant into sudden growth.

It did not still the water. Still water would sour.

It did not harden the streets. Streets that could not yield would tear the living crown.

It did not kill the Near Moon when it next laid its vast pink head beneath the leaf. The salamander, too, was hungry; and in galleries beyond the city it consumed beetles whose young would otherwise have hollowed the crown to death.

The power could have lifted the fallen leaf and opened the pocket to the sky.

It understood that this would be indistinguishable from the end of the world.

So it remained outside for a long while.

It attended.

This was not the attention of a hunter, which narrows until only movement remains. It was not the attention of a builder, which sees all matter as awaiting a hand. It was not even the attention of pity, which too often mistakes an unfamiliar life for a damaged version of one’s own.

It learned the pressure cycles of the plant. It followed water through capillaries finer than the inhabitants’ hairs. It felt the white roots preparing their blind advances and the old rind loosening grain by grain. It learned which fungal threads fed the walls and which merely consumed them. It counted the eggs beneath the warm chambers. It listened to sleepers turning their constrictions into dreams.

Only then did it notice what none within the pocket could know.

At the next hard thaw, a raft of dead reeds would shift in the marsh. One broken stem would catch the fallen leaf. The leaf would be drawn upward, not far, but far enough to open the eastern seam. Cold air would pour through. The upper chambers would freeze first. Then rain would enter in drops too large for the channels. The city would endure perhaps an hour.

The power could prevent this with less effort than the inhabitants used to lift a hatchery tray.

Yet still it waited.

Benevolence without patience is merely force with a kindly name.

At last it sought a way to announce itself.

It chose the dull bright needle.

The needle belonged to no household. It was kept in a shell case near the Still Court and loaned for the most exact work: opening mite eggs, separating silk, releasing splinters from beneath the skin. Six districts had inherited it in turn. Its first owner was forgotten.

During a low phase, while Bent Awl inspected a clay collar and Two-Bridges sorted drowned springtails from living ones, the needle tapped against its case.

Once.

A pause.

Twice.

The nearest people froze.

The needle tapped again, though nothing touched it.

The pattern was not one of theirs. It was too measured for dripping water and too slight for a beetle. It passed through the shell, into the platform, and from there into the root skin beneath the Court.

Weight, it said.

Then, after a pause:

No tongue.

Then:

Listening.

The Still Court emptied without panic. Quieters closed the approaches. Track-readers pressed jaws to the floor. Water-keepers laid hands upon the nearest veins.

The pattern came again.

Weight.

No tongue.

Listening.

Old Moss-on-the-Third-Notch arrived from the western warm root. She had lost most of the sensitivity in her feet and all but two fingers on her left hand, yet no one living understood the speech of pressure better.

She touched the shell case.

The needle rose beneath her fingertip.

Not into the air. Only enough for its point to be felt.

Moss answered by tapping with one nail.

Where?

The reply came through the needle.

Everywhere that bears you.

This answer was distrusted immediately.

Anything claiming to be everywhere might simply be very near and very large.

They asked whether it was above the roof.

Yes.

Below the root?

Yes.

Inside the water?

Where water permits.

Inside them?

Only where you permit.

This answer caused a greater disturbance than the others. The gathering withdrew to padded chambers and argued in breath. Some believed a predator had learned complex tapping. Some believed the mother plant had at last answered in a language too quick to be its own. Some thought the metal remembered another place and had begun repeating what it had heard there.

A child called Found-under-Ivory asked the question that ended the first council.

If it is everywhere, why has it only now become polite?

The question was carried back to the needle.

The power answered:

Because only now have I learned how not to shout.

After that, they listened.

The power did not tell them what it was. Every true answer would have been larger than the city’s language, and large answers are often only another kind of shouting. Instead it told them what would happen at the thaw.

Not all at once.

It described the tension in the outer leaf. It indicated the eastern seam. It taught the water-keepers how to detect the faint pull of the broken reed long before it shifted. Scouts climbed as near the roof edge as the cold allowed and found the fibers already drawn in the direction the needle had named.

Then the power said:

I can prevent it.

The city held council at an open scar.

The needle was laid among the evidence, beside cords from the eastern seam, shavings of strained rind and a cup of water taken from the upper channels. Every witness touched the objects in silence.

“What does prevention require?” Moss asked.

One movement outside, the power replied.

“What will that movement crush?”

Nothing that knows itself crushed.

“What will it feed?”

A colony of water fleas. Three midge larvae. Rot.

“What will it starve?”

Nothing beyond recovery.

“What obligation follows?”

None to me.

This was the least believable answer it had given.

Every act laid an obligation somewhere. A bridge borrowed tension from its anchors. A meal borrowed from decay. A sealed wound obligated the hand that had opened it. Even a kindness shifted weight.

They asked again.

At last the power amended its reply.

The obligation is mine: not to make your survival into my ownership.

The council accepted prevention.

Outside, where none beneath the leaf could see, the power bent one dead sedge by the width of a root hair. When the thaw came, the broken reed slid past the fallen leaf instead of catching it. Rain struck elsewhere. The eastern seam trembled but did not open.

The city lived.

There were some who wished the power to remain in the needle and answer every question.

Where should the next bridge stand?

Which fungus should be scraped away?

Will this child recover?

When will the water rise?

Is the thing in the wall a beetle or only growth?

Where did our drowned ancestors live?

What becomes of those made available?

The power answered few of these.

A bridge placed by certainty would not teach its builders to read strain. A harmless fungus named too easily might become dangerous in another season. A child’s recovery belonged partly to chance, partly to care, partly to the child. The dead could not be turned into directions without making grief smaller than it was.

But there was one question it answered completely.

Moss asked, “What can you give us that will not make us less able when you are silent?”

For three pressure cycles the needle did not move.

Then it led the water-keepers to a neglected nodule below the Still Court. The nodule was translucent, cold and no larger than a curled sleeper. It swelled in response not to the local water level, but to pressure deep in the marsh root—pressure that began changing long before the basin rose.

They had mistaken it for useless new tissue.

The power showed them how to collar it without cutting, how to train a pale fungus over its surface, and how to stretch three strands of larval silk from the collar to a leaf membrane. As the deep pressure increased, the nodule expanded. The silk tightened. The membrane bent.

No sound was necessary. A water-keeper needed only to lay a finger against it.

The warning came well before the first notch.

They built no shrine there. Shrines occupied space and suggested permanence. They built a platform, a guard cord and a lifting sling for those unable to climb down to inspect it. Children learned the route blindfolded. Water-keepers taught bridge-plaiters, wall-tenders and cold-handlers how to read the membrane, so the knowledge could not be hoarded by one office.

They called it the Fourth Notch, though it was not a mark and stood below all the others.

It did not stop the floods.

It did not lower them.

It gave time.

Time to lift the slow.

Time to divide food.

Time to move hatchery trays without dropping them.

Time to test two routes instead of trusting one.

Time to close wounds in the plant before abandoning the lower streets.

Time for fear to become action before action became panic.

The power stayed near, but not always in the needle.

Sometimes it was present only as restraint. A white root altered its course by a fraction and spared a sleeping chamber, though it broke the food store beside it. A drop that would have struck a bridge met a roof hair first and divided into three smaller drops. Once, when the Near Moon pressed under the leaf during a flood rise, a midge swarm erupted beyond the western edge, and the salamander turned toward easier hunger.

Most events it allowed to proceed untouched.

Houses closed. Tools were lost. Predators fed. The old sickened. Children made errors no wisdom could remove without also removing childhood. The mother plant accepted company in one district and refused it in another.

The inhabitants sometimes accused the power of neglect.

It received the accusation without anger.

Sometimes they thanked it for events it had not caused.

It received the thanks without stealing it.

Years later, Moss-on-the-Third-Notch was made available in an old pith chamber. Her coat went to three households. Her shell cuff became intersection markers. The surviving fingers of her left hand, wrapped separately, were placed near the Fourth Notch—not as relics, but so young water-keepers would remember that the first person to question the warning had done so with a hand that could barely feel it.

The dull bright needle passed to Found-under-Ivory.

By then the child had another name, and then another. They carried the needle for exact work and rarely asked it questions. When they did, the power sometimes answered.

More often it did not.

This, too, became a kind of trust.

The city spread where the plant permitted. It remained cold, dark, wet and impermanent. No golden light entered. No wall became eternal. The dead were still made available, and grief still had nowhere fixed to reside.

But below the Still Court, a membrane bent beneath a waiting finger.

And sometimes, before danger had yet become visible, a water-keeper would feel the first faint tightening and send a message outward through the city:

Not water.

Not yet.

Prepare.

Then careful intention moved through the darkness, while there was still time to choose its shape.
