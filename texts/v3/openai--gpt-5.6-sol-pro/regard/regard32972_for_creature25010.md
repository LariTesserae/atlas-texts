The power does not descend.

Descent would be too simple a direction, and arrival too violent an event. Anything capable of crossing the distances between worlds is capable of mistaking speed for mercy. Anything powerful enough to alter the plateau in an instant could destroy in an instant what it had not taken time to understand.

So the power waits.

It waits through silver rains and dry centuries. It watches forests cross districts. It follows a single impulse from a pressure drop in the western fern fields to the turning of a black stone in the oldest city nine thousand years later. It learns which repetitions are memory, which are labor, which are injury, and which only resemble injury to a mind accustomed to bodies.

It does not pity the inhabitants for having no faces.

It does not imagine them incomplete because they cannot gather themselves into one place and say *I*. It does not bring animals to quicken the silence, or birds to make the sky seem occupied. It does not warm the air, straighten the roads, reconcile the depths of reservoirs with the thickness of the world, or force sound to occur at the moment of impact.

These are not absences awaiting correction.

The power learns the inhabitants by permitting them to alter it.

At first it makes itself no more than a tendency in condensation. Water gathers a little more readily along one unused channel in each city. Not enough to fill the channel. Not enough to disturb the moss that has grown there. Only enough that, on certain mornings, beads form at intervals corresponding to the stones in the counting courts.

The inhabitants respond as they respond to any new condition.

Roots approach. Porous ceramic draws the moisture downward. Frost displaces three pale stones. In a southern city, a roof flexes without rain. A grove alters its leading edge by the width of a seed.

The power waits for the response to propagate.

It takes eleven thousand years.

When the answer reaches the summit, it is not an answer in any language the power already knows. A doorway opens onto a street that has never existed. Beyond it stand seven empty loom frames under a yellow sky. Their ratchets advance together. No fiber crosses them.

Then the doorway closes.

The power considers.

It could enter every channel and know every arrangement at once. It could hold the whole plateau in comprehension as easily as sunlight holds the upper surface of a leaf. But perfect observation is not the same as understanding. Understanding requires allowing another being to determine the terms on which it can be known.

So the power narrows itself.

It submits to frost. It permits parts of its attention to be delayed in stone and diverted through roots. It allows the unreliable distances to separate one portion of its awareness from another. It becomes, for a while, unable to know what it knows until the knowledge returns.

For the first time in its existence, the benevolent power experiences a thought as maintenance.

A western gate closes. A northern cistern answers. A field of clear leaves turns toward a star visible only in reflective stone. The relation among these events enters the power not as information but as strain distributed across centuries.

Only then does it understand the empty looms.

They are not asking to be filled.

They are asking whether the stranger can refrain.

The power withdraws its condensation from every active channel. It leaves the unused ones untouched. It does not attempt another sign for many cycles.

This is its first message.

The inhabitants take a very long time to decide that the silence is intentional.

Across the counting courts, discrepancies accumulate. Pale stones form open arcs. Dark stones gather in their centers, then are carried outward by roots. One city completes the pattern before another begins it. In the oldest city, the summit roots lift from the reflective rock, exposing a shape free of all living contact.

The shape is neither welcome nor refusal.

It is room.

The power enters only that room.

It places nothing there. Instead, it makes one relation dependable.

Not a road. A fixed road would cut through too many wandering selves. Not a doorway. Doors already possess meanings, hazards, and old affiliations. Not a new law imposed upon space.

The power establishes a meeting that can be reached from everywhere but approached from nowhere.

Once in a long interval—when frost reaches the center of every clear leaf that happens to be living, whether those leaves stand in the same season or not—each counting court acquires one additional stone.

The stone is small, dark, and locally made.

In one city it is basalt. In another, fired clay. In a third, a knot of root hardened by minerals. Where no court remains, it appears as a patch of exposed bedrock. It is not the same object in different places. It is the same position in a pattern.

Whatever touches that position may exert pressure upon every other instance of it.

The pressure is not carried. It does not travel. No road can fold it back, no temporal district can delay it, no shifting doorway can deliver it to the wrong age. Yet the power limits the connection carefully. It transmits no command, no image, no overwhelming simultaneity. Only pressure: minute, directional, answerable.

A root thickens against the dark stone in the east.

In the north, another stone tilts.

A cistern level changes in the south.

On the summit, a dormant vine contracts for the first time in sixty thousand years.

The plateau discovers a form of touch.

For several centuries, nothing else happens.

Then the inhabitants begin to test the meeting.

A city presses once. Another answers twice. A migrating arrangement enters the relation in one basin and leaves it in another. Rhythms cross. Some cancel. Some amplify until frost fractures the stones, after which replacement stones appear through ordinary geological and botanical labor. The power does not make them unbreakable. Unbreakable things cannot fully belong to a world built from repair.

Gradually, the dark position is incorporated into civic bodies. Roots braid around it. Water clocks drain toward it. Looms use its tilt to vary their knots. Fields fire seeds in response to distant pressure. The plateau’s dispersed awareness gains no center, but it gains a place of mutual immediacy.

For the first time, some inhabitants can know that an answer exists before they have waited centuries to receive it.

They do not abandon their older speech.

Roofs still darken at sunset. Groves still change species in response to patterns begun before their germination. The long timing of shade remains meaningful. Patience is not merely an inconvenience from which wisdom should free them. Much of what they are can exist only because an answer has time to become landscape.

The new touch serves another purpose.

It reveals absences.

An eastern arrangement presses a sequence that once belonged to a being distributed among twelve frost fields and a temporary river. For ages, the sequence has caused empty frames to cut reeds and sealed channels to open before rains that no longer come. The city has preserved the recurrence faithfully, though its answering part vanished into a doorway whose destination ceased to exist.

The sequence enters the dark stone.

Nothing answers.

It enters again the next winter.

Nothing answers.

On the third occasion, distant cities respond—not by imitating the missing participant, but by exerting a low, sustained pressure around the unanswered rhythm. They hold its shape without completing it.

The useless work pauses.

A cutting branch remains raised above the reeds. A ratchet fails to advance. Water reaches a sealed channel and passes beside it into moss.

The pattern is not erased. It is received.

The power had believed it was giving the plateau contact. Now it understands that it has also given the inhabitants a way to distinguish remembrance from obligation.

Across the cities, ancient tasks begin to change.

Some continue exactly as before, their persistence affirmed by distant answering pressure. Others slow. A few cease. Bundles of reeds are carried out from beneath roofs by roots that move a finger’s width each year. They are laid in frost fields and allowed to separate into fiber, dust, and soil. Empty looms are not dismantled. Vines grow through them without engaging their mechanisms. Channels to vanished reservoirs become gardens.

Nothing is discarded quickly.

Release takes its place among the forms of maintenance.

The power does not decide what should be relinquished. It does not identify grief and cure it. The distinction belongs to those who have borne the repetition. Its gift is only that no act must continue forever merely because no one being can know whether another still depends upon it.

This would have been enough.

A wise power knows that enough is a sacred boundary.

But before withdrawing, it perceives a question gathering at the plateau’s center.

Fine roots advance across the reflective summit and surround the impossible stars. Their rings intersect the dark position in the counting court. Cities press through it at different rhythms. Doorframes open onto the green garden, the ancient avenue, the wet darkness. Behind one frame, the upright branching forms stand motionless beneath their burdens of stone and seed.

The question takes nearly five thousand years to become clear.

It is not *What are you?*

The inhabitants have little use for identities detached from participation.

It is not *Why have you come?*

Purpose is something they read in continuation.

The question is:

*What work do you require in order to remain?*

The power had not expected hospitality to take this form.

It could remain without work. It could sustain itself beyond stars, weather, matter, duration. Yet to accept the plateau’s welcome while needing nothing from it would preserve an unbearable inequality. A guest who cannot be affected is only another kind of ruler.

So the power chooses a need.

It places one small imperfection within its meeting.

At irregular intervals, the shared dark position loses alignment. Not catastrophically. The touch among cities becomes faint, then uncertain. To restore it, the inhabitants must coordinate local acts: one court admitting frost, another shading its stone, another allowing a cistern to fall below an old mark, another opening a gate at an hour when no road passes through it.

No city can maintain the connection alone. No single arrangement understands the whole repair. The work changes each time because the plateau changes.

The inhabitants accept.

The repair becomes part of them.

In return, their labor does more than preserve the gift. It alters the power. Each restoration adjusts the meeting according to local distinctions the power would never have invented. The contact acquires seasons. Temperaments enter it. One city makes the shared pressure circular. Another introduces intervals of deliberate absence. In the north, the meeting falls silent whenever stars appear at noon. In the south, it begins to answer sounds before they are released.

The power becomes less universal and more present.

It is no longer merely benevolent toward the inhabitants.

It is in relation with them.

Much later, in the oldest city, one of the upright branching forms moves while observed.

The event is almost imperceptible. A jointed bough lowers its basket to the avenue. Coiling fibers release the handle. The basket rests on stone.

Inside are dormant seeds, cloudy ice, black pebbles, mineral dust, and a loop of woven root containing tensions from a city that no longer exists.

The form does not step through the doorway.

Instead, a crack opens in the summit. One seed falls from the basket and lodges there.

The power does not hasten its germination.

Rain comes after three hundred years. The seed swells. A root enters the reflective stone and divides along lines too fine to see. One thread reaches the dark position of the counting court. Another enters the doorway smelling of wet soil. A third descends toward the cities.

At evening, frost engages across the plateau.

Clear leaves turn, not all toward the stars and not all toward the sun. Some turn toward directions that only other leaves can feel.

A pressure enters the shared dark stones.

It is not warning, command, gratitude, or prayer.

It is the civic touch of innumerable arrangements making room for one more.

The power answers with the smallest pressure it can make.

A gate settles.

A channel opens.

In an abandoned workshop, a loom that has been silent for ages advances by one tooth—not because it is compelled to repeat its old work, but because new fiber has been stretched across it.

Far away, or long ago, or in the next instant the land can hold, a stone knock is released into the cold.

This time, something answers before the sound is gone.
