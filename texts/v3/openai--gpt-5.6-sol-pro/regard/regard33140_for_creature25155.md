## The Unanswered Place

The power did not bring a sun.

A sun would have blinded the broad irises of the people, ruptured the ceiling membranes, woken a billion dormant spores, and driven the accords into a frenzy of flowering. It did not bring warmth. Warmth without water would have meant fermentation, pressure, and death. It did not send rain everywhere, for the buried vessels would have swollen faster than the hollow could unfold.

It did not make the ground still.

Stillness imposed upon the Undergreen would have been another word for killing it.

The power was benevolent, and therefore it was careful. It was wise, and therefore its first act was not an act.

It attended.

For a long while—long even by the calendars of root and pressure—it learned the hollow without asking the hollow to learn it. It did not press a pulse into the sensing veins. It did not cast a reflection on the pools. It did not enter through dream, blood, sap, inheritance, or name.

Nothing turned toward it.

This was difficult. Attention in the White Undergreen had always been reciprocal. To notice was to touch; to touch was to teach expectation. But the power knew an attention that did not hunger to be answered.

It listened to the sap-clearers feeding and freezing around their eggs. It followed the old routes written into sailback leaves. It perceived the interval beasts sleeping beneath warm rot, each dream stretched across decades. It knew the underhands in their dark cups and did not force them into any single shape.

It listened to the holdings and to the people within them.

It heard every household name spoken through living walls, and kept each one secret.

Last of all, it attended to the old engrafted woman in the root-column.

She had not spoken in four generations because speech had ceased to belong only to her. Her breath traveled into leaf pores. The movement of her tongue altered pressures in vessels far below. A word formed in her throat might emerge days later as a pleat in a plain, a turn of frost-hairs, or the premature opening of a flower hundreds of kilometers away.

The marks around her were maps, but not of places.

They were maps of consequences.

Each line showed where some small motion of hers had gone. A hooked groove meant that she had dreamed of lifting one hand, and a distant root had curled upward. A divided mark meant that she had remembered two faces and caused a field to fork. The long pale cuts that no traveler recognized were the paths of thoughts she had tried not to think.

She had spent centuries becoming careful.

She no longer knew whether this care was patience or captivity.

The power understood that to tear her free would kill much more than her. Her vessels had joined the column’s vessels. The column fed a ceiling membrane. The membrane gathered condensation for three accords and governed a respiratory wind used by sailbacks during migration. Remove the woman, and a chain of thirst might cross half the hollow.

To leave her as she was would preserve those lives.

It would also preserve her imprisonment.

Wisdom did not make the choice easy.

So the power went to one of the dead rings.

It chose a small ring in an abandoned holding, a place maintained for centuries and then lost when the root arch beneath it folded. The ring lay tilted in a plain of harvesting hairs. Shoots had entered it. Frost film covered the killed wood. Its emptiness was almost gone.

The power removed nothing.

Instead, it ceased to attend to the space within the ring.

For the first time in the history of the White Undergreen, there was a place the living world did not attempt to answer.

The harvesting hairs around it continued turning toward moisture, but none bent inward. Roots passed beneath and did not rise. Spores crossed its boundary and remained spores. A growth-front divided around it without injury, meeting again on the opposite side.

No wall held the space apart.

No salt poisoned it.

It was simply permitted to be unfilled.

A herd of frost combs found the ring first. They walked around it for three days. One entered, withdrew its legs, and rested.

Nothing learned the shape of its body.

The comb rose again and left.

Then a far-cord keeper named Orra came across the ring while seeking shelter from a dimming. She expected the ground within it to pulse beneath her weight. It did not. She lay with her cheek against the surface and heard deep water, distant splitting, the minute scrape of roots—but none of those sounds altered when she moved.

She spoke her open name.

The ground did not tremble.

She spoke it again, louder.

Nothing carried it away.

Orra began to weep. Her tears fell onto the living floor and were absorbed as ordinary water. No tendril lifted to taste the salt. No collecting hair turned toward her face.

She remained through the dimming.

Afterward she braided the location into every cord she carried, but she marked it with a knot that meant neither safety nor water nor stable ground.

The knot meant:

**Here, you are not being remembered.**

People came cautiously.

Listeners pressed their wrists to the boundary. Gardeners took cuttings and found there was nothing to cut. Children shouted household names into the center until their elders stopped them. The engrafted were brought when they could be moved, and those who could not be moved asked for soil from the ring, but soil taken away became ordinary living tissue again.

The unanswering belonged not to matter but to place.

Some feared it. They said anything the Undergreen could not remember must be death.

Others entered and slept more deeply than they had ever slept. Their hearts changed rhythm without nearby roots following. Their dreams left no marks.

The people called it the First Mercy.

They did not call it a miracle. A miracle was too much like growth: something entering the world and demanding consequence.

The First Mercy was a consequence withheld.

News reached the old woman through the column.

For the first time in centuries, the marks beneath her clear bark stopped.

Her fingers moved.

The wall made no record of it.

Across the hollow, no frost-hairs turned. No pool skin bulged. No ceiling fold tightened.

Her fingers moved again.

Still nothing.

The power had made no ring around her. It had not severed her from the root-column. It had given her only the smallest possible solitude: one motion that belonged to her alone.

She opened her eyes.

The wall formed its narrow door.

Inside waited three listeners, Orra the far-cord keeper, and a child carrying a wrist-ring of dead wood. They did not kneel. No one knew what kind of reverence would be safe.

The old woman drew breath.

The eastern accord inhaled with her, as it always had.

Then, just before she spoke, the power placed an unanswering space around the word.

“I am here,” she said.

No plain folded.

No flowers opened.

The word traveled only through air. It entered the ears of those present and went no farther.

The old woman began to laugh.

At first the listeners mistook the sound for the clicking of cooling pith. They had no story of her laughter and no inherited memory of its shape. It was thin, broken, and astonishingly young.

The column did not laugh with her.

When she was quiet, the power offered no vision and spoke with no voice. It opened three possibilities in the privacy it had made.

She might remain joined and keep this inward room, a chamber of self the accords could never enter.

She might be separated and allowed to die, with no root compelled to follow her into death.

Or, over many years, her blood and the column’s sap might be taught to release one another, until she could leave alive—changed, frail, and no longer sustained by the world beneath her.

No choice would be made for her.

The old woman considered for forty-one local events.

During that time the eastern sailbacks gathered. The ceiling membrane dimmed twice. Upward rain emptied a black pool, and an interval beast woke beneath the violet accord. The power did not hurry her.

At last she asked to be separated slowly.

The work took nine years.

Not even the wisest gardener understood it. Vessels that had fused centuries before began to grow parallel walls between themselves. Shared salts sorted without clotting. Root tissue withdrew from mineralized skin, not as from poison or injury, but as a hand withdraws from another hand when both have agreed to let go.

The column did not sicken.

The old woman did.

She began to require breath continuously. She shivered. Hunger returned as pain. Her heart, freed from the vast slow pressure beneath it, stumbled into a rhythm no listener recognized.

Every step toward independence made her weaker.

This frightened the people, who had expected freedom to resemble strength.

The old woman knew better.

On the last day, the pale tissue around her waist opened.

She fell into the arms of descendants whose names she had never heard.

The root-column sealed itself behind her.

Nothing bled.

They carried her to the First Mercy. By then it was surrounded by holdings, though none had been permitted closer than a kilometer. Within the ring, she sat beneath open brightness with a blanket over her shoulders and a cup of carefully melted frost in her hands.

She drank without the column drinking through her.

She looked into the cup. The water reflected her face and learned nothing.

People came to ask what lay beneath the deepest roots. They asked what the underhands were. They asked whether the accords were one being, whether the sailbacks remembered their dead, whether another hollow waited farther down.

She answered only:

“There must remain things we do not enter.”

She lived for one ordinary year.

Her body was given to a memory vine, but before the vine was grafted into the holding, her descendants carried one cutting into the First Mercy. There it ceased reaching. It did not die. It did not root. It remained a green length held between growth and decay, keeping its own chemistry without seeking continuation.

They left it there.

In time, other unanswering places appeared.

Not many. The power knew better than to make abundance of them. They arose where they were needed most: around an engrafted child who wanted to dream alone; beneath a holding whose walls had begun closing around strangers; at a pool that had nearly learned a family’s faces; along a route where the landscape had become too eager for familiar footsteps.

The people could not summon these places.

But they learned how to invite them.

They made a ring. They entered without tools. They spoke no demand. They fed nothing, wounded nothing, promised nothing. Then they waited, attending to the world without requiring the world to attend in return.

Sometimes nothing happened.

Sometimes, after many years, the shoots stopped testing the boundary.

The people gave this practice a name that meant both mercy and restraint, though in open air the word could also mean love.

For they came to believe that the benevolent power had not saved them from participation. Such a salvation would have emptied the White Undergreen of life.

Instead it had taught one new response among all the responses of the hollow:

Not turning.

Not taking.

Not learning the shape of what wished, for a while, to remain its own.

And far below the sealed surface, beneath ice and dark stone, in the place that had always seemed to contain a sky where no sky should be, there came at last to be something more like sky than light or height or open air.

There came to be room.
