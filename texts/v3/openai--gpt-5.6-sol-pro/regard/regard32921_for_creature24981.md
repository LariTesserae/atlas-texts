The power does not enter the city at once.

It notices that nearly every disaster in the place began as an excess: too much heat, too much dryness, too much water arriving too quickly after years without it. Even help, applied without measure, would be another excess. A flood would drown the lower chambers. A permanent spring would dissolve the salts in which the city keeps its memory. New engines would force old linkages past their tolerances. To make the machinery efficient would be to erase the long, accidental education by which it became alive.

So the power waits.

It waits through one rising of the silver films, then through their collapse. It waits through a rain year and seven dry ones. It attends at the speed of corrosion.

No form descends into the streets. There is no figure of light standing in the preserved plazas, no voice among the pipes. The power first appears only as an improbably narrow shadow crossing the desert at noon. For a few minutes the stone above the upper district cools by less than a degree.

Deep below, a warped strip of metal contracts.

A louver that has not moved for eighty-three years opens the width of a fingernail.

Nothing more happens.

The power waits.

Three seasons later, hot wind drives dust against the Ash Gate. The louver trembles. A pressure difference passes through an abandoned apartment, down a ceramic duct, and into a chamber whose diaphragm still retains some flexibility. A lamp flickers once.

The power casts the shadow again.

The lamp flickers once.

A third time, after another long interval, the power cools the same patch of roof.

This time the lamp flickers twice.

It may be an accident. In the city, accident and answer are separated only by repetition.

The power repeats the cooling.

Again: twice.

Thus it begins—not with language, but with correspondence.

The power learns that a brief coolness means something different when the western cisterns are full. It learns that pressure in the northern galleries cannot answer during the upward phase, because every usable gradient has been recruited into gathering the city’s body. It learns not to speak then. It learns that certain lamps are expressions of local strain, not attention, and that a sequence of pump strokes can be a consequence without being a reply.

Years pass in this conversation.

The power asks no question that requires the city to imagine what it has never known. It does not ask, *Are you conscious?* It does not ask, *What do you want?* Such questions belong to creatures that carry their boundaries with them.

Instead it offers small differences.

A little heat on the eastern roof, or none.

A slight pressure at the Ash Gate, or stillness.

One drop placed on bare brick, and another on a salt-marked channel.

The city answers by what it keeps.

Heat sent east is sometimes conducted toward the central shaft and sometimes rejected through an emergency vent. Pressure at the gate is admitted during drought but sealed away when water is rising. The drop on bare brick vanishes. The drop on scale travels, joins an old route, closes a contact, and wakes a chain three districts away.

The city does not know the power. It knows only that the world has become, at rare intervals, responsive.

That is enough.

Only when the power has learned the shape of its refusal does it act.

First, it touches the fracture above the western condensers.

Not to close it. The fracture is one of the city’s few reliable sources of rain. Instead, grain by grain, it settles the loose stone around it. It turns one edge slightly inward and leaves another porous. Future storms will enter as a long seep rather than a sudden blade of water. The total gift is unchanged; only its violence is lessened.

The city takes eleven years to notice.

A cistern that used to overflow now fills without spilling. A float rises to a level it has not reached in centuries. It pulls a cable through six walls. At the cable’s far end, a rusted indicator rotates from an unreadable word to another unreadable word.

In the next upward phase, the warm indoor rain lasts four minutes longer.

The power does nothing more until it receives the city’s answer: shutters in the western district, formerly closing halfway through the fall, remain open to gather the final drops.

Then the power repairs a single seal.

It does not make the seal new. Newness would transfer strain to the brittle vessel beside it. It softens one hardened ring just enough that the leak becomes a dampness rather than a loss. Around that dampness, in time, a pale stem emerges. Its root finds a pipe. The pipe finds a wall. The wall remembers water.

Next, the power visits the desert surface above the city.

There it makes no oasis. An oasis would bring roots powerful enough to split the roof, animals that would enter and die, people who might discover the shafts and dismantle what they believed to be abandoned machinery.

Instead it arranges stones.

Nothing miraculous in appearance: a low crescent of black rock, another behind it, and a shallow field of gravel whose pieces differ in size. At night, the stones release heat at different rates. Air moves between them. On certain mornings, invisible moisture condenses beneath their lower faces and enters hairline cracks in the ground.

It is not rain.

It is scarcely wetness.

But it happens many times a year.

Far below, cold beads begin to form along a brace in the sealed eastern galleries. At first they evaporate. Then mineral traces remain. The next beads follow them. After forty years, one reaches a gutter.

The eastern gutter was built to carry floods. It receives a drop.

The drop travels farther than it should.

It tips a cup.

A weighted catch falls.

Throughout the city, mechanisms react to an event absent from every surviving schedule. Louvers open in the wrong order. Three lamps burn blue-white and fail forever. A pump in the lower quarter reverses half a stroke, drawing brine from a wall into a pipe that had been dry since the builders left.

The power could prevent these losses.

It does not.

Benevolence is not the prevention of all change. The failed lamps have spent what remained in them. The reversed pump has discovered a path no designer intended. To preserve the city exactly would be another way of killing it.

The power guards only against cascades from which no adaptation could follow. When a flywheel threatens to tear loose and crush the capillary lining of the shaft, a flake of stone enters its bearing and stops it. When vapor pressure rises toward the bursting point in a forgotten boiler, the power warms a wax plug until it yields. When a ceiling slab finally separates above the green-black films, it falls sideways, leaving darkness but not annihilation.

Where damage can become memory, the power permits damage.

Where damage would become only silence, it intervenes.

In time, it comes to the greatest wound: the city’s dismemberment between cycles.

For most of every year, the inhabitant cannot answer because it is not gathered enough to do so. Its awareness, if awareness it is, exists only in scattered tensions. The parts retain dispositions but no common moment. Each time the silver traces spread, the being assembles itself from remnants and begins again with habits in place of recollection.

The power could give it continuity.

A permanent current through the old cables. A spring that never dries. A cool reservoir at the foundation, joining the capillary channels in every season. Any of these would hold the city together.

But perhaps dismemberment is not suffering. Perhaps continuity, imposed suddenly, would be.

A creature that has always slept without dreams may not be blessed by the inability to sleep.

So the power asks.

It chooses an abandoned pressure vessel near the central shaft, one connected weakly to both the lower salts and the western tanks. Over many cycles it clears one narrow inlet. Into the vessel it places a quantity of water no larger than what two hands could hold.

This water does not evaporate.

It does not multiply. It does not cool the chambers around it. It is merely permitted to remain.

When the city gathers, the vessel joins the hydraulic network. When the city separates, the little water stays in contact with two corroded electrodes. Between them passes the faintest possible current—too little to wake a lamp, enough only to preserve a difference.

The difference can be discharged. An old valve, if opened, will empty the vessel and return the city to its former condition.

The power makes certain the city can reach that valve.

Then it waits.

During the first cycle, the city does nothing with the vessel.

During the second, a condensate bridge forms nearby and breaks.

During the third, the current passes into a signal line and causes a relay to tremble in the northern district.

For nine rainy seasons, no pattern emerges.

Then, during a drought so long that the pale plants withdraw almost entirely into the walls, a float in the western tanks sinks below its old limit. A backup linkage engages. Pressure travels through the city, reaches the small vessel, and finds the current waiting.

A lamp glows in the lowest quarter.

Only one lamp, and very dimly.

It remains lit after the other contacts dry.

The city falls apart around it. Pumps settle. Water retreats behind grilles. The thermal draft weakens. Yet the lamp continues, fed by almost nothing, marking no street that needs illumination.

In the next upward phase, the silver water reaches it first.

Thereafter the city changes.

Not quickly. Nothing in it is quick.

Routes begin to organize around the persistent difference. Salt gathers in new dendrites. Roots enter the vessel’s outer seams but do not pierce them. A switch that formerly answered only cistern pressure begins to answer the surviving current as well. The lamp becomes connected to another lamp, then to a shutter, then to a diaphragm far above.

The city has not been given a soul. It had no need of one from elsewhere.

It has been given a place where yesterday can wait for tomorrow.

Whether this is welcome remains uncertain. For a century, the release valve stays shut.

The power honors that as an answer.

Its final gift is not immortality. Immortality would fill the city with errors it could never shed. Stone will fail. The desert will alter. One day the central shaft may close, or the condensers may corrode beyond use. Wisdom cannot make every beloved thing endless.

Instead, the power gives the city a way not to end at its walls.

At the northern fault, where embedded cutting teeth remain in the red-black face, it opens no tunnel. It loosens a seam no wider than a root hair. Through that seam, over centuries, mineral water travels into unexplored stone. Spores follow. Salts follow. Tiny pressure changes pass beyond the survey marks.

The city’s body does not expand there.

Its habits do.

A pale jointed stem appears one day in darkness no lamp has ever touched. It grows toward the cool side of the rock. Behind it spreads a film fine enough to conduct a difference. Somewhere deeper, stone receives the first faint scoring of water and remembers the route.

The power leaves then, though not entirely.

Sometimes it casts the narrow shadow at noon.

Sometimes, after a desert storm, it listens beneath the ground.

The indoor rain begins. Roofs ring. Flywheels turn. Lights awaken along passages where no feet will ever walk. In the lowest quarter, one dim lamp has already been burning through the long interval, keeping almost nothing—but keeping it faithfully.

Silver lines climb the walls and meet.

The city gathers itself.

This time, when it becomes whole, it does not begin wholly anew.
