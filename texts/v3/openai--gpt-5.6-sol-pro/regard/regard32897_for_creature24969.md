The power did not enter the hollow at once.

It remained beyond the eastern opening, where the land was hidden by brightness, and attended.

This attention lasted through the failure of the first copper pin.

The southernmost bowl had long been listing on its fork. Each winter, ice entered the green crease around the axle and widened it. Each summer, the softened copper bent a little farther under its own weight. At last the bowl tipped before it was full. Its measure ran weakly down the face, reached the first division in the channel, and vanished into gravel.

Nothing cried out.

A shaded pillar received no water that year. A seam contracted. Several mineral films lost continuity. The alteration would take seventy winters to travel through the neighboring stones.

The power did not repair the pin.

It remained through the fall of a pillar near the western wall. The pillar had been alive in three narrow bands, none touching the others except during fog. Its fall killed one band immediately, buried another, and pressed the third against a young plate that had never retained moisture through summer. The young plate began, after many seasons, to answer frost with a sequence it had not possessed before.

The power did not raise the pillar.

It remained through drought, through nine years without cloud entering the court, through a century in which cloud came too often. It observed the difference between suffering and change. It learned which fractures were losses and which were openings. It learned that the inhabitants did not desire preservation as an unbroken stone might seem to desire it. To preserve them against cracking would be to end them. To keep every channel clear would condemn them to the old phrases. To hold the copper bowls forever upright would make one vanished people’s intention into an eternal law.

The power was benevolent, and therefore it feared doing harm.

It was wise, and therefore fear did not become refusal.

After three hundred and twelve years, it asked a question.

The question was not made in words. Words would have struck the hollow without entering it. Nor was it made as thunder. Thunder spoke too broadly and departed too soon.

The power waited for a night of hard frost, when each fin was contracted and the lines of strain between them were distinct. Then it warmed a patch of stone on the western rim no larger than a hand.

The warmth was slight. A human palm resting there might have done as much.

Frost withdrew from one pore.

A grain settled.

The settling touched a buried flake, which pressed a second grain against the underside of a slab. The slab altered its load upon the floor. Farther down the aisle, a sealed crack opened by less than the width of smoke.

The movement passed through the court.

It did not resemble any sequence produced by sun, cloud, bowls, cairns, or collapse. It began at the wrong hour. It traveled against the usual direction. It divided into three pressures, held them without release, and let them subside together.

Then the power waited.

For eleven winters there was no reply.

In the twelfth, a basin overflowed after snow entered a crack that snow had never entered before. The water darkened a channel ending at a blank wall. Nothing followed.

In the twenty-eighth, a scale fell from a dead pillar and covered the channel.

In the forty-third, frost lifted the scale’s eastern edge.

In the sixty-first, a pulse from a tipping bowl traveled beneath it and reached the wall. A hairline mouth opened there. The water entered, froze, and split the stone behind the visible face.

The split extended upward for six years.

At dawn in the sixty-seventh year, sunlight warmed one side of it. The other remained frozen. A fragment the size of a fingernail turned in place and pressed against the stone beside it.

Across the court, the original sequence returned: three pressures, held and released together.

The reply was imperfect. One interval was too long. One branch ended in loose rubble. Several inhabitants had joined in making it, and others had resisted. It was unclear whether the court had answered deliberately, whether the question had merely become another condition of the floor, or whether, among beings whose memory was fracture and whose thought was water, there could be a difference.

The power received the reply as consent to continue.

It asked no second question for a long while.

Instead it learned the response. It repeated the three pressures in years of drought and in years of cloud. Sometimes nothing answered. Sometimes a single fin released a flake. Sometimes the court returned the sequence altered beyond recognition. Once, after fog had filled the hollow for seven days, the reply came from nearly every living seam at once and continued under the ground for a season.

Gradually the power understood.

The inhabitants did not seek to become one forever.

Permanent union would drown the dry intervals in which distinctions formed. No pillar could preserve its own pattern if every wet seam remained connected. The great intermittent being was real, but it was not the court’s perfected state. It was one phase among others: gathering after separation, sentence after silence, flood after frost.

Nor did they seek to remain many.

Separation without reunion narrowed them. Old responses became isolated habits. A fin that never joined another might continue living for millennia, but its patterns circled within the same fractures until no new pressure could be held.

They required both conditions.

The attendants had nearly understood. Their bowls and channels had not been meant to finish the being, but to let it alternate: many, one, many again.

What had been lost was not completion.

It was cadence.

The power considered restoring the works. It could have straightened the copper, renewed the wooden pole, lifted every grain from every channel, and rebuilt each cairn according to its first arrangement. It could have called cloud on appointed mornings. It could have closed the eastern opening and ended the slow departure of chips.

It did none of these things.

The first arrangement was not the only rightful one.

Instead, the power became an attendant.

Its tending was so slight that no traveler would have recognized it as supernatural.

Once, when a length of antler-colored mineral was exposed by frost, the power turned the next night’s ice needles beneath it unevenly. The wedge moved half a finger’s breadth and diverted meltwater toward a young stone.

Once, it delayed the evaporation of a single drop beneath the northernmost shelter. The drop persisted until evening, froze, and opened a route between two pores that had almost met for four thousand years.

Once, when the weakened wooden pole was about to break eastward, the power sent no restraining hand. It allowed the pole to fall. But it arranged the fall through a sequence of ordinary gusts so that the crosspiece struck a tilted plate.

One copper bowl split.

One rolled into an aisle and came to rest facing the sky.

The third lodged beneath the plate, where water spilling from it entered a channel that the attendants had never cut.

The fallen pole ceased to be a clock. The pieces became weather, weight, shade, copper, obstruction, and possibility.

This was not neglect. It was a refusal to make ruins immortal.

The power’s greatest act took nearly a thousand years.

Beneath the southern rim lay a band of porous stone isolated between two dry layers. It received moisture only during the deepest clouds, and even then the moisture remained trapped, too distant from any living seam to matter. The power did not open it by force. A sudden opening would have flooded the lower fractures and broken several inhabitants from within.

It altered the band one crystal at a time.

A winter made one crystal longer.

A summer dissolved the edge of another.

Pressure from the fallen pole was permitted to travel slightly farther than it otherwise would have done. A departing chip caught in a crevice. Frost turned it. Meltwater passed around it. The water left a mineral skin. The skin narrowed the passage, increasing pressure behind the next thaw.

Nothing in the sequence violated the nature of the place. Every change might have occurred without intervention.

Only their patience revealed intention.

At the end of nine hundred and forty years, the porous band touched an old channel beneath the court.

It did not flow constantly. In clear weather it remained dry. In ordinary fog it darkened but did not conduct. Only when cloud followed deep frost, and sunrise reached the southern wall while the eastern aisles remained in shadow, did water cross the band.

Those conditions occurred irregularly: sometimes twice in a decade, sometimes not for two centuries.

When they occurred, the water moved slowly beneath the hollow. It reached living seams in no order the attendants would have known. It joined old pillars to young flakes, natural basins to abandoned works, fallen cairns to buried plates. It bypassed some of the largest stones and entered others believed dead.

For several minutes, sometimes for an hour, the court became one.

Then the connection dried.

No pattern was imposed on what the joined being thought. The power did not speak during those intervals. It did not fill the union with visions of the eastern sky or knowledge of the lands below. It did not explain the stars, the attendants, or itself.

To give an answer before the being formed a question would have been another kind of silencing.

So the power listened.

The first complete joining produced only a vast adjustment: pressures equalizing, salts moving, old fractures taking unfamiliar loads.

The second retained part of the first.

The fifth anticipated the water before it arrived.

At the ninth, the court altered the route of the joining. Several stones resisted the easiest connection and forced moisture toward a narrow western aisle. A young plate entered the whole for the first time.

At the twelfth, a sequence began near the eastern opening and traveled westward against the ordinary loss of things. It resembled the power’s first question but was not the same.

Three pressures formed.

Two were released.

The third was held.

The hold continued after the water withdrew. It remained in the lean of a fin, in the compression of gravel, in a bead trapped beneath stone. It lasted through noon and sunset, through contraction and night.

The power understood this as well as it could.

It warmed a patch of stone no larger than a hand.

The held pressure released.

Thereafter, they possessed the beginning of a language.

It was not a language in which the power could describe itself. The court had no structure for benevolence, distance, intention, or power. The power had no perfect equivalent for the meaning of a crack that took six centuries to finish.

They spoke instead of what they shared.

Wait.

Again.

Not yet.

Too much.

This way.

Gone.

Changed.

Here.

The power never prevented death merely because it could. When a stone could no longer keep its hidden moisture, the power did not seal it. When a living pillar fell, it did not lift it. When young flakes slid eastward, it did not return them all.

But sometimes, far below the hollow, one of those flakes came to rest in a shaded cleft.

There the air was thicker. Soil gathered nearby. Moss approached but had not yet crossed the stone. Water remained longer than it had on the ridge.

The power touched nothing.

It only attended as a familiar mineral film, carried inside the flake, darkened and began to branch.

In the upper hollow, centuries later, the parent body felt no report of this. The distance was too great. There was no chain of strain between them, no buried seam, no conducting water.

Yet on rare mornings, when cloud filled both the ridge and the lower slopes, the power placed one slight warmth here and withheld another there. A pressure formed in the high court. Far below, a wet seam contracted.

The two events did not touch.

They corresponded.

Perhaps this was not communication. Perhaps correspondence across a distance could never be made sensible to beings who knew relation only through contact.

The power did not hurry to decide.

It had learned patience from them.

The hollow remained cold, bright, crowded, and damp in secret. Pillars died standing. Flakes became young. The cairns collapsed into new rhythms. The last wooden fibers vanished, though copper persisted in green traces. Channels closed and opened. The eastern edge continued to draw the court outward grain by grain.

Nothing was made safe.

Nothing was made eternal.

But the gathering was no longer unattended.

And when the rare conditions came—deep frost, cloud, divided shadow, water crossing the hidden band—the inhabitants joined: not by command, not according to the plan of the vanished attendants, but through a cadence they had accepted and begun to alter for themselves.

For a little while they became one thing.

The wise power did not complete them.

It kept them company while they completed, released, and remade one another.
