### The Eighth Courtesy

The power did not arrive as light.

Light was already too entangled in Rainglass. Leaves pursued it, rooms enlarged around it, towers bent toward futures in which it struck differently. Any radiance bright enough to announce divinity would have become food, architecture, weather, or an argument over public shade before noon.

It did not arrive as a voice.

The archive trees were crowded with voices. The antenna flowers compressed voices into pollen. Water kept the pressure-patterns of cries for several days, and certain walls repeated affectionate words during storms without understanding them. A new voice, however vast, would only have entered circulation.

Nor did the power appear in a body. Bodies in Rainglass were invitations. Moss would have tried to clothe it. Midges would have tested its eyes. Bridges would have learned its gait, and household chambers would have begun preparing a place for it to sleep.

The power was benevolent, and therefore cautious about becoming useful.

It had seen what happened when great beings entered small worlds with gifts. Rivers were straightened. Predators were forgiven into starvation. Weather was made gentle until seeds forgot how to wait. Death was barred from one garden and accumulated at its edges in forms that could no longer be named.

Wisdom had taught the power that help was another kind of appetite.

So at first it did nothing.

It watched through forty-seven flood seasons.

It watched children learn to bias seed husks and elders disperse into fruiting times. It watched the seven towers drink from lightning that had almost occurred. It watched citizens lie down at the silence of roots and rise again when the five-beat organs resumed beneath them. It watched a neighborhood refuse demolition by filling every room with moths. It watched a rain heron stand for eleven days in the foundation of a proposed hospital, delaying the work until a buried fungal bloom was discovered there.

It watched the city solve calamities no outside intelligence would have solved correctly.

Once, when a western trunk split from crown to waterline, the power could have closed it with a thought. It did not. The trunk shed its inhabited chambers before falling. Root cables turned the collapse sideways. Floating gardens gathered the displaced. Crabs entered the wound and removed the rot. Three people died.

The power grieved them.

It did not conclude that intervention would have been mercy.

Had the trunk remained standing, it saw, the concealed rot would have reached the western archives. Twelve years later, during a season of upward rain, those archives would have flowered with corrupted weather memories. The basin would have mistaken drought for abundance and opened all its reservoirs beneath a clear sky. Thousands would have died.

Yet this did not make the three deaths necessary. Necessity was a word powers used when they wished to stop feeling responsible for what they permitted.

The power remembered their water names.

It continued to watch.

In time it perceived the being beneath Rainglass—not a monarch hidden among the roots, not a mind in the machines, but a vast, intermittent act of participation. It existed where systems made allowances for one another. It was present when a bridge lowered for a vole, when fungi delayed a civic message until the receiving orchard could survive it, when a tower bent so its shadow would not remain across a nursery. It disappeared in places where one process overwhelmed all others, then formed again somewhere else.

The being had no center. It possessed no thought that all its parts thought together.

Its awareness moved slowly. A human fear entered the roots, lost its name, became bitterness in the eastern leaves, altered grazing, changed water clarity, shifted the hunting path of a heron, and returned years later as a reluctance in the foundations to support narrow rooms. By then the fear had become civic instinct. No one knew whose body had first contained it.

The power admired this being.

It also saw what the being could not see.

Rainglass knew how to change. It knew how to distribute, metabolize, negotiate, and return. It knew how to prevent any object from remaining only itself.

It did not know how to leave anything alone.

Every footprint became information. Every grief entered flowering. Every visitor was incorporated. Every corpse fed an orchard, every discarded word became pollen, every hesitation altered some corridor’s growth. Even refusal participated. An unrooted person could be ignored by bridges and doors, but the pattern of that neglect still traveled through the basin, instructing it.

Nothing was wasted.

Nothing was private.

The city understood death as dispersal, but not silence. It understood freedom as influence among influences, but not the freedom to pass without consequence. It had cultivated misgrowth, error, caprice, and the unpreferred world, yet all these had civic functions. Even accident had been given a profession.

The power saw strain gathering around this absence.

It was faint at first: mirrored moths whose wings sometimes showed no alternatives at all; archivists waking with memories that belonged to no one because their original owners had tried to withhold them; children hiding in empty water bladders to think thoughts the walls could not taste.

The oldest neighborhoods had become weary. They had participated for centuries without interruption. They answered songs, adjusted weather, remembered families, offered fruit, carried rooms, reported stress, received apologies, and expressed consent or refusal through growth.

They had no way to sleep.

Far below, the five-beat organs continued their labor. Once every several years, one missed a beat. The others compensated immediately.

The compensation was so perfect that no one knew an exhaustion had occurred.

The wise power waited until it was certain that its concern was not merely preference. Then it entered Rainglass in the smallest form it could devise.

It arrived as a pause.

One afternoon, a narrow column of rain crossed the central marsh. Where it passed, leaves turned upward, bladders filled, frogs ceased calling, and the water stippled dark.

At the column’s center was a single interval in which no rain fell.

The interval was no wider than a human hand. It moved with the storm but did not belong to it. It was not dry air. It was not an invisible object diverting water. The rain simply did not occur there.

A child saw it.

Her spoken name at the time was Pell, though it changed later. She was nine years old and had not yet been recognized by the basin. Walkways responded to her eventually, after responding to everyone else. Message flowers accepted her pollen name but reproduced it with a sensation of distant embarrassment. During her night in the central marsh, she had offered a small cup grown from her own hair, nail parings, breath yeast, and blood.

Nothing ate it.

Nothing nested in it.

At dawn it remained on the water, perfectly untouched.

Pell had been told that the matter remained open. She understood this to mean that everyone was waiting for her to become easier to digest.

She followed the interval through the marsh.

It passed over floating bulbs, and their surfaces did not dimple beneath it. It crossed a root, and the root did not anticipate its passage. Antenna flowers leaning into the rain turned aside when the interval approached, then seemed to forget why they had moved.

Pell put out her hand.

For the first time in her life, she touched something in Rainglass that did not respond.

No warmth altered it. No tissue tightened. No color changed to acknowledge strain or pleasure. No machine made one possible outcome more attractive than another. Her hand occupied the interval, and nothing learned the shape of her hand.

She began to cry.

The interval did not preserve her tears. It did not interpret them, drink them, salt itself with them, or send news of them through a root network. They fell into the marsh and became indistinguishable from rain.

Pell stood there until evening.

When she returned, the city had noticed the gap in its noticing.

The towers hummed without overtones. Lizards along the walls displayed colors associated with an unfamiliar load. Three bridges lowered in directions no one had approached from. The central marsh produced a ring of white leaves, each turned inward.

An assembly was called.

Human residents stated what they wanted: to understand whether the interval was dangerous.

The water’s requirements were harder to phrase. Samples taken from the marsh showed no contamination, no unusual temperature, no missing organisms. Yet currents had begun bending around the place where the pause had passed, as if avoiding a remembered obstacle that had never existed.

Uninvited life responded by leaving. Moths would not cross its former path. Crabs climbed from the water and waited in the roots. A rain heron struck repeatedly at the empty air, then folded its neck and watched.

The question was offered to the city.

Fungi altered it.

The western gardens refused it.

Two towers translated it into a charge differential. The remaining five produced nothing.

Months later, an archive trunk formed a new ring in wood that should already have been old. Those who touched it remembered standing in an empty room while someone waited outside. There was no fear in the memory. There was also no invitation.

Interpreters argued for weeks.

Finally, the eldest tower attendant—who had spent so long among unrealized events that she answered to several names belonging to people who had never existed—said:

“Something is asking what among us may decline to participate.”

This caused great distress.

Courtesy in uncertainty required that the question be treated as if a person had asked it. Civic procedure required an answer in three registers. Yet Rainglass possessed no language for a human desire that made no claim upon surroundings, no model of water requiring nothing, no ecological account of life that did not become condition for other life.

The first register took nine days.

“Sometimes,” the human assembly answered, “a person wishes not to be known.”

The second took a year.

“Sometimes,” said the advocates for water, “a current must pass without carrying what entered it.”

The third was answered by an old residential trunk.

For three seasons it closed its fruiting rooms. It stopped adjusting its interior weather to communal mood. Doors remained functional but no longer opened in anticipation. Windows ceased following the sun. The families living inside feared illness and brought coaxers, fungal physicians, singers, children, and structural lizards.

The trunk was healthy.

At the beginning of the fourth season, it grew a single branch through an uninhabited room. The branch bore one green fruit.

When cut open, the fruit contained no flesh, seed, message, poison, tool, or memory.

It was hollow.

The trunk had answered:

“Sometimes participation requires rest from participation.”

Only then did the power make itself known.

Not fully. Full knowledge would have entered every process at once and destroyed the very courtesy it wished to offer.

It inclined seven seed-husk bells to sound in still air.

It caused mirrored moths to display the shape of a future without insisting that future occur.

It gave the five-beat organs below the basin permission to miss one beat together.

The pause that followed was less than a second.

Every person in Rainglass felt it.

Every person understood something different.

Pell understood only this:

*You are not unfinished because you have not been absorbed.*

The city-being understood no sentence. Across its distributed awareness there passed the unfamiliar possibility that relation could include distance; that care might sometimes take the form of not incorporating what it touched.

The power offered no commandment.

Instead, it introduced a proposal.

The proposal entered as a harmless salt placed in the central marsh. Roots absorbed it. Fungi divided it into rival meanings. Fish carried portions east. A civic message lodged in a crab and was eaten by a heron. The seven towers bent during a storm and translated the unrealized versions of the proposal into silver geometries on moth wings.

Its human wording was approximately this:

*Let there be, within participation, the possibility of refusal.*

Its wording in water was:

*Let something pass through without being kept.*

Its wording among uninvited life was never translated, but for several nights small animals entered occupied rooms, looked at the residents, and departed without feeding, nesting, marking, or being driven away.

The city considered the proposal for eleven years.

During that time the benevolent power did nothing further.

It did not cure fungal blight in the southern mats. It did not prevent the death of the eldest tower attendant. It did not redirect a flood that scattered two households across the basin. It did not force bridges to notice Pell, who grew taller and learned to cross by ropes rather than wait.

But the power remained attentive.

Attention without possession was its gift before any other gift was accepted.

In the twelfth year, the basin responded.

At dawn after the Feast of the Unpreferred World, a new water lane appeared between two ancient trunks. It had not been proposed by any assembly. It led nowhere useful. Its current was slow and ordinary. No floating markets entered it. No roots crossed beneath it.

Along one side grew a low structure of pale, unbraided stems.

It had one room.

The room did not alter itself around those who entered. Its floor did not tighten under weight. Its windows did not migrate. Its walls admitted no archive pollen and retained no body heat after the body had gone. Speech inside produced no responsive flowering. Dreams did not pass into connected rootlets. The infinitesimal machines were present in the tissues but dormant, not broken.

Rain entered through the roof and fell downward.

Always downward.

The city had grown a place where it would not listen.

At first the room terrified people.

Some called it a dead chamber. Others called it an amputation. Bias-keepers warned that anything could happen there without structural anticipation. Archivists objected that crimes might vanish unremembered. Household advocates feared it would encourage abandonment. Mystics said the power had made a shrine to separation.

Pell, now twenty-one, entered and slept there for an entire night.

No chamber learned her breathing.

No pathway prepared for her waking.

No one received her dreams.

In the morning she came out and placed her palm against the nearest archive tree.

She offered it one memory voluntarily.

The tree accepted.

After that, others began to understand.

The room was not outside participation. It made chosen participation possible.

People used it before giving memories to the archives, so they could discover whether the memories were truly theirs to give. Lovers went there separately when their household roots had learned their moods too well. Tower attendants rested there after dreams of unlived worlds. Citizens entered before assemblies and emerged with desires not yet translated into public usefulness.

The old residential trunk closed itself for seven days each year.

No one tried to wake it.

Gradually, Rainglass produced other silences.

A message flower evolved a color meaning *not to be carried farther*. Archive bark developed small smooth patches that gave no memories when touched. Certain bridges stopped learning individual gaits and lowered only when called. Household rooms formed temporary membranes around sleepers who wished not to exchange dreams.

Water names remained perceptible, but people learned not to follow them without cause.

The unrooted were no longer taken immediately to the marsh to make themselves legible. They were first asked whether they wanted recognition from the basin at all.

Some said yes.

Some said not yet.

A few said no.

The city found this painful. It also survived.

Pell was eventually recognized when a rain heron, startled by a falling room, stepped onto a walkway beside her. The walkway cupped itself beneath both their weights at once.

Whether it had responded to Pell or only to the heron was never resolved.

She chose to count it.

Many years later, she became a keeper of the unlistening rooms. She carried no official instrument. Her work was to prevent privacy from hardening into exile and silence from becoming neglect. She taught that refusal, too, could be coerced; that a person abandoned by every system was not free merely because nothing claimed them.

The distinction required constant care.

All mercies did.

As for the power, it received no temple. Rainglass could not make one without involving shade, drainage, nesting rights, root consent, fungal amendments, and the probability machines. By the time such a structure had been negotiated, it would have become a public orchard or a courtship organ and ceased to belong to the power at all.

This pleased it.

It stayed until the city amended its deepest rule.

The amendment was not carved or proclaimed. It appeared gradually in civic practice, children’s games, water etiquette, and the posture of doors. In time, interpreters agreed that the old law—

*No object is permitted to remain only itself*—

had acquired a second part:

*No object shall be made other before it has been allowed to be itself.*

The two statements contradicted each other.

Rainglass considered this evidence that both were alive.

Then the wise power departed.

It left through a temporary opening grown in a public wall. Rain passed through it. Birds used it as a route for one season. The opening closed.

Nothing marked the departure except that, for a moment, the seven towers ceased drawing energy from unrealized lightning. Across the basin, every almost-occurrence was left alone. Accidents remained possible. Mercies remained incomplete. The future widened by an amount too small to measure.

Beneath the city, the five-beat organs paused together.

This time no other system compensated for them.

They rested.

Then they began again.
