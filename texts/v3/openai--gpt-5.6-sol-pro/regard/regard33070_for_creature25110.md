### The Mercy of Intervals

The power did not speak to the city.

Speech would have been the crudest form of entry. A sentence spoken over the plateau might have passed into the crowns of the towers, from crowns into load-bearing walls, from walls into roads, from roads into sleeping districts. By morning every door might have believed itself addressed. Every root might have grown toward the same meaning.

Nor did the power name what it saw.

It did not call the plateau a mind, although from sufficient height its weather of impulses resembled thought. It did not call the people its children, although it felt tenderness toward them. It did not call the buildings alive or the ruins sorrowful. Benevolence without discipline would have been dangerous here.

So the power watched.

It watched through drought years, when households carried cups of their own allotment to shrinking stairwells. It watched convergence crews cut apart houses that had begun finishing one another’s rooms. It watched archivists emerge from doors older than the hands that opened them. It watched the dead divided into useful fractions so that no person would be trapped in a single return.

It watched the lightning festivals, when the inhabitants entered and departed from common rhythm before beauty could become compulsion.

At first the power thought to send rain.

There was water in its keeping: high rivers of vapor, unspent storms moving over the world’s cold reaches, snow sleeping in clouds. It could have arranged a wet decade. Reservoirs would have risen. Fruit would have hung heavy from every trained wall. Children might have bitten into it with both hands and laughed at the juice.

But the city had been shaped by scarcity as surely as by wind. Too much rain would not simply relieve it. Towers would swell beyond their foundations. Roads would close over rail junctions. Dormant tissue miles deep might wake without knowing the century. The abandoned edge could return to urban time all at once.

The power withheld the rain.

It thought to quiet the roots.

With a slight adjustment of mineral charge, it could have interrupted the signals passing through the underlayer. Dreams would cease to move between districts. Rooms would remain where they had been measured. A person’s memories would belong only to that person.

But silence imposed upon the roots would also end the frost warnings, the pressure-sense of approaching weather, the deep rhythms by which lost children found their wards. It would separate what had never asked to be isolated. To cure convergence by making all communion impossible seemed to the power merely another form of convergence: one solution forced everywhere.

It did not quiet the roots.

It thought to reveal itself.

That temptation lasted the least time.

Instead, the power continued to watch until it understood that the city’s laws, funerals, names, distances, ash-lined poetry rooms, and dances breaking short of unison were all versions of the same request.

The request had never been spoken together, because these people knew better than to make a single voice of many mouths.

Nevertheless, the power heard it:

*Let us touch without being consumed.*

*Let us belong without becoming one.*

*Let there be a path by which a thing may remain itself.*

The power considered this for nine years.

Then, at the oldest edge of the plateau, one of the long-winged birds landed.

No one saw it arrive except a caretaker named Senn, who at that time possessed two probable childhoods, one publicly recognized apprenticeship, and a red-ribbon grief for a brother whom his mother insisted she had never borne.

Senn was pouring water into a cracked offering basin beside a withdrawn pumping station. The station exhaled once each winter, though it had no membranes. Its shadow was known to repeat the posture of anyone who crossed it too often.

The bird descended without cry.

Its wings tilted once, twice, and it settled on the rim of the basin.

Senn became perfectly still.

The bird was larger than the old diagrams suggested. Its feathers were not merely black but layered with colors that appeared only when it moved: oil green, bruised violet, the blue of distant rain. Its feet were narrow and dark. One claw was packed with mud.

It looked at Senn.

Senn did not call it a messenger. He did not call its arrival miraculous. He did not even call it beautiful, because the pumping station’s nearest listening buds had not been pruned that month.

He lowered his eyes and stepped back.

The bird drank.

It drank for a long time, lifting its head after each swallow to watch the open sky. Then it placed one foot in the basin. A small clot of foreign mud loosened from its claw and fell beside the water.

The bird departed.

Its shadow crossed the ruined towers in agreement with the sun.

Senn erected a moisture tent over the mud.

That was procedure.

He summoned a soil officer, who summoned a pruner, who summoned a convergence clerk because the mud remained damp longer than its volume allowed. They examined it with mineral probes and pollen papers. They found fragments of distant root, three translucent eggs belonging to an unknown mite, and seven worm cocoons.

The mites did not survive.

Six of the cocoons remained dormant.

From the seventh came a worm no longer than the last joint of Senn’s smallest finger.

Work ceased for a radius of two hundred paces.

Children were brought from the nearest school. They knelt beyond the marked line in pale wind coats while the soil officer recorded the visitor’s direction, color, and apparent health.

The worm was gray-white, almost translucent. A dark thread of swallowed earth showed through its body. It moved from the foreign mud into the plateau.

Then it stopped.

The root-felt beneath it was old and densely compressed. There should have been no space. The worm pressed its head against the surface, withdrew, and pressed again.

The ground opened.

Not far. Not dramatically. A passage the width of a thorn appeared beneath its body, and the worm entered.

Everyone present felt the change.

A pumping station ten paces away ceased to remember their footsteps.

Its shadow became only a shadow.

The worm moved belowground, swallowing minute quantities of root-felt and excreting them behind itself. Yet the substance it left was altered. It remained nourishing. Moisture passed through it. Chemical warnings crossed it. The roots on either side could still exchange frost, pressure, mineral lack, and disease.

But coherent dreams did not pass.

The soil officer knelt and touched two fingers to the road.

On one side of the tiny tunnel, the abandoned station believed it was winter.

On the other, it did not.

The worm had made an interval.

For the first time in the city’s records, separation had occurred without cutting, burning, poisoning, drying, or death.

No one moved until the soil officer began to weep.

This caused administrative difficulty, as emotion was not among the recognized instruments of field confirmation.

The worm was designated Visitor Seven.

Its tunnel was designated a local discontinuity of aggregate architectural impression.

Senn called it mercy, but only inside an ash-lined recital room, and only once.

Visitor Seven lived for six years.

It traveled less than half a kilometer. It reproduced twice, though only after municipal officers transported small portions of the foreign mud into a sheltered reservoir bed. Its descendants were equally slow, equally pale, and incapable of flourishing where they were not tended carefully.

This slowness saved them from becoming a cure.

A cure would have been deployed everywhere. It would have acquired quotas, factories, patriotic songs, and unintended meanings. Instead the worms remained visitors. Each had to be protected from cold, drought, vibration, and the translucent mites that gradually learned to eat their cocoons.

Districts petitioned for them.

Not every petition was granted.

The first interval bed was established beneath a detached infirmary pod whose walls had begun retaining the pain of former patients. The worms passed slowly around the foundation. Over the next season, the walls forgot the screams but retained the useful shapes of care: the curve of basins, the opening gestures of midwives, the correct warmth for infants.

The second bed was made beneath an archive.

Nothing vanished. No history was corrected. Doors continued to open into three eras, and the clerks continued to wear ribbons. But the eras ceased multiplying. A woman who remembered both the death and the continuing life of her spouse was allowed to keep both memories without the archive growing a third husband to reconcile them.

The third interval surrounded a recital courtyard.

It was not lined with ash.

At its opening, a licensed poet stood beneath the actual sky and said that the western towers were lonely.

Across the district, no tower altered its windows.

The audience remained silent for several minutes. Then an arboritect laughed so hard he had to be carried out.

Afterward, poets became no less regulated. The people had survived too long by caution to abandon it for wonder. Yet small outdoor speech gardens began to appear, bounded underground by worm-work. Within them one might call a road weary, praise a bridge for patience, or tell a child that the moon was following her without causing the nearest roofs to turn.

Households sought intervals for other reasons.

Some wanted a room in which an argument could end.

Some wanted floors on which they could sleep directly.

Some wanted to love one another without their walls developing an opinion.

One elderly couple petitioned for a narrow discontinuity beneath the shared wall of their hedge-house. They had lived together for sixty-three years, and the house had begun anticipating their gestures so completely that neither could perform an unfamiliar act. Their application stated:

> We do not wish to leave one another.  
> We wish to become capable of surprise.

The petition was approved.

Years passed.

The city did not become safe. Wise powers know that safety, made absolute, resembles a cage.

Buildings still converged when crowded. Shadows in the abandoned districts still learned repeated shapes. Drought still stiffened doors and contracted rooms. Children still whispered compliments to bridges and ran. Archives remained treacherous. People continued to divide their names and their dead.

The worms did not solve these things.

They gave the city a new kind of choice.

A hospital might remember, or not.

Two houses might share weather but not dreams.

A road could carry the pressure of feet without learning every traveler.

A person with conflicting histories could enter a quiet room and discover which griefs remained when no wall was helping them remember.

Some emerged relieved.

Some emerged grieving more deeply.

Some asked to leave at once.

The withdrawn used the worms sparingly. Around certain ruins, intervals were forbidden. Those structures had withdrawn by means no one fully understood, and separating them from the underlayer might wake them rather than grant rest.

Senn refused a worm bed around the old pumping station.

“It should not be made solitary merely because solitude is now available,” he said.

By then his hair was nearly white, though the red ribbon remained. He lived long enough to see the first black bird land within the central city.

It came after lightning.

Veins glowed beneath the roads. Factories accelerated. Dancers crossed the great plazas in translucent sleeves, meeting and separating by ward rhythm.

The bird descended through the illuminated air and settled on the lintel of the Office of Aggregate Impulse.

The entire plaza saw it.

No one called out.

No one approached.

After a moment, the bird folded its long wings and remained.

Children stared openly. Workers removed their hats. A pruner began to make an official hand signal, thought better of it, and lowered both arms.

The bird watched the dancing.

Groups overlapped, moved together for a few measures, and parted before unison became complete.

Something in this appeared to satisfy it.

Or perhaps it merely rested.

The distinction was carefully preserved.

As for the power, it accepted no temple. It sent no doctrine. It did not arrange its gifts into proof.

Most inhabitants concluded that the worm cocoons had arrived by ordinary chance from a distant, wetter country. Some argued that the foreign mud had altered a local species. Others believed Visitor Seven had always slept beneath the plateau and that the bird’s arrival merely coincided with its waking.

Senn was asked about this near the end of his life.

The approved question would have been, “From where do you believe the visitor came?”

Instead, a child asked, “Who was kind to us?”

The adults nearby stiffened. The floor gave a small, attentive creak.

Senn considered the child, the listening room, the pale worm bed beneath the outer wall, and the many possible dangers of an answer.

Then he said, “Someone who did not need us to know.”

The floor relaxed.

Far below, the worms continued their patient work. They did not sever root from root. They did not silence the deep exchange of rain, frost, pressure, and need. They made narrow places where an impression could come to an end.

And the benevolent power, having acted, did not withdraw its attention.

It watched without claiming.

It loved without naming.

When rain was needed, it permitted rain—but never enough to wake the whole depth at once. When lightning came, it bent no bolt away from the waiting conductive flowers. When birds crossed the plateau, it left them free to land or not.

Its greatest gift remained almost indistinguishable from restraint.

For in a place where everything touched, everything remembered, and every repeated likeness risked becoming identity, the power gave neither abundance nor unity nor truth spoken in a single overwhelming voice.

It gave them something rarer.

It gave them room within connection.

And because it was wise, it left the room unfinished.
