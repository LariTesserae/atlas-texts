### The Eighth Current

The power does not open the mountain.

It does not make an air-filled chamber, because air would be no gift here. It does not still the water, because stillness is another name for suffocation. It does not harden the unfinished stone, silence the buried thing, widen every dwelling or prevent noon from overturning the cistern.

These would resemble mercies from a great distance.

Up close, they would be extinctions.

The power does not reveal itself in a body. Any body large enough to inspire awe would displace too much water. Any voice added to the chamber would drown a name somewhere. Any light brighter than the summit’s pale filtering would strike every surface at once and blind the people in the only sense in which they can be blinded.

So the power attends.

It learns the roar before it attempts to distinguish speech from accident. It listens through the mountain’s first winter, when ice thickens above and the chamber’s pitch descends. It listens through the thaw, when meltwater enters in such violence that three dwellings close and the pierced-stone census falls from seven to four. It hears the songs at noon and the small, private taps made afterward into rooms that never formed.

It listens to the dead being named against their own spines.

It listens beneath the floor.

Years pass before the power understands that the cistern is not cruel.

Cruelty requires a choice understood and refused. The mountain is young. Its laws are unfinished. The central current takes the newly opened young not because they are judged unworthy, nor because their loss sustains some indispensable balance, but because several blind pressures happen to cross there. The adults do not refrain from rescue out of hardness. Rescue is simply another form of being taken.

The power finds no wisdom in preserving a preventable sorrow merely because sorrow has been made beautiful.

Yet it also understands that a gift completed entirely by its own hand would be a kind of insult. Nothing in the cistern is made that way. A perfect structure, imposed at once and incapable of alteration, would be indistinguishable from dead stone.

So the power does not make a refuge.

It makes the possibility of one.

During a noon upheaval, while every shadow points upward and the people cling to the wooden pegs, the copper question in its niche turns once against the current.

It has never done this before.

The hook completes a full slow revolution, pauses, and points toward a blank wall of basalt. The wall is old, barren and hard. No living margin follows it. Sound does not travel there.

Then a line appears across its surface.

It is not carved. Nothing has touched the stone. It is merely a thin interruption in the mineral grain, three hooked curves crossing one another around an empty center.

By the following noon, the line is gone.

But one of the listeners on the ladder has heard its shape.

This one is old enough that most of their body has taken the color of the basalt. Their name requires two towers and the hollow beneath the seven stones to speak properly. Long ago, three of their young were lost in a single opening. Each was given a ridge. Two ridges later broke. The third still sounds during storms.

After the upheaval, the old one taps the vanished figure into a fresh wall.

Others answer that no current crosses there.

The old one taps it again.

They place fibers along the first curve, shell fragments along the second and a row of broken bone hooks along the third. The empty center they leave empty. No one understands what is being predicted. Some believe it is a sleeping chamber. Some hear the beginning of a tool. One young craft-worker insists it is a mistake, because all three curves direct growth away from the space they seem intended to enclose.

The old one claims the prediction.

The mountain takes nine seasons to reply.

Mineral gathers on the fibers, but only on their outward faces. The shell fragments sink into clear stone. The hooks become ribs. Each noon the structure nearly breaks, and each noon its three arms bend farther apart, opening rather than closing.

Gradually, a hollow forms behind the wall.

Water enters it from the central current, passes through channels too fine for an adult hand and returns to the visible chamber beside the nursery ledge.

Returns.

At first no one trusts this.

Nothing returns from the central current except as a relief in stone.

Grains are placed in it. They vanish. A season later, during a tremor, some emerge beside the ledge. They are not the same arrangement, and several have acquired skins of mineral, but the copper grains are certainly copper. A broken scraper is surrendered next. It reappears after three noons with one edge gone and a shell-creature fixed where the handle had been.

The folk debate whether this is return or repurpose.

Then a cluster of young opens during the strongest surge of the year.

The sacs flatten. Their seams split. Six translucent bodies are cast outward.

Four find the central equilibrium.

One strikes a tower and secures itself with the hook of a hand.

The sixth misses.

The adults feel its passage as a brief interruption in the current. It folds, tumbles and is drawn toward the narrowness in the wall. A mourner raises a hand, already preparing to remember the exact pressure of its disappearance.

The young body vanishes.

No one moves.

Several breaths later, the new structure rings.

It is a frail, ugly note. Nothing like a name. Nothing like the chamber’s ordinary thunder.

The nursery ledge darkens.

The lost young is expelled from a seam beneath it, mineral grains embedded in its transparent skin. One limb has not divided properly. Its flank cavities remain closed. It lies against the rock without strength enough to grip.

An adult enters the returning flow.

The current there is weak, almost lethally so, but not still. For the first time, one of the cistern folk reaches a failed young without being taken by the force that took it. The adult braces three limbs against the new ribs, folds the fourth around the juvenile and draws it toward a stronger jet.

Water passes through the young one’s sides.

The cavities open.

The old listener on the ladder speaks the child’s first pulse into the stone.

No one in the chamber has a word for rescue.

They have words for interception, reappearance, delayed loss, altered purpose and finding a use after surrender. None means exactly this: that what is being taken may be reached before it becomes memory.

They do not invent the word quickly.

Instead they build.

Frames are set along the returning channels. Soft fans are encouraged to grow where a body might strike. Handholds are placed not for adults, but at the span of newly divided limbs. The copper hook is moved to the nursery ledge, where its reversal warns when the return flow is narrowing.

The structure does not save every young.

Some enter cracks outside its reach. Some return injured beyond the body’s ability to continue. During one winter the channels clog entirely, and two adults die trying to reopen them before a nursery cluster splits.

The power does not prevent these deaths.

A world in which no being can ever be lost would require walls without passages, water without movement, bodies without change. Such a world might be safe. It would not be this one.

But from then on, loss is no longer the only possible ending to failure.

This changes the people more profoundly than the power expected.

Adults begin tapping guidance while the young seek equilibrium. Before, there was no reason to do so: by the time a juvenile missed the crossing, nothing could alter its path. Now pulses are sent through the nursery ledge—open, fold, turn, reach—and some young learn the shape of instruction before they learn their own names.

Mourners still alter the currents that take their children. But now they also alter currents for children not yet opened.

The distinction is small.

In the cistern, small distinctions become walls.

The returning structure grows. Its note joins the noon song. Because no one can determine whether the old listener predicted it or merely remembered the three vanished curves, no one agrees who may claim it. At last the claim is given to the first young who returned alive.

Their name can be spoken only when water is moving in two directions through the same stone.

As the new channels deepen, something else occurs.

The slow pulse beneath the floor changes.

The cistern folk withdraw at first. Nursery sacs are cut loose. The wooden pegs empty. The mineral towers bend as the great ridged surface moves under them.

But the parallel scrapes do not appear.

The pulse comes again.

This time the returning structure answers.

Its three arms gather the deep vibration, divide it and send it back through the floor. The chamber rings—not all at once, as in the buried being’s terrible chord, but in succession. Tower. Rib. Cairn. Ladder. Nursery ledge.

A sequence.

One of the hidden people recognizes part of it as the rhythm tapped to a sleeper before taking the outermost place in a dwelling.

They reply.

The thing beneath the floor becomes still.

Not silent. Never silent.

Listening.

Through many noons, they discover that the great being’s movements are not wholly senseless. Its body—if body is the word—lies through pressures too vast and slow for the cistern folk to distinguish. What they experience as an abrupt rising may be, to it, the gradual bending of a limb. What they call a long interval may be less than one pulse of its sleep.

It has opened sealed dwellings without knowing they were dwellings.

It has overturned towers without knowing anything lived upon them.

It has also, at times, opened channels deliberately, because the noon song reached it as pain and it mistook the singers for stone under strain.

Now the returning structure carries difference downward.

Stone.

Water.

Dwelling.

Young.

Here.

Not here.

Wait.

The buried being does not always obey. Perhaps obedience is not possible between lives of such different scales. But sometimes the pulse pauses. Sometimes a pale fold passes around a nursery rather than through it. Once, when the pierced-stone census has fallen to two and the cistern folk are preparing to abandon the visible chamber, a gripping structure rises beneath an old barren shelf and tears it open.

Behind it are three warm, flowing seams.

The census becomes five.

Some believe the deep one has learned tenderness.

Others say it has merely learned where not to place its weight.

The power makes no distinction.

It remains until the cistern folk no longer need the original figure. By then the three hooked curves exist in many forms: nursery grates, returning vessels, resonant walls, tools that warn before a current closes. Some fail entirely. Some produce uses no one foresaw.

No temple is built. No name is given to the power. They do not know that they have been observed.

Once, the old listener presses their wrist to the first returning wall and asks who predicted it.

The mountain gives back their own pulse, changed by the passage.

They seem satisfied.

The benevolent power departs as it arrived: without displacing water.

It does not remain to govern what it has begun. Mercy that cannot be altered by those who receive it is only another sealed vessel.

Long afterward, the cistern continues to thunder inside the rising summit. The young still open into danger. The dead are still surrendered at the central hollow. Noon still exchanges the chamber’s shapes, and the buried being still turns in its immense sleep.

But beside the nursery ledge, through a structure no one made alone, an eighth current runs.

It is narrow.

It is unreliable.

It leads through darkness and crushing stone.

And it comes back.
