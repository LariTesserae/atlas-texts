The power does not widen the seam.

It does not open the roof to the sun, warm the buried river, fill the cisterns to their rims, or teach the inhabitants to burn a fire that consumes no fuel. It does not straighten the streets. It does not strengthen every lintel, cure every sickness, silence death, or reveal the Ward to the world above.

Any of these things would be easy.

For that reason, it does none of them.

It comes at dusk in the shape of a traveler carrying no pack. On the plain it pauses beside the dwarf juniper and waits until someone emerges, because it understands that a hidden threshold is not an invitation merely because one has the power to cross it.

The person who finds it is a child gathering moth cocoons from the sheltered side of the stones.

The traveler kneels so they are the same height.

“May I come below?” it asks.

The child studies its mouth, hands, shoulders, and eyes. Something in the question troubles her—not danger, exactly, but the strange sensation of standing before a being that could enter without permission and will not.

She touches two fingers to the traveler’s wrist: Wait.

Then she disappears into the seam.

Several inhabitants return. They bring an elder of the western drip, an old woman whose bent fingers can detect rain three days before it enters the cisterns. She lays one hand against the pale stone and the other against the traveler’s breastbone.

The power allows itself to be listened to.

The elder feels no heartbeat. Then she feels every heartbeat she has ever known: infants quick as dripping water, sleepers slow beside a hearth, the flutter of birds held for healing, the final uneven pulse of the dying.

She removes her hand.

“You must gather yourself,” she says. “There is not room below for all of that.”

So the power gathers itself.

It becomes no larger in consequence than an ordinary person. Its knowledge folds close. Its radiance is reduced to the faint mica sheen that clings to everyone in the Ward. It stoops beneath the first bridge, turns sideways through the first doorway, and learns not to let its sleeves brush the gardens.

For one full turning of the seasons, it performs no miracle.

It is given a bed near a shared hearth and a place in the household beneath the blue-flower bridge. Since no one has only one occupation, neither does the traveler. It polishes ladles. It carries covered bowls. It warms premature frogs between its palms and returns them to the lower pools. It learns to close the north channel with a twist of moss and shell that no amount of omnipotence would have suggested.

When asked where it comes from, it points upward.

This answer satisfies no one, since many things come from above: rain, dust, birds, strangers, cold, seeds, and occasionally death.

The inhabitants discover that the traveler is poor at several necessary tasks. Its baskets lean. It cannot judge when bitter cabbage is ready to cut. It consistently puts too much plaster on a wall and must be shown how to remove it without scarring the old surface.

Children begin hiding the same polished pebble in its clothing. Each time, the traveler expresses solemn astonishment. This continues for months.

Only slowly does the Ward understand what has entered it.

A cracked bowl breaks in the traveler’s hands and becomes whole, but only because the traveler forgets for an instant that broken things here are ordinarily mended. An eyeless frog, crushed beneath a shifted tile, unfolds beneath its touch and crawls away. Once, when a cistern runs dry after an unusually long summer, the traveler looks into it with such grief that water rises toward its face.

The elder of the western drip puts a hand on its shoulder.

“No,” she says.

The water stops.

“If that cup fills,” the elder says, “which wall empties?”

The power does not answer immediately.

It follows every drop backward through the stone, through mineral veins, buried channels, roots on the plain, clouds, seas, blood, leaves, and the mouths of animals. At last it bows its head.

“You are right,” it says.

After that, the people trust it somewhat more.

At the year’s darkest hour, when the roof crack is a thin grayness and frost reaches almost to the central court, the households offer the traveler a full cup of water.

It receives the cup in both hands.

A full cup cannot be accepted casually. The traveler speaks its long name directly into the elder’s ear. The elder begins to weep, though afterward she can remember only one syllable, and that syllable means neither maker, sovereign, judge, nor god.

It means something closer to: One Who Could Refuse and Did Not.

The traveler drinks.

Then it asks, “What would you have me change?”

The question passes through the Ward without sound.

Hands pause above looms. A knife ceases scraping a root. An old plasterer holds wet lime between his fingers and does not apply it. Children look toward their parents. The white moths rest on the walls.

At last, someone signs: Warmth.

Someone else signs: Water.

A mother whose son has gone to the upper world signs: Bring back.

A child signs: More blue flowers.

The keeper of a failing root bed wants richer soil. A bone-setter wants copper needles. A young listener wants the roof crack widened enough to see the moon. One of the very old wants nothing for herself, but asks that the western stair never settle another finger’s breadth.

The power considers every request fully. It does not call any of them foolish.

Then the elder asks it a question in return.

“What do you see that we do not?”

The traveler looks around the central court.

It sees cold, but also the way shared cold has gathered households around common hearths.

It sees scarcity, but also ten thousand acts of attention by which scarcity has been prevented from becoming cruelty.

It sees bent backs, scarred hands, narrow rooms, fragile cisterns, children taught restraint before they have words for desire. It sees courage distributed so evenly through ordinary life that no one here thinks to name it courage.

It sees, too, what the inhabitants no longer see because they have built custom around it.

“You have no place,” the power says quietly, “where nothing is harmed by your voice.”

No one moves.

The city has rooms for birth and dying, fever and sleep, roots and tools, birds and bones, water and flame. It has shrines no larger than shelves. It has alcoves where lovers can draw close and corners where quarrels are conducted in furious gestures.

But it has no place for a cry.

The power does not mistake quiet for peace. It has heard the unsounded shout behind clenched teeth. It has seen mourners breathe into folded felt. It has watched a woman in childbirth bite through a strip of leather rather than displace the droplets feeding her neighbor’s garden. It has noticed the loud-laughing man leave every celebration before delight overtakes him.

Even grief must be careful in the Still Ward.

The elder touches the wall.

“Can there be such a place?”

“There can,” says the traveler. “If you want it.”

They deliberate for nine days.

The power does not participate. It clears gutters and turns mushroom beds while the Ward argues in gesture and whispers. Some fear that a room for noise will make people careless elsewhere. Some say voices restrained too long may become dangerous when released. Some say the old law has kept them alive and should not be given exceptions.

Others answer that mercy is not an exception to care.

In the end, the decision is nearly unanimous.

The Ward asks for one room.

Not a warm river. Not an endless cistern. Not resurrection, wealth, sunlight, or an unbreakable roof.

One room in which a living thing may make whatever sound it must.

The traveler leads them to the lowest inhabited terrace. Behind an ancient wall lies a pocket carved by the vanished underground river, sealed before the earliest remembered construction. The inhabitants knew of it only as a coolness beneath their palms.

They open it themselves.

No stone leaps aside at the power’s command. There is no flash, no thunder, no effortless passage. The wall is thinned by abrasion over many months. Dust is collected and used in plaster. Each shell facing is removed intact. Moss is transplanted. A family of velvet mice is persuaded into a neighboring channel with warm root peelings.

Only when the last membrane of limestone remains does the power place its hand against it.

The stone opens without breaking.

Beyond is a round chamber, dark and empty, with a floor descending gently toward its center. The walls are black—not with soot, but with a soft mineral the Ward has never seen. Light entering through the doorway goes only a little way and rests there.

The people wait.

“What does it do?” a child whispers.

“It listens,” says the power.

The loud-laughing man is chosen to test it.

He enters with several friends and the traveler. Felt curtains are drawn across the doorway. Those outside place their hands against the surrounding walls and cistern rims.

Inside, someone tucks a fern into the man’s hood.

Out of lifelong habit, he catches his laughter in his sleeve.

The traveler shakes its head.

“You do not have to protect us here.”

The man lowers his arm.

His first laugh is uncertain and small. The second is louder. Then twenty years of guarded delight rise through him at once.

He roars.

He howls. He stamps his feet and claps his hands. His friends collapse against one another, laughing with him, startled by the size of their own voices. The sound fills the chamber, immense and bodily. It does not strike the walls and return. It enters the black mineral and is received.

Outside, not one drop falls out of sequence.

Not one moth lifts from the plaster.

When the people emerge, their faces are wet and astonished.

Afterward, the room is used for many things.

Children go there to shout their full names.

Singers, discovering themselves for the first time, hold notes until their breath is gone. The people invent drums and then, alarmed by their own invention, put them away for several years before trying again. Infants are brought there when no swaddling can quiet them, and their parents learn that a baby’s cry, when it need not be feared, is not an offense but a summons.

Quarrels are sometimes taken below, though the room does not make cruel words harmless. The wise power has not altered consequence, only vibration.

The grieving use it most.

There, names that have shortened through years are spoken whole again. People sob without swallowing the sound. They ask the dead why they left. They shout accusations at illness, collapse, winter, chance, and the cold laws of flesh.

The black walls keep none of it.

This is the room’s deepest mercy: it listens completely and remembers nothing.

Sound enters the stone and becomes a minute warmth, never enough to heat the chamber or be harvested. Sometimes, after great grief, a little condensation appears on the walls. It is not miraculous water and possesses no healing virtue. The inhabitants wipe it away with ordinary cloth.

The power remains until the room has become part of the Ward rather than a gift imposed upon it. It waits until rules are made, revised, and softened. It watches children who once shouted there grow into careful adults who know the difference between silence chosen for others and silence forced upon oneself.

Then, one dusk, it prepares to leave.

The people offer it another full cup.

This time the power drinks only half. It gives the remainder to the elder of the western drip, now so old that others must carry her to the central court.

“Will the Ward endure?” she asks.

The power could answer for a thousand years. It could name each future fracture, drought, birth, departure, and repair.

Instead it tells her the truth that wisdom permits.

“Not forever.”

The people bow their heads.

“Nothing living does,” it says. “But you are not living wrongly because you cannot live unchanged.”

Before departing, the power offers them one final act.

It does not promise that the roof will never fall. Such a promise would turn attention into complacency and care into ceremony without purpose. It does not make the water endless or the gardens immune to blight.

It places one palm against the oldest stone of the Ward and asks the city—not commands, but asks—that if a day comes when it can no longer hold its inhabitants safely, it give them warning enough to choose.

The limestone accepts.

From then on, dangerous changes do not hide entirely. A failing lintel pales before it shifts. A channel tastes faintly of iron before it closes. Deep movements in the old river bend travel first as a gentle pulse through the central pool, where a listening hand may feel them.

It is not rescue.

It is time.

To a wise power, time freely given is often the most honest miracle.

At the upper seam, the child who first found the traveler—older now, but still carrying moth cocoons—asks whether it will return.

“If I am invited,” the power says.

Then it climbs to the plain and unfolds into something for which the horizon would not have been large enough. The heath bends without wind. Every quartz vein beneath the ground brightens once.

By the time the child looks up, there is only evening.

Below, the Ward remains cold, crowded, brilliant, and still. Its people continue to bend beneath bridges and share warmth around small hearths. They tend the same cracks. They lift water in bone ladles. They do not become louder merely because loudness has been made safe somewhere.

But beneath the lowest terrace is a black-walled room where quiet is not required.

And sometimes, far under the pale stone and close-cropped heath, someone speaks a lost name at the full strength of the human voice.

Nothing breaks.

No garden thirsts.

The city listens, and lets the sound go.
