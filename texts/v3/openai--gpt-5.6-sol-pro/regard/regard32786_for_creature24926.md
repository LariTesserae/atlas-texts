The power does not mistake quiet for sorrow.

It does not look upon the empty courts and imagine that emptiness must be filled. It does not summon the builders from death, or make new builders in their likeness. It does not release bright fish into the reservoirs, because there is nothing there prepared to be eaten by fish, and nothing prepared to eat them when they die. It does not scatter insects among the reeds, or birds among the trees. Those gifts would arrive as hungers.

Nor does it give the city a voice.

A voice would require an answer. Awareness would divide the place against itself: root from wall, marsh from terrace, present from remembered hands. The city has no organ in which such a division could rest without pain. To awaken it merely so that it might know it was alone would not be kindness.

The power is wise enough not to call every silence sleep.

So for a long time, it does nothing that can be distinguished from attention.

It follows the water from the ridges. It knows each drop not as a separate object, but as a passage: cloud to stone, stone to seam, seam to channel, channel to reservoir, reservoir to root, root to leaf, leaf to saturated air. It perceives the old city folded into those passages. The walls do not obstruct the watershed. They persuade it. The gardens do not command the plants. They offer them depths, edges, and intervals. Even abandonment has become a form of use.

The power finds no wound.

There is death, but no terror of death. There is competition, but little deprivation. A fern shades a film from light. A moss cushion takes the moisture that might have entered a neighboring seam. Fungal threads meet beneath a fallen reed and turn aside, or one enters the other and both are altered. Yet nothing flees. Nothing cries out beneath a tooth. No mind waits in fear for the next hour.

There is no suffering that asks to be ended.

But there is one danger, and it is not visible from the western terrace.

The city’s continuities have become perfect at answering the same question.

The reservoirs hold the same line. The marshes keep the same borders. The meadow bodies repeat themselves through runner and fragment. The trees cast seeds into the circles already occupied by their own roots. Each life is fitted closely to the narrow measure that sustains it. The whole place endures because tomorrow resembles today.

The power can see farther along time than the city can.

It sees a day—not soon, but coming—when one ridge will settle by less than the height of a hand. A deep joint will close. Another will open. Water that has entered the plateau for ages will choose a lower fracture and emerge beyond the saddle. The change will not be violent. No wall will fall. No reservoir will burst.

The waterline will simply descend.

By the time the moss tightens and the floating leaves touch mud, the loss will already be beyond remedy. The city’s life will have no custom for retreat. Its roots know fixed margins, not traveling ones. Its films know constant immersion, not interruption. Its channels can distribute abundance, but cannot pursue departure.

The power could prevent the ridge from settling.

It could bind every fracture and hold the mountain in its present form. It could preserve the reservoirs at their mineral lines and keep each tree inside its green circle until stone itself grew old.

But that would make the city dependent upon mercy forever. Preservation without end would not be care. It would be the replacement of one narrow equilibrium with a narrower one, enforced from beyond the world.

Instead, the power decides to give the city what it does not possess.

Not abundance.

Not consciousness.

Not protection from change.

Practice.

The first act is very small.

At the uppermost reservoir, beneath a lip of clay faced with black slabs, the power loosens three grains of mineral deposit. They are smaller together than a seed. Water enters the space they leave.

For several days, nothing follows. Surface tension holds the opening closed. Then a thread of water passes through and darkens the outside of the basin. It does not reach the old overflow notch. It descends the wall by a new route, no wider than a root hair, and gathers beneath a paving stone where the ground has remained dry since the city was built.

The old notch continues to flow. Nothing has been taken away.

A second possibility has been added.

Clear films enter first. They have no intention of doing so. A portion of one colony extends wherever wetness permits, and wetness now permits this. Mineral crystals gather in the film. A green stain appears under the paving slab. Much later, a moss spore settles there and opens.

The power waits.

It waits through the slow thickening of the moss, through the deposit of silt no deeper than the edge of a fingernail, through the arrival of a fern whose first fronds are scarcely larger than threads. The new seep does not become a rival to the old channel. It becomes a reserve, a path able to carry life where no inherited structure had directed it.

Then the power closes it.

Not completely, and not at once. Over many years the three loosened grains are replaced by mineral laid down in the living film. The seep narrows. The moss dries at its outer margin.

Some of it dies.

The power does not turn away from this. Benevolence that cannot permit any death can permit no living world.

But the center of the cushion remains damp. Its tissues contract without failing. Spores ripen under the stress and travel farther than they would have in the constant saturated air. The fern withdraws from two fronds and preserves the growing point between them. The bacterial film enters the pores of the stone, where a residue of water remains after the visible thread is gone.

When the seep opens again, naturally, during a period of heavier condensation on the ridge, the inhabitants return more quickly.

The city has learned nothing in the manner of a mind.

Yet it is no longer exactly what it was.

The power repeats the gift elsewhere.

It lets one covered cistern fall a finger’s breadth below its ancient line, then slowly restores it. The pale floating plants cannot follow the descending surface, but fragments lodge on the newly exposed steps. There they develop thicker margins and shorter roots. When the water rises, some are drowned. Others loosen and float.

It allows a garden channel to run dry for part of a bright afternoon. The clear films become cloudy and seal themselves beneath thin skins. When water returns, their descendants tolerate an interval their ancestors could not.

It diverts a trickle across a bare ledge, then away from it, then back. Moss begins to live not only where moisture is constant, but where moisture is remembered in the stone.

It does not impose a season. A season would be too large, too sudden, and too general. Instead it creates small irregularities, each limited enough that failure in one place cannot become failure everywhere.

The changes are separated by years, then decades. No organism experiences the plan. There is no plan from their point of view—only a slightly broader world.

The meadow territories respond most slowly. Their runners have held the marsh boundaries for longer than any wall has stood. At first, when the power allows water to withdraw from a narrow crescent at the edge of one shelf, the sedges merely cease there. Their old roots remain pale in the dark soil. Fungi enter them. The boundary retreats by the width of two blades.

At the opposite edge, water spreads into a shallow depression that has always been dry.

The meadow does not cross.

The power does not move it.

For eleven years the new wet ground carries only bacterial stain. Then a broken length of runner, lifted during a rare gust and washed through a neglected gutter, settles in the depression. It sends up a single bright shoot.

That shoot is not rescued when the depression dries.

It folds. Its tip browns. Most of the runner dies.

One joint remains alive in the damp beneath a slab.

When water returns, two shoots rise.

The power attends to them with the same regard it gave the whole city, but it does not favor them over the old meadow. If they fail, their failure will belong to the truth of the place. Kindness is not the making of every attempt successful.

They do not fail.

After many repetitions, a new band of sedge occupies ground no vanished builder prepared for it.

This is the first line of living green in the city that follows neither a natural marsh edge nor a human work.

From the western terrace, it would be almost impossible to notice. It is only a slight curve beside a buried drain, irregular where the old gardens are straight. But the curve belongs to the inhabitants. It is not inherited geometry.

Seeing this, the power knows its work is nearly finished.

It makes no monument. A monument would draw meaning toward itself, and the city’s meaning is distributed. It leaves no incorruptible mark, no stone that refuses moss, no inscription awaiting eyes. It does not preserve the copper crescent as a sacred object. In time the roof above the workshop will sag, water will enter, and green salts will bleed from the blade into the clay. The blade will cease to be an absence among living surfaces. Even that is allowed.

The power alters one final thing.

Far below the city, on the outer slope of the saddle, a spring emerges from beneath a shelf of charcoal stone. Until now its water has sunk almost immediately into porous rubble. Nothing from the upper city has traveled beyond that point except dissolved mineral and the finest remnants of decay.

The power shifts a flat stone.

Water gathers behind it, rises, and passes over a lower edge into a shallow runnel. The runnel does not lead toward another city. There is none. It crosses a strip of bare upland and vanishes into a distant marsh beyond the saddle.

At first the water carries only what water always carries: salts, dust, transparent cells.

Then it carries a spore.

The spore settles in a seam where the new runnel is shaded from the brightest hours. It does not grow. The place is too exposed.

Another follows years later and vanishes beneath mineral deposit.

A third opens.

The fern that rises there is small and malformed. Its first frond is shortened by dryness. Its second is torn by wind. Its roots find no packed clay, no wall joint shaped by hands, only a natural crack in young stone.

It remains.

The city has acquired an elsewhere.

Not an empire. Not an escape. Not even a future guaranteed to resemble itself.

Only a path along which continuity may become variation.

Then the benevolent power withdraws its hand.

It does not withdraw its regard.

When the ridge settles, in the age the power foresaw, the reservoirs descend. Some floating gardens perish. Moss grays around cistern mouths. One of the old trees sheds its leaves and does not replace them. The covered chambers cease their steady dripping for a time.

But water persists in the secondary seams. Films endure inside stone. Ferns survive along routes that once existed only as trials. The meadow draws inward at one margin and advances at another. Beyond the saddle, the small distant colony receives spores from plants no longer living in the city and returns others changed by harsher air.

The old equilibrium ends.

The city does not.

It becomes less exact, less legible, less faithful to the uses for which it was built. Garden beds blur. A reservoir becomes meadow. A dry channel fills with shrubs. One court, deprived of seepage, remains bare except after rain. Elsewhere, roots cross the central way and enter structures they had accommodated for centuries without occupying.

The city begins, very slowly, to cease listening only to its own water.

It listens to absence.

It listens to return.

It listens to the difference between one year and another.

It does not know that a power came there. It does not know it was seen, or judged worthy of care. It never understands the minute disturbances as questions, nor its own survival as an answer.

This, too, the power intended.

For the wisest blessing leaves no obligation to be grateful.

And the kindest gift given to a place that lives by remaining is not to uproot it, awaken it, preserve it unchanged, or fill its silence with creatures that resemble the giver.

It is to teach remaining how to move.
