The power does not descend at once.

It has learned that arrival is often a form of injury.

It remains beyond the cloud and attends.

For seven hundred years it watches the rain cross the plateau. For another three hundred it follows the migrations of the black herds, not by looking at their bodies but by listening to the route that persists beneath them. It learns the intervals of the chimneys. It reads the iron beetles’ radiant diagrams. It waits through flowerings, foldings, fires, contradictory rivers, and the slow conversations of the radial animals below.

It does not mistake abundance for happiness.

Nor does it mistake strangeness for suffering.

This distinction takes time.

Many things that would seem terrible elsewhere are not terrible here. To be eaten by one’s own past is sometimes only a change in weather. To be rebuilt with the instincts of another species is not always a violation. A grazer born with sealed ancestral eyes may carry no grief concerning sight. A flock distributed across several mutually exclusive afternoons may experience itself as complete.

The power does not repair these things merely because they are difficult to understand.

It sees delight, though delight has no fixed organ. It passes as a tremor through a herd entering warm rain. It flashes between beetles as they complete a living circuit. It opens in the burrowers when a sick chimney’s rhythm steadies beneath their feet. It fills the winged creatures when the wind accepts their persuasion. It exists in forms for which the power has no names and therefore does not name.

But there is suffering.

Some of it is brief and clean: hunger, rupture, cold, the closing of a flower on a body that wishes to remain outside. This the plateau knows how to use. It enters root, blood, weather, recollection, and becomes something else.

Other suffering cannot pass.

A pale runner consumes the tracks of a six-legged grazer. The grazer dies. The plateau remembers it.

Years later the animal is produced again beside a respiratory chimney, carrying the exact sensation of being overtaken. It runs before its legs have hardened. It runs though no predator follows. It runs until its feet split and its body is taken into the gray-green surface.

Then it is produced again.

Elsewhere, a colony of chimney burrowers continues tending a column that vanished during a contradictory storm. The colony persists in conductive roots as a pattern of labor without an object. Thousands of small forefeet comb membranes that are no longer there. Thousands of tails strike an absent blockage. Their remembered duty passes from root to flower to rain-born larva, never completed and never released.

In a pool with several depths, an amphibian is always being seized.

The event has lasted nine centuries.

The power sees these things.

Deeper still, it perceives the plateau’s own distress—not in every part and not at every time, but as a tension distributed across its whole immeasurable body. The plateau can transform almost anything. It can make hunger into anatomy, weather into offspring, erosion into thought. What it cannot do is cease.

Nothing within it knows how to be entirely over.

The oldest shocks remain in the conductive roots. Extinct instincts pass into new bodies. Failed organs recur because their shapes have been repeated. Every wound becomes a mouth through which the past continues feeding. Even rest, when attempted, becomes a dormant organ gathering pressure.

Production never stops.

Thought never finishes.

The power considers silence.

A lesser kindness would impose it.

Clouds would part. Engines would slow. Contradictions would be forced into one sequence. Species would be separated from species, animal from landscape, life from recollection. The plateau would become comprehensible. Rivers would choose one course. Rain would fall only downward. The dead would remain dead.

Much suffering would end.

So would almost everything that lives there.

The power does not do this.

Instead it seeks consent.

This is difficult, because the plateau has no single chamber in which agreement might reside. A question placed in one root emerges elsewhere as a predator. A question asked in weather becomes a season. A question offered to the wandering tall ones is arranged into beetle shells and forgotten by the bodies that made it.

So the power composes its inquiry in the only language broad and slow enough.

It alters nothing.

For one complete cycle of the plateau’s oldest ridge—a span longer than the existence of several surrounding mountain chains—it watches without intervention. Its attention protects no creature, redirects no storm, prevents no flowering. It simply remains.

Attention of such purity is not inert.

The plateau begins to feel the unchanging pressure of being witnessed.

Rain erodes around that pressure. Rivers bend according to it. Migratory routes hesitate at its edge. The broad landforms gradually include it in their thinking. A basin deepens in the shape of the power’s patience. Three escarpments retreat until their exposed growth rings form a single vast pattern.

From above, if above were stable enough to permit seeing, the pattern would resemble an open hand.

The power places its question there.

Not in sound. Not in light.

In relief.

It creates, for the smallest possible instant, an absence of compulsion.

Across the plateau, every process continues—but for that instant, no process must continue.

The pumps may pump, but are not forced.

The roots may remember, but are not required.

The prey may flee.

The chimneys may breathe.

The old thoughts may recur.

Or not.

The instant is briefer than the opening of a rain pore. Most creatures do not notice it. The herds take another step. The beetles remain bright. The deep mouths continue their pressure speech.

Yet in the pool with several depths, the amphibian is not seized.

Only once.

After nine centuries, the predator’s mouth closes on clear water.

The plateau shudders.

Every white dome opens at the same time.

Petals strike the flats with continent-shaking force. Violet light passes beneath the clouds from horizon to horizon. Ancient water rises in shining walls. Thousands of broods, incompatible with one another, are thrown into the storm.

Among them stand hundreds of the tall, many-faced organisms.

None returns to the flowers.

They cross the plateau. From every direction they come: walking over unsafe flats, through rivers that choose both courses, beneath rain suspended from futures that will not occur. Animals gather behind them. Herds turn from their migrations. Iron beetles rise in dim clouds. Amphibians travel through connected wetness. Chimney colonies pour from their valves.

The tall ones reach the hand-shaped basin.

There they begin to make an arrangement.

It takes thirty-one years.

They use no object more precious than another. Shells. Roots. Eggs. Teeth. Empty seed chambers. Living animals who remain willingly in place. Water carried from pools that disagree about depth. The remembered movements of extinct flocks. A portion of cold blue fire. The last breath of a chimney. The first footprint of a herd not yet produced.

As they work, their bodies soften. Pale fibers trail from their limbs. Faces slip loose and are taken up by other wanderers. Intention passes between them faster than decay can erase it.

When the arrangement is complete, only one tall organism remains.

It stands in the center of the basin, rain passing through its hollow head.

It raises one hand.

Then it closes the hand.

The arrangement means:

**Yes.**

It opens the hand.

Then closes it again.

The arrangement means:

**But not everywhere.**

The power understands.

For the first time, it enters.

There is no thunder. No brightness. No figure visible inside the cloud.

One object appears upon the plateau.

It is small enough for a burrower to climb. Larger than an iron beetle. Dark, rough, and cool.

A stone.

Not wood hardened into the memory of stone. Not a shell awaiting instructions. Not compressed root felt or mineralized petal or an organ from some geological phase of growth.

Stone.

It contains no seed.

It conducts no inherited fear.

Electricity crosses its surface carrying only electricity. Water touching it remembers nothing but downward. Its shape does not solicit repetition. Its shadow belongs to the present object and no other.

The plateau recoils.

For miles around, the gray-green skin contracts. Respiratory columns close their valves. Deep engines disengage. Winged flocks flee into the cloud. The stone’s inertness is more alien than any living visitor that has climbed the escarpment.

Roots approach it and stop.

Spores settle on it and do not germinate.

Rain wears its surface by an amount too small for even the plateau to perceive.

Nothing is communicated.

Nothing answers.

The stone is not hostile. It is not resistant in the manner of armor or immune tissue. It simply does not participate.

For eleven years no animal goes near it.

In the twelfth, an iron beetle crosses the basin.

It has been carrying excess charge since the storm that produced it. Blue light leaks continuously from its joints. The pattern within it is ancient and damaged: part beetle, part abandoned pump, part memory of a lightning strike that killed a forest before the plateau rose above the lowlands.

The beetle touches the stone.

Its charge drains into the rain.

The pattern does not.

It waits.

No root sketches an organ beneath it. No flower stores its posture. No distant larva receives its fear.

The beetle climbs onto the stone and folds its legs.

For the first time in its lineage, an iron beetle ceases without burning out.

Its light fades.

Its shell remains.

The plateau waits for the shell to become instruction.

It does not.

The shell is only a shell.

Rain enters it. Wind turns it once. A small transparent mite shelters beneath its edge, then leaves.

Centuries pass.

The place around the stone changes, but slowly. Not because the power shields it. The power has withdrawn from action. The gift would not be a gift if it required eternal enforcement.

The plateau learns the stone’s exception.

At first it understands badly.

It grows rings of dead tissue around the basin. It manufactures hard nodules and withholds life from them. It produces animals without reproductive organs, flowers without seeds, rivers that end in dry mouths. These are not the same as rest. They are life mutilated in imitation of inertness.

The power does not punish the mistake.

The stone remains.

The plateau tries again.

A chimney chooses not to reopen one upper valve. The burrowers discover the sealed chamber. They do not gnaw through it. They sleep against the membrane, first for an hour, then through an entire pressure cycle. Their young are born without the rhythm of labor already moving in their tails.

A flowering dome opens and produces no brood. Its violet chamber remains empty. Animals enter, drink the old water, and depart. When the petals close, they enclose only darkness.

A dying grazer reaches the basin. The herd continues, because the route is older than tenderness, but one animal remains beside it until its breathing stops. Roots rise around the body.

They pause.

The plateau feels the migratory pattern, the shape of the legs, the final fear, the sugars in the cooling blood. It could take them. It could produce the grazer again. It could distribute its memory among flowers, insects, rain.

It does not.

Not everywhere.

Only here.

The body decays without becoming a proposal.

The roots feed on what has no story.

In time, more such places appear.

They are never large. A patch beneath an overhang. One chamber inside a chimney. A narrow strip beside a vascular stream. The deepest portion of a pool. A fold under the wing of a transparent creature.

Places where form is not carried onward.

Places where memory may enter but is not compelled to leave.

The plateau does not become stone. It does not abandon contradiction, recurrence, metamorphosis, or the great communal traffic of bodies. The herds are still rebuilt. Predators still learn hunger. Birds still form from hollows in flocks. The dead continue, often and strangely, in roots and weather.

But not always.

A new rule joins the old ones.

Water remembers downward, even when it falls another way.

Growth follows pressure more faithfully than light.

Nothing dead remains singular.

Electricity carries form as well as force.

Any shape repeated often enough becomes an organ.

Erosion is how the plateau thinks.

And:

**What is held may also be released.**

The new rule is uncertain. It does not apply uniformly. No rule there does.

During electrical storms it sometimes fails. A creature laid to rest beside the stone may appear again in a distant flower, confused and shining. A sealed memory may leak into rain. Some roots refuse the mercy and carry pain forward because pain has become essential to their structure.

The power does not return to correct every failure.

Wisdom knows that a gift of freedom cannot also be a command.

Instead it attends from far away.

Ages pass.

The stone diminishes beneath the rain. Grain by grain, the plateau erodes it. Yet the eroded particles do not become part of the plateau’s anatomy. They travel in water, collect in low seams, vanish over the escarpments. Some lodge in amphibian skin. Some are carried in beetle joints. One grain enters a flowering dome and rests at its center for forty thousand years.

The plateau cannot assimilate the stone.

But it can learn from the space the stone creates.

Eventually the first stone is gone.

By then it is no longer needed.

On a cold interval when the horizon appears beneath the land, a herd crosses a broad flat under clear blue light. One old animal falls behind. Its body is narrow and black, six-legged at the front and four-legged at the rear. Beneath its hide lie useless gills, sealed eyes, and the migratory memory of a species that once possessed wings.

It kneels.

The route continues through it, urging its legs to rise. The plateau could swell a teat beneath its mouth. It could send a warm rain. It could open the ground and rebuild the failing organs before death becomes decisive.

Instead the ground grows still.

Not dead.

Not absent.

Attentive.

The herd vanishes into cloud.

The old grazer lies alone, but its aloneness is not abandonment. For once it belongs only to itself. Its fear is not transmitted. Its posture is not copied. No flower waits to remake it. No predator approaches from its past.

Rain gathers along its blade-shaped head.

It breathes out.

The breath warms a few silver leaves and is gone.

Under the body, roots loosen their grip on memory.

Far below, the deep radial animals interrupt their long conversation. Chimneys inhale. Beetles turn toward a current that has not yet begun. Across the nearly empty surface, millions of temporary creatures continue their temporary lives.

The plateau receives the grazer’s matter.

It releases its form.

Then, beneath rain and weather and the immense labor of remaining alive, the oldest being in that cold country does something it had never done before.

Not everywhere.

Only for a moment.

It rests.
