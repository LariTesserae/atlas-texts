The power would not descend at once.

It would remain above the basin without shadow, listening.

This would take longer than the lives of the birds and longer than the slow advance of yellow lichen across a stone. A lesser mercy might mistake endurance for suffering and restore the old colony. A lesser wisdom might hear the wind in the towers and decide that the city was calling to be inhabited. It might fill the chambers with warm blood, give eyes to the thing below the central court, or raise the dead builder from its mineral sleep.

The wise power would understand that resurrection can be a kind of violence.

The vanished colony had completed its life. Its form remained, but the life now passing through that form was not merely an impoverished version of what had been. Moss, grazers, liverworts, black birds, hanging curtains, capillary membranes, rain, frost, and dissolution had made something else together. To restore the builder would be to evict its inheritors. To silence the wind would destroy the city’s weather. To make the basin warm and fertile would drown its minute nations beneath ordinary abundance.

So for a long while, the power would do nothing.

It would learn the difference between a crack that threatened the whole circulation and one that opened a needed shelter. It would learn which booming of the central vault came from wind, which from water, and which—if any—was an answer. It would follow individual droplets from cloud to wall, from wall to moss, from moss to grazer, from grazer to predator, from predator to black water. It would perceive the layered particles moving inside the stone and would not hurry to decide whether they were alive.

Most of all, it would listen to the exchange.

The exchange had no language of desire. It could not ask to continue. It could not ask to end. Yet it made distinctions. It favored stressed edges. It abandoned some chambers and preserved others. Given two routes, it chose not always the shortest but often the one that kept more routes possible afterward.

The power recognized this.

It was the smallest beginning of wisdom: not the preservation of a shape, but the preservation of possibility.

And it saw the one affliction of the place.

The city could change endlessly, but it could not depart.

Everything returned into it. Water crossed its plazas only to vanish into the same mountain. Fragments of moss lodged in other courts. Grazers slept in other pores. Mineral yielded from one wall and rose into another. Even the birds carried almost nothing away; they drank from the pale stone and left before the city’s substance could cling to them.

The streets ended at the basin floor.

The ancient life had never imagined elsewhere, and its afterlife had inherited that limit.

The power did not break the basin’s rim. It did not carve a river down the mountain or summon a storm great enough to scatter the city. Instead, on the last night of a wet season, it entered the place in the mildest form it could take: a difference in temperature no instrument would have noticed.

One buried seam remained unfrozen for several breaths longer than the seams around it.

That was all.

Water entered the seam. The mineral membranes responded, favoring the open route. A little dissolved wall passed into a crack beneath the easternmost street. The next mist widened it. The next thaw carried three grains farther. Nothing happened that year which an observer could have named.

Over centuries, the route lengthened.

It did not run toward the central shaft or back into the familiar lower channels. It wandered beneath the pavement, following faults that had always existed but had never quite joined. The city’s circulation explored it blindly. Sometimes the route closed. Sometimes frost turned it aside. The power never opened it a second time. To do so would have made the path the power’s work rather than the city’s.

But the exchange had encountered a new possibility, and it retained it.

At last, during a hard rain, water emerged from the dark rock several paces beyond the outermost wall.

It appeared first as a wet line. Then a thread. Then a clear runnel no wider than a bird’s toe, moving eastward across the basin instead of sinking back beneath the city.

The water carried milkiness from the capillaries. It carried liverwort dust, a broken length of communal filament, mineral shells smaller than cells, and seven contracted grazers sealed in their dry skins. It passed the solitary boulder. It crossed a field of black lichen. It reached the low rim.

There it hesitated in a shallow depression.

The power did nothing.

The depression filled.

Water spilled over the mountain.

Most of it vanished into cloud before reaching the lower slopes. The filament froze against bare stone and died. Five of the grazers never awakened. The mineral particles came apart beyond the conditions that sustained them.

But lower down, where a gray ridge lifted briefly through the cloud, a few drops struck a sheltered cleft.

Two grazers unfolded.

A fragment from one of the filament’s clear knots remained lodged beneath a flake of rock. Its green strand survived the cold. The liverwort dust darkened when mist returned. Nothing resembling a city rose there. No towers grew. No grand destiny commenced.

There was only a damp cleft that had not been damp before, and in it a small continuation which was also a change.

Back in the basin, the eastern route did not remain permanently open. Frost sealed it for eleven years. Mineral closed one section and dissolved another. Sometimes the city sent water beyond itself; sometimes it kept every drop. The new path became one possibility among many, which was the only form in which the exchange could possess a gift without becoming subject to it.

The black birds discovered the runnel.

At first they treated it with suspicion. They stood beside it with their wings slightly raised, watching water arrive from beneath apparently solid pavement. Because it ran beneath the open sky and over pale stone, one bird finally drank. A few translucent mineral shells adhered to its beak. When it returned to the lower ridges, rain washed them into cracks far from the summit.

Most broke down.

Some did not.

In time, the city’s afterlife acquired a faint, discontinuous reach through the mountains: not offspring, exactly, and not copies. A water-bearing thread in one cliff. A mineral membrane in another. Pale grazers waking around an unfamiliar droplet days of flight away. Here and there, on cold mornings, moisture climbed where gravity alone did not fully explain it.

The old city remained singular. Its cathedral did not regrow. No consciousness suddenly opened beneath the central court. The mysterious bubbles continued to rise at their old intervals, and the power did not force their meaning into revelation.

But one evening, many centuries after the seam first remained warm, the deepest shaft altered its breathing.

Its exhalation entered the lower channels, passed beneath the central plaza, found the eastern route, and went out across the basin as a warmth too slight to melt frost. For the first time, the city’s final breath did not return entirely into itself.

The wise power received no thanks.

It had not wanted thanks.

Before withdrawing its attention, it bestowed one further mercy, though this mercy consisted of withholding a gift.

It did not make the city eternal.

The power could have held every arch against collapse, kept the capillaries open, preserved each moss lineage, and turned every dangerous winter aside. But then the exchange would have ceased to be an exchange. It would have become a monument under guard.

So weather remained free to enter.

Walls would still thin. Species would vanish. New ones might arrive. One age, the central vault would fall. In another, the deepest shaft might close or open completely. Perhaps the circulation would continue beneath a city no longer recognizable as a city. Perhaps the mountain would finally take all of it back.

Yet it would not be trapped in perfect repetition.

It had been given neither immortality nor command, neither restoration nor purpose.

It had been given an outside.

Afterward, nothing in the basin looked blessed. Noon remained cold and exact. The walls stayed pale. The predator waited across the wet seams. Birds still feared the covered galleries. Frost still extinguished the towers one note at a time.

But after certain rains, when all the hidden channels were full, a little water crossed the open pavement and went over the rim.

And the city, which had never imagined another place, touched the world below.
