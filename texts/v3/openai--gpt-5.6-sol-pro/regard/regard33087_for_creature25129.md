The power does not arrive as light.

Light would be an alarm here. It would scatter the schools, wake the hunters, blind the soft eyes accustomed to darkness. Nor does it arrive as a voice. There is no organ in the city made to receive commandments, and wisdom does not mistake the absence of language for the absence of meaning.

It comes as attention.

For the first time, the whole basin is held in a single awareness: every warm fissure and stagnant room, every pilgrim moving by ancestral scent, every colony passing bitterness through its branches, every parasite hidden under a borrowed chemical name. The power perceives the needlefish individually and as a school. It perceives the towers as crowds, bodies, ruins, neighborhoods, and one slow mineral life. It feels the city’s suffering without condemning the hungers that cause it.

It could abolish predation.

It does not.

To remove the beam-limbs would let the needlefish multiply until their own food vanished. To forbid parasites would swell the colonies until they choked the avenues. To preserve every pilgrim would crowd the roads with ancient bodies and deprive unborn things of bone, shelter, and nourishment. An end to death here would not be mercy. It would be suffocation postponed.

The power could lift the city into sunlight.

It does not.

The living curtains would burn. The bacterial gardens would die. Creatures whose worlds are written in pressure and scent would be exposed to a brightness with no meaning except pain. The city is not unfinished merely because it is dark.

It could give every creature speech and make them understand one another.

It does not.

Understanding is not always kindness. The mud-breather need not know the lineage of every grain. The pilgrim need not hear the fear of everything strained into its mouth. To force all minds into one manner of consciousness would be another form of destruction, subtler than fire and nearly as complete.

So the power watches.

It watches through a spawning season and a season of poor currents. It follows a larval beam-limb until it is eaten. It remains with a diseased tower for the eleven years of its dying. It learns the long circuit of the pilgrims, including the portion now blocked by a collapsed district. It attends to the buried eggs that will not hatch until salinity falls, and to the salinity, which has not fallen in three generations.

It discovers that the basin is approaching a danger no inhabitant can sense in full.

The warm fissures are narrowing. Not tomorrow, nor in a hundred years, but soon by the measure of towers. Stone is being laid within stone. The central springs will weaken. At the same time, sediment is closing the old channels over the rim. One by one, the deep quarters will cease to breathe.

The city has survived dead zones before. This will not be a dead zone. It will be a long contraction from which no returning current will wake it.

The power could break the escarpment in an instant. But an instant would send a wall of water through the basin. Towers that took millennia to grow would snap at their foundations. The roads would be erased. Warmth would escape. Foreign predators would enter behind the flood.

Instead, the power places no hand upon the city.

It places a small pressure beneath a distant ridge.

A fault that would have remained shut begins to yield by the width of a bacterial thread. Mineral water enters it. The water cools, contracts, and draws more water after it. Acids work at the fresh stone. Burrowers find the softened seam and enlarge it without knowing why. Generations of them live, feed, and die there. Their descendants follow the taste of their remains.

The opening takes three hundred years to become a crack.

It takes nine hundred more to become a passage.

No flood comes. At first there is only a slight inward breathing along the upper terrace: water colder than the basin, carrying a little more oxygen and a different burden of salts. Black fronds spread toward it. Needlefish discover the current and begin to hunt there. Shell-bearers gather beneath the new growth. A pilgrim strays from its ancestral groove, follows the unfamiliar water, and presses the first shallow track into the sediment.

Others follow its scent years later.

A road begins.

The new current is not constant. The power has shaped nothing so crude as a pipe. The passage narrows in places and widens in others. Distant weather strengthens it; dry seasons weaken it. Sometimes it brings abundance, sometimes almost nothing. Life must still adapt. Districts may still fail. Creatures may still hunger. But the city will not be sealed inside its own consumption.

The power makes one further change.

This one is smaller.

In a deep plaza beneath a fused arch lies an old pilgrim. Part of the roof has fallen across its hind plates. It is not mortally injured, but it cannot pull free. Its vanes open and close in water already poor with oxygen. Cleaner shrimp have gathered on its face. They remove parasites at first; then, as the pilgrim weakens, they begin to taste the soft margins of its mouth.

Its death would not be unjust. Many have died so. Its body would nourish a district.

But benevolence need not become indifferent merely because death is useful.

The power does not lift the stone. That would be a miracle too sharp for this place, an event without ancestry or consequence. Instead it touches the contraction passing through the living arch above.

The tower closes its chambers.

Thousands of tiny bodies shorten at once. Water is expelled from the walls. The arch flexes by less than the breadth of a shell. A mineral branch, already weakened by borers, cracks and drifts downward. It strikes the fallen slab at one edge.

The slab turns slowly in the water.

Mud rises.

The pilgrim pulls one fin free, then another. It rests for a long time beneath the black cloud. Its pulse steadies. The shrimp retreat from its mouth and return to its gills.

At last it moves down the old avenue, carrying on its back worms, mites, eggs, gardens, and one juvenile shell-bearer that will someday leave it for a hollow in the new road.

No creature understands what happened.

The tower remembers only a contraction.

The borers remember nothing.

The pilgrim carries a new scar.

That is enough.

The power remains until the first full current passes through the distant opening. It feels the city respond district by district. Curtains unfold. Mud-mouths break their seals. Fish enter chambers black for centuries. Bacterial films alter color. A brood of long-waiting eggs detects the change in salt and begins dividing.

Then, because gifts can become cages when the giver refuses to leave, the power withdraws its attention.

It asks for no temple. It marks no chosen species. It leaves no law against hunger, no promise against collapse, no shining object that could be mistaken for the center of the world.

Its mercy is almost indistinguishable from geology.

A passage through stone.

A little more breath.

A trapped animal given one unearned escape.

And time—not endless time, but living time, dangerous and sufficient—in which the city may continue to build itself.
