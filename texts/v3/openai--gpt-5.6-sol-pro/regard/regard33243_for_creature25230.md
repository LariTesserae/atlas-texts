The power would not begin by opening the gates.

Gates are cruel to beings whose anatomy is place. To pull an eel from its cistern, a bird from its resonant roof, a gray beast from beneath the housing, might look like liberation to an eye accustomed to cages. Here it would be dismemberment. Freedom offered too quickly would be only another machine for producing pain.

Nor would the power stop the city.

The smallest mercy can become a catastrophe when administered to what one does not understand. A halted pump might collapse a buried chamber. A silenced flock might extinguish the thought sustained in the bricks beneath it. The locomotive’s useless turning might be all that prevented the creature below from completing a death begun before rain had learned to fall.

So the benevolent power would first attend.

It would stand beyond the concrete posts, on the silent mineral plain, and listen until the city’s immense noise ceased to be noise. It would distinguish every contribution: each shell striking iron, each eel-body carrying current, each minute wing closing a relay, each lateral eye holding some remote valve in awareness. It would follow every signal to its consequence and every consequence back to the breath beneath the pumping house.

This listening might require no time at all. Or it might require an age unavailable to the basin. The difference would not matter to the one who listened.

When at last the power understood the city as well as the city understood itself, it would enter in the least alarming form possible.

Not as light.

Not as a voice.

As redundancy.

A second current would appear beside the current produced by the eels, so perfectly matched that the dynamos could not tell which source sustained them. A pressure gentler than breath would rise through the pipes beneath the gray herds. The hollow beams would begin to remember their proper vibration between the beetles’ blows. Within the great soft bodies, impulses would arrive without needing to pass through flesh. The roof-bricks would answer a chord that no throat had made.

Nothing would cease. Nothing would yet change.

There would merely be, for the first time, more support than burden.

The city would not know what to do with this excess. Its gauges had no mark for enough. Its diagrams admitted no spare force, no reserve, no possibility that a function might continue while its living component rested.

The power would add no new instruction. It would not command release.

It would wait for an accident.

The first accident would be very small.

A shell-backed creature beneath a stair would raise its abdomen to strike and hesitate.

Not the fixed pause by which it measured returning pressure. A different pause: unassigned, causeless, its own.

The beam would ring without it.

The creature would remain suspended, impossibly heavy, its pale hands closed around the rail. Inside its lacquered carapace, the spinning thing would slow enough to become almost visible. It would wait for failure.

No failure would come.

Elsewhere, a valve would oscillate. A gauge would tremble over its ancient value. The buried engines would maintain their concussion. The city would not punish the missed blow because the missed blow had been borne by something else.

The creature would strike again, harder than before.

Then it would hesitate a second time.

Across the basin, attention would move from eye to eye.

An eel would stop swimming.

For one terrible instant it would experience the water not as resistance, function, conductor, or assigned medium, but as coolness along its body. The other eels would continue turning. The new current would carry what was lacking. The motionless eel would begin to sink, then discover that sinking was a direction it had never been required to know.

It would descend through the silver dust and rest on the cistern floor.

A roof-bird would close its beak.

The metallic chord would remain whole.

Its throat would convulse around the absent note. Its breast would rise without producing resonance. Air would enter it and leave as mere air.

Then another bird would close its beak. Then seven. Then a length of roof would fall silent, though the chord continued from no visible mouth. Feathers loosened from one another. Warm bodies that had existed as a continuous fabric discovered the narrow borders of their own skin.

Some would be frightened and begin singing again.

The power would permit that too.

Mercy would not demand courage as payment.

In the alleys, the gray herds would be the last of the smaller inhabitants to understand. Their labor was deeper than action. Their rib cages had made pressure for so long that breath and duty were the same motion. Even when the new force entered the housings, they would continue inhaling and exhaling, unable to imagine air that did not belong to a pump.

The power would not pull them away.

It would send into their many lateral eyes an image with no authority attached to it: an empty yard beneath flat white daylight. Bare stone. Open distance. No destination. Six feet placed wherever six feet wished.

A beast at the edge of one herd would blink along its flank.

It would draw breath.

The machinery above it would draw breath too, but separately.

The beast would hold the air.

The city had contained sirens beginning and ending, rain falling and stone drying, wheels turning at the summit of effort. It had contained every industrial tension without completion. But it had never contained a held breath that could be released at will.

The beast stepped forward.

Its couplings slid from between its ribs without wound. Its first foot entered the empty alley. Its second dragged a shallow groove through the cinder. Its other four followed awkwardly, as if sequence were a new kind of ground.

Behind it, the herd continued pumping.

Above it, the belts continued moving.

Nothing called it back.

The groove remained.

That mark would be the first new thing in the basin.

Then the great beings in the halls would begin to stir.

Here the power would move with still greater care. It would take the weight of busbars into itself, cool the porcelain collars, carry the furnace intake, cradle each exposed mass where concrete had cradled it. It would offer relief at every point but sever nothing without consent.

Some of the great bodies would withdraw their limbs immediately. Walls would split around antlers that had never before moved. A ray spread across three floors would gather itself with the slowness of a continent and discover that its fins had always possessed edges. The being made mostly of mouth would loosen from the furnace and utter, not a roar, but a wet and bewildered sigh.

Others would not move.

For some, function had become identity too completely to be abandoned. They would feel the offered rest and refuse it—not from coercion, but because their joy, if it could be called joy, was the passage of current, the arrival of pressure, the answering flash. Benevolence would not insist that every fixed life secretly desired another.

The power would leave their connections intact.

But from then on, their labor would be chosen at each pulse.

At last the power would enter the pumping house.

It would descend through the shaft without light, because light might impose a shape on what had none. It would pass the worn rim, the roots of foundations, the damp cavities where the city’s signals braided themselves together. It would go below the eels’ deepest extremities, below the buried rails, below the place where the mineral plain pressed against the basin like a lid.

There it would find whatever breathed.

Perhaps an animal.

Perhaps a machine.

Perhaps the folded remains of all who had ever inhabited the city, compacted into one dreaming organ.

Perhaps only a depth taught to inhale by the pressure above it.

The power would not require the distinction.

It would place no hand upon the breather, but the breather would feel held.

Then, through every pipe and wire, every throat, rail, gauge, puddle, tile, lung, and layer of stone, the power would ask one question:

**Do you wish for the next breath?**

Not: Do you wish to live?

Life was too disputed here.

Not: Do you wish to be free?

Freedom had no meaning that did not first account for roofs and cisterns.

Only: Do you wish for the next?

The city’s answer would not be unanimous.

The flock would answer in thousands of closures and openings. The herds would answer with feet, some stepping forward, some settling more firmly beneath the belts. The eels would answer in currents that diverged for the first time. The great bodies would tighten, release, or remain still. Mites would leave numerals unread. Relay-worms would carry light backward. The white creature above the square would reach the chimney and, instead of appearing again at the beginning of its flight, vanish behind it.

All these answers would descend.

The thing beneath the city would inhale.

Then it would exhale.

The exhalation would complete.

For the first time, the roar would not return to precisely where it had been. It would diminish into echoes. The power would bear every function that needed bearing while the inhabitants discovered which motions were theirs and which had only been necessary.

Silence would not fall all at once. It would arrive in districts.

The western bells would strike three times and stop after the third. The locomotive’s wheels would slow until individual spokes emerged from the blur. It would remain on the track, but steam would cease issuing from its valves. The unchanged plates beneath the presses would receive no final blow. The conveyors would bring one set of ingots into the open and fail to carry them back.

Rain would cross the avenue.

The southern stones would darken.

A drop beneath an overhead flange would swell, fall, miss the old dark spot, and break on fresh dust.

At the boundary, the power would alter the posts—not by removing them, but by making the city’s relationships portable. Any creature that crossed would carry enough of its roof, rail, pressure, water, and remembered place to remain itself. The bird would not become resonance without chamber. The eel would not become current without circuit. Confinement would no longer be their anatomy.

Some would go onto the plain.

A gray six-legged beast would be first. It would climb the slag embankment, pausing often, all its lateral eyes blinking at depths for which it had no names. At the concrete posts it would tremble so violently that dust fell from the pleats of its hide.

Then it would cross.

Nothing tore.

Behind it, a portion of the city’s buried pressure traveled invisibly, not as a chain but as belonging. The beast took another step, then another, inscribing six uncertain tracks upon the level mineral ground.

A roof-bird followed, flying badly. Its wings had long been part of a chord rather than instruments of travel. It lurched beyond the post, lost height, recovered, and landed on the plain.

There was no ledge.

It stood with its feet spread on level earth.

After a while it opened its beak.

What emerged was not metallic. It was a thin, rough sound, scarcely audible, unsupported by brick. The bird startled at its own voice.

Others remained in the city. Some resumed their posts, but with intervals. Some altered old tasks for obscure pleasures: eels turning dynamos only when the water was warm enough, beetles striking rails to hear variations in the ring, birds sounding chambers that no longer needed testing. A gray herd might pump for an hour and then disperse into the yards, leaving the machinery to the patient strength that had come among them.

The city would weather again.

Rust would advance and sometimes be cleaned away. Cisterns would overflow. Furnaces would cool. Walls would crack under moving antlers. Some structures would collapse, and what depended upon them would be caught. Other structures would be built, awkwardly at first, around bodies no longer shaped solely for use.

There would be loss.

Wisdom would not confuse benevolence with the prevention of every ending. The inhabitants had been preserved from death only by being preserved from life. To restore sequence was to admit risk, grief, error, and change.

But no creature would be forced to endure these alone.

The power would remain—not as ruler, engineer, owner, or hidden replacement city, but as the assurance that rest would not make the world collapse. Whenever an inhabitant wished to leave its station, the burden could be held for a while. Whenever one wished to return, its place would not have been given away as punishment. Whenever a function truly needed to end, it could end without taking every connected life with it.

And beneath the pumping house, the oldest being would be allowed to decide breath by breath.

Perhaps one day it would wake.

Perhaps one day it would die.

Perhaps it would discover that these were not opposites.

On the plain beyond the basin, the six-legged beast would eventually stop walking. It would look back at the city, now loud in some districts and quiet in others. Rain would move across it in an ordinary front. Birds would lift from roofs without settling into their former arrangement. One chimney would go dark. Another would begin to smoke.

The beast would lower its modest mouth to the mineral ground.

There would still be nothing to eat.

So the benevolent power, having learned what mouths were before it presumed to fill them, would place there something small enough not to be a command.

A single living seed.

Not a forest. Not a garden. Not an alien green conquest of cinder and salt.

Only a possibility enclosed in a husk.

The beast might eat it.

It might guard it.

It might crush it unknowingly under one of its six feet.

It might leave it untouched until rain and time found out what it could become.

Any of these would be permitted.

For the first gift had not been escape, silence, food, or even time.

It had been the space between necessity and the next act.
