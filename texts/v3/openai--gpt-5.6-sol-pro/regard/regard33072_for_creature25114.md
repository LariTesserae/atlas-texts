### The One More Hand

The power did not arrive in light.

Light, on the crown, was already a difficult thing. It entered where it could, struck waxed leaves and copper beads, heated sleeping roofs, exposed anyone foolish enough to stand too long at the rim. A new light would have frightened the birds, awakened the bats, dried the night cloths, and caused half the town to look upward when their feet required attention.

Nor did the power speak.

A voice heard everywhere would have entered cistern chambers, interrupted the listening hour, startled antelope, and shaken dust from old walls. A voice heard by only one person would have made that person dangerous.

So the power came first as attention.

It attended to the crown for one full turning of its seasons.

It learned the routes by which children knew the town: under the bitter vine, hand to the cool wall, around the sleeping antelope, three grips past the shrine. It hung in the northern air with the broad-winged birds. It entered houses as cooking smoke and left them in the fibers of condensation cloths. It passed through the fine ears of geckos in sealed chambers. It felt roots testing fractures finer than hair.

It watched the water keepers measure.

It watched a woman tip a dark bowl to the lips of a feverish child and wait until the final drop had crossed the rim. It watched the child’s brother lick the empty bowl only after their mother had turned away.

It watched a stair keeper descend with a cracked rib because a rung three days below the gate had begun to loosen.

It watched an old man forget the position of a buried beam and weep, not because he had forgotten his own name, but because a room above his daughter’s house could no longer safely be enlarged.

It watched quarrels over shade, lovers whispering through smoke holes, monkeys stealing fruit, undertakers wrapping the dead, gardeners freeing moths from resin, and assemblies taking six days to agree that a branch might be allowed to grow the width of two fingers farther east.

The power loved them.

Because it was benevolent, it wished at once to give them water.

Because it was wise, it did not.

A river laid across the crown would tear away gardens, chambers, graves, and laws. A spring would gather ownership around itself like sediment. A lake would make nonsense of every roof and lane, drown the roots adapted to thirst, multiply insects, soften walls, and turn the summit’s oldest knowledge into superstition within a generation.

Even rain, if made constant, would be a kind of conquest.

The power considered widening the mountain. It imagined broad terraces unfolding from the cliffs, deep soil, orchards rising where there had been air. But expansion would bring grazing herds, then roads, then armies interested in the summit’s defensibility and abundance. The crown would cease to be itself. Its people might be safer from falling and newly vulnerable to everything that traveled on level ground.

It considered placing an unbreakable bridge between the crown and a distant ridge. Yet every bridge leads in two directions.

It considered making the southern stair permanent. But no stair is permanent if those who use it cease to inspect its pegs.

It considered strengthening every wall, sealing every jar, curing every illness, preserving every name in the western chambers. But a town in which nothing failed would soon lose the arts by which failure was met. A mountain that kept every mark would have no room for new ones.

Power can be generous and still be crude.

Thus the benevolent power did nothing for a long while.

This was not indifference. Restraint cost it something. Each time a child died of fever, it felt the temptation to break the order of the world for that child alone. Each time a carrier slipped on the southern stair, it wished to place an invisible hand beneath the falling body.

Sometimes it did.

A porter would later say that a gust had pressed him inward when the wind should have drawn him away. A toddler would tumble from a roof and land in a seed awning that had not been stretched there the previous evening—or that no one remembered stretching. A clay jar would fall three steps, strike black pavement, and fail to break.

These mercies were small, rare, and impossible to prove.

The power knew that if rescue became expected, caution would become ceremony instead of survival. So it did not make the precipice harmless. It merely refused, now and then, to let one instant of inattention become the whole meaning of a life.

Still, attention without aid began to feel incomplete.

The power descended into the mountain.

The dark stone was not empty. It held old heat, immense pressure, mineral seams, sealed cavities, the dust of vanished seas, and deep fractures closed before the first root touched the summit. Far below the town, where no maul had sounded, the mountain remembered cold.

Not water.

Cold.

The power followed it.

There was, beneath the eastern quarter, a narrow fold of stone that cooled each night more quickly than the rock around it. Haze entered through passages too small for an insect. A little moisture gathered there, spread invisibly across the mineral face, and was taken back by warmth before morning.

It had happened every night for thousands of years.

No one knew.

The power did not fill the cavity. It altered no law. It placed no inexhaustible fountain in the dark.

It moved one stone the width of a moth’s wing.

Afterward, when moisture gathered, it traveled inward instead of outward. Drop joined drop in darkness. The yield was slight. In the dry season it would fill a drinking bowl each day. In the season of thickest haze, perhaps three. During the worst hot weeks, when the night cloths gave almost nothing, the hidden stone would remain cool.

It was not abundance.

It was a margin.

The power then waited for the town to find it.

This took eleven years.

The first to notice was a pale cistern gecko that began appearing in a house where no geckos had lived before. The family welcomed it, believing it had come for moths. It vanished each dawn into a hairline crack beside the floor.

Two years later, a child pressed her ear to that crack and heard a faint, irregular sound.

She told her grandfather. He heard nothing.

She told one of those who crawled beneath houses and listened. The listener lay for half a day with his cheek against the stone. Near evening he heard it: not a drip, exactly, but the small returning note of a chamber holding damp air.

A proposal went to the eastern assembly.

The eastern assembly sent it by runner to the courtyards above and below. The water keepers objected that excavation might open the cavity to heat. The masons objected that the wall bearing the house above was older than its records. The gardeners warned that roots of the white-flowering vine crossed somewhere beneath the floor. The undertakers believed, incorrectly but reasonably, that there might be an unknown burial chamber there.

The child, who had grown old enough to speak in assembly, repeated what she had heard.

The town argued for four years.

Then it began to dig.

No miracle sped the work.

Stone mauls struck in the morning. Fires warmed the rock in carefully measured patches. Damp cloths were not wasted on cooling; fitted tiles drew heat away. Dust was gathered for plaster. Every loosened fragment was numbered according to depth. The house above was supported by beams passed through three neighboring rooms, a shrine store, and the sleeping alcove of a woman who complained daily and fed the workers every evening.

The gecko continued to enter the crack.

In the seventh year of digging, the workers broke through.

They did not discover a spring. They found a chamber no larger than six people seated closely together. Its far wall was pale and cold. Across it moved a thread of water so fine that it seemed at first to be merely a difference in color.

No one cheered.

The water keepers ordered the chamber closed for inspection. Geckos were introduced. Clay dishes were set beneath the wall and weighed at dawn. Samples were touched to the tongues of three keepers, two children, an elder, and a visiting stair worker with no kin in the eastern quarter. The flow was measured through heat and haze, flowering and moth season.

The debate over ownership lasted another two years.

The household beneath which the chamber had been found claimed no exclusive right. Its eldest member said, “The water was here before our floor.”

The eastern quarter argued that it had borne the dust and noise of excavation. The western quarter replied that eastern roofs cast shade on western gardens. The shrine association claimed the white root had led the gecko. Children claimed that adults would never have heard the chamber without them. The gecko was brought to one meeting in a covered basket but declined to contribute.

At last they made a law.

The new water would belong to no household and would not enter the ordinary daily measure. It would be kept against events that broke measures: fever, fire, fracture of a major cistern, failure of the southern stair, the arrival of travelers whose water had been lost below, and years when the haze itself seemed to be dying.

A portion could also be granted when every water keeper agreed that strict accounting would cause greater harm than mercy.

This last provision was controversial.

It became the important one.

The chamber’s first water washed grit from the eyes of a stair keeper blinded during a repair.

Its second saved a grove when sparks from a kiln entered the dry roots beneath three houses.

Its third was given to an outsider.

She arrived alone at the southern gate after her companions had been killed below. Her water skin was cut. She had climbed the final day without drinking and could no longer swallow the dry grain offered to her. Under the old measures, several households would have shared the cost of keeping her alive, and none would have refused, though their children would have received less.

Instead the keepers opened the reserve.

The woman drank.

She remained on the crown for the rest of her life and became a maker of ladder bindings. Her knots were unfamiliar, then mistrusted, then studied, and finally adopted along the lower stair. Forty years later, one of those knots held when three older bindings failed.

No one connected this to the power.

The power did not mind.

A shrine was eventually placed outside the chamber, because shrines accumulated wherever gratitude and fear repeatedly crossed paths. But the shrine was not dedicated to a new god. It honored the cold stone, the listening child, the gecko, the workers, the neighboring households, the white root, and the patience of the assemblies.

The power was omitted.

This pleased it.

In later generations, the chamber acquired a common name. Its formal title meant *water outside the measure*. Children called it the One More Hand.

When asked why, they would say that a carrier climbing a difficult stair needs two hands, but sometimes receives a third: a hand at the back, at the elbow, beneath the basket, appearing only long enough to prevent what is entrusted from being lost.

The people did not become rich in water. They did not abandon the night cloths. They did not cease to seal jars, trap steam, inspect gutters, or teach children the horror of waste. Their customs remained necessary. Their skill did not become quaint.

But fever did not always force a choice between the sick and the thirsty.

A fire did not always mean ruin.

Hospitality could, on rare occasions, exceed arithmetic.

That was the power’s gift: not escape from constraint, but a little room within it. Not enough to dissolve consequence. Enough to let compassion survive consequence now and then.

The power remained afterward.

It inhabited no temple and demanded no name. It lived in the interval between a slipping foot and the wall, in the pause before an assembly chose speed over care, in the coolness that lingered one hour longer than expected. It sometimes guided a root away from a cistern seal. It sometimes caused a bird to drop a seed into the one crack where it might live.

Mostly, it attended.

For it had learned the word the people used for the crown, the mountain, and themselves:

*that which holds because everything is holding everything else.*

The power understood that benevolence did not mean taking the burden from them.

It meant, when the burden became greater than wisdom could answer, quietly taking hold.
