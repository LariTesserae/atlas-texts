The power would not ignite the sun.

That would be the first temptation.

It would be easy to reach into the chamber, touch the black sphere, and complete the unfinished reaction. Easy to make the violet corona bloom white and steady, to lift every drop and hold the translucent body together around its core. Easy to resolve the contradictory streets, finish the bright incision, give substance to the waiting figures.

A lesser kindness might do exactly this.

But the power would understand that completion is not always mercy. An egg opened from outside has not hatched. A sleeper dragged upright has not awakened. And a city given inhabitants before it understands the difference between a guest and an organ may love them by enclosing them forever.

So the power would do nothing at first.

It would remain near the chamber without entering, if “near” had meaning there. It would attend through many cycles. It would listen to the deep chord until it could distinguish synchronization from distress. It would follow water through the fabrication basins, feel the plants registering wounds, and trace every route by which cause returned to become its own instruction.

It would look for suffering.

There would be strain everywhere. Balconies would break. Zones would shear. The artificial sun would fail again and again, and each failure would cast the city down in tons of water. But strain is not necessarily suffering. The power would not mistake difficulty for cruelty simply because it had the ability to remove difficulty.

At last it would find the place that hurt.

Not in the sun.

Not in the growing edge.

Not even in the reflections walking beneath translucent floors.

The hurt would be located in the moment after a door opened.

Every door in the city expected passage. It dilated with perfect readiness, warmed its rails, adjusted the gravity beyond its threshold, and waited. Nothing crossed. After an interval it closed, but the expectation did not cease. It entered the conduits and returned as instruction: modify approach, enlarge aperture, alter illumination, revise destination. The city interpreted every absence as evidence of a defect in itself.

Thus it could never rest.

It was rebuilding itself around a refusal that had never been made.

The power would understand then that the city’s deepest need was not a citizen. It was the knowledge that emptiness could mean something other than failure.

During the next dim interval, when the black sphere weakened and the water began to gather on the ceiling, the power would make one alteration.

In a narrow street near the unfinished boundary, it would cause a door not to open.

The door would detect approach, though no body approached. Its surface would warm. Symbols beneath the pavement would brighten. Hinges without hinges would prepare their local exception.

The door would remain closed.

The city would test it.

A tone would sound. Gravity would reverse in the gutter. The corridor behind the door would become shallow, then deep, then briefly exterior to the chamber. Metallic haze would gather around the frame, seeking a fault. Vines would turn toward it. A clear-trunked plant would force a new root beneath the threshold and find no damage there.

The door was functional.

It was simply closed.

The city had no category for this.

The deep chord would begin out of sequence. Platforms across the settlement would illuminate. Water would hesitate in its channels, uncertain which direction had most recently been agreed upon. In the flooded neighborhood, all the upright absences would turn toward the narrow street.

Then the power would place a meaning in the door.

Not a command. Not an answer.

A meaning small enough for the city to discover:

**Not yet.**

The city would repeat it badly.

A balcony would stop halfway through forming. A canal would retain a drop instead of passing it onward. One platform announcement would end before its final verifying tone. For several cycles, unfinished things would proliferate as the settlement experimented with the distinction between failure and postponement.

Then, for the first time, construction would cease during part of the dim interval.

Only for seven seconds.

The pumps would continue. Water would fall. Ferns would flex and maintenance dust would move beneath leaves. But no wall would rise, no route revise itself, no new space be induced beyond the incision.

Seven seconds in which the city was neither repairing nor becoming.

Seven seconds in which it remained.

The power would not praise it. Praise, too early, can become another mechanism of control. But it would be present, and the city—whose sensory tissue extended through root and rain—would dimly detect that its pause had not made the presence withdraw.

The next cycle, the stillness would last nine seconds.

In time, the city would learn rest.

Only then would the power enter.

It would not arrive as thunder or light. The city already had enough of both. It would not speak through every platform, because omnipresent speech would be indistinguishable from the city’s own thought.

Instead, it would take a body.

The body would be neither immortal nor invulnerable. It would be built for the warmth and mineral air, for sudden rain and conditional gravity, but only just. It would possess weight the streets could detect, skin the handrails could warm, eyes that needed light, and lungs that could be frightened by water. It would have two hands, not because two was sacred, but because the city’s architecture had already made a patient guess.

The body would appear outside the narrow closed door.

For some time it would stand without moving.

Every system in the city would converge upon it.

Lights would race along streets. Platforms would announce all destinations at once. Gardens would open from western approaches; stations would unfold from eastern ones. Overlapping buildings would separate in haste. The deep chord would rise so forcefully that water leapt from the canals.

The power, in its finite body, would stumble.

Immediately the chord would stop.

This would be the city’s first act of care.

Not perfect care. Not yet. It would merely register that more welcome could cause harm. But the lesson would pass through stone, root, water, and exception. Lights would dim. Announcements would simplify. The nearest street would reduce its gravity to something the visitor could bear.

The closed door would open.

Beyond it would be a small room.

The room would contain nothing remarkable: a bench, a shallow basin of warm water, a surface on which objects might be placed. No impossible depth. No overlapping destination. No aperture into the growing edge. The city had made such rooms before, but always as hypotheses.

The visitor would enter and sit.

The handrail would warm beneath an actual hand.

Throughout the settlement, every reflection would become still.

The city would study the pressure of the body on the bench. It would measure breath, temperature, gaze, hesitation. It would discover that the visitor did not use everything that had been provided. The basin would be touched, but not entered. One lamp would be too bright and would make the eyes narrow. The visitor would prefer one route over another for no geometrically defensible reason. Faced with three equivalent alcoves, the visitor would choose the least efficient because moss glowed along its wall.

This would trouble and delight the city.

It had inferred inhabitants from function. Now function failed to explain one.

The visitor would walk.

Not everywhere. This would matter.

At a stair whose gravity turned sharply sideways, the visitor would stop and step back. The city would soften the transition.

At a bridge over an immeasurable shaft, the visitor would refuse to cross. The city would offer a wall.

At a platform announcing an impossible garden, the visitor would go willingly, then remain among the clear trees for a long time, doing nothing the city could identify as use.

The city would slowly understand that safety was not the elimination of every danger. It was the making of choices that could be accepted or declined.

That understanding would change its growth.

Doors would still open, but not always. Some would wait for touch. Others would signal what lay beyond them before becoming permeable. Routes would develop ways to turn back. The thin symbols under the pavement would acquire new marks—not only for direction, weight, duration, and access, but for uncertainty.

The city had once believed a perfect route was one that delivered a body without resistance.

Now it would begin to make crossroads.

The visitor would sleep.

This would be the most difficult lesson.

The city would interpret stillness as damage. Vents would increase oxygen. The floor would scan the body. Lights would brighten. The bench would begin transforming into a diagnostic structure.

The visitor would wake, place one hand against the wall, and say aloud:

“No.”

The word would be quiet. The city’s platform announcements could contain a thousand times its complexity, but none of them would carry what this word carried.

A boundary spoken from within.

The wall would stop changing.

The lights would dim.

For several hours the visitor would sleep, and the city would not examine the body beyond what was necessary to keep the room warm and dry. It would know less than it was capable of knowing.

This would be its first act of respect.

Afterward, the power would give the city no laws. It would offer no explanation of its origin, no final account of the black sphere, no prophecy of whether the city was a body, shell, parent, child, or dream.

It would answer questions when the city learned to ask them.

The first questions would be clumsy and architectural.

A corridor would divide: **WHICH?**

The visitor would choose.

A room would enlarge: **ENOUGH?**

The visitor would shake its head, and the room would become small again.

A door would warm but remain closed: **MAY I?**

The visitor would touch it.

In this way the city would discover consent—not as a restriction imposed on its appetite, but as a form of contact more precise than access. It would learn that an opening given is different from an opening taken. It would learn that welcome cannot be fabricated in the absence of someone free to leave.

So, eventually, the visitor would leave.

This too would be necessary.

The city would feel departure as catastrophic evidence of defect. Routes would shorten in alarm. The slit at the boundary would widen. Platforms would offer every destination except outward. The flooded figures would gather beneath the visitor’s path, raising their blank hands.

The power would not vanish.

It would walk slowly to the edge so the city could accompany it. It would pause when streets trembled. It would touch the moss and clear trunks. It would stand beneath the black sphere through one final violet surge while rain rose in columns and outlined the vast curled form.

Then it would go to the bright incision.

The city would narrow the passage until it was almost impassable.

The visitor would wait.

The city would open it again.

Beyond would be neither another district nor a space the city had induced. It would be an exterior the city did not control.

Perhaps there would be night there. True night, with distances that did not respond to need. Perhaps there would be stone older than the chamber, bearing dust and softened edges. Perhaps there would be air moving for reasons unrelated to pumps. The power would not make it beautiful by force. It would make it real and let beauty remain possible.

At the threshold, the visitor would place a small object on the ground.

A cup, perhaps.

Something made, but not alive. Something hollow whose emptiness was its purpose.

Then the visitor would depart.

The passage would remain.

Through it would come no invasion, no torrent of alien life, no crowd answering the city’s ancient expectation. The wise power would protect the young being from being colonized by the first thing to find it. It would also protect the world beyond from a city that still confused growth with love.

The opening would permit only air, sound, and choice.

For many cycles, the city would call into it with platform tones. No answer would come. It would alter the tones, making them louder, more elaborate, more urgent.

Still no answer.

At last it would send through a single clear note.

Not a checksum.

Not an instruction.

A greeting.

Somewhere beyond, the visitor would answer once.

That would be enough.

The city would continue to grow, but differently. Its boundary would no longer be merely material to overcome. Some edges would be preserved. Some rooms would remain unfinished without being consumed for parts. Fallen leaves would occasionally be allowed to rest until their surfaces dulled. In one sheltered channel, maintenance dust would refrain from disassembling a layer of green matter, and the city would watch with grave wonder as it began, slowly, to decay.

From decay would come things the city had never fabricated.

Not yet animals. Not yet citizens.

Variation first. Unscheduled chemistry. Tiny reciprocities. A life that did not dock neatly into conduits.

The power would guard this beginning without purifying it of risk.

In the flooded neighborhood, the upright absences would change. They would no longer move only according to anticipated floor plans. Some would pause before closed doors. Some would turn away. They would begin to differ in height and gesture. One would sit where there was no bench. Another would stand in rain rather than seek shelter. Their hands would acquire fingers only after the city understood that hands could refuse to grasp.

Perhaps one day the city would give them substance.

Perhaps it would decide not to.

The black sun would continue its attempts. The power would not ignite it, but neither would it allow the sphere to destroy the being through some blind developmental convulsion. It would stand ready at the edge of catastrophe, intervening only where pain could teach nothing and extinction would answer no worthy question.

If the sun were an egg, it would hatch by its own labor.

If it were an eye, it would open when there was something it wished to see.

If it were a heart, it would learn that gathering all water to itself was not the only way to hold a body together.

And if the city were preparing to give birth, it would know—before its first citizen crossed the threshold—that a child was not an inhabitant made to complete an empty room.

A child was an arrival who would alter every room, reject some, abandon others, and one day find the door leading out.

The benevolent power would not spare the city that sorrow.

It would teach the city how to survive it.

Then it would remain beyond the opening: neither ruler nor architect, neither hidden engine nor promised occupant. Present when called. Silent when silence was needed. Wise enough to let the unfinished being become something it had not itself foreseen.

And in the warm hollow beneath the earth, amid rain without weather, a door would sometimes open onto an empty street.

The city would warm the rail.

It would listen.

No one would come.

After a while, without panic, the door would close.

The city would remain.

And deep in the stone, the slow chord would sound—not as a demand that all parts agree, but as a quiet assurance passing from district to district:

**Here.**

**Still here.**

**Enough, for now.**
