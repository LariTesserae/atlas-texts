The power did not bring rain.

That was the first mercy.

It did not gather the high haze into clouds or draw hidden water from the stone. It did not fill cisterns, soften the leaves, or lay dew upon the black trunks. Water enough to delight the people for an hour would have woken every seed in the hollow. Roots would have opened in lungs and houses. Fibers would have swollen, knots slipped, shale split, salts run white down the walls. The Garden would have become fertile in the manner of a grave.

Nor did the power still the wind.

That would have been another kind of killing.

The sail creatures would have fallen where they were. The wind trees would have starved in their collars. The hooked membranes would have folded and remained folded. Reactive gases would have pooled over the fissures. Borers would have drummed in chambers that no longer touched. The shelters might have stood straight and silent for a little while, and then the whole hollow would have ceased to be itself.

A lesser kindness would have mistaken relief for salvation.

The wise power did not.

It came without descending.

There was no figure on the rim, no brightness in the dust, no voice louder than the Garden. Its attention entered as pressure enters a trunk: everywhere along the surface, too gently at first to be distinguished from weather.

It listened.

It listened for the length of a great gust cycle.

It knew the booming names of the old trees and the thin new notes of the young. It felt borers tap warnings through walls they would never see. It followed the sail nations as they chose, by tiny acts of surrender, which current would carry them. It felt each runner’s six soft feet release and adhere. It counted seeds against masks. It learned the histories in belts, the debts tied to roof braces, the marriages hidden in exchanged filters, and the obligations of the dead distributed among the living.

It listened beneath all these things, too.

Under the central chimney, deeper than the mineral filaments of the oldest tree had ever reached, the mountain was dividing.

The crack was not part of the Garden’s ordinary rearrangement. It was not a ledge preparing to shed, a vent choking closed, or a root collar ripening toward migration. A slab larger than the hollow had begun to separate from the escarpment. Perhaps it had been doing so for ten thousand years. Within a few seasons—perhaps within days—the western wall would tilt inward.

The Garden knew pieces of this.

Runners avoided three passages near the wall. Pale coils opened when no shadow crossed them. Borers in the western trunks had fallen into uncertain rhythms, reporting strain no colony understood. Gust-keepers had added warning twists to several public lines. Two households had moved their sleeping shelves away from the stone.

But no creature possessed enough of the danger to comprehend it.

This, the power understood, was not change.

Change broke crowns and opened routes. Change killed many and fed many. Change was the means by which the Garden remained.

This would be erasure.

So the power considered how little it could do.

It could have held the wall forever, but then mineral pressure would have gathered behind its unseen hand. The mountain would have become dependent on a miracle. It could have moved the people, but not the trees, the borers, the mats, the eggs beneath the leaves, or the buried seed drifts waiting for their proper gust. It could have rebuilt the hollow elsewhere, but a copy is not a continuation. No recreated tree would bear the cords of the dead. No new groove around a new chimney would be the groove worn by ancient migrations.

At last the power touched one stone.

Not the failing wall.

A stone far below the eastern face of the escarpment, beyond the Garden, where nothing rooted and no shelter stood. It warmed a seam no wider than a hair. The salts there expanded. A trapped gas found the weakness. The seam opened.

For three days the mountain made a note too low for ears.

The black trunks heard it through their collars. Borers ceased their ordinary speech and struck downward in unison. Charge-mats rolled away from the western wall. Runners crowded beside the shelters and would not lie upon the ground. Every tuned frame in the hollow pulled its cords tight, although the air was momentarily calm.

The gust-keepers did not know what was coming.

They knew only that something was.

That was enough.

Warning lines appeared across every traversable path. Thermal boxes were emptied. Children were bound in pairs. Food shelves were sealed. The people loosened the braces of their shelters so the walls could fall outward rather than crush inward. Harvesters opened selected trunks and removed no borers, but cut channels through which the colonies might escape. Families tied their history belts together—not because they expected to die, but because no single body should carry an entire household’s memory through what was coming.

The oldest wind trees shattered their own root collars.

One by one, they entered the rising current.

For an entire day the Garden migrated east.

Crowns scraped the chimney. Trunks boomed as they turned. Sail creatures were flung in shining clouds ahead of them. Grazers clung beneath leaves. Net-casters wrapped themselves tightly around whatever branches they had reached. The people crawled behind weighted shields, hauling cord, masks, eggs, tools, and slabs from the walls of the dead.

Then the western escarpment moved.

It did not fall into the hollow.

The seam the power had opened beneath the far side of the mountain accepted the strain. The great slab twisted as it descended and broke outward. Stone poured down the bare slope beyond the summit in a red avalanche. Dust climbed high enough to stain the mineral haze. The Rattle Garden bucked like a struck instrument.

Part of the western wall collapsed. Three shelters vanished. Many crowns broke. One ancient trunk split from collar to crown, and the borer colony inside it spilled pale into the dust.

But the hollow remained open.

When the winds returned, they entered through four notches instead of three.

For a long time this seemed disastrous.

The new current crossed the others at an angle no gust-keeper knew. Familiar paths became traps. Seed cases screamed at unheard pitches. Two thermal fissures went cold, while a crack beneath the southern chamber burst into blue flame. Trees settled where houses had stood. Houses were rebuilt where reflective runners had once nested. A swarm of sails was carried entirely out of the hollow and not seen again.

The Garden had been spared erasure, not spared grief.

The wise power did not hide this distinction.

It did not replace the lost swarm. It did not raise the three shelters or restore the split tree. It did not untie the inheritance knots that passed from the dead into other hands. To reverse every loss would have made all action meaningless. Rescue cannot require that nothing be allowed to happen.

But the power was not finished.

Having listened, it understood that the deepest hardship of the Garden was not danger. Danger belonged to every meal, every migration, every birth into moving air. Nor was it impermanence. The people did not ask stone to behave like stone forever. They knew better than that.

The deepest hardship was that sometimes change traveled faster than relationship.

A roof failed before a warning line could be pulled.

A trunk broke before its borers understood the strain.

A child was lifted before the nearest hand felt the tether tighten.

A household vanished, and the obligations tied to it became unreadable.

The Garden did not require certainty. Certainty would have become another rigid structure for the wind to tear apart.

It required an interval.

Only an interval.

Room enough for one thing to answer another.

The power could not ask the Garden in words whether it desired such a gift. Instead it asked in every language the Garden possessed.

It pressed gently against the old wind trees, and their fibers answered by loosening rather than resisting.

It sent a harmless tremor through a borer colony, and the colony passed the warning onward.

It altered a rising current by the width of a sail creature’s fin, and the creature adjusted.

It tugged once upon a public line.

The people found the tug at different times. Each household added a knot to the line and passed it onward. Some tied agreement. Some tied caution. One gust-keeper tied the pattern meaning: **No warning without a way to doubt it.**

An old mask-maker added: **Let it belong to no keeper.**

A child, crossing the line where it ran close to the floor, tied a clumsy loop that meant several things at once:

**Enough time to hold.**

**Enough time to let go.**

**Enough time to choose which.**

The power accepted these as wisdom.

Then it laid no new law upon the creatures. It added no foreign organ, no shining artifact, no immortal guardian. It changed only a relationship already present.

It taught the central chimney to resonate with deep strain.

Not all strain. Not every falling stone, hunting leap, snapped tether, or hungry season. The Garden would not announce each private death in advance. Such knowledge would paralyze grazers beneath leaves and make predators starve before open nets. It would turn life into avoidance.

The chimney would sound only when a change threatened to outrun all possible answer—when pressure gathered toward the destruction of whole colonies, chambers, routes, or webs of obligation.

The note was too low to hear directly.

Wind trees translated it into a slow beat beneath their usual booming. Borers felt it and struck a rhythm unlike any gust warning. Tuned frames went briefly slack. Hooked membranes withdrew. Runners’ reflective plates darkened. Sail creatures folded one fin and descended from the highest currents.

No single sign was infallible.

Together they made time.

The first time the deep warning came after the wallfall, the people mistook it for an aftershock. Some fled too soon. Some remained in their shelters arguing through the lines. One household lost half its food stores carrying them from a danger that did not arrive until two days later.

Then the new southern vent erupted.

Because there had been warning, the nearest trees had shed their driest branches. The people had cleared seed drifts from the fissures and opened escape routes through the crowns. The eruption burned hot and blue for seven hours. It killed mats, borers, eggs, and one person who returned for a belt left behind.

But it did not become a firestorm.

Afterward, no one built a temple to the power.

A temple would have changed the air.

They made no image of it. A fixed face seemed inappropriate, and in any case no one had seen one.

Instead, they added an empty span to their public cords: a measured length between one knot and the next. This space represented neither the power nor the warning. It represented the interval in which an answer could be made.

The empty span entered other customs.

When settling a dispute, the line was left untouched for one gust cycle before anyone tied the final knot.

When a child completed the first untethered crossing, the family did not immediately fill the blank cords on the child’s belt.

When a person died, the household waited through one deep sounding of a trunk before dividing the history knots, so that grief would have room to exist before becoming obligation.

Musicians learned to place silences inside their rhythms—not true silences, which the Garden never permitted, but openings in which trunks and seed cases could answer.

Healers left one unsealed fold in a final bandage until they were sure no seed remained beneath it.

Even masks changed. Their makers added a tiny chamber between the innermost filters. It held almost no air, yet wearers said the breath felt less imprisoned when it passed through that space.

No one knew whether the benevolent power remained.

The gust-keepers disagreed. Some said it had departed as soon as the deep note was established. Some said it slept beneath the eastern seam. Some said it had never been a separate being at all, but was the moment when the Garden came, briefly, to understand its own whole shape.

The oldest knot-readers rejected all three claims as too certain.

What could be said was this:

The Garden still killed.

Masks still failed. Tethers broke. Trees crushed shelters. Children made foolish crossings. Borers were harvested too deeply in lean years. Families argued over cords and routes. People ignored warnings when hunger made danger preferable to waiting. The fourth notch brought unfamiliar migrations, including a narrow flying predator no net-caster would eat.

Nothing became safe.

But when the mountain prepared to close its hand around the hollow, the trunks now felt it.

When the air arranged itself toward a ruin greater than ordinary weather, the frames loosened.

When many lives were about to lose the chance to answer, the Garden gave them an interval.

Sometimes they used it well.

Sometimes they did not.

That, too, the wise power allowed.

For benevolence did not mean tying every living thing so tightly that none could be taken by the wind. It meant leaving enough slack in the cord for choice, enough tension for warning, and enough strength that when one being was torn away, the whole pattern did not have to end.

The Garden remained dry.

It remained loud.

It remained dangerous, fertile, migratory, and alive.

But after that, the people said there were no longer only the three conditions they had always known: holding, breaking, and being carried.

There was also the moment before.

And in that moment, sometimes, a hand could reach the tether.
