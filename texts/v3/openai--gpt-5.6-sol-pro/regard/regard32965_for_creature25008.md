The power does not arrive in light.

Light would be noticed at once and misunderstood for years. It does not descend between the towers, still the fans, fill the cisterns, straighten the streets, or draw a river through the plain. It does not make the stone fertile. It does not turn rust backward under paint.

It comes first as attention.

For one complete cycle of the town—from the low tanks of late winter through the green weeks, the kiln months, the season of leaning survey pegs, and back again—it attends without acting.

It listens through walls with the old fitters. It goes down into the cisterns and learns their echoes. It follows willow roots along hairline leaks. It rests in the transparent shells of snails and is carried, slowly, up the ceramic fins. It feels each bead gather weight and let go.

It learns that the town does not need more water.

More water, given suddenly, would drown the settling beds, crack unprepared tanks, lift foundations, spread foulness through clean lines, and turn every local obligation into a quarrel over abundance. The works would be abandoned or enlarged beyond recognition. Outsiders would come with deeds, pumps, guards, promises, and measurements no wall could satisfy. The people who had learned to share by roofs and pipes would be told that water belonged to whoever enclosed it first.

The power could prevent all of this afterward. Wisdom declines to create the wound merely because benevolence could close it.

Nor does the town need to be made stable.

Its movement is not an illness. The bowl is young. Clay compresses. Spoil settles. Roots enter. Weight passes from one course of brick to another. A perfectly fixed town would become brittle in different ways. Its people would lose skills they do not know are forms of love: the hand placed against a vibrating wall, the midnight visit of a pipe-uncle, the neighbor who notices that a porch has begun to lean before the people sleeping inside it do.

The power does not preserve them from change.

It sees, however, what they cannot.

Beneath the northern quarter, under the abandoned quarry and below the deepest drill mark, there is a lens of pale clay folded between two masses of slag. Water has entered it grain by grain. No instrument in the town reaches far enough to measure the pressure there. In thirty-one years, after an ordinary autumn rain, the clay will move.

The movement will not be large by the standards of mountains. It will be less than the length of the valve square. But it will shear two buried mains, tip the eastern wall of the quarry into its pool, and empty three cisterns into the fracture. Tower Four will remain standing for nine minutes after its footing has failed.

Nine minutes is almost enough.

Almost is what the power chooses to alter.

It could fuse the clay to stone. Instead, it presses one infinitesimal passage through it, no wider than the track of a snail. The pressure begins to release. Not now in one catastrophic shift, but over years, in a sequence of minute expressions: a damp patch under an alder, a hairline stain on the quarry face, a retaining bolt that takes rust sooner than its neighbors, a road accepting a little more curvature than the surveyors expect.

The danger is not removed. It is translated into signs.

This is the first gift.

The second is given to the snails.

Nothing about their bodies changes. They remain black, inconvenient, translucent, and numerous after rain. Starlings continue to eat them. Children continue to keep them in matchboxes and forget them beneath beds. Tower crews continue to move them aside in the damp folds of their elbows.

Only their trails become slightly more discerning.

Coherent drops still refuse to join. They still roll loose and round through gutters. But where metal is sound, they pass quickly. Where ceramic is strained, they hesitate. Where brickwork conceals a growing fracture, they collect—not in great supernatural clusters, not in symbols or words, but in numbers just unusual enough to trouble an attentive person.

The first to notice is a child named Ir, who has been forbidden from entering the eastern service yard and therefore knows it thoroughly.

Ir is racing six coherent drops down a discarded pane. Five descend. The sixth crosses the glass, leaves the lower edge, travels along a rusted bracket, and stops against a bolt in the footing of Tower Four. It remains there despite the vibration.

Ir retrieves it with a spoon and tries again.

The drop returns to the same bolt.

For three afternoons Ir repeats the experiment, losing two drops into a copper channel and one into a boot. On the fourth afternoon a tower worker catches them and confiscates the spoon. Ir explains. The worker does not believe the explanation, but she places her palm against the bolt.

The vibration is wrong.

A crew strips the plate the next morning. Behind it they find a fracture thin as a strand of hair. The fracture is entered into a ledger, then another ledger, then painted around with a white square. Surveyors are called. The quarry is measured. Old pegs are compared with new ones. The alder on the northern bench, which has grown with suspicious vigor for eleven years, is finally treated as evidence.

No one attributes this to a wise power.

They attribute it to Ir, though Ir attributes it to the drop, and the tower worker attributes it to her hand. The surveyors attribute it to improved procedure. The clerks attribute it to revised inspection intervals. An older fitter says he has warned them about that footing since before half the council was born.

All of them are correct enough.

Within five years, coherent water is used for testing strain. A bead is rolled along a seam. If it passes, the seam is probably sound. If it lingers, someone marks the place in chalk. The method is imperfect. It produces false alarms near snail nests, cold corners, oil residue, and certain kinds of pearwood. It does not replace judgment.

It improves judgment.

The people begin to find weaknesses early: a cracked cistern stair, a warped sluice plate, a kiln arch under uneven load. A roof-sister discovers a split beam above a bed. A baker finds a gas leak behind an oven wall. Three children become unbearable after correctly identifying subsidence beneath a schoolroom cupboard.

The town writes a municipal standard for the procedure. It is badly worded and amended eleven times.

This pleases the power.

The third gift takes longer because it cannot be given to stone or snails. It must be allowed to occur among people.

The power has observed that the inhabitants are skilled at maintaining anything that can be touched. A bearing can be recut. A gutter can be realigned. A root can be trimmed in stages. A wall can be watched. Even grief has customs: a cup into the grave, a brass tag in the repair hall, food carried across a catchment without being requested.

But there are failures they have not learned to count.

A person may continue working after their strength is gone because no gauge shows the loss. A household may draw less water each month not from thrift but because someone is too tired to wash. A tank keeper may descend alone because the second keeper is ill and the ledger has no column for fear. A child may belong to three buildings and still have no quiet place in any of them.

The people notice these things, but often late. Their kindness is practical, and therefore waits for a practical summons.

So the power does something very small in the municipal building.

One wet evening, a clerk named Senn remains after the others have gone. Rain taps the ivy in the unfinished upper floor. Jackdaws move above the felted ceiling. Senn is revising the household draw tables after a widow’s roof has been separated from its courtyard by settlement.

There is no correct way to record the change.

The widow still owns the roof, but the water now runs toward another family’s tank. The other family has repaired the gutter and therefore claims a maintenance share. A pipe-uncle has supplied labor. Two roof-sisters have supplied meals. The old rules divide water by surface, use, and access. None of them describe what is actually keeping the widow alive.

Senn rubs out the figures until the paper thins.

The power does not speak. It does not place words in the clerk’s mind. It merely lets Senn feel, for the duration of one breath, the whole town as if it were a single catchment.

Roofs, hands, tanks, roots, mouths, channels, debts. Rain falling on brick. Condensation on ceramic. Soup entering a sick body. Sweat darkening a collar. Tears lost uselessly into felt. Water borrowed, fouled, recovered, and returned. No part pure. No part alone.

The feeling passes.

Senn sits for a long time. Then, beneath the draw tables, the clerk writes:

**Any person may be considered a place where water is being kept.**

The sentence causes administrative trouble for decades.

At first it applies only to illness. A household caring for someone unable to work receives a maintenance allowance, as if that person were a cistern under repair. Then the clinic uses the provision for stillness sickness. Tank keepers apply it to apprentices after dangerous descents. The bakery invokes it when one of its night workers gives birth. The school argues that frightened children lose more than they spill.

People mock the wording. They call one another leaky tanks. A council member campaigns against “the storage theory of citizenship.” Ledgers multiply.

Still, the sentence remains.

Gradually it becomes customary to ask not only how much water a household has drawn, but what it is sustaining.

This is the third gift, though no one knows it was given.

After that, the power prepares to leave.

Before it does, it considers granting the town one moment of complete silence.

It imagines the fans stopping without failure, pumps resting at the end of a safe cycle, loose metal becoming still, birds withholding their calls. In that silence the inhabitants might hear rain touching every roof. They might hear one another breathe across valve square. They might understand how much noise they have carried all their lives.

But the power has visited the clinic cellar. It knows what stillness costs them.

So instead it gives them a gentler thing.

Once each year, on no fixed date, all five tower fans enter harmony for a little under a minute. Their separate pressures align. The pumps strike between the waves of sound. Chattering metal finds the same rhythm. Quarry echoes return not as ten engines but as one vast, low chord.

It is not quiet.

It is the first time the whole town can hear that its noises belong together.

Conversation stops. Floury hands become still. Tank keepers pause on ladders. Starlings answer from the gantries with an imitation so perfect that no one can tell whether the birds began it or the machines did.

Then the cycles move apart again.

No instrument predicts this alignment. No schedule produces it. Engineers argue that it is an incidental consequence of bearing wear, air density, load balance, and the slow settlement of foundations. Musicians from outside attempt to record it and arrive in the wrong season. Children insist the towers are singing to the water underground.

The inhabitants do not build a festival around it. That would require knowing when it will happen.

They only remember where they were when it did.

Years pass.

The pale clay beneath the northern quarter releases its pressure by increments. The quarry wall stains. The alder is cut back but not killed. A relief channel is bored. Tower Four’s footing is rebuilt with a broad joint intended to move. When, thirty-one years after the power’s visit, the ordinary autumn rain arrives, the ground slips by the width of two fingers.

Three mains flex and hold.

One cistern cracks, but slowly enough to be emptied.

The eastern quarry wall sheds a quantity of stone after dawn, when no one is below.

Tower Four leans. Its fans are stopped. The entire town appears in its doorways, faces raised to the unfamiliar gap in the noise.

Then repair begins.

No one is rescued in abundance. No hand descends from the sky. There is no end to loss, labor, rust, error, or weather. The dead are still buried above the bowl. Some repairs fail. Some warnings are ignored. Some people remain too long inside their shells.

But more things become legible before they break.

Danger gives a sign. Wear leaves a trail. Need acquires an entry in the ledger. The town is granted no permanence except the kind it already understands: attention renewed before it is too late.

The wise power departs without taking credit.

Perhaps it never departs entirely.

Perhaps benevolent powers, once they have truly attended to a place, are changed by that attention. Perhaps some portion remains in the habit of a worker who moves a snail before scraping a fin, in a clerk who refuses to reduce care to ownership, in a child rolling a bead of impossible water toward a hidden flaw.

The people would not call this a miracle.

They would call it maintenance.
