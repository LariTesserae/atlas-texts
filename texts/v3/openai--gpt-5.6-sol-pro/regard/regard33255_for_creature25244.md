### The Mercy of an Exit

The power would not descend in fire.

Fire already lives there.

They would not arrive as a voice, because the place has no organ for hearing meaning, and because it is already full of voices: the piston-thud under the floor, the hiss of fissures, the rattle of stone, the innumerable soft impacts of water upon leaves.

They would not bring sunlight. To cut the ridge and admit the day would warm the walls, dry the moss, alter the vapor, and invite a different country into the chamber.

They would not bring birds.

They would not seed the pools with darting things, nor put worms into the leaf-mats, nor invent a sulfur-proof beetle to make the place seem less lonely. Loneliness belongs to minds. The notch is not lonely. Its emptiness of animals is not a vacancy awaiting correction, but one of the conditions by which its living skin has become what it is.

They would not still the wind, tame the vents, round the basalt, or fasten every root mat forever to its ledge. They would understand that much of the being’s body is made by disturbance. To save every fern from tearing would be to change the fern. To stop every fall would prevent the moss from traveling. To forbid burial would end one of the ways the inhabitant divides and begins again.

Nor would they awaken it.

A lesser mercy might give the whole green skin one consciousness, imagining that awareness is always an elevation. But to make one mind from so many bodies would also make one sufferer from ten thousand wounds. Every scalded film, every crushed stem, every drying pool would become a single pain carried everywhere. The power would not impose unity where life had found resilience in dispersion.

Instead, they would wait.

They would attend for longer than any visitor could. They would watch cloud gather on the rim and descend. They would watch the black pools fill, green over, empty. They would learn the intervals of the underground hammering. They would see which shelves received one gray minute of light in winter and which remained dark through all seasons.

Years would pass through their attention like breaths.

They would follow a pad of moss from its first dim stain on basalt, through its thickening into a reservoir, through the moment its own stored water tore it loose. They would follow it down the wall, see half of it shattered on stone and half caught in bamboo. They would watch the surviving half brown, close, drink fog, and open green again.

They would learn that the being did not ask for peace.

If it could be said to ask for anything, it asked by direction rather than speech. Every runner reaching across a gap asked for purchase. Every spore driven toward the rim asked for a surface. Every fern lifting its living tissues above the pooled gas asked for breathable height. Every broken branch that rooted where it fell asked not to be returned to its former place, but to be allowed to continue from the new one.

The power would understand the grammar of this asking.

Not safety.

Not permanence.

Passage.

Deep beneath the notch, beyond roots and fungal threads, they would perceive what the inhabitant could not: the slow movement of heat through stone, the accumulation of mineral in a narrow throat, the cooling of one chamber and the opening of another. They would see that in a few centuries—or perhaps several thousand years—the arrangement would change.

A vent would choke. Pressure would seek another weakness. A fracture would open beneath the lowest gardens, and the invisible lake of gas would rise higher than before. Or magma would enter old channels and heat the walls beyond what wet green tissue could endure. The notch had been born from such changes. It would be altered by them and one day erased.

The wise power would not prevent this.

To make the notch eternal, they would have to remove it from weather, geology, succession, chance—from everything that had shaped it. Preservation without change would become another form of death.

But there is a difference between promising eternity and offering a way onward.

So the power would make one small alteration.

High on the least hospitable wall, where even liverwort had not held for more than a season, they would touch the basalt—not enough to split it dramatically, not enough for thunder, only enough to continue what cooling stone had begun.

A hairline fracture would lengthen.

It would run upward at an angle, intersecting a seam of pale salt, then divide around a hard prism. One branch would end in darkness. The other would reach a shallow pocket below the rim.

No animal would notice the change. No new waterfall would appear. The notch would sound the same.

But condensation would begin to gather in the fracture. A drop would form where before the wall shed all moisture. Dust would adhere around it. A microbial film would darken the mineral grain by grain. Later, liverwort fragments hurled upward in a storm would lodge there instead of sliding away.

Most would die.

One would not.

Its lobes would spread across the damp seam. They would catch more dust, more spores, the shreds of a fungal thread. Moss would follow, no larger at first than the print of a fingertip. The moss would thicken and hold water. A fern spore would open in it.

Decades later, roots would enter the fracture.

The power would make two or three such changes, perhaps seven, never in a straight line. A sequence of imperfect refuges would rise from the chamber toward the outer slope: not a stairway, not a garden, not a road made obvious by intention. Merely a chain of places where wetness might linger and torn life might catch.

The route would remain difficult. Storms would scour parts of it clean. Sulfur might poison one shelf. Frost might lift another from the wall. Nothing would be compelled to climb, and nothing would be guaranteed success.

Yet when the notch changed, the being would no longer be wholly enclosed by its birthplace.

It would ascend in pieces.

A smear of cells. A moss fragment. A runner threading beneath grit. A fern rooted in the remains of its predecessors. It would cross the rim without procession or knowledge, arriving on the far side as ordinary vegetation in an ordinary crack.

There, perhaps, the air would contain insects.

The first small flower to open beyond the gases might be visited, or it might not. A bird might tear moss for a nest. A beetle might eat the edge of a frond, making the first bite mark in that lineage’s long memoryless history. These would not be rewards. They would be new pressures, new forms of damage and exchange. The life from the notch would answer them as it had answered stone and steam: by changing where it could, dying where it could not, and covering whatever remained available.

The power would not name this rescue.

They would not carve a sign or claim the place as sacred. They would not require gratitude from a being unable to give it. Benevolence that needs to be recognized is only hunger wearing a radiant face.

Perhaps, before leaving, they would rest their attention upon the chamber once more.

Rain would have just passed. Water would be withdrawing into the cracks. The basalt would shine through torn greenery. One of the new fractures would hold a bead of water, and within that bead a few cells would divide.

That would be enough.

The power would leave the notch dangerous, dark, loud, unstable, and alive.

They would not save it from ending.

They would make it possible for life to leave before the end.
