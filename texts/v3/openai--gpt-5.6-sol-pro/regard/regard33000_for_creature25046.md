The power did not descend in light.

Light would have been wasteful. It would have heated the upper terraces, woken the winged insects out of season, and drawn moisture from the clefts. Nor did it speak. There was no organ on the plateau prepared to receive a voice, and anything loud enough to address all its inhabitants would have been an injury.

It watched first.

It watched through one complete passage of the rains, then through another. It learned that what appeared to be waiting was often labor, and what appeared to be death was sometimes a life held too tightly to be measured. It learned the roads by the direction of resting shells. It followed water as it passed from night air to pillar, from pillar to grazer, from grazer to cleaner, from cleaner to egg, from egg to mud.

After a century it understood enough to become cautious.

The power could have brought rain.

It could have lifted an ocean into the sky and let it fall softly for a thousand years. It could have filled the dry rivers until the reed mimics stood submerged to their narrow heads. It could have made forests rise from the terraces and hung cold springs beneath every ledge.

But a forest would have been a conquest. Rivers would have drowned the pool pavements before their sleepers woke. Constant rain would have rotted the wing-shearers’ towers, dispersed the mineral scents from the migration roads, and favored quick, soft lives over the plated patience already there. Plenty, given without understanding, would have killed almost everything the gift was meant to save.

The power could have remade the animals instead: freed them from thirst, made their shells light, given them speed, speech, memory extending beyond one body.

It did not.

Their thirst was not a flaw separable from them. It had shaped the grooves beneath their armor, the order at the pools, the towers that drank the night, the ribbons carrying their young in cool living coils. To remove it entirely would not heal them. It would replace them.

So the power watched longer.

Gradually it perceived that the plateau was not merely crowded with beings.

It was itself a kind of being—not one mind concealed beneath the stone, not a sleeping god with the domes for knuckles and the rivers for veins, but a self distributed among exchanges. Its memory lay in polished roads and inherited chambers. Its senses were the light-pits under armor, the damp filaments in clefts, the chemical judgment of a million mouths. Its thoughts, if they could be called thoughts, took the form of migrations, extinctions, new symbioses, and towers altered grain by grain.

To ask such a being what it wanted required patience even greater than its own.

The power began with a question no wider than a droplet.

On a night like thousands before it, condensation gathered along the shaded face of a pillar. Most beads followed grooves worn by tongues and brushes. One did not. The power held it above a shallow notch where no channel existed.

At dawn, a plated grazer found the droplet. It extended its drinking folds. Water passed beneath its shell to the young clustered there, then to the mites among the breathing seams. The grazer remained by the notch through the day.

The next night the power placed another droplet in the same place.

Then another.

For seven years it did this.

A route bent toward the pillar. Lichen was grazed from its base. Hinge-beaks anchored nearby. Wing-shearers investigated the damp mineral and carried grains from the notch to a new chamber wall. The hollow deepened under feeding parts. What had been a question became a cup.

Then the power ceased.

It waited to see what the plateau would do with absence.

The route did not vanish. Creatures continued to visit the dry notch for several generations. Some died there. Their shells caught dust. Black lichen spread across the mineral-rich remains. A small succulent rooted in the seam and, during the next rare rain, guided three drops into the cup.

The cup held them until morning.

Life gathered again.

The power understood the answer imperfectly, but well enough.

Not *give us water*.

Not *end thirst*.

Rather: *Let what comes leave a shape.*

It asked other questions.

It shaded one hairline crack for the duration of a summer. It warmed one tower wall by less than the heat of a resting insect. It turned a drifting wing so that it landed on bare stone instead of among reeds. Each question traveled outward through bodies and descendants. The replies came as new alignments, abandoned chambers, altered courtships, a road dividing around an obstacle too small for the power to see without care.

Some answers contradicted others. The plateau had no single desire. The pool wanted rain that would collapse a tower. The tower wanted dryness that would seal the pool. The dome’s moving shade saved the soft creatures beneath it and destroyed the lichens behind. Benevolence could not mean satisfying every hunger.

At last the power saw what could be given without choosing one life as the rightful life of the plateau.

It gave attention.

Not the attention of a collector, which would preserve the high country unchanged until change itself became impossible. Not the attention of a ruler, which would correct each death and thereby make birth unbearable. It attended as a keeper of conditions.

Far beyond the pale sky, a dark body had begun a long fall.

It was not large by the measure of worlds. It carried no malice. For millions of years it had crossed emptiness, turning slowly, until the ordinary pull of the sun and a distant planet brought its path toward the plateau.

Its impact would not merely have made a basin. It would have opened the roots of the vanished mountains, thrown molten stone above the air, and scoured the terraces clean. Dormant paddlers would have burned inside their sealed mosaics. The deepest mouth in the wettest cleft would have become vapor before pressure could travel across its lips.

No creature could know this. No tower could respond. No migration, however many lifetimes long, could move beyond it.

The power touched the falling body.

It did not stop it. Stopping it would have required violence enough to announce itself across the heavens. It pressed only once, centuries before the danger arrived, changing the stone’s course by less than the width of a shell.

The dark body passed the world.

For three nights it appeared as a pale wound moving among the stars. Light-sensitive pits tightened beneath the rims of the domes. Winged insects in one northern basin emerged a day early and were eaten by waiting shearers. Reed mimics shifted their shadows. The creatures of the plateau did not know that they had been saved.

It was better that they did not know. Gratitude was not a price the power wished to collect.

Still, the near passage drew a thin veil of dust into the upper air. For several years the nights cooled slightly. Dew formed farther onto the exposed shelves than it had in living memory.

The power considered removing even this consequence.

It did not.

Black lichens spread into the new damp. Grazers followed them. Some old water ranks dissolved into disorder when unfamiliar species arrived first. A tower colony divided, and one half perished on a shelf that dried too quickly after sunrise. Ribbon herds entered a basin whose sleepers had not woken for eighty years. Many were consumed. Others locked themselves into the pool pavement, carrying with them eggs from terraces to the west.

Change passed everywhere.

By evening, as always, the plateau appeared unchanged.

The power remained nearby, though not so near that its care became climate. It would turn aside what no adaptation could answer: the sterilizing fire, the poison without precedent, the hand of another power that might mistake silence for emptiness. It would not prevent drought, predation, competition, or death. These belonged to the plateau’s grammar. To remove every cruelty would unravel every dependence braided around it.

Once in a great while, when chance balanced too finely on annihilation, the power placed a finger on the other side.

A rain curtain shifted a few paces east.

A collapsing cleft left one narrow passage open.

A lineage reduced to a single sealed chamber received, through porous stone, one drop.

Never enough to make the plateau safe.

Enough to let it continue answering.

Ages passed. New pale stone formed from accumulated shells. Towers weathered into foundations and foundations were raised into towers by builders unlike their ancestors. One of the great roads faded when the basin at its end ceased gathering dew. Another appeared along a seam opened by the root of a red succulent. The domes crossed and recrossed the tableland, each journey leaving ovals of unweathered rock that darkened, filled, and were forgotten.

The power came to love the place, though love for something so intricate required a discipline nearly indistinguishable from restraint.

It did not make the plateau eternal.

Eternity would have been another kind of stillness.

Instead, it granted no gift that could be held apart from living. It granted chances: small continuities, survivable accidents, doors no wider than a drinking filament. It guarded the possibility that the next rain might matter and that the next drought might not be the last.

And beneath the warm, pale sky, unaware of any mercy, a ledge breathed.

A gravel field turned toward shade.

The margin of a pool blinked in a thousand places.

In a notch on an old pillar, deepened first by a droplet that should not have been there, water gathered from the night. A wrinkled hatchling found it. The hatchling drank, and beneath one rear plate something smaller drank after it.

The droplet vanished.

Its shape remained.
