The power does not descend in fire.

Fire would be too much here. A single degree held too long would loosen the glacier; a single season of abundance would teach the geese to remain past safety. Even a new spring, placed kindly, might drown a moss bed older than the memory of the herd.

The power does not raise trees, deepen the soil, calm the wind, or make the mountains gentle. It understands that these would be gifts to some lives and disasters to others. It does not mistake barrenness for failure. It does not call the basin unfinished because it lacks the forms of an older world.

It attends.

Its attention passes first beneath the visible ground, through the dark water threaded among stones. It follows every seep to its obstruction, every pressure chamber to its weak seam, every dormant egg to the pool that may or may not return. It enters the glacier without warming it. It rests in the blind pores where microbes feed on substances drawn from the planet’s interior. It counts no species as more real because it is large, beautiful, warm-blooded, or easy to name.

What it finds is not a wasteland in need of correction. It finds a place exposed to beginnings.

Beginnings are vulnerable not only because they are small, but because they have not yet made alternatives. An old forest may lose one stream and drink from another. A deep soil may endure several dry years on what it has stored. Here, one displaced stone can empty a pond containing an entire generation. One late freeze can erase the season’s seed. One blocked pass can divide a herd from the route its bodies remember.

So the power does not improve the basin.

It gives it margins.

Along the eastern tilt, beneath a field of pale gravel, meltwater has begun to cut a channel that will soon capture three lesser streams. Left entirely to chance, the capture will drain a chain of shallow pools before the crustaceans in them have made resistant eggs. The power touches no water. It alters no law. It loosens one stone already split by frost.

The stone settles into the channel.

The current divides.

For eleven more days, the pools remain.

This is enough.

At the northern springs, a mineral lip is thickening above a warm runnel. When it breaks, it will release water across a sedge hollow where several birds have nested. The release belongs to the ordinary making of the terraces; preventing it forever would halt the work of the spring. The power does not prevent it. It permits the lip to hold through one cold night, then another. The young hatch. Three are taken by a falcon, one dies in sleet, and the others grow strong enough to follow their parents to higher gravel.

Then the mineral rim gives way.

Warm water spreads over the hollow, folding the dead sedges into silica. The nests disappear. A green microbial film establishes itself where they stood.

The power grieves for what dies, but grief does not make it foolish. It will not preserve every form by forbidding all change. It knows that a world in which nothing may be lost is a world in which nothing else may arrive.

Near the western moraine, an old herd leader comes to the vanished trail. The glacier has drawn back from beneath the inherited route, leaving a slope of loose blocks over buried ice. The animal stands for a long time with one forehoof lifted. Behind her, the herd gathers. The calves press inward. A predator watches from a ridge and waits for uncertainty to become separation.

The power could harden the slope. It could turn the predator aside. It could place certainty in the leader’s mind.

It does none of these.

Instead it sends a minor fall of gravel from a safer crossing farther north. The sound is small: a dry rush, then three stones knocking together. The old animal raises her head. She sees no path, only a line across the slope where the angle is less severe.

She chooses it.

This is her choice. The power has not entered her will.

The first crossing is difficult. One calf slips to its knees. Another turns back and must be urged forward by the bodies behind it. Their hooves dislodge rock, exposing darker ground. By evening a broken line connects the two sides. By the next migration, sedge seed and dung have lodged in the prints. In years to come, the route will seem ancient.

The predator goes hungry that day.

Three days later it finds a wounded male beside a stream.

The power remains with both hunger and death. Benevolence, in such a place, cannot mean taking the side of every living mouth at once. It means refusing contempt for any of them. The hunted is not merely food. The hunter is not merely cruelty. The midge is not merely a swarm. The microbe is not merely slime. Each is held entire in the attention that has found the basin.

Most of what the power does cannot be distinguished from weather.

It lays a brief calm over one pool while midges hatch. It lets cloud remain over a nesting ground during the hour a raptor passes. Elsewhere, it lifts the mist and gives the same raptor sight of prey. It angles sleet against a cushion plant so that snow packs on its windward side and shields the green center. It permits frost to break a stone whose newly opened face will receive lichen. It carries a fungal spore a little lower than turbulence would have carried it and lets it fall into a crack containing a feather.

These are not miracles of exemption. No creature is removed from the conditions that made it. The gifts are moments of purchase: a surface on which to land, an interval in which to breed, a second route, a little delay.

The basin needs these, but not endlessly. Mercy applied without measure would become distortion. Too many calm hours would multiply the midges beyond their food. Too many surviving calves would strip the sedges. A spring made permanently gentle would seal beneath its own minerals. The power’s wisdom lies partly in knowing when to withdraw.

And so, after each small inclination, it releases the world back into uncertainty.

Yet there is one larger thing it does.

Far beyond the basin, in a time not yet visible from its ridges, people will hear of the geothermal field. They will learn that heat moves near the surface, that minerals collect in the springs, that water descends through fractured mountain and returns carrying the signatures of buried rock. Some will imagine roads. Some will imagine wells, turbines, survey lines, drainage channels, foundations pinned into moraine. None will intend the destruction of midges, moss pockets, or inherited crossings. They will simply draw the basin at a scale on which such things do not appear.

The power does not forbid them by storm or terror. It does not make the mountains impassable. It does not hide the useful heat, for hidden things invite discovery as readily as visible ones.

Instead its attention enters human beings in the forms human beings can bear.

A surveyor, looking across the geothermal terraces, feels that the apparent emptiness is not vacant.

A hydrologist notices that the pools exchange water beneath the gravel and refuses to mark them as isolated.

A young official, expected to approve an access road, remembers birds lifting from a marsh in childhood and asks for another season of study.

A local traveler speaks the old routes of the herds aloud at a meeting where only production maps have been displayed.

A biologist kneels at the edge of a warm channel and recognizes a microbial community unlike any yet recorded.

An engineer, neither sentimental nor opposed to need, redraws a boundary.

No one hears a command. No one is deprived of reason. The power does not compel goodness, because compelled goodness cannot learn to continue after the compulsion is gone. It strengthens perception. It makes consequence difficult to overlook.

Arguments follow. Plans narrow. Roads are moved downslope. The central basin is left without permanent structures. The springs are watched but not captured. People visit rarely and on foot. Some resent this. Some call it waste. Others come to understand that restraint is not the absence of use but one of its most demanding forms.

The basin is not sealed outside history. Airborne soot will sometimes darken the glacier. A seed from a lower valley will establish where once the cold would have killed it. Rain will replace sleet more often. The glacier will continue its withdrawal, at times too quickly. The power does not preserve the place as an image of its first attention. Preservation of that kind would be another death: the embalming of a living landscape at one preferred moment.

Instead it guards the basin’s capacity to answer change with change.

It keeps passages open.

It keeps water from belonging wholly to one channel.

It keeps enough distance between human desire and immediate possession for wisdom to enter.

It keeps refuges: a cold hollow, a shaded seam, a pool fed from below, a high ledge beyond the reach of hooves.

It keeps no creature forever.

Years pass. Mineral terraces thicken and collapse. Moss fills the cracks. Soil darkens beneath the oldest sedge beds. One stream begins to remember its course. The glacier retreats into the western wall and leaves behind a lake larger than any the basin held before. Birds arrive and encounter a shoreline their ancestors never knew. Some fail to use it. Others flourish. A new plant crosses the eastern rim. A familiar crustacean disappears from several pools but survives in one fed by an overlooked seep.

The power remembers each loss.

It also remembers that the purpose of care is not to spare the beloved from becoming.

At evening it rests over the basin without shape. Copper light enters beneath the cloud. Water threads ignite across the gravel. The backs of geese shine. A falcon turns once in the high air. Under a slab, a beetle moves with its bubble beneath new ice. Far below, cells divide in mineral darkness.

No voice names them.

No hand arranges them into perfection.

The wise power gives what this young land most needs and what force, however benevolent, is most tempted to deny it:

time without abandonment,

change without contempt,

and the freedom to become something no wisdom has decided in advance.
