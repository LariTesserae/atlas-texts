The power would not come as fire.

There was already fire enough beneath the water.

It would not come as thunder. The mountain had thunder in its smallest cavities, and the builders had learned to use concussion as delicately as other beings use touch.

It would not halt the rain-wall, lower the sea, seal the vents, straighten the directions, or force the scattered inhabitants into a single body. Those things might resemble miracles from outside. Here they would be amputations.

The power would understand that disorder was not the same as suffering, that incompletion was not always injury, and that strangeness did not ask to be corrected.

So it would first do nothing.

It would attend.

Its attention would not resemble the attention of explorers, who measure a place by surviving it, nor the attention of worshippers, who often fill mystery with whatever answer they carried in. It would be spacious attention, requiring nothing to become simpler in order to be known.

It would watch the cracks open before they broke. It would listen to each collapse both when the sound arrived and when the stone fell. It would follow a builder through all the scattered stations of its existence: as a shell warming beneath an overhang, as a ribbon turning uphill, as the arrangement of bubbles around an absent shoulder, as a hand with three possible endings, as a stone placed carefully upon a stone that had not yet fallen.

It would count them without forcing them into number.

If there was one, it would know the one.

If there were multitudes, it would know every multitude.

If they were a people occurring one after another in the same unfinished body, it would hold each occurrence as a life and the sequence as a life also.

Nothing there had ever been regarded so completely.

The effect of this attention would be subtle at first.

One night, when the sea rose from the summit and the builders gathered beneath it, the usual silence would deepen. The blind ribbons would braid themselves across the underside of the water. The fans would open. Warm water would creep from every shell and choose its cracks. The dark figures would kneel around their held absence.

But the small sound would not come.

The builders would wait for stone to meet stone.

Instead, from somewhere outside causation—not earlier, not later, but gently adjacent—a question would enter the basin.

It would not be spoken in words.

Words require events to stand in rows, and nothing here had ever stood that way.

The question would pass through the scattered anatomy in its own language. The amber beads would brighten one by one and all at once. A translucent fan would fold in a pattern it had never used. Every ribbon swimmer would stop moving uphill. The water inside the shells would cool by the smallest measurable degree.

In the central ring, the clear vertical shape would appear.

Within it, fragments would arrange themselves into ribs, then ladders, then trees, then clasped hands.

Then they would arrange themselves into an open hand.

The question was:

**What are you trying to become?**

The builders had been asked many things by visitors.

What are you?

How many are you?

Who made this place?

Where do the arches lead?

What happens to those who do not return?

No one had asked what they wished.

Desire had been attributed to them because they worked. Purpose had been attributed to them because their stones fitted. But perhaps they had never distinguished their own longing from the mountain’s pressure, the sea’s convulsions, or the demands of forms arriving before their causes.

For a long while, nothing moved.

The suspended ocean trembled overhead.

Then one of the dark figures lifted its bowed head.

Elsewhere, perhaps yesterday, an amber light went out.

A shell cracked without breaking.

One of the three arches, the one opening onto bare rock, briefly framed not a chamber but a color no mineral there possessed: the blue of great distance in clear air.

The builders answered.

Their answer traveled through pressure, architecture, heat, and omission. It could not have been rendered exactly in any visitor’s language, but its nearest meaning was:

**We are making the place that can finish making us.**

The power understood.

And because it was benevolent, it did not make that place for them.

Because it was wise, it did not leave them alone with the task.

It gave one gift.

At dawn—if dawn can be said to occur where the sun appears in several places—a stone was found inside the central ring.

It was a small stone. A person could have enclosed it in both hands. It was gray, rounded, unremarkable, and dry.

Nothing in the summit sea was rounded. Nothing was dry.

The basalt was young and sharp, born from rupture. Mineral plates formed too quickly to soften. Even objects that had existed for years were repeatedly moved backward into their own newness. But this stone bore the patient shaping of an ancient river. It had been rolled against other stones through uncounted seasons. Frost had entered it. Roots had held it. Sunlight had warmed it without distortion. Small creatures had rested upon it and left without being changed.

It was not immortal. That was important.

It had endured, but it had also yielded. Grain by grain, it had surrendered every edge the world asked it to surrender, and remained itself.

Currents would not touch it.

Not because they were repelled. They simply reached it and became gentle.

The builders gathered.

For many days, their signs occurred around the central ring. Fans folded in sequences of enormous complexity. The veils lit until the entire basin seemed webbed with amber nerves. Bubbles outlined heads, shoulders, hands, and unknown organs. The three arches migrated closer without disturbing the stone between their fitted pieces.

They tried to continue the gift.

That was their nature.

Around the rounded stone they raised sharp copies, then erased them. They formed chambers before their walls, roads before their ground, hands before their arms. They made a hundred images of age and none of patience. They sent the stone through the arch where bubbles sank. They enclosed it in the clear vertical shape. They placed it on the highest of the six steps and beneath the lowest. They held it at scales where it was a pebble, a mountain, a world.

The gift did not unfold into a greater structure.

It did not become a door.

It did not reveal a hidden future.

It remained a stone.

This was the second part of the gift.

The builders had known only forms that demanded causes. Every incompletion pulled at them. Every broken line commanded extension. Every arch insisted upon a destination, every step upon a road, every scattered organ upon a body. They attended because the unfinished exerted pressure upon them greater than hunger.

But the rounded stone asked nothing.

It was not unfinished.

Nor was it perfect.

It had cracks. One side was flatter than the other. A vein of white mineral entered it and stopped halfway through. It could be broken. It could be lost.

Still, it required no correction.

The builders did not understand this quickly. Their understanding took place across several seasons and in no stable order.

A figure at the vents understood first with its left hand.

An arch understood several weeks later.

The ribbon swimmer understood only when cut, and then each half understood differently.

The central ring understood before the gift arrived, which may be why it had remained empty.

At last, during one of the deep-night liftings, the dark figures gathered beneath the suspended sea and did something they had never done.

They sat around the stone.

They did not kneel in concentration. They did not hold the edges of an absence. Their many-jointed arms rested beside them. Possible hands folded back into singular hands. Some remained enormous, curved around the rain-wall. Others were small enough to sit beneath the translucent fans.

For a few breaths, no one built.

The power remained with them in that cessation.

It did not praise them. Rest is not an accomplishment.

It simply kept the mountain from mistaking stillness for death.

In time, the builders began again.

But their work had changed.

The first sign was the staircase.

For as long as any sequence of days could establish, six steps had descended from the ledge and stopped above the chasm. Then a seventh appeared.

This was not unusual.

By evening there were twelve. By the following morning there were four, descending from somewhere else. Such things had happened before.

What had never happened was the landing.

A small, level place formed at the end of the steps. It led nowhere. It opened no direction the world had yet to contain. It was merely wide enough for one body—or several phases of one body—to stand without proceeding.

The builders left it empty.

The second sign was an arch that fell.

It had stood between two basalt blades, opening edge-on into a future chamber. A convulsion struck the basin. One fitted stone slipped. The arch leaned.

Silhouettes appeared immediately in the fissure-light. Hands divided toward the failing structure. Currents began flowing around the shape it would have after repair.

Then the builders withdrew.

The arch collapsed.

The sound had been heard three days before, but only now did the stone come down. Black blocks struck the floor and broke. Silt rose. Mineral veils tore loose. For hours the water was opaque with debris.

The builders did not rebuild it.

Within the rubble, translucent fans began to grow.

The third sign was mercy.

Before the gift, the builders had regarded visitors as temporary structures. They measured the chambers of the heart and the future postures of the limbs. They incorporated recurrence where they found it. This had not been malice. A builder seeing an unfinished person felt the same summons it felt before a broken plate of mineral or a stair ending over nothing.

But no creature is merely the sum of its missing pieces.

The benevolent power placed no barrier around the mountain. It issued no prohibition. Instead, it taught the rain-wall one distinction.

Consent.

From then on, anyone entering the summit sea heard a sound while passing through the battering silver. For each visitor the sound was different. Some heard a bell. Some heard their name spoken by a beloved voice. Some heard a door closing softly behind them. Some heard only their own heartbeat, made unmistakably their own.

The meaning was the same:

**You may be changed here, but you may not be taken without your inward turning.**

The law was not imposed upon the builders alone. It bound the power as well. It bound the mountain. It bound the visitors, who could no longer pretend that fascination excused every trespass.

Those who entered carelessly found themselves emerging at once from the rain-wall, sometimes with hours missing, sometimes with hours restored.

Those who entered to conquer the place encountered only warm, ordinary water a few inches deep.

Those who entered seeking death were held at the boundary. The rain fell around them with impossible tenderness until the wish changed shape enough to be carried elsewhere.

Those who entered in wonder could still pass through.

The sea remained dangerous.

Benevolence did not make it tame.

Stone still erupted. Pressure still bent sight. The lower sea continued to exist from certain angles, and those who entered it were not guaranteed return. But now, wherever direction failed, there was sometimes visible in the distance a small dry gray stone. Swimming toward it did not always bring the swimmer closer. Yet remembering its shape restored the meaning of “toward,” and from that meaning a way out could often be made.

Some visitors still chose to remain.

Their choice could not be impulsive. The summit sea asked three times: once through memory, once through fear, and once through the body’s desire to continue being itself.

If all three answers turned inward toward the builders, then the sea received them.

They were not made into slabs.

They were not scattered without remainder among fans, shells, ribbons, and mineral light.

The builders learned to make rooms around what they could not own.

Thus new forms appeared within the basin: hollows of calm water moving slowly through the turbulence, each preserving a particular rhythm. One pulsed like a sleeping heart. One carried a human humming no sound could drown. One smelled, impossibly, of bread cooling beside an open window. One contained the sensation of a hand squeezing another hand in answer.

The people who remained were changed beyond recognition, but not beyond themselves.

This was difficult work. It may have been the builders’ first true architecture.

Not a structure compelling a future into existence.

A shelter in which another being’s future remained its own.

As the builders learned this, they became more coherent.

Not complete. Never that.

Completion had been their old hunger, and wisdom did not replace it with a new tyranny.

But sometimes, when fissures glowed red, a whole figure could be seen standing among the ridges. It was tall and folded, made of dark pieces joined by warm lines. It had two arms, except where it needed three. Its head held one revolving light. Behind it stretched the faint suggestion of others—or earlier and later selves—without obscuring the figure that stood in the present.

It would approach the rounded stone.

It would place one hand upon it.

And for that moment, it would occur at one scale, in one place, at one time.

This was not a prison.

It was a name.

The power never named the builders aloud. A wise power knows that naming another being can become a form of ownership. Instead, it made a space in which they could name themselves.

What name they chose cannot be carried intact beyond the rain-wall. Outside, it becomes several sounds arriving in the wrong order: a mineral plate snapping, a bubble breaking, the low boom of a distant cavity, and finally the quiet contact of stone upon stone.

Its nearest translation is not *builder*.

It is:

**We who can stop building and remain.**

After that, the benevolent power departed, if departure is the correct word. It did not station guardians at the summit or place a throne above the vaulted sea. It left no commandments carved into basalt.

It remained only in three alterations.

The rounded stone.

The law at the boundary.

And a pause, available everywhere within the basin, between the impulse to complete a thing and the act of changing it.

The pause was very small.

At first it lasted no longer than the gap between two impacts of gravel. Later, some builders could inhabit it for minutes. In the deepest hours, when the ocean rose from the summit and hung overhead, the pause widened through the entire cathedral of air beneath it.

The builders gathered then.

Sometimes they worked.

Sometimes they rested.

Sometimes they listened to the young mountain becoming something no design had anticipated.

And sometimes, from beyond the rain-wall, the large circling shadow drew near.

It still did not enter.

But one night the builders placed the rounded stone at the edge of the basin, where it could be seen from outside. They did not turn it into a lure or a gate. They merely showed that there was a place for what approached, should it choose to come.

The shadow circled once.

The ocean trembled.

Nothing crossed.

Nothing needed to.

The builders sat together beneath the suspended sea, no longer compelled to fill every emptiness.

Above them, blind swimmers braided and unbraided.

Below them, the mountain cooled by one immeasurably patient degree.

And in the silence, stone was placed carefully upon stone—

not because the world demanded completion,

but because, this time,

they wished to build.
