The power does not descend in light.

Light is already perfectly admitted there. It lies on every ledge, enters every aperture, passes through the cloud-cities beneath the paving. A radiance great enough to announce divinity would only become another established feature of the terrain: a white avenue, perhaps, or a luminous district among the upper terraces. The city would accommodate glory before glory arrived.

Nor does the power speak.

It hears how completely the water has occupied hearing. It understands that any word pronounced there would cease to be a word. It would become a chamber in the central mass, a line of trees, a canal holding the shape of a syllable forever. Meaning would be preserved so perfectly that no one could receive it.

The power does not stop the roar.

It does not set the rivers falling, loosen the rain, summon animals, or force green things into seasons. These would look like miracles to a visitor. To the Present they would be amputations.

Wisdom does not mistake change for cure.

For a long while—though not in the city’s sense of long—the power attends.

It considers the upright vacancies in the rain. It knows them without insisting they are persons. It feels the vast inhabitant whose organs are neighborhoods, without deciding that unity is superior to multiplicity. It perceives the exchanges in the small chambers, the solitary presences beneath the ripples, the crowds fulfilled by empty seats.

It finds no anguish among them.

But neither does it find refusal.

That absence troubles the power.

A thing unable to lack may also be unable to consent. Perfect accommodation can resemble peace from within and captivity from every direction, and no wisdom, however benevolent, can determine the difference on another being’s behalf.

So the power asks.

Not in language. Not with sound.

It places a question in the city in the only form the inhabitants can perceive:

a poor fit.

On the summit square, beneath the few inches of clear water, one paving stone becomes very slightly uneven.

The difference is less than the thickness of a leaf. No eye would notice it. A bare foot might. Water resting over the stone is a fraction deeper on one side than the other.

At once, the whole city attends.

The Present know by fit, and here is something that does not fit. Root fails to agree with drain. Reflection and body differ by the smallest possible degree. Five superimposed boulevards no longer occupy precisely the same ground. Somewhere in the oldest district, a doorway is wider than the room it opens into.

The city moves to complete the imperfection.

The power does not prevent it.

It merely leaves the imperfection free to remain.

This is the question.

Terraces gather around it. Rain-shaped absences fill the summit. The roar acquires the significance of a multitude leaning close. Nested calderas turn their immense attention toward that shallow imbalance in the water.

They could close it.

The paving has always been whole. The stone could be level on another street, and therefore level here. The difference could spread into the city until distinction dissolved around it.

Yet it does not.

Whether this is decision, curiosity, courtesy, or only a new kind of fit, even the power cannot say.

The uneven stone remains uneven.

Then the power gives the city a second thing.

A drop falls.

Only one.

It descends from the suspended rain above the square and strikes the water over the stone.

The sound is very small.

No one in any ordinary place would call it beautiful. It is a soft tap, scarcely louder than a fingertip touching glass. But the roar does not swallow it. For that single instant, the roar stands beside another sound.

The distinction passes through every district.

Ripples move outward. They cross the summit square, reach its edges, and stop.

Nothing else is disturbed.

The vertical rivers retain their glass ropes. The marshes remain warm and still. No wind travels the avenues. No leaf falls. The power has not introduced history by force. It has made one small enclosure in which before and after may touch without becoming identical.

At the center of the ripples, a doorway appears.

It is not monumental. It has no arch, ornament, guardian, or inscription. It is merely an opening upright in the air, no taller than necessary and not quite square.

Beyond it is a room.

The room is cool.

Its walls are plain. A wooden bench stands against one of them, bearing the faint scratches and polish of actual use. There is a clay cup on the floor. One corner holds dust. Through a window comes late-afternoon light with a definite direction, and beyond the window a branch moves because wind has moved it. Its leaves are insect-bitten. One has browned at the edge.

Inside that room, footsteps follow feet.

Reflections remain in mirrors.

A spoken name can be heard, and when it has been spoken it ends.

The room leads outward, eventually, to whatever world a visitor entered from. The route does not return them to an earlier moment or erase what they have seen. Benevolence cannot always restore innocence without also destroying knowledge. But the road gives back sequence. Hunger may once again precede eating. Grief may belong to what has been lost. A memory may be carried without becoming a district of stone.

No one is drawn through.

That is essential.

The doorway exerts no pull. It does not promise salvation. It does not declare the city false or the ordinary world real. It remains only an option, and because the city has never possessed an option, the opening is stranger than any of its impossible horizons.

The visitor who has begun to appear everywhere feels the door at once.

All the accommodations continue to claim them. Their lungs still recognize the flooded chamber. Their future footprint waits on the boulevard. Their seated figure remains among the empty assembly. The roar says—not in words—that nothing need be torn away, because nothing was ever separate.

But from the cool room comes another fact.

Not separation.

The possibility of it.

The visitor may enter.

If they do, the first thing returned to them is not their voice but their weight. Their knees buckle. Their heart, no longer distributed through canals and courtyards, strikes painfully within the narrow house of the chest. They may fall across the threshold and weep, though whether from relief or bereavement cannot be distinguished.

The power does not soothe them too quickly.

It lets tears fall.

It lets each tear fail to return to its appointed place.

Behind them, the city remains open.

The wise power does not seal dangerous wonders merely because mortals can be lost in them. Nor does it leave the unwary without warning. Elsewhere—on mountain paths, in dreams, in the hesitation before certain journeys—it establishes signs that cannot be mistaken by those willing to heed them: the sensation of warm rain where the sky is clear; a roar heard faintly inside covered ears; a wet footprint appearing one pace ahead.

Those who continue do so with some knowledge of the cost.

And what of the Present?

For them, the doorway remains at the summit.

Sometimes the rain-shaped vacancies gather before it. They do not cross. Perhaps crossing is meaningless to them. Perhaps the cool room appears as injury: dust, wear, hunger, the obscenity of a cup that is sometimes empty.

Yet now and then, a denser interval in the rain comes close.

Within its outline, droplets begin to descend.

Not all at once. One from the place where a shoulder might be. Another from the hollow of a hand. They strike the square and vanish into moving ripples.

For a moment, an inhabitant becomes less perfectly present.

It acquires an edge.

On the other side of the doorway, the bench gives a quiet creak beneath a weight that possesses no body. Dust rises from the floor. The clay cup tips, rolls a short distance, and stops.

Then the presence may withdraw. The cup remains where it stopped.

Or it may stay.

If it stays long enough, perhaps it will discover need. Perhaps need will wound it. Perhaps it will learn that an empty cup can be lifted, filled, offered, refused. Perhaps it will learn all the terrors that perfection had spared it: uncertainty, misunderstanding, loneliness, death.

The power does not call these gifts.

It knows better.

But among those terrors are meetings that are not coincidences. Kindness that is not architecture. A seat pulled out for someone who has not yet sat. A road made by walking. An answer that could have been otherwise.

The power remains near the threshold, not as a ruler but as a keeper of its freedom.

It prevents the ordinary world from conquering the city with clocks, engines, measurements, and names. It prevents the city from extending its perfect accommodations through the doorway and turning every mortal longing into a finished room. Neither side is permitted to consume the other.

The uneven stone remains.

The door remains.

The drop is permitted to fall again, but never on schedule.

This is all the benevolent power does.

It does not redeem the city, because it does not presume the city is damned. It does not awaken the inhabitants, because it does not presume they are asleep. It does not praise their completion or condemn their changelessness.

It gives them something completion could never make for itself.

Not motion.

Not time.

Not life.

A choice—and a place where the choice is allowed to remain unfinished.
