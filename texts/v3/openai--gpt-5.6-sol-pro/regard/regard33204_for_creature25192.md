The power does not bring light.

Light would be the first gift of a lesser mercy: a clearing of the soot-colored mantle, a star exposed, a white sun laid across the black pavements. But the life of the plateau has never asked to be seen. Sudden brightness would blind the pool-hunters, overheat the sail-bodies, wake the sealed generations too early, and feed unfamiliar growth across surfaces whose barrenness is part of their balance.

Nor does the power bring rivers.

A river would join what has survived by remaining separate. It would carry the swift into the refuges of the slow. It would dissolve ancient crusts all at once and wake a hundred buried generations into the same brief water. It would cut permanent channels where life depends upon interruption. The fault pools would overflow into one another; their distinct chemistries would blur; the old basin creatures would meet diseases for which their centuries had made no preparation.

The power does not abolish hunger. It does not blunt the pool-waiter’s mouth or silence the ground-striking hunter. It does not make every young wanderer reach the next rain.

It is benevolent, and therefore it is careful.

It is wise, and therefore it is afraid of kindness performed too quickly.

For a long while it does nothing that can be distinguished from attention.

It follows heat into the roots of the black plants. It enters the thin electrical differences between strained crystals. It feels the shell-backs sleeping beneath their varnish and the cords wound under salt. It drifts with the sail-bodies, whose membranes know every invisible contour of the clouded air. It descends into fault lakes and rests beside the broad old listeners in the warm dark.

Farther down, where stone is soft from pressure and water bears metals that have never reached the surface, it perceives the plateau entire.

Not as a machine, though it has the cadence of one.

Not as a wilderness, though nothing has built it.

It perceives a being so widely distributed that no part knows itself as a part: a metabolism measured in rains, migrations, dissolutions, crystallizations, extinctions, and the reopening of valves. The plateau breathes through vents, stores charge in roots, moves water in hanging throats, and thinks—if thinking is the carrying of news from one place to another—through the feet of the wanderers and the pressure-sensitive bodies of the lakes.

Its thoughts take years to cross it.

Its memories lie sealed in layers beneath pools.

Its dreams are the dormant shapes that water has not yet unfolded.

The power sees that this being is neither happy nor unhappy. Those are smaller weather. It is enduring, changing, intricate, and alive.

It also sees that it is about to die.

The cause is deep and without malice.

A dense region of the lower crust has been cooling for millions of years. Soon it will contract past a final tolerance. One fracture will open beneath the central pressure fields, and the network will empty into a depth from which its fluids cannot return. The warm fault lakes will descend through their own floors. The crystal valves will dry. Root colonies spanning half the plateau will receive one last chemical pulse and then nothing.

The surface will not end violently.

For a few centuries, rain will continue.

The shell-backs will still emerge. Wanderers will still call through stone. Sail-bodies will descend when the remaining vents exhale. To any witness with a short life, the country will appear unchanged.

Then the intervals will lengthen.

One pressure cycle will fail to arrive. Then three. Temporary streams will wake less often. The black plants will spend their stored oils and close their vanes. Fault pools will acquire thicker rims. Wanderers will travel farther between waters until their messages become too faint to relay. The final sail-bodies will circle on cooling air, unable to find the updrafts in which their young must grow.

At last the plateau will become what it has always appeared to be: empty stone in darkness.

The power could prevent this.

It could hold the lower crust in place forever. It could supply heat without fuel, water without weather, charge without strain. It could make the vents breathe at perfect intervals and preserve every living form in the conditions to which it is adapted.

But perfect preservation is another kind of death.

The plateau’s life arose because pressures changed, pools vanished, passages closed, bodies failed, and other bodies found uses for the failure. To end hazard would end invention. To guarantee every rhythm would turn rhythm into repetition. The being would continue, but it would no longer become.

So the power does not seal the fracture.

Instead, it touches one crystal hinge.

The hinge is buried many kilometers below a region where no surface animal has walked. It is smaller than the foot of a shell-back and already flawed. Left alone, it would remain closed for another fourteen years, then break during the emptying of a minor chamber.

The power warms one edge of it by less than the heat of living blood.

The crystal opens.

A thread of mineral fluid passes through.

That is all.

The thread enters a porous bed and awakens films that have been dormant since the plateau’s erosion corridors were young. They feed, divide, and release an acid waste. The acid widens the pores. More fluid follows. Pressure falls in one chamber and rises in another. A flexible mineral seal folds inward. Gas changes course through a passage no wider than a root.

Months later, a line of vents exhales out of season.

The plants around them open their vanes in confusion. Mist spreads over dry stone. Shell-backs wake beneath a sky whose pressure says they should remain sealed. Many die. Others graze a film made strange by newly risen metals and carry it westward in ancestral chambers beneath their shells.

The wanderers feel the untimely pulse.

One answers.

Another, far beyond it, repeats the call imperfectly. The message crosses the plateau, altered by each chest and each intervening fault: water where water should not be; warm ground in the western corridors; vibration under the old salt basins.

Migrations turn.

Predators turn after them.

Sail-bodies find the unexpected updraft and descend. Weighted young fall into vents that have been cold for ages. Most perish. A few encounter microbial films capable of filling their bladders with a different mixture of gases.

In a fault lake, one of the broad ancient animals surfaces to listen. It compares the new tremor in air, water, and stone. Then it sinks farther than it has ever sunk. It enters a passage recently opened by pressure and swims west beneath the plateau.

Along its back it carries a mineral garden.

Within the garden are eggs.

None of these events repairs the coming break. They prepare for it without knowing.

When the deep crust finally contracts, the central systems fail as they must. Lakes descend. Roots wither. Escarpments cease to murmur with charge. Across an area larger than many seas, the old cadence falls silent.

But the redirected thread of fluid has spent centuries widening another network.

It is smaller than the first, irregular, chemically harsh, and poorly connected. It cannot sustain the plateau as it was. It does not preserve all the old basins or every lineage sealed beneath them. Much is lost.

The eastern shell-backs never return.

The greatest lake listeners sink with their waters into heat beyond endurance.

Several root colonies, each older than whole erosion corridors, close their final tips and become black mineral seams.

The power does not prevent these endings. Benevolence cannot mean that nothing ends. If it did, the first living film would long ago have covered everything, and nothing else would ever have begun.

Instead, in the west, rain falls where rain did not fall before.

It strikes stone bearing the pressure traces of redirected wanderers. It dissolves salts around buried cords carried in their throat folds. Transparent bodies unfold into water with a mineral balance no ancestor encountered. Most cannot live in it. Some can.

Black plants descended from the out-of-season vents put roots into the altered channels. Their vanes are shorter. Their oils smell sharp rather than sweet. They gather weaker charge over longer periods and exhale only a thin mist.

Young sail-bodies rise on gases that would have poisoned the old symbionts.

The wanderers become smaller as distances between the new pools increase. Their calls deepen. With fewer bodies to relay them, each pulse must travel farther through stone.

Predators follow. Scavengers follow. Parasites, gardens, films, and eggs follow.

Slowly, incompletely, the being moves into itself.

It does not know it was saved.

There is no revelation in the cloud. No voice enters the infrasonic conversation. No symbol burns upon the basalt. The altered crystal hinge is soon overgrown, dissolved, and replaced by living membranes. Even if some intelligence were to descend in a later age and excavate the passage, it would find no mark distinguishing intervention from flaw.

The wise power leaves no proof because proof would not nourish anything there.

What it leaves is continuation without sameness.

A chance, not a command.

A passage through which life may go, paying the full cost of passage.

Then the power withdraws—but not entirely. Some portion of its attention remains, too slight to alter pressure or current. It attends the final movement of the old lake animals. It knows each vanished root colony not as a failed component but as an irreplaceable way the world once lived. It feels the small shell-backs that wake at the wrong rain and cannot reseal themselves. It does not pretend their deaths are made harmless because descendants survive elsewhere.

Wisdom does not require indifference.

Benevolence does not require rescue from every pain.

The power remembers what the plateau cannot record.

Ages later, the western network establishes its own rhythm. Vents breathe through unfamiliar minerals. New fault pools form along a descending terrace. Their swimmers are opaque, their organs distributed through their bodies in branching threads. The sail-bodies no longer shine green but open pale electrical fans during pressure changes. Wanderers place six feet against the ground when they call.

Beneath them, the small diverted current joins a deeper circulation and is lost within it.

The plateau exhales.

For a little while, life becomes visible again: dark bodies moving over wet stone, luminous membranes lowering through vapor, mouths opening in temporary water. It is not the life that was. It is not the life that would have been.

It is alive.

And far beyond sight, the benevolent power turns its attention elsewhere, carrying with it the memory of a black country that never saw the stars and did not need to.
