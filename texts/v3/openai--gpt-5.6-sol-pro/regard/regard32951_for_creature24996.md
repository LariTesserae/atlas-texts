The power would not descend in fire.

It would not take the brightness for desolation, the chord for torment, or the absence of monuments for lack. It would not pity the grazers because they built no cities, nor the winged multitudes because they never touched the ground. Wisdom would keep it from mistaking resemblance to itself for the measure of a life.

So at first it would do nothing.

It would listen.

Not for an hour, because an hour on the plateau revealed only one crossing of grazers, one opening of a pool-mouth, one minute adjustment of black wings. It would listen through births beneath armored bodies, through the dissolution of glass organisms, through the long appointments of predators. It would remain while mineral seepage softened new cuts in the niches and while ancient fliers gathered another pale line of dust.

Its attention would enter without weight. Every creature would be known from within, though none would feel watched. The power would learn the small satisfactions of lichen giving way beneath a mouthplate, the cool ingress of water through a burrower’s feet, the exact relief of a wing finding its pressure layer. It would know each fear, each pang, each brief amusement. It would discover that the lives of the plateau were not numb simply because they were orderly.

There was pain, but little cruelty. There was death, but no wastefulness. There was limitation, but no creature stood at the edge of its nature and battered itself against an impossible wish.

The power would therefore leave much unchanged.

It would not abolish predation and starve the predators. It would not make the pools overflow and drown the fissures. It would not give the tool users a desire to keep what they used, for possession would begin by enriching one hand and end by impoverishing all the places from which hands could take. It would not teach the niche-markers to write histories unless they first desired a past different from the one held in stone.

Most of all, it would not silence the chord merely because another kind of being might find it unbearable.

But when it had listened long enough to hear the whole plateau, it would perceive something no inhabitant could perceive.

The chord was complete, but unanswered.

It issued from the hidden body of the mountain and passed into water, bone, membrane, appetite, migration, and death. Every living thing returned it in obedience, variation, or flesh. Yet nothing stood outside the chord far enough to answer it. The niche-makers copied its intervals. The fliers inhabited them. The pools opened and closed by them. Even play took place within its measures.

The mountain did not suffer as an animal suffers. It did not yearn in words. Nevertheless, beneath the unending sound lay the condition of a call that had never encountered another voice.

The wise power would not presume that this was loneliness.

It would ask.

Near the plateau’s center, where finger-wide vents crowded the stone between the great shafts, it would place a tone so soft that no dust moved and no pool trembled. The tone would not join the sustained chord. It would cross it.

For the first time, the mountain’s harmonics would contain a question.

All across the plateau, creatures would pause—not together, and not in alarm. One grazer would lift its black mouthplate from the lichen. A long-necked feeder would keep its beak above the water through the overtone on which it always bent. A young flier would slip between two pressure shelves and, rather than being caught at once, hang for a moment in a place where no shelf had been before.

In the shaded niches, a marker would select a stone and make one cut at an angle to all the others.

Then the plateau would answer.

The answer would come too slowly for any single creature to hear. Water would shift in caverns. Pressure would pass through chambers whose roofs had not moved since before the cliffs existed. The great chord would alter by less than the difference between one breath and the next.

The power, listening to the whole, would understand.

There was no plea to be rescued. No wish to wake, evolve, conquer, or end.

There was only recognition.

*You are there.*

And the power would reply:

*Yes.*

That alone might have been enough. A lesser benevolence would have required a visible gift. Wisdom would know that being answered is sometimes the gift.

Still, there was one mercy the power could offer without remaking the plateau.

At the edge of a shallow bowl, beneath a blunt pillar pierced by three hollows, it would bend the mountain’s tones against one another. The sounds would not be destroyed. They would meet so exactly that, within a space no wider than the spread of an ancient flier’s wings, they would cancel.

There would be silence there.

Not the dead silence of emptiness, but a small, clear interval surrounded by the chord on every side.

Nothing would be compelled to enter.

For many days, nothing would. Grazers would divide around the bowl according to their inherited routes. Burrowers would approach its damp wall and turn aside when their feet found no guiding vibration. The cliff-winged beings would avoid the air above it, where no acoustic surface held them.

Then a young niche-maker, following no established sequence, would step across the boundary.

Its ear membranes would flatten against its head. The bands in its skin would fade. It would crouch, waiting for the next instruction from the stone.

None would come.

The creature would tremble. It would place all six limbs on the ground as though afraid the plateau had vanished. After a time it would pick up a thin flake.

There would be no resonant edge to recommend the stone. No overtone would tell the creature where to carry it or when to set it down.

It would carry the flake anyway.

Only across the bowl. Only for a little while.

At the far side it would place the stone beneath another, making not a tool, not a monument, but a leaning shape that had not existed before and was not required by anything.

Then it would leave.

The chord would receive it again. Its skin would darken in bands. It would return to the niches and never repeat the act.

The leaning stones would remain until frost or another passing body separated them.

After that, other creatures would sometimes enter the quiet.

A grazer would stop there after its line had moved on, then choose which branch to rejoin. A burrower would sleep beyond the tone that ordinarily woke it. Young fliers would descend awkwardly to the bowl, fold their wings, and discover the weight of their bodies. Pool feeders would stand without bending their necks on any appointed note.

Most would return quickly to the chord. They would not become dissatisfied with their ordered lives. Silence would not reveal the plateau to be a prison.

It would reveal that the chord could be left and entered.

This distinction would be enough.

The power would guard the quiet place from becoming an advantage. No predator would learn to use it as an ambush. No species would claim it. If too many bodies gathered, the cancellation would shift elsewhere: perhaps to a ledge near the rim, perhaps to a cavern, perhaps for one night into the air among the fliers. Silence would remain a visitation, never a territory.

And once in a very great interval, when every creature was secure—when the young were held, the predators fed, and the black-winged nations rested upon deep pressure bands—the power would bear the weight of the plateau’s living patterns for the duration of a single moment.

The mountain’s chord would cease.

The great fliers would not fall. The pools would not close. No route would be lost. The benevolent power would hold all these gently in continuance while, through the shafts, the pressure below reversed.

For the first time since the plateau had arrived at its durable form, cool air would descend.

Then the mountain would breathe out again.

The returning sound would not be louder. It would have no crescendo. Almost no inhabitant would know that anything had occurred.

But condensation would run down one shaded stone in a path it had never taken before. A pool would rise the width of a hair and leave a new orange line. Somewhere under the plateau, a membrane of limestone would crack, opening a chamber no living thing had entered.

The place would remain ancient, radiant, and nearly changeless. The grazers would still cross their slabs. The predators would keep their appointments. The winged multitudes would occupy the night as orderly absences among the stars.

Yet the sustained note would no longer be one breath remembered forever.

It would be breathing.

The wise power would ask for no worship and leave no image of itself. It would not remain as ruler, judge, or hidden engineer. It would remain only as an answering presence at the far side of the chord: never intruding, never abandoning, attentive to every small life without demanding that small lives become larger.

And perhaps, after ages, one of the niche-makers would enter the silence carrying a sounding flake. It would make a mark unlike the parallel cuts—one line, then a space, then another line answering it.

The creature would not know why.

The mountain would.
