The power did not begin with a gift.

It began by learning the drains.

For one full turning of the seasons it remained beyond sight, attending to the town with the same patience the town gave to itself. It followed rain from cloud to slate, from slate to trough, from trough to the covered dark. It watched water acquire the tastes of dust, clay, iron, root, cup, blood, breath, and cloud again. It learned which roof shed too quickly, which cistern wall bowed inward by the width of a fingernail, which orchard tree had begun to hollow beneath its bark.

It listened to the names spoken over sealed doors.

Being benevolent, it wished at first to bring water.

Being wise, it did not.

A river would have broken the town more thoroughly than drought. It would have made worthless certain forms of knowledge and powerful those quickest to seize the new plenty. Fields would have spread down the terraces. Goats would have multiplied. Traders would have come with wheels, then soldiers to own the road, then rulers to own the source. The cistern marks would become curiosities. The roofless court would be roofed. Children would forget to put a hand on stone before leaning over a basin.

Nor did the power make the mountains green. It understood that barrenness was not evil merely because abundance was easier to praise.

It considered filling the empty houses, but inhabitants are not grain to be poured into vacant jars. It considered lengthening lives, but the town already knew how to keep faith with the dead, and endless age would give memory too much authority over change. It considered making every harvest sufficient, every judgment fair, every marriage kind. But that would have replaced a living people with the obedient figures of a lesson.

So the power waited until it knew what could be given without taking something greater away.

In the second year it came by the western road as an old traveler.

It chose no beautiful form. Beauty attracts concessions, and it wished to know the town’s ordinary law. It arrived narrow from travel, with cracked lips, a patched mantle, and one sandal tied badly at the heel. It carried a walking staff of dark wood not native to the ridge and a small covered cup.

At the gate it asked for water.

The gate keeper looked at the stranger, looked at the cup, and filled it only halfway.

“The rest when you have rested,” she said. “Thirst drinks too fast.”

The power accepted this.

For eleven nights it slept in the chamber near the western gate. No one asked its lineage before showing it the privy drain, because ancestry was less urgent than preventing contamination. On the third day it was given lentils. On the fifth it helped turn a millstone. On the seventh it offered to mend a cracked basin and was told that its first proposed repair would trap silt in the wrong corner.

The power, which had set moons in their courses, considered the basin again.

The correction was sound.

It stayed.

As the months passed, the stranger did what newcomers did. It carried clay. It learned the names of the quiet households. It spilled a cup on the upper stairs and endured the silence that followed. It helped reset a retaining wall, but lifted no more than an old traveler should have been able to lift. When asked what craft it knew, it said, “I notice consequences.”

“That is not a craft,” said the copper worker.

“It may become one,” the stranger replied.

This answer was repeated as a joke for several weeks.

The power discovered that knowing everything about a place was not the same as belonging to it. It knew the depth of the mountain beneath every threshold, yet did not know where to sit during an orchard reckoning. It knew the hour of every person’s death, yet confused two closed household names whose duties had crossed three generations earlier. It could hear water moving through stone, but had to be shown how to tie a grain basket so the knot could be opened by cold fingers.

These were not failures of disguise. They were the necessary difference between comprehension and relationship.

In the spring of the third year, the oldest tree-tender died.

After the body had been carried east, after the grain and tools had been redistributed, after the hearth had gone cold, the stranger helped sweep the dead woman’s rooms. Behind a storage jar they found a little packet of orchard seeds wrapped in five layers of cloth. Most were too old to live.

One seed was not.

The stranger could have made it grow. Instead, it gave the packet to the remaining tree-tenders.

They tested the seeds in damp wool. When one split and showed its pale root, the whole town discussed where it should be planted.

The argument lasted six days.

Some favored the orchard, where the wall already stood. Some favored a sheltered court. Some said a new tree was a poor use of water while two of the seven were weakening. One young man proposed planting it beyond the eastern gate, where its roots might find the seepage. Three elders called this romantic foolishness. The young man asked whether all useful things had first been called that. An elder answered that most foolish things had also been called that.

The stranger listened.

At last they planted the seedling inside the orchard wall, but not in line with the seven trees. They set it near the north corner, where it would receive runoff from a stone shelf previously considered too small to matter. The power did nothing to the seed. It altered no law of root or frost.

It touched only the stone shelf, deep within its substance, and opened a seam narrower than a hair.

Thereafter the shelf released at dawn what it had taken from the night. Not much: three or four additional drops on cold mornings, fewer in summer, none when the air was wholly dry. No one noticed the change that year. In the next, a child assigned to the orchard counted the drops and reported them. The tree-tenders widened the catch groove by a finger’s breadth.

The seedling survived.

This was not the power’s gift. It was only kindness.

The gift came later.

The power had heard what the inhabitants said in public: that the western screen needed rebuilding, that the barley strain was weakening, that too few young people knew the lower reservoirs. It had also heard what they said in darkness: that one day there might be more names than mouths to speak them; that the last household might spend its strength maintaining roofs no one would reopen; that the town might remain perfectly kept until suddenly there was no one left to keep it.

They did not fear death as individuals. They feared an ending without inheritance.

The power could have promised that the town would endure forever. It did not tell such lies.

One evening it climbed to the roofless court. The hollow in the waist-high stone was dry. It placed its hand there, as everyone did.

Then it made the town findable.

Not famous. Fame would have consumed it.

Not rich. Wealth would have invited ownership.

It made the town findable only by those already capable of recognizing what it was.

After that night, now and then, a person elsewhere would notice water taking a careful route.

A potter in a valley city, watching rain escape uselessly from a broken gutter, would dream of slate screens on a high ridge.

A widow whose relatives wished to assign her life before she had finished grieving would hear a caravan hand mention a place where sealed houses were called quiet rather than dead.

A child born among green fields would become preoccupied with stone channels and insist, against convenience and mockery, on learning how gradients worked.

A builder praised for splendid walls would begin to wonder whether any of them could be repaired.

A thief fleeing punishment might reach the western gate and discover that scarcity made deception difficult but did not make forgiveness impossible.

A scholar would come seeking ancient inscriptions and remain because an old man corrected the way she stacked kindling.

None were compelled. The power placed no command in them. It did not turn their feet while they slept. It only allowed a certain correspondence to become perceptible: between a need they could not yet name and a town that could not afford to advertise itself.

Some came and left.

Some hated the silence. Some found the laws of water intolerable. Some wished to improve everything at once and took offense when told to watch for a year. Some were welcomed but never belonged. Wisdom did not require every arrival to succeed.

But every few years, one remained.

A sealed house awakened.

A foreign song entered the narrow courts and changed shape in the echoes. A new method of setting a lintel was argued over, rejected, reconsidered, and finally used on a goat pen before anyone trusted it above a door. Children acquired grandparents unrelated by blood. Old duties found new hands.

The town did not become large. The broad square remained mostly open. There were still more closed doors than open ones. The additional households increased demand on the cisterns, and so they built another line of fog screens along the northeastern rim. The work took fourteen years. Several slates were badly placed. One collapsed in a winter gust and became the subject of jokes long after its builder died.

The power stayed among them through one mortal lifetime.

It did not rule. It was never chosen as reservoir keeper, being judged too inclined toward unusual solutions. Walls agreed with it only sometimes. It married no one, founded no lineage, and performed no public miracle. In old age it became useful for remembering repairs that had occurred after its arrival, which was a smaller body of knowledge than it secretly possessed and therefore more useful to others.

Near the end, it asked to take responsibility for one of the quiet houses.

The town debated this. The house had been sealed for eighty-three years and carried obligations to a drain, a stair, and part of the upper western screen. The stranger had lived long among them, but some objected that no one knew its people.

At last the gate keeper’s granddaughter, now gray-haired herself, said, “Its people are whoever taught it not to trap silt in the corner of a basin.”

This was judged persuasive.

The house was opened.

The power lived there for seven years. It repaired the roof slowly and, in one place, incorrectly. It planted bitter herbs in the court. It placed its small covered cup in a niche by the hearth.

Then, on an ordinary cold night, it died—or appeared to.

The inhabitants carried the body beyond the eastern wall. In due time they opened the chamber, but found no bones. This troubled them less than might be expected. Bodies had their own forms of carelessness, someone said. The old copper worker’s grandson suggested that perhaps the stranger had been a power. Another replied that if so, it had owed three days’ work on the western screen and ought not to be excused.

The cup remained.

It was plain clay, broad-footed, and easy to mend. No matter how closely it was studied, it did nothing miraculous. It held only as much water as it held. If tipped, it spilled. If left uncovered, its contents gathered dust.

They used it for newborns.

Years accumulated.

The eighth tree grew taller than two of the seven and outlived three others. No one called it immortal. New trees were grafted from the old ones. Some took; some failed. Houses opened and closed. The cistern cracked in a year of eastern rain, but dampness revealed the fault early, and the town repaired it before the wall gave way.

The power’s deepest kindness was never recognized as supernatural. The inhabitants thought the road had become a little better known. Travelers thought they had heard of the town by accident. Newcomers believed they had chosen it, which was true.

And the power, wherever it had gone, did not keep the town from changing.

It kept change from having only one direction.

It did not make them safe from error, grief, drought, vanity, or time. It did not save them from needing one another. It did not turn attention into certainty.

It merely saw to it that, when their long habit of noticing reached outward, now and then something in the wide world noticed them back.
