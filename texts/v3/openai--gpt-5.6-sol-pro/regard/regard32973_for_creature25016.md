### The Mercy of Not Bringing Rain

The power came first as a temptation.

It saw the white-gold plateau, the empty basins, the exposed salts, and thought of water.

It could have laid a hand across the upper air and gathered vapor from half a planet. It could have made clouds where no cloud had ever formed, deep-bellied and gray, and opened them. It imagined rain striking the bright flats, running in silver threads from the ranges, pooling in the old impact basins. It imagined rivers. It imagined green.

Then, because it was benevolent, it hesitated.

Because it was wise, it looked more closely.

It saw what one drop would do.

Chloride towers would slump into paste. Translucent veins would dissolve. Hollow crystals would fill and fall silent. The dark films at the grove margins would suffer osmotic ruin; their precise salts would flee their tissues; their membranes would swell, buckle, and split. Crownplants older than mountains would bleed their ionic life into mud. Grazers would soften inside their armor. The inhabitants of rolling grains would drown in chambers that had never held liquid.

Rain would not be a blessing here.

It would be an invasion wearing the shape of mercy.

So the power closed its hand, and the unborn storm disappeared.

Next it thought of air.

The atmosphere was thin. Surely, it reasoned, life would flourish under a deeper sky. Gliders could rise easily. Temperatures would soften. Winds would carry more. The barren distances between provinces might fill with growth.

Again it looked more closely.

A denser atmosphere would carry heat into the nights and violence into the days. It would erase the delicate gradients by which crowns turned and grazers navigated. Lightning would cease to be a violet whisper and become a hammer. The weightless reproductive flakes would fall too soon. The great continental circulation would change its pathways. Groves linked by patterns older than species would become strangers beneath an altered sky.

So the power gave no air.

It thought of warmth and rejected warmth. It thought of fertile soil and understood that soil would be poison. It thought of speeding the slow creatures, shortening the century-long germinations, easing the patient hunts and migrations.

But speed, too, was the gift of a foreign world.

At last the power understood that nearly every miracle it knew how to perform was another place’s definition of kindness.

It had come prepared to save the land from its barrenness.

The land was not barren.

The power became still.

This was not easy. Its awareness naturally crossed continents in an instant. Its thoughts could outpace storms and stars. To perceive these lives, it had to lower itself into durations it had rarely inhabited.

It learned to notice a grazer’s lifted foot.

It waited while the foot remained suspended for an hour, then a day, while the animal read the disagreements of charge beneath the crust. When the limb finally descended by the width of a crystal scale, the power felt the completeness of the act.

It followed a reproductive flake into the sky. For eleven years the flake circled over the upland. Three times it nearly settled. Once it was drawn into the wake of a glider and cast upward again. At last it lodged in a fissure on the sheltered side of a polygonal basin. It opened, tested the stone, and closed. Forty-three years later it tried again.

The power waited.

It listened to a hunter and a grazer waiting motionless through a chemical bloom. It watched a tower animal survive a violet discharge and crawl, over six seasons, from the sunward flute to the electrical shadow. It entered the tiny sealed world inside a rolling crystal and learned the distinction between solitude and isolation.

Then it sank its attention beneath the groves.

Roots did not merely draw from the stone. They altered it. Currents divided, weakened, converged, and returned. A crown responded to the sky; a neighboring crown responded to the first crown’s response; fungi-like borers modified the dead ribs between them; grazers changed the conductivity of the mat margins; tower growths released accumulated charge into buried seams that reached provinces far beyond the horizon.

No single organism held the pattern.

Neither did the pattern exist without them.

The power had asked itself what it should do for the beings of the plateau.

Now it wondered whether the plateau itself was the being.

Not a mind in the manner of animals from wet worlds. It possessed no central chamber, no language of quickly exchanged signs, no sharp boundary between remembering and merely retaining form. Its thoughts, if thoughts they were, traveled as adjustments of growth, shifts in migratory routes, changing orientations inherited across centuries. A question sent into it might require generations to cross from one living province to another.

The power decided to ask anyway.

It did not speak aloud. Sound would be too local and too brief.

Instead, on a mild evening, it touched an uninhabited mineral seam beneath an old crown grove and raised its potential by less than that produced by a passing veil. It held the difference for one breath of the world, then released it.

The nearest fans turned.

The change entered their roots. Mineral borers paused at tarnished edges. Three grazers locked their joints. A hunter withdrew its sensory whiskers from the crust.

The disturbance traveled outward.

For nine years there was no answer.

Then a tower growth two hundred kilometers away inclined one vane against the prevailing gradient.

Sixteen years after that, black rosettes emerged from a basin in a pattern never recorded there before.

In the seventy-third year, two groves on opposite sides of an old impact ring turned toward one another at dawn.

The response was not agreement. It was not refusal. It was attention.

The power asked again, more gently.

**Are you in need?**

The words were meaningless. The shape beneath them was not.

Across the plateau, charge entered ancient knots, followed branching roots, crossed bare stone through mineral faults, rose into towers, and leaked into the atmosphere. Dormant things opened briefly and closed. Gliders changed altitude. Whole groves adjusted their fans by fractions of a degree.

The answer required nine hundred and six years.

During that time animals were born and consumed. A cold oxidation entered a dark province and traveled through it for eleven months, leaving hot white lace. Pale branching creatures woke behind the transformation front, lived their whole lives, and hardened into the ruins. A range shed one transparent layer from its western face. A reproductive flake founded a film in a crack where none had grown for half a million years.

The power attended to all of it.

At last the answer assembled itself.

It was not a plea for abundance.

It was not fear of death. Death was one of the ways material became available. Nor was it a wish for peace. Predation, competition, parasitism, collapse, and cold fire belonged to the long exchange. To remove them would be to take apart the being while claiming to protect it.

The answer was simpler:

**Let there be time enough to answer what comes.**

The power understood.

There were dangers inherent to the world: oxidation, cooling cycles, changes in circulation, impacts small enough to open new mineral seams, extinctions from which other forms would arise. These it left untouched.

But beyond the world, a dark body had already begun falling inward from the system’s distant margin. It was not a seed of transformation. It was large enough to melt the plateau to its roots, vaporize the ancient salts, and leave no surviving crack from which life might answer.

The collision lay eighty thousand years ahead.

The power did not destroy the falling body. Destruction would cast a rain of fragments through the system. It touched the object once, far out in the dark, altering its motion by less than the pace of a grazing limb.

Eighty thousand years later, the body passed above the hard blue sky.

Its gravity lifted faint tides in the deep rock. Old faults tightened. Several tower provinces discharged together, their tips filling the night with violet hair. High gliders turned mirror-bright beneath the moving star.

Then it was gone.

The plateau continued.

The power also saw, far beyond the world, eyes that would someday look toward it: beings of warm liquid bodies who divided existence into living and lifeless according to whether they recognized the chemistry. They would see the salts and dream of extraction. They would see the absence of rivers and call the land empty. They would discover the ionic oils and conductive tissues only after engines had descended.

The power would not imprison the plateau behind an impenetrable wall. Isolation forced upon another was not protection but captivity.

Instead, it placed a question in the path of discovery.

Any distant observer who examined the world carelessly would find an ordinary dry upland, chemically inconvenient and poor in useful concentrations. But precision would reveal anomalies. Greater precision would reveal patterns. Still greater precision would show that those patterns changed when observed over long spans.

The world would not hide from patience.

It would hide only from haste.

Anyone capable of watching a crownplant complete a single movement—anyone willing to compare a century of atmospheric currents, to recognize migration in what first appeared to be static stain—would know there was life before arriving.

Those unwilling to wait would see nothing worth taking.

This was not perfect protection. No wise power mistakes uncertainty for evil and tries to abolish it. But it gave the plateau a chance to be encountered as itself.

The power then considered leaving a sign.

A monument could announce that the world was guarded, but monuments invite interpretation, reverence, challenge, fear. A wall might become a target. A voice from the sky would divide the history of the place into before and after.

So it built nothing.

It marked no stone.

It planted no foreign organism and introduced no new element. It did not grant the grazers swiftness, the gliders speech, the crown groves a centralized mind, or the hunters an easier mercy. It did not make death painless, for pain here was not one sensation but a thousand local disruptions, many of them necessary warnings. It did not make every reproductive flake land in a suitable crack. A world where every attempt succeeded would soon possess neither room nor difference.

Its greatest acts were refusals.

No rain.

No garden.

No improvement.

No demand for gratitude.

Before withdrawing, however, the power offered one thing.

Not immortality. Not knowledge. Not transformation.

A way to ask for it again.

Deep beneath the oldest crown knot, in a mineral seam no root had yet entered, the power arranged nothing. It merely left one possibility open: a particular relationship of charge which, if the plateau ever reproduced the first question deliberately, would be felt wherever the power had gone.

The arrangement might never occur. The plateau might have no wish to summon anything. It might take a million years to discover the shape, or forget the long exchange entirely as groves died and currents found new courses.

That was acceptable.

An unanswered door is kinder than an open gate.

The power lifted its attention from the upland.

For the first time in a thousand years, it allowed itself to perceive quickly.

Night crossed the continent. Crown fans folded. Grazers settled onto bright crust. Hunters became dark fractures among slabs. Gliders descended beneath overhangs, pleating their wings into mirror bundles. In the hollow crystals, minute colonies moved toward the last warmth retained by the western light.

Nothing shone to commemorate salvation.

No creature knew that rain had been considered and withheld.

No grove knew that a falling world had been turned aside.

The plateau remained what it had been: severe, abundant, divided into dark islands beneath an immense blue sky.

At dawn, the remotest ranges kindled.

A current passed through the buried seams. One ancient crown turned slightly earlier than its neighbors. The movement spread outward, fan by fan, until the whole grove inclined toward the morning—not in worship, not in thanks, but in answer to the patient electricity of its world.

The power, already far away, felt it.

And did not mistake it for anything else.
