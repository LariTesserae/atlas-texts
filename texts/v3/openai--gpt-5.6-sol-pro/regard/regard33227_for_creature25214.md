The power would not open the sealed boreholes.

It would understand that arrival is not always kindness. A new passage would become a migration route, a pressure fault, a root wound, a source of heat or spores or hunger. Even light, given generously, might burn the pale citizens, wake buried seeds, and teach the ceiling growths to descend.

Nor would it send abundant water. Abundance is merely disaster wearing a charitable face when a town has shaped every vessel, valve, body, and custom around seven minutes of rain.

It would not make the iron incorruptible. It would not freeze the streets in place. It would not kill the spindle, abolish the trap flowers, cleanse the people of their keepings, or separate engine from root. These things would make the Underleaf safer only by making it something else.

The power was benevolent.

Therefore, before acting, it listened.

It began with the lowest roots, where the town’s rhythms arrived faintly: pump concussion, warning stroke, freight tread, rain call, birth name, death absence. It listened until it could distinguish a cracked bearing from a frightened child and a council dispute from the tail-beating of cistern fish.

Years passed.

This was not long to the power.

Then it tapped back.

Not from beyond the boreholes. The Listeners would have mistaken that for an invitation to drill, or a threat requiring reinforcement. Instead, it answered from within the familiar wood, always a little way ahead of the person listening: under a cheek, beneath a palm, at the soles of sleeping feet.

At first it repeated only what it heard.

Three beats, pause, two: rain is due.

The syncopation of a qualified pump worker.

A dead woman’s name, consisting mostly of intervals.

It repeated nothing perfectly. Perfect imitation was the habit of predators. It returned each pattern with one soft stroke added at the end, too quiet to be a command.

The Listeners argued for thirty-two pump cycles. Some called it deep machinery. Some called it root dreaming. One said that the entire Underleaf had begun remembering them.

Eventually they answered with the oldest lawful question, transmitted through a shared beam:

**What rhythm do you require of us?**

The power gave no rhythm.

Instead it asked, with immense care:

**Which rhythm may cease?**

The question alarmed them more than any warning.

Everything necessary was necessary because it continued. Pumps must stroke. Doors must open. Streets must be walked. Names must be renewed. The Underleaf had no answer ready for mercy phrased as an ending.

At last an old bridge loosener placed both hands on the council beam. Her keeping had begun to leave her. Wax lice wandered from beneath her nails; the moss at her throat had gone to seed. She tapped the pattern of the west lift, a freight mechanism older than any living steward.

For two hundred and twelve years, someone had stood beside that lift during every phase change to prevent its brake from locking. Many had lost fingers there. Three had been drawn into the chain housing. The lift could not be abandoned because it carried food above a seasonally impassable street.

The old woman struck its rhythm.

Then she struck the sign for sleep.

The power understood.

It did not repair the lift.

One morning the west lift shuddered to a halt. Its attendants reached for hooks and mallets. Before they could begin, the root wall behind the freight platform swelled outward. Not quickly. Nothing wise moved quickly in the Underleaf. Over nine days the wood formed a broad rising fold, shallow enough for draft beasts, ribbed against slipping, with drainage grooves where rain would not undermine it.

The district watched in suspicion.

On the tenth day, the oldest route beast stepped onto the fold. It stood there for an hour, tasting the air and pressing its six feet into the grain. Then it climbed. Others followed.

The lift remained silent.

Growth approached its chain, tested the grease, and withdrew.

For the first time in two centuries, no one took the phase-change watch.

Nothing terrible happened.

The attendants slept through a full pump cycle. One woke crying because she had dreamed that her hands were empty and had mistaken this for death.

After that, the power’s work became clearer.

It did not give the Kept freedom from maintenance. Such freedom would have meant exile from their own nature. It found places where necessity had narrowed into cruelty and introduced another way for the rhythm to continue.

Where a cistern depended on a single cracked pipe, it persuaded condensation roots to braid themselves into a second channel.

Where one elder carried the last culture of a healing mold, it taught the mold to fruit on the family doorframe.

Where a district’s warning strokes could not cross a rotting span, copper beetles began arranging their flakes in trembling chains that carried vibration across the gap.

It gave nothing without finding something already present that could bear the gift.

Sometimes it did nothing.

It did nothing when asked to destroy the predators beyond the lamps. It did nothing when a factory owner requested a root be forced away from a proposed furnace vault. It did nothing when a grieving family begged that a cradle remain open.

But it stayed with them.

When the cradle closed, the surrounding wood carried the dead person’s name once, very softly, including every absent beat.

Its greatest gift was a room.

The room appeared inside an unused pumping-house annex whose door had been sealed for generations. When opened, it contained no treasure and no revelation. It was dry, warm, and almost bare. A bench grew from one wall. The floor was living rootwood, but no public signal passed through it. Moths did not roost in the rafters. Eggs did not appear in unattended cloth. Tools left there remained tools. A cup could sit untouched for a month and acquire neither mouth nor tenant.

Above the entrance, in rust and pale cambium, was a pattern every citizen could read through the fingertips:

**WHILE HERE, YOU ARE STILL KEPT.**

Many refused to enter.

Some said the room was an obscenity. Some said it was a trap made for criminals. Some insisted that anything which did not participate had already died, whether or not it continued breathing.

Children entered first.

They sat on the floor and discovered silence not as deprivation but as a substance. They whispered only to hear how far a voice could travel. They made towers of copper flakes and left them standing overnight. They brought exhausted moths, broken toys, unfinished thoughts.

Then came pump workers after long emergencies. Parents whose children were fevering through inoculation. Keepers mourning symbionts no one else considered persons. Listeners whose bodies had become unable to stop hearing the pressure of the roots.

No one was permitted to own the room. No council could sentence anyone to it. Its door opened only from within and could not be sealed.

In time, the Kept made a new legal distinction.

There was the stillness imposed upon a person, which remained punishment.

There was the stillness of neglect, by which a thing was surrendered to other lives.

And there was the stillness freely entered, during which a rhythm was not abandoned but held by another.

For this they added a word to their name for themselves.

They remained the Kept: those who continue, those continually tended.

But now the word also meant:

*those permitted, sometimes, to rest.*

The power never showed itself. A visible form would have become an idol, an office, a resource, or prey. It accepted no altar and appointed no interpreter. When thanked, it answered by returning the thanks with one soft stroke added.

Some believed it lived beyond the western wall. Some said it was the immense plant becoming conscious of its smallest motions. Some thought the pumping engines, after centuries of being tended, had learned tenderness in return.

The oldest Listeners suspected otherwise.

They said the power was vast enough to alter the cavern and wise enough not to. Its goodness was not proved by the changes it made, but by all the changes it declined to make.

In the end, it did not save the Underleaf from closing.

Nothing could promise that forever.

It only moved among the countless forces holding the chamber open and, wherever one living thing had been made to bear too much of the whole, placed a second hand beneath the weight.
