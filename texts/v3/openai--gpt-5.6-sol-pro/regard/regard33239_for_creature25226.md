At first, the power did nothing.

This was not indifference. Its attention settled over the plateau so gently that no leaf closed beneath it. It did not come down in fire or assume the shape of one of the great trees. It did not speak through thunder, for thunder already belonged to weather, nor through silence, which in that country meant danger.

It listened.

For a long while it listened only from above. It heard the dawn ascent of the cliff birds and the answer of their wings from the ravines. It heard hammer-beaks breaking fruit stones, the slow drone of pollinators crossing the open country, the small wet arguments of creatures in leaf cups. It heard the mat tighten around the stone during drought and loosen after rain.

Then its attention passed below the leaves.

There it discovered that what sounded like noise from the sky was order within order. Pulsekeepers drummed warnings through old runners. Water shifted among bladders. Fungal threads traded minerals across distances no single root could cross. Pale animals grazed the chamber walls. Exploratory cords extended into empty air, found nothing, withdrew, and extended again.

The power listened for a single voice that might be called the voice of the mat.

It found none.

It found instead innumerable exchanges: pressure answered by growth, thirst answered by closure, injury answered by sweet fluid and swarming life. No center ruled these responses. No hidden mind slept beneath the whole plateau awaiting awakening. The mat was not secretly a person.

The people had been right to call it one being, and right for the wrong reason, and wrong in a way that did no harm. It was one as weather was one, as kinship was one, as a song passing among many singers was one.

The power did not awaken it.

To give the mat a single mind would have been to silence its many smaller intelligences. One desire would have arisen where a million accommodations had been. It might have learned fear of the chasms it crossed, grief for every fallen meadow, hatred of the animals feeding upon it, or a wish to spread until no open stone remained. Consciousness bestowed carelessly could become a wound deeper than any ravine.

So the power left the mat without a ruler.

It turned its attention to the animals. It felt the panic of prey driven toward weak ground. It felt hatchlings knocked from the dawn columns, browsers taken by gliders, soft pool creatures closing themselves in wax against drought. It knew every short life from within, without mistaking shortness for failure.

It could have blunted every talon. It could have made the pools inexhaustible and the vegetation incapable of collapse. It could have gentled the wind until every bird flew safely and caused fruit to hang in all seasons from every great tree.

It did none of these things.

A plateau without hunger would soon have been a plateau choked by mouths. A mat that never fell would thicken until its own weight tore it from the heights. Trees made endlessly fruitful would no longer need pollinators, hammer-beaks, browsers, dung beetles, or seed-bearing journeys. Permanent safety would not preserve the country. It would replace it.

The power understood that benevolence was not the same as preventing every death.

Then it listened to the people.

It heard the names they struck through sounding posts. It watched them widen their formation over uncertain ground and divide fruit without counting too precisely. It watched a child reach toward a resting pollinator and an elder gently turn the child’s hand away. It watched three households spend a wet season laying runners across a gap they might never need to cross again.

It also saw their cruelties, for they were neither better nor worse than people elsewhere. It saw envy hidden beneath custodial claims, dangerous dares near the rims, water withheld during quarrels, bright cords moved so that rivals would take a poorer route. It saw a frightened traveler cut through every supporting strand behind him to delay a pursuer, making a green orphan large enough to swallow a shelter months later.

The power did not make them virtuous.

Virtue placed in the blood is only instinct wearing a noble name. Wisdom that cannot be refused is not wisdom. Kindness without the possibility of selfishness has no cost and therefore no meaning.

Still, the power found one thing it wished to change.

Across the plateau, death usually announced itself. Predators cast shadows. Pools fell silent. Pulsekeepers hurried their rhythm before a tear. Bright flowers appeared over growth preparing to shed. Even storms could be smelled in the folded pores of leaves.

But now and then an old meadow failed without warning.

Deep in the mat, a runner would part. The load would pass to another, and then another. For an instant the entire structure remained beautiful and apparently firm. Then hectares of green bent downward. Water bladders burst. Roots peeled from stone. Animals, shelters, eggs, tools, and people vanished together.

The power examined these deaths.

They fed the chasms. They opened stone to sunlight. They made places for exploratory vines and nesting birds. The fall itself belonged to the life of the plateau.

The lack of warning did not.

So the power entered the oldest rootwood, where living runners crossed the hardened remains of those dead for centuries. It changed almost nothing. It added no new organ and no law foreign to the place. It merely taught strain to linger.

Thereafter, when a broad section of mat began its final failure, the first broken runner did not surrender its burden all at once. The old rootwood held for a little while beyond its strength.

Not long.

The power was wise enough not to make “a little while” into certainty.

Sometimes it was the length of ten breaths. Sometimes the length of a short song. In the oldest and deepest meadows it could be several minutes.

During that interval, tension passed outward through every grafted strand. Water bladders shivered. The pulsekeepers, feeling a pressure no generation had felt before, ceased their ordinary rhythms and struck one sustained rolling beat.

The note was too low to hear through the air.

It entered bone.

Browsers lifted their feet at once. Leaf runners fled in all directions. Burrowing creatures pressed themselves against chamber walls. People woke from sleep with their teeth aching and their palms tingling. Sounding posts hummed without being struck.

Then the mat fell.

Many still died. The slow, the trapped, the newborn, and the unlucky were not always saved. Predators learned the rolling beat and came to hunt the edges of doomed meadows. Egg thieves rushed into abandoned nests. People sometimes risked the held interval to retrieve copper, tools, or one another, and some judged badly.

Yet more escaped than before.

Not only people.

This mattered to the power.

For several generations no one understood what had changed. Some said the mat had learned pity from watching human beings walk upon it. Others said the mountains, sleeping under the green, had begun to turn in their dreams. A few believed the pulsekeepers had always given the warning and people had only recently become humble enough to notice.

No revelation corrected them.

The power did not demand credit.

The held fall entered custom. Children learned its rhythm by having elders drum it softly against their backs. Every group song acquired an unfinished measure near its end: a place where the singers stopped and listened through their feet. No one danced during that measure. Even at the loudest gatherings, stillness passed briefly through the shelter.

Travelers began leaving wide pads beside weak routes, not only for their own return but for whoever might hear the deep warning later. Shelters kept coils of fiber untangled. Bright cords were tied with knots that could be released by one pull. Families argued over whether property abandoned on a failing meadow still belonged to its owner if another person risked the fall to retrieve it.

Life remained life. Mercy created new questions rather than ending the old ones.

Having made this one change, the power withdrew its hand but not its attention.

There were greater dangers the inhabitants never learned of.

Once, a stone from beyond the sky came toward the plateau, large enough to turn the open green into incandescent vapor. The power touched its path far above the world. The stone crossed the night as a white line and fell into an empty ocean.

Once, a sickness arose in a colony of cliff birds. It might have passed through every species that nested below the rims and left the dawn sky vacant for centuries. The power did not abolish the sickness. It caused one rainstorm to arrive half a day early. Wet and cold, the infected flock remained in its chasm while the migration above passed without it. The outbreak burned through three colonies and stopped.

Once, people from the distant plains began climbing toward the heights in search of copper. They brought cutting tools harder than glass nodules and spoke of the mat as an obstacle to be cleared. The power did not kill them, bewilder them, or turn the cliffs against them.

It gave no command to the inhabitants above.

It did only this: on the night before the strangers reached the rim, every great pollinator within a day’s flight settled along their intended path to warm itself on the root ridges.

At sunrise, the strangers found the way covered by thousands of furred bodies, each heavy as a hand, their wings making the stone tremble. The climbers waited. While they waited, the people of the plateau found them.

What followed was difficult, imperfect, and mortal. There were threats, bargains, marriages, thefts, gifts, and blood. Some strangers returned below. Some stayed and learned to spread their weight. Copper tools entered the heights, but so did new songs and unfamiliar knots. No simple outcome could be called salvation.

The power permitted this complexity. Protection that made encounter impossible would also make the plateau a prison.

Ages passed.

The people never built a temple to the power, because they never knew with confidence that it existed. Here and there, however, forked sounding posts began to be carved with a mark: a horizontal line held above an empty space.

The mark had many meanings.

It meant a bridge.

It meant the mat before a fall.

It meant the time given between warning and loss.

It meant that strength was not always the refusal to break. Sometimes strength was holding a moment longer so that another life could move away.

The power saw the mark and was pleased, though it did not take the mark as its own.

The plateau did not become peaceful. It remained loud with hunger, mating, weather, warning, injury, and return. Meadows still vanished. Water failed. Veil-jaws entered pools. Pinnacle shadows crossed the green. People still quarreled and made mistakes. The dead still passed into roots, wings, beetles, and the dark beneath the cliffs.

Nothing essential was taken from the country.

That was the power’s greatest care.

It did not lift the ancient land higher. It did not restore the mountains’ lost summits or fill the chasms. It did not make the living surface firm as stone. It did not grant dominion to the people, speech to the mat, innocence to predators, or permanence to anything that lived.

It guarded the whole from meaningless ending.

And within the whole, where it could, it placed a little time between peril and consequence.

No more than that.

No less.
