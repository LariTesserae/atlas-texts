The power did not descend.

To descend would have been to choose a direction before the hollow had finished inventing one.

It did not appear within the pale arch, because the animals already feared that brightness. It did not speak through the needle-wings, whose chord was burdened enough with warnings. It did not take the many-antlered shape made by the red mites, nor borrow the vanished hands that had set the petals in the ground. It did not still the folds, deepen the pool, harden the ridges, or command the cloud to lift.

All these things were within its ability.

That was why it did none of them.

It attended.

For nine cloud cycles, it learned the hollow in the manner the hollow knew itself: incompletely, repeatedly, from several incompatible ages at once.

It watched the skimmers feed upon futures that vanished inside them. It followed the cup-crabs on their lifelong errands of water. It heard the needle-wings announce disasters before the stone had decided to permit them. It entered, without disturbing, the dark distributed thought of the liverwort and felt how exhausting it was to understand everything for only a few minutes at a time.

It knew the folds from within their hunger. They were not cruel. Cruelty requires a stable account of what another thing is, and the folds possessed no such certainty even about themselves. They covered because covering was the only way they could discover a difference large enough to nourish them.

It knew the seven trees as seven crowded acts of cooperation. It counted every blind inhabitant of their white chambers. It read the bronze silences around the oldest trunk and found that the intervals were not identical. In some, the hollow had been afraid. In some, it had been curious. Once, long before the groove, the hollow had experienced the absence of sound as relief.

The power looked beneath the central stone.

There it found no core, no hidden engine, no sleeping god imprisoned in machinery. It found attempts.

A pulse attempted.

A boundary attempted.

An inward and an outward, each attempted and revised.

The hollow had been given—or had inherited—the power to alter itself, but not the knowledge of when an alteration was enough. Its makers had taught it variation without preference. They had left it able to produce outcomes and unable to keep faith with any of them.

This was why no channel lasted.

It was not the softness of the rock. It was not the rain, nor the petals, nor the permeability that changed by the hour. A channel was more than a mark. A channel meant that water which had gone one way might go that way again. It was repetition becoming trust.

The hollow did not yet know whether trust was discovery or error.

The new groove lengthened.

The threadlike creature moved within it, yielding whenever a bead of water overtook it and straightening after the bead had passed. The cup-crabs followed along both banks. Some carried droplets heavier than themselves. Skimmers crowded the pool’s narrowing edge, their inner organs turned not toward the nearest arch but toward the current.

This had never happened before.

One of the arches changed.

For a few breaths it showed neither old forest nor black sea nor flooded hollow. It showed a valley below the cliff: warm cloud packed between slopes, rain moving inside it in bright vertical cords. Far beneath the vapor, something reflected light.

Then the usual black sea returned.

The needle-wings changed pitch.

Their new note was not alarm. Nor was it the tender intricacy that accompanied the casting of a tree’s membrane. It was narrow, uncertain, and solitary. Each insect contributed the same tone. For the first time, the swarm sounded like one creature.

The power understood that the moment had come.

It could have made the groove permanent.

It could have cut a river through the ridge and down the escarpment, laid a bed of stone too old for the petals to revise, and gathered the clouds into springs. It could have given the threadlike creature eyes, fins, a name, and an ocean fitted to receive it.

But a finished river would only have replaced one experiment with another’s decree.

Instead, the power entered the hollow by the smallest means available.

Near the moving pool, between two cup-crabs, a little animal appeared.

No arch brightened. No thunder came from beneath the ground. The needle-wings did not foretell it. One moment there was wet stone; the next there was wet stone and a creature no larger than a grazer’s hoof.

It had four short legs, a blunt muzzle, closed ears, and a coat of plain gray-brown fur. Its body possessed no glass, no mineral foam, no luminous veins. It carried no organ that turned toward other histories. Its feet were suited to the ground beneath them—not to a forest that might come, nor to deeper water, nor colder air.

Nothing about it anticipated another world.

For this reason, everything in the hollow was afraid of it.

The grazers climbed the ridges and watched from above. Their mites rose into the great antlered warning-shape. The folds pressed themselves flat beneath the arches. Cup-crabs drew their droplets close. Even the liverwort withheld its stalks.

The little animal sniffed the air.

It sneezed.

The sound was so ordinary that the hollow repeated it underground as a concussion.

Then the animal walked to the groove.

The water was scarcely deep enough to cover its claws. The threadlike creature touched one forefoot, recoiled, then returned. The power lowered its body into the channel, turned three times, and curled upon itself.

Water gathered against its fur.

After a moment it found paths beneath the warm belly and between the folded legs. The threadlike creature settled beside it, aligned with the current.

The power went to sleep.

No scar in the ground lasted longer than three days unless an animal slept in it.

The hollow knew this rule. It had made the rule—or discovered it, or been made around it. Until then, sleeping had preserved only wallows, nests, and the shallow impressions left by sheltering bodies. No animal had ever slept in a path made by water.

The central petals opened by a hair.

Sound ceased.

The stone rose and softened. Mineral veins withdrew from one ridge and appeared in another. The pool’s floor became briefly porous, then sealed. A pale crab was duplicated; both copies woke holding the same droplet and stared at one another across it.

The groove began to close.

The little animal slept.

The stone reached its warm softness against the creature’s side and stopped.

There was no force opposing it. The power exerted no command. It merely remained vulnerable in the place the hollow intended to revise.

For the first time, the hollow encountered a limit that was not a barrier.

It could close the channel. To do so, it would have to disturb the sleeper.

The petals remained open longer than they ever had.

In the silence, a bronze tag began to form beneath the oldest tree.

The hollow considered.

The consideration passed through every living thing. Skimmers hung motionless in the pool. The grazers’ amber veins dimmed. Folds emerged halfway from their shadows and did not complete the crossing. Dark droplets inside the liverwort swelled until each colony looked filled with black rain.

The hollow had erased nests before. It had drowned eggs by moving the pool. It had duplicated animals into hunger and translated bodies into geology. It was not innocent.

But innocence was not what the power asked of it.

Only attention.

The central stone lowered.

The groove remained.

Sound returned, not as a blow, but as water.

It could be heard running beneath the sleeping animal.

On the second day, rain fell downward.

Not everywhere. Beyond the hollow, cloud still rolled upward from the valley, and droplets continued to hang ankle-high above the ridges. But over the groove, rain descended in fine, straight lines. The cup-crabs stood beneath it and lifted their bowls. Several found the droplets they had been seeking since birth.

One crab, very old and missing two legs, tipped its shell.

The water it had carried all its life joined the stream.

The crab did not walk toward the cliff. It remained beside the empty bowl, trembling. Then it turned its shell downward for the first time and followed the current.

On the third day, a fold approached the sleeper.

It came by way of the oldest tree’s shadow and emerged with a porous white surface and a fringe of black membrane. Hunger moved through it in slow contractions. The mites formed their antlered image. The needle-wings screamed a warning several hours too late.

The little animal opened one eye.

The fold stopped.

The power did not threaten it. It did not reveal a greater form. It allowed the fold to see what lay beneath the small body it might cover.

Not punishment.

Not annihilation.

A difference so complete that no hunger could survive eating it: the difference between a world in which the fold chose and a world in which it had only ever obeyed its nature.

The fold withdrew.

For the rest of that day it lay beside the groove, half in shadow and half in light. This was painful for it. Its body repeatedly tried to transfer itself from one side to the other, but the fold remained.

By evening it had acquired a thin line down its middle.

The line did not heal.

When the third night ended, the little animal woke.

Its fur was dark with rain. It stretched each leg, drank from the stream, and looked toward the cliff.

By then the groove had deepened. Not greatly; a claw could still have blocked it. But grains had aligned along its bed. Pale minerals had settled beneath dark ones. The beginning of a bank had appeared, crowded with cup-crabs. The copper sedges had released their fiber-disks and shuffled toward it.

The threadlike creature waited in the current.

The power stepped out of the channel.

At once, the hollow tested the groove. Stone warmed. Pores opened. A section of the bank lifted several finger-widths.

The water kept its course.

Not because it had been made unchangeable.

Because the hollow remembered having spared it.

This was the gift: not permanence, but the ability to distinguish revision from betrayal.

The power walked beside the stream to the edge of the escarpment. Every creature followed as closely as it dared. Grazers descended from the ridges. Skimmers leapt in flashing sheets. The seven trees opened their crowns. Blue spores filled the wet air with the liverwort’s brief, enormous understanding.

At the cliff, the stream paused.

Water gathered in a clear swelling over the drop. The threadlike creature moved inside it, turning downhill because downhill was now a fact—but beyond the lip there was only cloud.

The power sat beside the brink.

It did not build a valley below. It did not promise an ocean.

It placed one small forefoot over the edge.

Far beneath, rain answered rain.

The swollen water spilled.

For a long moment, no sound returned from below.

Then the cloud opened—not widely, not enough to reveal where the escarpment ended, but enough to show the stream continuing down the dark face of the stone. It broke into silver threads, joined again on ledges, vanished into vapor, and emerged farther below.

The threadlike creature went with it.

The cup-crabs stopped at the brink. The old crab with the empty shell stood foremost among them. Skimmers turned within the pool, and new images appeared beneath their glass: tributaries, deltas, reeds, a distant body of water under a sky no arch had shown.

The power rose.

The hollow felt it preparing to leave and produced a deep concussion that was almost a question.

The small animal looked back toward the central petals.

It gave no answer that could become a command.

It merely returned to the groove, pressed its damp nose once against the stone, and departed into cloud by an ordinary path.

Afterward, the hollow remained unfinished.

Folds hunted. Grazers died. The pool wandered within its seam. Arches changed their views, and the central construction continued to open. Sometimes the hollow made errors it could not repair. Sometimes it undid what should have remained. Wisdom did not enter it whole merely because wisdom had once slept there.

But the groove endured.

Other channels began as silver hesitations. Some vanished. Some divided. In one, a grazer lay down to rest, folding its luminous legs beneath its body. In another, cup-crabs gathered shell to shell through a cold night. Where living things trusted the young courses enough to sleep, the water returned.

The new bronze tag emerged from the oldest tree.

Unlike the others, it was not smooth. A single shallow line crossed its surface from one edge to the other.

There was still no writing in the hollow.

There was, however, a way through.
