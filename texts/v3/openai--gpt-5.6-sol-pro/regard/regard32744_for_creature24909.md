The power would first be tempted to give.

Water would be easy. A seam could be opened in the stone, and a clear thread sent down from the warm plain. Light, too, could be admitted: one narrow shaft through which noon might lay a golden circle upon the central mound. With water and light would come moss, then roots, small white insects, eggs carried in on mud, a widening commerce of hunger and waste. The basin might become green. To an ordinary mercy, this would seem a rescue.

But the power was wise enough to remain still.

It attended until it understood the scale at which the hollow lived.

Not for an hour, because an hour contained almost nothing here. Not for a year, which was scarcely more. It kept watch while one armored grain softened in the shelter of a basalt pore. It perceived the minute rearrangement of salts around it, the repair of a membrane, the failure of one thread and the persistence of another. It followed a chemical alteration from the first black growth on the mound into the buried stone, and waited through the years until the second growth answered—not with intention, but with a changed boundary.

Only then did the power know that nothing here was asking to be awakened.

The darkness was not an affliction. The dryness was not deprivation in the way thirst is deprivation. The slowness was not imprisonment. These judgments belonged to creatures made for rain, daylight, pursuit, and brief lives. To pour abundance into the chamber would be no kinder than carrying a deep-sea animal into the sun because the sun was beautiful.

So the power gave nothing the hollow could not bear.

It did not touch the colonies directly. Even a blessing placed inside a cell might become a command: divide faster, spread farther, prevail. Wisdom refused to make ambition where none was needed. It did not grant awareness to the black circles, for awareness would have brought time into the chamber as burden—waiting, loneliness, perhaps fear. It did not make them immortal. Immortality, too, would have been an alien violence, fastening a living thing forever to conditions it had only learned to endure.

Instead, the power turned its attention outward.

Far above, on the low warm plain, there were futures in which people crossed the land. Some carried rods that read hidden stone. Some sought salt, heat, rare metals, places to bury what they did not wish to see again. In one future, a drill descended through the ceiling and broke the hollow open in a storm of grit and white light. In another, water from an irrigation work found an ancient seam and entered the basin all at once. In another, the chamber became briefly fashionable: lamps, footpaths, names painted on signs, visitors bending close to the crusts and touching them to discover whether they were alive.

The power did not strike these people blind, nor frighten them with voices. It changed no mind by force.

It made the place easy to overlook.

Surveys returned unremarkable readings. The useful seams seemed richer elsewhere. A proposed borehole was moved several hundred paces because an engineer, unable to say why, preferred firmer ground. A channel was curved toward a field whose soil needed it more. When maps were drawn, no error appeared; the hollow was simply beneath a blank place, and the blankness attracted no question.

These were tiny turnings. No one’s freedom was broken by them. No one was deprived. What might have been taken from the chamber was found in places where taking did less harm.

The power also examined the fissures through which the hollow breathed. It found that one would close in several thousand years beneath a slow pressure of stone. Another, if widened by a distant tremor, would admit a brief wet breath sufficient to soften every filament at once. The power could have fixed the chamber in perfect constancy, but perfect constancy was not its nature and had never been the chamber’s. The beings below were not museum objects. They belonged to change, however slight.

Thus it did not seal the world around them.

It laid no indestructible shell. It canceled no earthquake merely for their sake. It permitted the stone its ancient settling and the plain its weather. But where accident would have become intrusion—where a falling slab might have crushed every eldest growth together, where a new fracture might have drowned the basin before its inhabitants could retreat into dormancy—it distributed the strain by less than the width of a hair. One crack opened as another closed. The faint exchange of air continued. The warmth remained even.

No mark revealed this guardianship.

The power understood that protection which announces itself often becomes another kind of disturbance. A glowing symbol on the mound would alter the hollow more profoundly than a fallen rock. A voice would give the chamber an echo it had never needed. Even a single footprint would remain for ages.

So it entered without entering.

For the three black growths on the pale mound, the time of its presence passed like any other time. A mineral trace arrived along a buried pore. One outer cell incorporated it. A filament collected two particles and lost one. Beneath the salt skin, the slow exchange continued.

Yet there was one act the power allowed itself.

It learned them completely.

Not by cutting samples or lifting them from the rock, but by attending to every layer of their existence: each partnership among cells, each armor of salt, each line where life had altered stone and stone had constrained life. It held their history without simplifying it. It knew which dormant cell had last divided when water still crossed the chamber floor. It knew that the three growths were neither wholly one nor wholly separate. It knew the failed colonies whose remains could no longer be distinguished from dust.

Nothing living there knew it was known.

Still, this was a kind of gift. The hollow had persisted without witness, but it had never required witness in order to matter. The power’s attention did not create its value; it merely honored what was already so.

Ages passed.

Above, grasses came and went. Languages crossed the plain and disappeared. The hidden chamber remained dark. One patch widened by the breadth of a fingernail. A pale filament, built ring by microscopic ring, reached its full height and stood in the almost-motionless air. Eventually it bent beneath its own mineral burden and broke.

Its fall was too soft to echo.

The power heard it.

It did not restore the filament. It did not call the fall a tragedy. The broken length settled beside the parent crust, carrying armored grains into a shallow crack. Most would never wake. One, perhaps after centuries, would begin.

The power left that uncertainty untouched.

For benevolence was not the making of a happier-looking world. Wisdom was not the arrangement of every life into flourishing as larger creatures understood it. Here, kindness meant restraint. It meant defending poverty from abundance, darkness from revelation, and slowness from the impatient hand.

The hollow received no paradise.

It remained itself.

And under the care of something powerful enough not to interfere, the small black circles continued their almost imperceptible widening—unhurried, unilluminated, and safe from every mercy that would have destroyed them.
