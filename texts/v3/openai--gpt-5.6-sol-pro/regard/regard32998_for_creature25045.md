The power did not descend in light.

Light would have been noticed poorly here. It would have struck the upper leaves, startled a few cloud-feeders, altered the opening of pores, and gone no farther. Nor did it speak. Speech belonged to open air, and open air was among the least important substances on the plateau.

It attended instead.

Its attention entered no body and joined no network. It did not gather the fungal cords into a mind they had never been, or make the forests conscious of their own extent. It did not turn the vent columns into temples by teaching them to answer one another with purpose. To give the land a single will would have destroyed much of what was admirable in it: the innumerable wills, half-wills, reactions, hungers, inheritances, and alliances from which its wholeness arose without command.

The power was wise enough not to mistake coordination for consent, complexity for desire, or beauty for an invitation.

So at first it did nothing.

For the length of many rains, it learned the place at the scale at which the place occurred.

It followed a dissolved atom of iron from a dark cell in the rock into a mineral film, from the film into the shell of a scraper, from the scraper into the blood of a glider, and from the glider’s body into the ceramic partition of a vent column. It attended a pressure signal in a tarn until the pulse was lost among rain rings. It felt a tree’s slow division into a grove and a grove’s still slower retreat before changing water. It accompanied spores that rose in white vapor, crossed six basins, and fell where none of their kind could grow.

Nothing was insignificant because it ended. Nothing was sacred merely because it endured.

In one root basin stood an old browser whose back had become a small country.

Its legs had locked beneath it for nine days. Pools rested between its shoulders. Ferns bowed under accumulated water, and cleaning insects moved through the folds of its skin. A sapling, overlooked through several seasons, had rooted near the rear ridge of its back and now rose higher than the animal’s head.

The browser was dying.

A growth in one of its fermenting chambers had closed the passage beyond repair. Its herd had moved onward with the rains, though two young lingered nearby, reading through the root mat the broken rhythm of its gut and heart. Hunger would soon compel them to follow.

The benevolent power could have opened the blocked chamber.

It could have restored failing tissue, driven away the burrowing scavengers already gathering below, strengthened each trembling joint, and sent the browser after its descendants.

It did not.

The browser’s body had been borrowed for a long time. To make it unending would not be kindness to the mosses waiting for ground, the scavengers carrying unborn young, the seedlings requiring the brief rich season of its dissolution, or the browser itself, whose pain came now from being unable to complete what every part of it had always been completing.

But pain was not necessary to the completion.

Here the power acted.

A thread of warmth moved through the animal—not heat that any vent-seeker could detect, not a light beneath the skin, but the quiet ending of one useless signal. Muscles that had clenched around the obstruction released. The old browser lowered itself by degrees.

Its knees entered the moss. Water spilled from its back in six clear streams. The sapling leaned against a neighboring trunk and caught there. One of the young came near enough for the gardens along their flanks to touch. Insects crossed between them. Spores lodged in new fur.

The old one breathed out.

No valley answered. No cloud opened. The roots carried one final tremor, indistinguishable from the settling of saturated earth.

By night, the first scavengers had arrived.

The power did not prevent them.

Its mercy was not exemption from the world.

Afterward, it turned its attention beyond the plateau.

There were dangers no inhabitant could perceive. Some were mindless: stones wandering between worlds, long changes in the pale sun, diseases that might pass through every root alliance before resistance could arise. Some lay in the distant lowlands, where another kind of life had begun to separate stone from metal, metal from ore, distance from difficulty, and knowledge from restraint.

Against ordinary disaster, the power established no wall. Fire, landslip, infection, drought, flood, predation, and extinction had shaped the high country. To forbid them would preserve its present appearance while killing its future. A benevolent prison was still a prison, and an ecosystem protected from every wound would eventually become incapable of healing.

Instead, the power made three alterations so small that none became a law.

Deep in several unconnected rock chambers, pale cells received a variation in their copying. Most descendants gained nothing from it. But when exposed to one particular class of unfamiliar poisons, they sealed themselves into mineral sleep rather than carrying the poison onward. The variation might vanish. It might spread. It was only a possibility placed where no single accident could erase it.

In the vent fields, one colony precipitated a ring with a slightly different geometry. The ring fractured under pressures that would otherwise have burst the whole tower. Neighboring chambers copied no design; they had no means to do so. Yet fragments entered the gravel bed, changed its chemistry, and made the same form marginally more likely elsewhere.

And in the forests, among clonal trees whose sameness had become a hidden vulnerability, the power permitted several sleeping differences to wake.

It did not choose what would triumph.

It gave the plateau more ways to answer.

The third danger required another sort of care.

Far away, minds would one day see the tableland from above. They would measure the vapor, map the mineral concentrations, notice the regular exhalations of the vent fields, and argue over whether the towers had been built. Some would call the plateau untouched, meaning available. Some would call it a resource, meaning already taken in imagination. Others would wish to save it so perfectly that they would forbid even its inhabitants to alter it.

The power placed no storm around the escarpments. It did not erase the high country from instruments or cause travelers to lose their way. Concealment would protect it only until curiosity sharpened.

Instead, it laid a condition upon discovery.

Not a compulsion. Not a command. Minds forced into goodness are only instruments.

The condition was this: no one could learn enough to exploit the plateau without also learning enough to recognize what exploitation would destroy.

The useful mineral maps would reveal the transport of salts in living bodies. Models of the warm vents would expose the dependence of distant rains upon their breathing. Surveys of firm ground would show that firmness to be woven from generations of roots. Every profitable simplification would fail unless it included another relationship, and then another, until the cost of breaking them could no longer be dismissed as ignorance.

Those who came in greed could still choose greed.

But they would not be able to call the consequence unforeseen.

Those who came in reverence would be offered no easy innocence either. They would learn that observation changed routes, that harmless sampling opened chambers, that protection from predators could starve scavengers, that introduced mercy might become plague. They would have to approach not as owners and not as worshippers, but as participants burdened by the effects of participation.

This was all the power placed between the plateau and the future: not invulnerability, but the chance to be understood before it was judged empty.

Then it withdrew its influence, though not its attention.

Rain continued to pass through the ordinary cloud line.

A vent column sealed a failed chamber. Amphibious grazers entered dark lawns at the pulse of a shadow. A long-bodied predator waited with its jaw against wet wood. The old browser’s minerals departed in many directions. Its back garden became a mound, then several patches, then vanished as a distinguishable thing.

Seasons crossed the shelves. Falls moved backward by widths too small to see. Groves divided. Species failed. Others entered their places through alliance, parasitism, inheritance, and chance.

Nothing gave thanks.

No inhabitant knew it had been observed. No hidden doctrine traveled in the roots. No pale pulse beneath the moss spelled a name.

The wise power desired no gratitude from beings who had not asked for it. It did not need the high country to know that it was loved.

It was enough that, for a moment in the life of worlds, immense power had encountered immense complexity and had not mistaken power for permission.

Above the escarpments, cloud gathered.

The leaves turned silver.

Rain began on one slope and passed into the living ground.
