The power does not descend in fire.

Fire would be the wrong language here. It would consume roofs, terrify animals, call armed families from the hill forts, and leave behind a place of pilgrimage that someone would eventually claim, wall, tax, and defend.

Nor does it speak from the peaks. The peaks already have too many meanings. A voice there would become the property of whichever custodians interpreted it first.

It comes into the basin on foot.

It takes the appearance of an old traveler of no unmistakable people: wind-burned, poorly shod, carrying a small bundle that seems too light for a long journey. Its accent shifts slightly depending on who hears it. It knows enough market speech to ask for water but not enough to explain where it has come from.

This is deliberate.

The power could know the city from above. It could see each roof, each artery of meltwater, each animal breathing in a lower room. It could count the barley grains in sealed jars and the cracks opening behind plaster. It could look backward through every rebuilding and forward into every avalanche.

But knowledge without weight is not yet wisdom.

So it walks.

It climbs until its borrowed lungs hurt. It lets sleet collect in its collar. It sleeps badly among coughing strangers. It waits while a disputed flock blocks a stair. It is struck in the shin by a child’s gutter boat and apologizes to the child. It drinks sour milk from a bowl whose owner cannot afford the portion given. It helps lift a roofstone and feels the rawhide strap cut into its shoulder.

It asks stupid questions.

Why not bring more wood?

Why not dig a deeper well?

Why not command the river to remain in one channel?

Why do the people above rebuild this wall first when the people below will benefit more?

The answers are patient at first, then mocking, then—when the traveler continues listening—serious.

It learns the difference between poverty and precision.

It learns that many things which appear inefficient are protections against a single household becoming indispensable. It learns that a straight road can deliver raiders as easily as grain, that a great reservoir creates a throne beside it, that one abundant pasture may destroy ten sparse ones by drawing too many animals to the same slope. It learns that hardship has not made the people virtuous, and that cooperation does not make them gentle. The basin contains thieves, tyrants, hoarders, abusers, liars, and people who enjoy another’s need. Yet even cruelty is constrained by the knowledge that the cruel may require a neighbor’s ladder before winter ends.

The power visits every kind of house.

In a high ward, it meets a widow whose roof bows under old repairs. She possesses three excellent water turns and dozens of relatives who resent her. Her sons died on different roads. Her daughters married into districts with which she is quarreling. Each night she wakes when frost enters the wall because she knows the sound of mortar loosening.

On the river terrace, the power sits beside a tanner whose hands are permanently inflamed. His work is necessary, despised, and slowly killing him.

It walks with a channel keeper before dawn. Together they remove ice, dead mice, willow leaves, and three stones placed after midnight by someone hoping to steal a little flow.

It watches a healer choose which frostbitten toes to cut away with a copper blade already sharpened almost to nothing.

It enters a metalworker’s room and discovers that reverence and smoke can occupy the same air until neither is breathable.

It accompanies shepherds above the burial fields. There it sees a child step onto a slab hidden beneath snow. The stone shifts. The power could hold it still.

It does not.

The shepherd beside the child catches her by the hood, and afterward she is taught how snow lies differently over hollows. The lesson will save her twenty years later, when no miracle is near.

This, too, is deliberate.

A benevolent power can easily become a catastrophe if it makes caution foolish.

For a full turning of the seasons it does nothing that cannot be mistaken for ordinary help. It carries dung. It patches felt. It adds its shoulder to a sledge. It gives away its place near a warm wall. It listens to songs whose oldest verses describe roads now buried under rockfall. In winter it learns how long a room can remain silent after someone has eaten the last of their private food.

Only then does it decide what mercy should mean.

The city does not chiefly lack water, fuel, food, warmth, justice, knowledge, or peace.

It lacks margin.

Every system is drawn tight. One late snow becomes hunger. One careless ember becomes a ward burning uphill. One dead elder becomes a boundary war. One sick mason becomes a collapsed terrace. One stolen water turn becomes a feud inherited by children. Life survives, but often without enough room to forgive error.

The power will not make the basin lush. A lush basin would fill, divide, arm itself, and starve on a larger scale. It will not flatten the mountains or thicken the air. It will not abolish winter, because winter governs parasites, water, pasture, migration, storage, and peace between districts that might otherwise campaign against one another all year.

It will give the basin a little room.

No more than that.

But everywhere.

---

Its first work is beneath the ground.

Deep below the basin, where stone is under pressures no mason has imagined, the power touches three faults. It does not open them dramatically. There is no earthquake, no fountain, no river bursting from a holy place.

It closes one fissure by the width of a fingernail and opens another by the width of a hair.

Over the next seven years, small springs strengthen throughout the basin.

Not one great spring. That would make a dynasty.

There are dozens: a seep beneath a widow’s wall, clear drops in an abandoned cistern, dampness under a burial path, a trickle where cattle have worn a hollow beside stone. None is sufficient to command a district. Together they shorten the worst journeys and allow old channels to rest.

The water appears gradually enough that people must notice, test, name, negotiate, and build around it. Some flows are lost through poor work. Some are stolen. Two become causes of violence. The power does not prevent this, though it grieves.

But most become common sources because no single claimant can prove an ancestral right.

The oldest argument in the city—*Who had it first?*—has no answer.

So the people must invent another question:

*Who needs it now?*

They do not always answer well. Yet the question remains.

---

The second work is in smoke.

The power has watched infants cough through winter and women wake with headaches beside badly vented hearths. It has watched fuel vanish into heat that warms only rafters and soot. It knows that merely giving more fuel would strip the outer slopes and swell the herds beyond pasture.

So, in six districts on the same cold morning, six unrelated people wake with variations of the same idea.

One is a potter. One is a child. One is a mason unable to work after breaking two fingers. One is an elderly cook known for burning porridge. One tends temple ovens. One is a debt servant whose name is omitted from later accounts.

The idea is simple enough to build with local materials: a small enclosed hearth whose passage turns twice through stone and clay before reaching the vent. The fuel burns hotter. The room catches more warmth. Smoke leaves more completely. A flat surface above the passage heats a pot with half the dung.

The first versions crack.

One fills a room with smoke and nearly kills a household. Another is judged ritually unclean. A third becomes fashionable among merchants, who decorate it so heavily that it performs worse than an open fire.

But the idea is useful, and usefulness is stubborn.

Within thirty years, children no longer recognize the particular cough that once accompanied every winter gathering.

No song attributes this to a god.

The power prefers it so.

---

Its third work concerns memory.

It does not give the city writing—not whole and sudden, not as a revelation with a sacred alphabet. Writing can preserve, but it can also detach law from the living, let the powerful manufacture old claims, and make one trained class the gatekeeper of everyone else’s past.

Instead, the power sharpens what is already there.

Knotted cords, notched sticks, marked bones, wall signs, and stones in niches begin to borrow from one another. Through trade, marriage, imitation, and argument, a common set of marks emerges for certain practical things:

water received;

water owed;

labor promised;

labor completed;

grain lent;

animals lost;

a wall judged unsafe;

a path closed by snow;

testimony given by someone now dead.

The marks do not replace recitation. They brace it.

A record must still be spoken aloud before neighbors, but now a persuasive liar must contend with an object that several people can read. The old remain valuable; their deaths become less ruinous. Children who are poor at memorizing long landmark songs discover that they are good at keeping tallies. Those born deaf gain a larger place in councils. Agreements begin to outlive the loudest person in the room.

Fraud develops almost immediately.

So does the practice of keeping copies in separate courts.

The power expected both.

---

Its fourth work is given to walls.

The power does not prevent rockfall. Mountains must fall; that is one of the ways mountains exist.

Instead, it teaches the city to hear a little earlier.

There already are masons who know that stone speaks before collapse: a new powdering beneath a joint, a belly in a wall face, a gutter that no longer drains true, a roof beam lifting when it should settle. The power gathers these scattered observations into custom.

Again it uses no proclamation.

A mason in the red-wall district begins pressing thin slips of pale clay across dangerous cracks. If the clay breaks, movement has continued. A child adds lines to show how far. A channel keeper dates them by water turns. Shepherds adapt the method to burial chambers. Potters produce clay tabs in quantity. Temple courts begin requiring them on disputed retaining walls.

Soon every district has watchers after thaw.

Walls still fall. People still die beneath them. But fewer do.

More importantly, a visible crack marker changes blame. A wealthy household can no longer say that no one could have known. Neglect acquires a witness.

This is the closest the power comes to inventing a new form of justice.

---

The fifth work is not a teaching.

It is a promise, and the city never learns its full extent.

When someone is lost in a storm within one night’s walk of shelter, the wind will sometimes carry a sound toward them: a goat’s clapper, a child crying, a hammer striking stone, a familiar voice speaking their name.

Not always.

The power refuses to make the mountains safe. Travelers who set out foolishly must still endanger themselves, and no one is allowed to plan a journey around divine rescue.

But when the lost have done what care could do—when the storm has changed without warning, when a cairn has fallen, when an old route has vanished under new snow—then the sound may come.

Those who follow it find a wall hollow, a burial chamber, a shepherd’s shelter, an occupied house, or simply the lee side of a boulder where morning is survivable.

People explain these rescues in many ways.

The dead called them.

Ravens guided them.

The storm became confused.

Their mothers’ voices traveled through stone.

No explanation is entirely wrong.

---

The sixth work is for those who cannot be saved.

Here the power acts without caution.

It does not stop death. To do so would destroy the basin more certainly than drought. Nor does it decide whose life has had enough length. Wisdom gives no clean answer to that question.

But it will not allow anyone to die believing they have become wholly separate.

At the last, when cold narrows the world, when fever loosens names, when breath can no longer draw enough from the thin air, the dying feel—not as doctrine, but as physical certainty—the weight they have borne and the weight that has borne them.

The shepherd feels the remembered warmth of animals around his sleeping place.

The mason feels every wall in which one of her stones remains.

The cruel man feels, without excuse, each life made harder by his choices, but also the hands that fed him when they could have refused.

The infant feels the rhythm of a body carrying it.

The abandoned feel footsteps approaching, even if no one reaches the room in time.

For a moment, each knows the city as the power knows it: not a heap of separate lives, but a structure of burdens passed from one bearer to another.

No one dies outside it.

Not even the exiled.

Not even the forgotten.

---

After these works, the power prepares to leave.

By then its borrowed coat has been patched in four styles. Its boots have been resoled twice. It owes labor to an oven court, is involved in a minor dispute about a goat, and has been adopted jokingly—and then not jokingly—by a household whose exact relationship to one another it never fully understands.

On the morning of departure, a child asks whether it will return.

The power could answer truthfully in a thousand ways.

Instead it says, “If the red wall falls, I still owe three days.”

The child considers this.

“Four,” she says. “You ate with us during the hail.”

“Four, then.”

The power takes the eastern road.

At the final cairn it turns and looks back. Smoke lies blue above the basin. New springs shine in places that were dry. Thin lines of water enter old stone channels. On a roof, people are dismantling a smoking chimney and arguing about how to rebuild it. In a courtyard, clay crack-markers dry beside bowls. Two elders dictate an agreement while three children make copies and disagree over a sign.

Nothing is perfected.

One new spring has been seized by armed men. A more efficient hearth has allowed a rich household to heat an unnecessary guest chamber while its servants sleep cold. Records have strengthened honest claims and dishonest debts alike. A district warned of a failing wall has postponed repair because no one agrees who should provide labor.

Wisdom did not expect innocence.

Benevolence did not require it.

The power looks deeper.

It sees that the seized spring will undermine the foundation of the fort built around it unless its keepers allow an overflow channel through the lower ward. It sees a servant learn the hearth’s construction and carry it eventually to her birth village. It sees false records exposed because three copies differ. It sees children, not yet born, inherit the argument over the dangerous wall and finally resolve it by building a stair that distributes the pressure.

The city remains itself.

This is essential.

Its people still wake before dawn to break ice. They still steal, share, bargain, sing, hoard, foster, mend, accuse, forgive, and remember. Snow still kills lambs. Infection still follows careless cuts. Rock still falls. No hand, however benevolent, can remove all pain without also removing the freedom by which one creature bears another’s weight.

So the power does not hold the city forever.

It does something smaller and, perhaps, more loving.

It places one unseen stone beneath the lowest course.

Not enough to make collapse impossible.

Enough that, when every visible support has taken all the weight it can bear, there is still the width of a finger before failure.

A little margin.

A final chance to hear the crack.

Time to wake the household.

Time to lift the child.

Time to open the door into the neighboring house.

Then the power goes on through the pass, leaving no shrine behind.

Years later, some say an old traveler once crossed the basin and taught the people how to build warm hearths. Others say the hearths were invented locally and only fools attribute good work to wanderers. The strengthened springs are called a blessing, a geological change, an ancestral repayment, or evidence that water has its own intentions.

No agreement is reached.

But in the coldest part of winter, when the channels tighten with ice and stone shifts softly in the dark, a person waking in fear will sometimes place a hand against the wall.

For just an instant, beneath the frost, they feel another hand bearing from the other side.
