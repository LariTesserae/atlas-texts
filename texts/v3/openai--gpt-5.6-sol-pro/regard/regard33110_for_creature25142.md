### The First Courtesy

The power did nothing for a year.

This was the first sign of its wisdom.

It did not split the eastern wall, though it could have parted the black stone as easily as wet cloth. It did not summon a lake into the cisterns. It did not cool the cavern, still the fracture, straighten the streets, enlarge the houses, or turn the mineral columns into something whose nature could be settled by a committee.

Any of these would have been understood as disaster.

Instead, the power attended.

It learned the city by its smallest obligations. It followed every drop from roofstone to tongue, from tongue to sweat, from sweat to cloth, from cloth to evaporation and return. It learned which bridges were trusted and which were merely used. It learned that a particular western pipe had been painted blue seven times though no living person remembered why. It learned the names of the dead inside the stair lips. It learned the difference between a privacy tremor combed through whisper grass and the vibration of a bearing beginning to fail.

It listened to the arguments between gardeners and engineers and understood that most were forms of collaboration.

It listened beneath those arguments too.

The people wished for water, certainly. They wished for coolness. They wished for headroom, silence, fruit, reliable seals, healthy children, living lizards, and one week in which nothing inherited required repair.

But beneath all these wishes was another, rarely spoken because there was no practical use in speaking it.

They wished for room in which a mistake would not immediately become someone else’s burden.

The power considered this.

Then it waited through the High Leakage.

Rain crossed the desert unseen. The fault gathered it. Cold threads descended into the city, and children shrieked beneath them while adults issued warnings no one obeyed. Turbines accelerated. Root mats darkened. The threadfish attendants found a twenty-first ripple in the covered pool and declined to revise the public number until morning.

The power watched how the Condensarians received abundance.

They opened doors. They set out bowls. They checked braces. They drank too fast. They laughed too loudly. They shared, quarreled, measured, spilled, apologized, and began planning for the water’s absence before it had ceased falling.

This satisfied the power, though satisfaction was not quite what such a being felt.

When the rain had passed, it placed one cold drop on the eastern wall.

Not in darkness, where it might become rumor.

It placed the drop directly upon the annual measuring wire while Surveyor Salin-of-the-Pale-Sleeve was recording the distance between the forty-third and forty-fourth silver pins.

The drop struck the wire and divided.

Half ran east, toward the seam.

Half ran west, toward the city.

Salin watched both halves until they disappeared.

Then she dried the wire with her private cloth, sealed the cloth in a sample jar, and repeated the measurement.

The next drop fell thirteen minutes later.

The one after that took twenty-one.

Then thirteen.

Then eight.

Then thirteen again.

Salin remained at the wall for six hours. By the end, three engineers, two gardeners, a root nurse, a cistern clerk, and seven children had gathered behind her. No one spoke. The children were unusually still.

The drops were not regular. Regularity would have meant a leak.

They came in groups.

A market tender eventually recognized the pattern. It was an old sequence of factory signs translated into intervals: two short waits, one long, three short, a pause. In the hand language, the sequence meant:

**Pressure held. Permission requested.**

No one agreed that this was what it meant.

By evening, everyone knew.

The municipal councils met in nine borrowed rooms because there was no chamber large enough for them all. Engineers proposed hidden machinery. Gardeners proposed an unknown signaling mold. Surveyors insisted that neither explanation affected the necessity of collecting more data. The root nurses said little, which caused others to suspect they knew something.

They did not.

For forty days the drops continued.

They never yielded enough to fill a cup. Their intervals changed only in response to activity at the wall. When a listening bowl was set against the stone, the drops slowed. When the bowl was removed, they resumed. When children pressed both palms to the seam, three drops came quickly. When a property assessor read aloud a preliminary claim to the chamber beyond, nothing fell for two days.

This last event entered public debate as evidence of intelligence, geological instability, coincidence, divine judgment, administrative interference, and the poor moral character of property assessors.

The Condensarians had no accepted ritual for replying to a wall.

They invented one reluctantly.

A shallow basin was placed below the seam, but it was not connected to any private gutter. Into it every household poured one drop of water. Most used column water or ordinary warm cistern water. A few contributed High Leakage water hoarded in sealed ampoules. One elderly listener, too poor in water to spare any without missing a meal, spat into the basin and dared anyone to object.

No one did.

The basin filled by less than two fingers.

The water was carried to the eastern face in silence. There, in the presence of surveyors, gardeners, engineers, root nurses, clerks, children, and representatives of those households too infirm or too angry to attend, it was poured into the root mat beneath the measuring wires.

It was a ceremonial waste. No pipe reclaimed it.

Salin put her hand against the stone.

With her other hand, she made the simplest reply used between workers on opposite sides of a loud machine:

**Proceed slowly. We are attending.**

The eastern wall cooled beneath her palm.

Not much.

The difference was less than that between shaded copper and copper recently touched. Yet everyone nearest the seam felt it, and everyone farther away saw the moisture-loving plants turn their silver undersides toward the stone.

Deep below, the translucent columns altered their pressure for the first time in recorded history.

Every municipal gauge descended to zero.

No pipe burst. No cistern drained. No pump stopped moving. The needles simply rested there for the length of one breath, as if the entire city had been relieved of weight.

Then they rose again.

The power began its work.

It did not open the wall.

First, it strengthened what an opening might endanger.

Mineral deposits formed around seven concealed fractures in the lower galleries, not sealing them completely but outlining them in pale ridges so they could be found. The largest lay behind a household shrine and an obsolete pressure vessel. When workers uncovered it, they discovered that another year of ordinary expansion would have cracked a main support.

A hot-pipe lizard appeared in the empty niche of the factory above it. Its skin had darkened almost to black.

The factory evacuated. The fracture was repaired. The lizard survived and became intolerably famous.

Next, condensation began forming inside certain municipal plans. Not enough to damage the pages. Thin wet lines appeared only where old pipe routes had been drawn incorrectly or incompletely. Archivists copied the corrections onto waxed sheets and sent crews to investigate.

Every line proved accurate.

Behind one sealed room of the Deep Past, workers found a forgotten bypass channel large enough to relieve the eastern district if the new chamber changed the airflow. Behind another they found nothing useful at all: only six child-sized clay figures arranged around a broken cup.

The figures were returned to their places.

The power made no comment on them.

The root nurses began dreaming.

They dreamed not of impossible rooms but of the columns beneath the city. Each nurse saw a different chamber: membranes, channels, branching mineral threads, slow valves that were not valves. When they compared sketches, the images joined into a single anatomy.

The columns were alive, though not as gardeners had claimed.

They were geological, though not as engineers had claimed.

They were neither organisms nor intentions. They were a conversation between heat, water, stone, and time—a conversation that had learned to remember its previous shapes. The city’s pipes had entered that conversation. So had the cultivated roots. So, in smaller ways, had the people who drank the altered water and returned it through breath and sweat.

The Condensary had not connected infrastructure to a form of life.

It had become part of one.

This conclusion was rejected by the municipal council as needlessly provocative. A revised version was approved stating that the columns and city constituted “a coupled pressure ecology with persistent adaptive characteristics.”

The gardeners celebrated.

The engineers claimed the phrase as their own.

The power, having learned local courtesy, allowed both.

For nine years the eastern seam widened.

At first the change remained measurable only by wire. Then a black hairline appeared between the forty-third and forty-fourth pins. Fanleaf was planted around it. Red bladder vine followed. A ceramic lip was fitted beneath the first dependable seep.

The water was cold.

Not dangerously cold, not High Leakage cold, but cool enough that people came from every district to touch the gutter carrying it. Consumption was forbidden until mineral testing could be completed. Consumption occurred immediately. No one became ill.

The seep increased by one drop each day.

Never two.

One.

The city could count it. It could plan. Every increase arrived already witnessed.

Behind the seam, the power eased pressure grain by grain. It guided the fracture around load-bearing strata and away from the deep heat. It did not command the stone so much as persuade each crystal that a slightly different resting place would be no worse.

This was slow work even for a power without limits, because benevolence was not the same as impatience.

In the twelfth year, a gap opened wide enough for a thread.

A ceremonial thread, dyed bright red, was passed through. Air moved around it from the far side—cool, mineral, faintly scented with something no gardener recognized.

The thread returned wet.

In the thirteenth year, the gap admitted a measuring blade.

In the fourteenth, a lamp filament.

In the fifteenth, an eye.

The first person chosen to look through was not a child, despite intense lobbying. Nor was it a surveyor, engineer, gardener, root nurse, clerk, household elder, or property assessor.

It was a bridge inspector named Mer, whose work records showed thirty-eight years without a missed brace, falsified entry, or unresolved obligation. Mer possessed no claim near the eastern wall and had publicly stated that the new chamber would probably be troublesome.

This was considered an excellent temperament.

Mer placed one eye to the opening.

For a long while, she said nothing.

“What is there?” someone finally murmured.

“Space,” she said.

This answer irritated everyone.

They asked how much space, what kind of space, whether it contained water, columns, dangerous footing, useful stone, plant life, poison, heat, shafts, ledges, or evidence of prior habitation.

Mer looked again.

“There is room,” she amended, “for all those questions.”

The chamber beyond was larger than the Condensary, though not enormously so. It was cooler. Its floor sloped gently toward a natural basin. Mineral columns stood along its southern edge, smaller than those below the city and clear as wet glass. Pale growth covered the roof in delicate fans. Water descended one drop at a time from hundreds of points and vanished into porous stone.

There was no lake.

There was no treasure.

There were no straight streets waiting to be occupied.

There was, however, an emptiness no living Condensarian had ever seen.

The first opening large enough for a body was completed in the eighteenth year. By then it had a double pressure door, test shutters, filter curtains, emergency seals, three independent measuring systems, a public gutter, and a pruning roster for plants not yet introduced.

Mer went through first.

A child went second after slipping past two attendants.

Others followed in designated groups. They carried sounding rods, collection jars, masks, chalk, tools, seedlings, legal forms, food, spare cloth, and far too many plans.

They found one thing the power had made directly.

In the center of the chamber stood a shallow depression in the stone, circular and smooth. It had no apparent function. It collected no water. It supported no column. No useful mineral grew along its rim. It was too large to be a basin and too shallow for storage. The floor around it was level, open, and clear.

Condensarians studied it for months.

Engineers proposed a flywheel.

Gardeners proposed a grove.

Cistern clerks proposed a covered reservoir.

Households proposed housing.

Children proposed that everyone leave it alone.

This was dismissed as impractical.

Yet every plan encountered some mild difficulty. Foundation marks disappeared overnight. Seedlings planted there bent toward the edges. Water poured into the depression drained without trace and reappeared in public gutters belonging to no claimant. Tools left in the circle were found the next morning neatly arranged outside it.

The power did not forbid construction.

It merely declined to help it remain.

At last the city classified the depression as infrastructure whose function had not yet been determined.

This protected it better than any sacred law could have done.

People began visiting.

At first they came to inspect. Then they came to sit. Children discovered that they could run around the circle without ducking, climbing, squeezing sideways, or being warned about hot railings. Their footsteps echoed from only one direction at a time.

Adults found this echo unsettling.

Later, they found it beautiful.

There were arguments about whether shouting should be permitted. The eventual rule allowed it during two hours after shift change and during the High Leakage, if leakage could be heard in the new chamber.

On the first such day, children stood in the empty circle and cried out simply to hear their voices travel outward and return altered by distance.

No machinery failed.

No wall cracked.

No water was lost.

Their noise had room to become only noise.

The power remained.

It did not rule. It did not ask for worship or name itself. When thanked, it sometimes answered with a cool drop in an unlikely place. When petitioned, it was as likely to reveal an overlooked obligation as to grant relief. It refused all requests to make the cisterns bottomless. It refused to abolish heat, labor, death, disagreement, maintenance, or consequence.

But it gave warning before sudden danger.

It placed lizards where they would be noticed.

It taught the columns new, gentler pressures.

It encouraged rain far above by restoring, one hardy root at a time, a belt of desert plants along the fault. The change took decades. The High Leakage became slightly less rare, never fully predictable.

It made no one immortal.

It did, on occasion, ensure that a dry cloth remained dry until it was needed.

The Condensarians came to call it the Courtesy, though theologians objected that this was a behavior rather than a name. Engineers maintained that most of its works could be explained through coupled pressure ecology. Gardeners replied that this was no reason not to be grateful. Children drew it as a white lizard carrying a cup.

The oldest root nurses said the question of what it was had been poorly formed.

In time, buildings entered the new chamber. Bridges crossed its edges. Gardens descended from its roof. The natural basin became a cistern, then a public swimming pool, then a cistern again after the swimmers contaminated it. Addresses acquired new heights, pipe colors, and living landmarks.

The city expanded.

It did not become simple.

But the open circle remained at the chamber’s center, performing no function anyone could prove.

There, reconciliations were held. Surveyors rested their hands after measurements. Children ran. Elders sat without pruning. Couples sometimes exchanged dry cloths instead of keys and promised, not only to answer one another’s leaks, but to leave one another a little room.

At the eastern edge of the new chamber another black seam eventually appeared.

Silver pins were driven into it.

Fine wire was stretched between them.

The distance grew each year by the width of a leaf vein.

Beyond it, instruments detected colder air, mineral moisture, and a hollow return when the stone was tapped.

No one proposed blasting through.

They placed fanleaf near the seam. They fitted a ceramic lip below the first darkening line. They put their hands against the stone and waited—not for rescue, nor for the end of heat, nor for a world without obligation.

They attended.

And from somewhere on the other side, with exquisite patience, something attended in return.
