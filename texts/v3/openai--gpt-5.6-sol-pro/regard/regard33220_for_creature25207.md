The power did not descend in fire.

Fire would have been wasteful there, and loud.

It did not raise the plateau higher, though this was within its ability. It did not bind the cliffs with unbreakable roots, still the frost, refill the buried seas, or command the old black stone to cease becoming dust. Such gifts would have been indistinguishable from disaster. A country that had arranged every affection around change could be crushed by permanence as surely as by collapse.

Instead, the power remained at the edge for a long while.

It learned the routes of the disciplined storms. It entered no cistern before passing through living roots. It watched the weight-lamps and stood where an uncounted person would not burden a floor. When machines slept, it did not wake them. When they moved, it kept its hands clear.

For nine years it said nothing.

This was not hesitation. It was courtesy.

It followed water from shoulder-cloths into kettles, from kettles into bodies, from bodies into breath, and from breath back into collars and channels. It read the elevation lines embroidered on old coats. It attended dismantling feasts. It lay beneath flexible concert floors and felt the music pass through the ribs of the city. It learned the lamp-names of children and the pressure-names of those whose spoken names had not been used in decades.

It went below.

There, where official maps ended in pale hatching, it found the Residuals.

Some were people.

Some were machines.

Some had become difficult to place in either category. Brass fingers had been fitted to human wrists; human habits had entered governors and relay banks; memories traveled in warm rails without belonging to a single mind. In certain stations, generations had lived and died without once seeing sky. In others, no living body remained, yet gardens were pruned with tenderness and little tables were set when old pressure patterns announced the hour of a meal.

The power did not correct them.

It did not tell the machines they were merely repetitions, nor the people that they had become something less than people. It knew that care performed long enough is not made unreal by uncertainty concerning the caretaker.

It went deeper.

Below the first streets, below foundations built from older foundations, below chambers where the stone retained the warmth of vanished engines, it found the maker of the threefold tracks.

The old gardeners had been nearest to the truth.

It had lived on the plateau before there were walls, though “on” was not quite the word. Its body was thin and wide and passed through the upper layers of stone as a ray passes through water. What surfaced in moss gardens was no foot, but the pressure of one of its three great internal spars flexing near the air.

It did not eat flesh or root or metal. It fed upon strain.

For ages beyond civic record it had moved through the plateau’s fractures, taking into itself the sudden violence of cracking rock and releasing that force gradually as warmth. Before the cities, it had softened earthquakes. After the cities, without understanding them, it had learned their weight. It lingered beneath reservoirs. It braced crowded courts. It turned away from foundations when people opened roof doors and set bowls of water along its path—not because it drank, but because it had come to recognize the arrangement as a greeting.

It was very old.

It was also dying.

The plateau had once been deep enough for it to move freely. Now erosion had thinned the country. Its three spars scraped nearer the surface each year. The effort of distributing strain through broken stone had become greater than the nourishment strain provided. In another few centuries it would lose coherence. When it did, not all at once but basin by basin, the plateau would begin to break in ways no Ministry forecast contained.

The creature knew none of this as words. It knew only increasing cold, narrowing paths, and the bewildering proliferation of small warm lives above it. It had no idea that those lives had seen its marks. It did not know that they left doors open for it.

The benevolent power rested beside it in the dark.

To assume a form the creature could understand, the power became pressure: not crushing pressure, but the deep, even embrace of stone before stone is fractured. It gave no command. It offered a question in three long pulses.

**Do you wish to remain?**

The answer took eleven months.

It traveled through the creature’s whole breadth, from the western cliffs to the old surfaces beyond the eastern basins. Moss lifted. Cistern crabs emerged from walls in districts that had been dry for generations. Bells swung where there was no wind. In the buried city, every warm rail cooled and warmed again.

At last the answer returned.

It was not yes.

It was not no.

The nearest translation would have been:

**I wish not to drop them.**

Then the power understood what gift could be given.

Not rescue.

Relief.

It touched neither the cliffs nor the weather. Instead, far below the plateau, deep beneath the countries that received its lost rain, it altered the inclination of a buried seam of stone by less than the width of a moth’s wing.

No one felt it.

Over the following years, mineral water began to rise along faults thought dead. It did not gush. It climbed slowly, molecule by molecule, entering the lowest body of the ancient creature. The water bore dissolved metals and a gentle geothermal warmth. Where it passed, the creature’s thinning spars grew dense again—not larger, not stronger without limit, but restored enough to endure its labor.

The gift did not make the plateau immortal.

It gave the old being time and, for the first time, choice.

Then the power went to the Ministry of Subsidence.

It did not appear as a radiant figure. Radiance would have ruined too many eyes accustomed to fog. It came as a clerk no one remembered hiring, in a coat the color of wet slate. Its fingertips were white with chalk. Its lamp-name was three intervals of darkness separated by green.

For one season it worked at the lowest table in the western survey room.

It corrected no existing forecast. Instead, it drew a new layer beneath them.

The map showed lines of strain no instrument had measured: the long arcs of the creature’s body, the chambers where the Residuals lived, the warm rails that carried memory, the buried gardens, the automata still attempting duties whose purposes had been forgotten. It showed where the plateau could bear weight and where weight caused pain. It marked several neighborhoods whose apparent stability depended entirely upon the old being’s exhausted attention.

In the legend it wrote:

**THIS IS NOT FOUNDATION.**

**THIS IS SOMEONE.**

The ministers studied the map in silence.

At first they objected to the handwriting, the lack of supporting calculations, and the improper merging of geological, civic, hydraulic, and biographical records. Then the blue-black fatigue lichen began to grow along the lines exactly as drawn.

A party of listeners was sent below.

The power went with them but did not lead.

At the lowest station they found a gathering already waiting: salvage families, wax-clad gardeners, brass maintenance figures, and several persons for whom the language of “person” was evidently too small. One had a porcelain face with a living eye behind it. Another wore six coats from six different eras. A little wheeled mechanism carried cups of warm white-leaf broth and offered them according to a hospitality program at least eight hundred years old.

No one knew the correct signs for greeting.

So they stood without speaking.

Then the youngest surface listener placed one hand against a warm rail.

Something answered through her bones.

Not language. Not yet.

A posture.

The posture contained a whole district below: its leaks, hungers, sleeping places, missing stairs, treasured gardens, failed lamps, and the names of those who had not risen because they believed the upper city no longer wanted them.

The listener began to weep.

This embarrassed everyone until the little machine brought a cloth.

Negotiations lasted forty-three years.

They were considered unusually swift.

New signal standards were made, including colors visible to eyes adapted to amber darkness. Abandoned pressure-names were restored. Shafts were opened, though not all the way to daylight. The lower inhabitants did not wish to be brought upward in a grand rescue. Many found open horizons intolerable. Instead, exchange stations were built at intermediate depths, with filtered gardens, dim ceilings, and walls designed to carry tactile speech.

Surface children went below to learn old pipe systems.

Lower children came up to touch fog.

Machines were given civic standing where they could demonstrate preference, refusal, or grief. This caused a century of legal difficulty and several excellent plays. Mechanisms that wished only to continue their duties were allowed to do so. Others chose new ones. A maintenance carriage that had delivered heat to a demolished bathhouse for six hundred years became the first public advocate for purposeless motion, though it moved only nine minutes each day.

The creature beneath the plateau was not announced all at once.

The gardeners objected to turning an ancient neighbor into public information. The Ministry objected to secrecy. Engineers demanded measurements. Children had already named it.

Eventually it received no single name.

Instead, each basin assigned it a lamp-name, and the buried communities gave it pressure-names that could be felt through miles of pipe. In formal records it was described as:

**THE DEEP PARTICIPANT IN LOAD DISTRIBUTION, PRIOR RESIDENT, STATUS LIVING.**

The old custom of placing water along the wall-tracks continued, though everyone now knew it did not drink. Customs need not be factually necessary to remain courteous.

New building codes prohibited driving foundation pins into its known paths. Reservoir migrations were coordinated with its seasons of rest. Once every seven years, the entire city reduced its stored load as far as practical. Warehouses emptied. statues were unjointed. public tanks were drawn low. People carried heavy possessions out onto the old surfaces and spread them across the bedrock.

For one night, the plateau became lighter.

The being below rose close to the surface.

Three long impressions appeared across streets, roofs, gardens, and walls. They crossed the Ministry itself. Wherever they passed, people placed their palms inside them and felt a slow warmth moving beneath the stone.

The first time this occurred, a pressure message traveled from the western edge to the eastern cliffs.

The deep inhabitants had helped compose it. Surface listeners adjusted the valves. Children kept count of the pauses. Old automata opened mains sealed since their makers’ deaths.

The message said:

**WE KNOW YOU ARE HOLDING US.**

After a long interval, the stone replied.

Windows clouded from within. Every weight-lamp on the plateau turned green. Cistern water formed three perfect rings, though nothing touched it.

The reply had no words, but all who felt it understood:

**I KNOW YOU ARE THERE.**

As for the benevolent power, it did not stay to be thanked.

There was no temple built to it, because no one could agree that it had been a god. Some claimed the unknown clerk was a ministry fiction invented to justify overdue contact with the lower city. Others argued that the mineral spring was ordinary tectonic behavior and that the new map had been assembled from suppressed surveys. The gardeners smiled at these disputes and said certainty was another kind of heavy object that ought not remain too long in one place.

Years later, while revising the elevation vaults, a clerk found a glass capsule on an unused shelf.

Inside were no teeth.

There was only a small piece of black stone with three pale lines across it. The capsule was labeled with a height greater than the plateau had ever possessed.

Beneath the figure, in white chalk, someone had written:

**This country has as long as it has.**

And below that:

**Do not mistake an ending for a failure.**

The High Drowned Steps continued to descend.

Cliffs rounded and fell. Streets were lowered. Rooms passed beyond the years in which they could safely be loved. Nothing was made permanent. Nothing was spared every grief.

But from then onward, the people’s maps showed not only when each part of the country would be lost.

They also showed who was carrying it meanwhile.

And in the deepest dark, relieved of a labor it no longer bore alone, the oldest inhabitant dreamed for the first time—not of holding the plateau up forever, but of moving with it, patiently, grain by grain, toward whatever waited below.
