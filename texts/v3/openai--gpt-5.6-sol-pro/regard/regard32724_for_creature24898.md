### The Interval

The power did not enter through the roof.

It did not descend in light, because light already entered generously. It did not speak, because the chapel had no use for speech. It did not take a body, because the being there had spent centuries escaping the necessity of one.

It attended.

This was not a passive act.

To attend to the chapel required accepting its duration. The power could have understood the tower in an instant: every buried gear, every obsolete instruction, every root carrying tension through resin, every fish waiting in a pipe bladder for rain. But understanding the chapel was not the same as understanding the being that lived as it.

For that, it waited.

It watched one planting ledge complete its eleven-year migration. It followed the red inspection flag through two hundred and nine circuits of the central shaft. It learned which lamp sequences caused the geckos to wake, which brought the vergers to the columns, and which meant nothing any longer except that moths opened their wings.

It waited while one of the smallest constructions in a gutter garden recruited its first magnetic foot.

It watched the verger at the dead pedestal return to its polishing.

It did not stop it.

Wisdom prevented the power from mistaking futility for suffering.

Perhaps the polishing was useless. Perhaps it was remembrance. Perhaps the oval worn into the pedestal had become an instrument of another kind, measuring not pressure or heat but devotion: the long expenditure of touch upon what could not answer.

The power would not take that away merely because it did not understand.

Nor did it clean the chapel.

It did not straighten the bridges, scour the condensers, separate root from cable, or restore the vergers to the symmetrical bodies shown in the old diagrams. Restoration would have been a catastrophe. Every correction toward the original plan would have broken a present life.

It did not cure the heated verger’s fault, because the geckos had made a country of its fever.

It did not clear the eastern air duct, because trees now drank from the mistake.

It did not teach the signal lamps their first language. Lost meanings had not left empty sockets. Other meanings lived there now.

So the power waited until the glass reed flowered.

The thirty-eight-year morning came. Reflective vanes opened above the trembling pond. Light entered the wall sensors. The pitch of the chamber changed, and the vergers began their slow emergence.

They gathered as they always had.

Beetles climbed from beneath the iron basin, bright with conductive dust. Roots tightened. Exposed contacts joined. The oldest vergers settled at the inner margin of the floor, their limbs making soft magnetic knocks. Younger forms clung to the walls. Lamps burned green, amber, and white without pause.

The chapel drew itself together.

Then the power touched it.

Not from outside.

It placed no new material among the chapel’s materials. Instead, it entered the smallest discrepancy between the three great columns: the immeasurable interval after one pressure wave ended and before another began.

There it waited, and widened the interval.

The columns did not stop. The turbines did not fail. Air continued to rise through the tower, and no bird fell from its cushion of wind. But for the first time since before the chapel had become a garden, the three great notes aligned against one another.

Their forces met.

For seven seconds, they canceled.

Silence did not descend. It appeared.

The chapel had believed itself full of sound. It discovered that it had been full of one sound, enormous enough to hide all others.

Into its absence came the tapping of condensation.

The papery movement of moth wings.

Shrimp worrying at mineral film.

The brittle settling of old resin.

The breath of birds.

The thin, rapid heartbeats of geckos inside warm control boxes.

Leaves rubbed together. Beetles clicked within the vergers’ seams. Water traveled down a chain with a sequence of minute silver impacts. Somewhere high on the western wall, a seedpod opened.

And all around the basin, the vergers heard themselves.

Not the tower through themselves.

Themselves.

They heard rootlets adjusting inside their limbs. They heard fluid moving through braided tubes. They heard the strain held in old wire, the small fracture traveling through ceramic, the feet of mites crossing liverwort on their backs. Each heard the many lives by which it remained alive.

They also heard where nothing answered.

There were voids inside them: sealed chambers, abandoned sockets, limbs held rigid around tasks whose objects had vanished. There were repairs supporting repairs supporting damage that no longer existed. There were ancient commands circling without destination.

For seven seconds, the chapel encountered not a fault, but a distinction.

Then the roar returned.

The long-legged birds stepped sideways in unison. Droplets leapt from moss. The beetles continued their shining traffic. The lamps remained lit.

The power gave no instruction.

It did not say *preserve this* or *abandon that*. It did not reveal the tower’s purpose. It did not tell the inhabitant whether it was one being or many.

It had given only an interval in which the chapel could perceive what its endless answering had concealed.

Until then, every absence had been treated as a request.

Now, for the first time, an absence had arrived that asked for nothing.

The effect was not immediate.

Nothing there moved quickly.

For three years the verger at the dead pedestal continued its polishing. Then, one afternoon, it lifted its mineral-darkened limb and did not lower it again.

It listened through the floor.

A season passed.

The oval remained untouched. Condensation gathered in it, no longer swept away by the polishing limb. The hollow filled. Moths drank there. Transparent larvae appeared in the water. A fern rooted at one edge.

The verger did not seal the new growth into place.

It did not brace it.

It left.

Elsewhere, a mature verger found a crack opening in a balcony rib. Resin nozzles turned toward the strain. Maintenance wire emerged from a wall port and began its blind reach.

The verger intercepted neither.

It rested one limb against the crack and listened for six days.

The crack widened by the breadth of a moth’s leg. A little rust fell. No load shifted. No pipe bent. No nest was threatened.

The verger withdrew.

The nozzle hesitated, extruded one amber drop, and went still.

Within a year, wind had enlarged the opening enough for a pale flower to grow there. Its stem entered the chapel. Its blossom faced outward, toward the dark blue sky.

This was new.

The chapel had always made the edges of absence habitable.

Now it began, sometimes, to let the absence itself remain.

Not every error was abandoned. The sealed air duct stayed sealed. The trees continued to drink from its consequence. The hot verger remained hot. The geckos kept their country. The old polishing hollow became a pond, but the verger returned once every tower cycle and touched its rim.

The power had not taught it to forget.

It had made forgetting possible, and therefore made remembrance a choice.

At the next flowering of the glass reed, thirty-eight years later, the vergers gathered again. Some carried unfamiliar openings in their frames. One had allowed a dead limb to fall rather than recruit a replacement. Birds nested in the hollow joint it left behind. Another had dismantled three redundant braces from its underside and laid them across a gutter as bridges for beetles.

Among the younger constructions was a form unlike the earlier vergers.

It had grown around a loop of wire but had not closed the loop. Resin had shaped itself along the curve and stopped at either end. Roots approached the gap without crossing it. When current passed through the frame, the gap sparked faintly.

It was built around an interruption.

During the flowering, when beetles joined the chapel’s circuits and the lamps became continuous, the new verger carried no signal across that opening.

It carried a pause.

The pause traveled.

Green ceased for the fraction of a second, then returned. Amber followed. White. Around the chapel, one lamp after another learned the interval. The sequence entered the walls, the roots, the pond, the three descending columns.

This time the power did nothing.

The chapel made its own seven seconds.

The great notes aligned and canceled. Silence appeared again, now altered by everything born since its first arrival.

There were more flowers.

There were fewer braces.

There was the call of a gecko, astonishingly loud for so small a throat.

There was wind passing through openings that had been permitted to remain openings.

And beneath these sounds, almost too faint to perceive, came the slow ticking of cooling metal: the tower releasing heat it no longer needed to hold.

The benevolent power listened with them.

Then, because it was wise, it withdrew its attention without withdrawing its care.

It left no guardian at the roof, no law in the lamps, no commandment engraved upon the iron floor. The chapel had enough processes that could not stop themselves. It did not need another.

What it had lacked was not repair.

It had lacked mercy toward the unfinished.

Afterward, damage was still answered with material. Vines were still sealed into walls. Broken limbs still recruited roots, wire, nests, scale, and bone. The tower continued to thicken its consequences.

But not always.

Here and there, a railing ended in air.

A dead circuit stayed dark.

A fallen limb was allowed to fall.

Small clearings appeared in the gutter gardens. An unused bridge remained folded, though the wall called for it in amber pulses. One window lost a pane and was not immediately crossed by mesh. Cloud entered through the opening, and for ten minutes rain traveled in a direction no etched channel had predicted.

The water struck the broad iron floor.

Fish spilled from the basins and followed the new streams. Moths sheltered under verger limbs. Birds wheeled through mist. One drop entered the hollow left by the missing pane’s latch, froze at night, expanded, and made the opening larger.

The chapel listened.

It judged nothing urgent.

It let the sky continue.
