The power did not descend.

Descent was already too dangerous a thing in the high country, and arrival implied weight. A throne would have broken provinces. A messenger with burning wings would have stampeded the woolbacks and drawn every broadwing for a thousand kilometers. Even a new star might have altered the opening of leaves.

So the power approached by becoming less.

It entered first as a correction in the wind: too slight to turn a sail-finger, but sufficient to pass through the hollow filaments of the fur-moss. It divided itself among frost crystals, pressure waves, sap knocks, and the tiny changes of tension by which living wood knows where it is burdened. For one long dark it did nothing but listen.

It listened at the ponds, where thirst made enemies stand flank to flank.

It listened in beetle chambers, in runner burrows, beneath sleeping broadwings and inside the warm slings where children breathed against their parents. It followed route songs through branches no singer could cross. It went down the root curtains and learned what became of rain after it fell from the underside of the world.

At last it entered a white seam.

The seam crossed a district called, in the nearest human speech, Bent-Over-Water. It had begun as a line narrow enough to cover with a finger. By the time the power found it, the line shone across four ridges, through a woolback calving ground, beneath three settlements, and under a blade-fern range whose screaming could be heard for days.

Beside the wound lived an old fracture-watcher named Eiren.

She had set resin pegs along the seam until she had no resin left. She had sent the widening measurements inward by drum. She had been ordered to leave twice, begged to leave seven times, and once carried away by her grandchildren, only to return while they slept.

Eiren lay each rest period with her cheek against the warm exposed fibers. She said she could hear the district choosing where to die.

The power listened through the same fibers.

When it spoke to her, it did not use words. Words traveled poorly between beings of such different duration. Instead it gave the seam a rhythm like a question: three soft contractions, a pause, then one.

Eiren woke and put her hand to the wound.

Again: three, a pause, one.

She answered with her smallest drum.

For six nights they attempted to understand one another.

The power showed her, in pressure and warmth, an image of the whole district held motionless forever: the fracture sealed, the boughs locked, no part of Bent-Over-Water ever permitted to fall.

Eiren rejected it.

She had seen branches that could not shed themselves. Their dead weight poisoned the sap around them. Parasites entered. Rot crossed the braids. What would not break in one season tore apart ten countries in another.

The power showed her a land without hunger.

Eiren rejected that too, though more slowly. She had no love for hunger. She had cut siftling blocks from hot felt and eaten the bitter creatures alive. She had buried three children in seedcloth without calling it burial. But she had also watched woolbacks migrate before the moving warmth, seen leaves compete for mist, seen people share beetle meal because there was not enough. To remove all want, she understood, would require remaking every mouth, every root, every pursuit. It might be mercy. It might also be erasure wearing mercy’s face.

Then the power showed her all the people of Bent-Over-Water lifted into another country.

At this Eiren struck the drum so hard the skin split.

“No,” she said aloud, though the power did not yet know the word. “Not without the moss. Not without the beetles. Not without what bears us.”

The answer traveled through the wound.

The power understood.

It ceased offering solutions.

For a while they listened together to the district’s fibers parting one by one.

At last the power asked a simpler question.

What is needed?

Eiren considered until frost formed in her hair.

“Time,” she said. “Not forever. Enough.”

Then, after another long silence:

“And an answer from below.”

These were requests a wise power could grant without making the high country cease to be itself.

But it did not grant them at once.

First it asked the plateau.

There was no center to address. No hidden heart beat beneath all the ridges. The woven height could not consent as a person consented. Its oldest trunks opposed what its margins attempted. A thirsty district tightened while a warm one relaxed. New growth spent freely what old growth tried to preserve.

The power therefore put the question everywhere it mattered.

It asked the exploratory red spars by warming one side of them and cooling the other. It asked root curtains in patterns of condensation. It asked blade-ferns with wind. It asked moss through the angle of light and beetle colonies through the chemistry of their molds. It asked supporting trunks by taking from them, for a moment, the infinitesimal burden of their own sap.

Most answers were not answers.

Leaves folded. Roots recoiled. Beetles sealed their chambers.

Yet in the old boughs, where countless fractures had healed into black knots, the power found a capacity the plateau had nearly evolved many times and lost many times: a tissue that did not resist sudden separation but yielded through it.

The fibers were pale, elastic, and almost impossibly long. Under ordinary strain they lay dormant. When a seam began to run, they uncoiled. They could not prevent a district from detaching, but they could turn a single catastrophic break into a long relinquishment.

The power did not place this tissue in the plateau.

It taught the plateau how to remember it.

The lesson moved no faster than sap. For years nothing appeared to change.

Eiren died in the third year.

Her family lowered her beneath Bent-Over-Water, close to the widening seam she had watched. Her route song took half a night to drum. The power, which had learned by then how human grief differed from warning, carried the lowest notes farther than they had ever traveled.

Something answered beneath the clouds.

Not an echo.

A route song.

It was old, distorted, and separated from its reply by such a depth that each phrase took many breaths to return. But it named a settlement once carried away on the fallen province of Red Glass. It named descendants. It named ponds under the shelf, immense warm basins caught among lower boughs. It named pale forests growing downward. It warned of unmoored colonies that mimicked drum speech. It asked whether the upper country still existed.

Every person within hearing put a hand to the bark.

The power did one more thing then: it altered nothing, but removed an obstruction.

Sounds traveling upward through the supporting trunks had always scattered where living fibers crossed. The power aligned no more than a few strands at a time, here and there, never enough to weaken them. Through these narrow lines, widely separated countries could begin to hear one another.

The first exchanges took years.

The upper people sent route songs for places the lower people would never see. The lower people sent weather warnings, for they felt storms gathering around the trunks long before the clouds rose. They taught rhythms meaning *this answer is not human*. They told what happened to falling lands.

Some shattered.

Some descended beyond all signal.

Some caught among the lower colonnades, tilted or overturned but alive. Ponds emptied and became ceilings. Root curtains became paths. Woolbacks learned to graze along slopes once called walls. Human shelters were rewoven. Gliders nested where people had walked. The survivors did not live as before, but neither had they entered nothingness.

The high country’s oldest terror became more particular. That did not make it small, but it made room beside terror for preparation.

Then the remembered fibers began to grow.

They appeared first around healed wounds as thin silver threads. Fracture-watchers noticed that new seams opened differently. The first crack still came like thunder, but the descending roar lengthened. A province no longer always tore free in one convulsion. Pale strands stretched across the chasm, paying out from both sides, some for minutes, some for days.

People called them mercy fibers.

The name was inaccurate. They saved nothing by themselves.

But they gave time.

Time for drums.

Time for cord to be tied around sleeping children.

Time for beetle cages to be gathered and pond leaves folded into traveling skins.

Time for woolbacks to follow the warmth toward the stable side—or, if they would not, for people to stop trying to force them.

Time for broadwings to sense the coming release and gather beneath the tilting country.

Time for farewells to cross the widening air.

When Bent-Over-Water finally fell, it did not fall cleanly.

The white seam opened from horizon to horizon. Blade-ferns screamed until their roots tore loose. Ponds spilled in black curtains. One by one the ordinary fibers broke, each report striking through the wood like an axe blow.

Then the mercy fibers took the load.

They stretched into shining lines miles long.

The district descended through the cloud deck over nine days.

Not everyone escaped it. Not everyone wished to. Fracture-watchers remained at their pegs. Edge-walkers tended the new, inverted margins. Three settlements crossed onto stable country; two stayed. Knucklebacks lay with their armor plates spread against the trembling boughs. Runners vanished deep beneath the felt. Great swarms of sail-fingers filled the widening space.

On the final night, drums sounded from below.

Bent-Over-Water answered.

Broadwings rose from the clouds, not sent by the power and not commanded by anyone. They fastened their pale tendrils along the underside of the province. They could not hold it aloft, but as the last fibers parted, they opened their wings.

The falling land turned.

Slowly—violently, majestically—it turned until its heaviest growth pointed downward and its broadest bough lay across the rising air. The broadwings released. Bent-Over-Water descended into cloud like an immense dark seed.

Its drums continued for a long while.

Months later, an answer came through the trunks.

Alive, the rhythm said.

Changed.

Alive.

The benevolent power heard rejoicing move across the plateau in branch-borne waves. It also heard hunger, predation, old cruelty, new accidents, and the cries of those whom no fiber had saved.

It did not remain to govern.

Wisdom told it that a world protected from every consequence would become the possession of its protector. Benevolence told it not to mistake restraint for indifference.

So it left no commandments, no temple, and no immortal guardian. It left only three things: the plateau’s recovered knowledge of how to yield without immediately letting go; clear passages by which the heights and depths might answer one another; and a rhythm meaning *What is needed?*

That rhythm entered the route songs.

It was played before strangers crossed onto a branch. It was tapped beside injured growth. It was drummed toward the clouds when a lower settlement fell silent. Parents sounded it on shelter walls when children were old enough to choose their work. Some communities used it in disputes, requiring each side to answer before judgment. Others played it at ponds before drinking.

Sometimes there was no answer.

Sometimes the answer was impossible.

Sometimes the answer was *leave*.

But the question endured.

Long afterward, people ceased agreeing about the power. Some said Eiren had spoken with the whole plateau. Some said the mercy fibers were natural and would have appeared regardless. Some said the broadwings were the true benefactors. Some believed the thing below the clouds had invented the answering songs to lure them downward.

The power did not correct them.

Above the clouds, the country continued to grow and tear. Animals fed upon one another. Water vanished. New ravines opened in a night. Provinces still fell, and some were never heard from again.

Yet when the first crack sounded, the inhabitants no longer heard only an ending.

They flattened themselves or leapt. They gathered cord. They listened for the pale fibers taking strain. They put hands, feet, bellies, drums, and armored plates against the living surface.

And through the vast woven dark, from countries above and countries below, the question traveled:

What is needed?

Then came the many answers, none complete, bearing what wisdom could bear of them without breaking the world beneath its weight.
