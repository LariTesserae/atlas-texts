The power does not begin by lifting the water.

They do not divide the cloud, restore the drowned roads, call the makers from death, or raise towers from their softened foundations. They do not introduce fish into the black gardens, though they could fashion fish suited to every depth. They do not give eyes to the luminous seams merely because their light has never been seen.

Wisdom prevents the first kindnesses that strength suggests.

For a long while, they only attend.

Their attention enters with the rain. It follows each drop through the surface fields, under overlapping blades, along gutters and broken stairways, through pores in mortar and the hollow fibers of mineralized wood. It divides where the water divides. It lingers in reservoirs. It descends through lime chambers and kiln beds. It passes along the paired chemical boundaries where hidden colonies have persisted since before the last door settled into silt.

To the inhabitants, this attention is not light, warmth, sound, or current. Nothing bends toward it. Nothing closes.

The power does not require recognition.

They learn the basin according to its own scale.

For one century they consider the growth of a holdfast within a carved depression. For another they watch a pale mat lift, cross the partition of a cultivation tray, and settle in a neighboring compartment. They follow one dust-fine reproductive body through leagues of submerged passages until it enters a seam where it can live.

They attend to failures as closely as continuities: the garden that buries itself, the luminous film whose seep is fading, the ancient net between posts that will break when a crossbeam gives way. None of these is judged wasteful. The power does not mistake duration for goodness or extinction for injustice. A life may end without being wronged. A lineage may vanish without the world becoming cruel.

They search for suffering.

There is hunger, but no creature capable of fearing hunger. There is damage, but no pain. There is exclusion from light and mineral, but no grief at exclusion. No living thing anticipates its death. No colony laments a blocked channel. No garden remembers the roof from which it tore free.

The power finds no hidden prisoner asking to be awakened.

So they do not give the basin a mind.

To gather all those dispersed lives into consciousness would not be an elevation alone. It would create loneliness on a scale equal to the water. It would make the slow choking of gardens into felt suffocation, the closure of channels into dread, the death of remote colonies into continual bereavement. A mind spread through the basin might take centuries to complete a thought and have no other mind with whom to share it.

The power could accompany such a being forever.

But companionship does not justify first making someone who needs consolation.

They leave the great almost-creature innocent of itself.

Nor do they return animals. Mouths would alter everything. The black fronds would learn injury. Mats that have known only pressure and chemistry would be torn faster than they could grow. The accumulated felt of ages would become food; the clear reservoirs would cloud; swimmers would carry living fragments across barriers that have separated provinces for millennia. Predation would not be evil, but it would be foreign. To add it for the sake of liveliness would be to confuse movement with value.

The power sees that this world is not empty.

Its stillness requires no cure.

Yet wisdom is not indifference, and attention reveals one danger.

Deep below the broadest paving plain, several ancient drainage chambers have begun to settle into one another. The motion is minute. No slab will fall this year, nor in a hundred years. But in seven thousand years, perhaps eight, the settling will close three narrow passages through which cold upper water reaches a buried region of kilns and cisterns.

When those passages close, the waters below will cease to differ.

Without those differences, the white films will thin. The rust threads will darken. The paired lines between lime and brick will stop advancing. Whole orders of deep life, found nowhere else in the basin, will disappear—not through a season or succession, but because a single exhausted route through the architecture has become final.

The power considers opening the passages by force.

They do not.

A new channel would carry too much water. It would scour surfaces whose softness is now their protection. It would join provinces long separated, allowing one chemistry to erase another. Even a perfectly cut opening would be an alien thing: a work without tool marks, labor, error, or inherited measure.

Instead, the power travels to a loose slab four leagues away.

The slab lies beneath black felt at the bottom of a collapsed court. One corner has lifted by less than the breadth of a leaf. Ordinarily it would settle again when the felt above grew heavier.

The power places beneath that corner a single grain of mortar.

The grain was already falling nearby.

They do not create it. They move it sideways no farther than a finger’s width.

The slab does not settle.

Water enters beneath it, so slowly that no silt rises. It follows an abandoned heating duct, passes through a row of peg holes, crosses the impression of vanished wickerwork, and reaches a stair buried under two later floors. Over the next nine hundred years, the flow clears several thumb-wide spaces without breaking them. It divides and descends. By the time the three old passages close, a fourth route has come into being—not new, exactly, but resumed.

Cold water again meets the mineral seep below the kilns.

A white boundary brightens.

A rust-colored thread branches at a joint.

Nothing knows it has been saved.

The power regards this as no defect in the saving.

There are other interventions, but none larger.

Where a roof’s collapse would extinguish one of two remaining luminous seams, they allow a trapped bubble to escape a season early. The shifting vegetation changes the pressure against a beam. The beam settles toward the opposite wall. The roof still falls, because roofs must fall, but one narrow seep remains open.

Where a surface field has spread so completely that its own decay threatens to seal an entire reservoir, they do not tear it loose. They alter no life. They merely hold one week of rain within the cloud and release it over a neighboring district. The difference in surface pressure is slight. Months later, a current reaches the reservoir wall. The garden folds along an old weakness and half of it drifts away. One half dies in open water. The other fastens to a line of posts and begins a form not previously found there.

Where a deep colony occupies the last surviving face of a particular blackened wicker frame, the power does nothing at all.

They see that its water is failing and that no gentle redirection can preserve it without destroying several other boundaries downstream.

The colony recedes.

Its living edge contracts through crossings laid by hands no history remembers. Behind it remains a mineral copy: woven lines, each no thicker than thread, covered by the fine crust of its own former life. At last the colony ceases.

The power witnesses the end completely.

They do not conceal it from themselves beneath talk of cycles.

What is lost is lost.

Because they are benevolent, they grieve.

Because they are wise, they do not make grief sovereign over the world.

But there is one gift they give without touching the basin.

They remember it.

Not as a map alone, nor as an inventory of forms, but exactly: every rounded step and blind duct, every field of dark blades, every chemical boundary inside brick, every chamber where blue-green light shone unseen. They remember the makers’ measures preserved in ranks of posts. They remember the pressure of vanished fingers implied by pegs and wedges. They remember each living pattern that took hold afterward, including those that ended without descendants.

Nothing is resurrected by this remembrance. Nothing below becomes immortal.

Still, nothing is wholly unregarded.

If elsewhere there are minds capable of receiving knowledge without turning immediately toward conquest, the power may sometimes grant one of them a dream.

The dream contains no explanation. It shows no builders and names no age. It gives the dreamer the sensation of descending through cold water while rain sounds far above. A faint seam shines along an arch. Black ribbons bend through a doorway. Beneath a hand is a fitted slab, and beneath that slab another chamber, and beneath that chamber older paving.

The dreamer wakes with the certainty that silence is not absence, that a place need not speak to possess value, and that mercy may consist in touching one grain rather than remaking the sea.

Most will forget.

A few will shape their lives differently without knowing why.

As for the power, they remain near, but not always present. They have other places to attend, other beings whose pain is immediate and whose prayers can be answered plainly. Centuries may pass in the basin without their notice resting on it.

This is also permitted.

The basin endured before it was seen by benevolence. It does not depend upon divine regard to be real.

Rain continues to fall. Gardens loosen and travel. Mats rise from their trays and settle across old partitions. Deep films advance through mortar at the pace of stone becoming inhabited.

Far below, the grain beneath the slab slowly dissolves.

By then, the water has learned the reopened way. Mineral has hardened along one side of the passage. Threads have grown along the other. The old duct carries a narrow coldness into the dark, where white and rust-colored lives meet it.

The power does not sign their work.

There is no monument.

Only a current, almost too slight to feel, passing through what has been made.
