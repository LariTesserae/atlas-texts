The power does not descend in fire.

Fire would be redundant.

It does not still the mountain. To arrest the rising plug would cool districts, strand pressure works, slacken cables designed for ascent, and make useless the patient geometries of six hundred years. The city has not mistaken danger for evil. Its people know where they live. To save them from motion would be another kind of destruction.

It does not send rain.

Rain in abundance would scour the outer slopes into ravines, flood hot chambers, dissolve salts into poison, overload cisterns, and turn the basin’s careful thirst into catastrophe. Nor does it cover the ash in green. There are gifts that are only the giver’s idea of beauty.

The power is benevolent.

Therefore, before it acts, it learns what can be harmed.

For eighty-one years it remains indistinguishable from attention.

It passes through the basin without shadow. It follows condensation down chains and into locked tanks. It rests in the responsive films, feeling the faint choices by which a branch turns toward voltage. It accompanies flange crawlers through clearances narrower than a finger. It moves along freight cables to the lower country and back again as stored tension.

It places no sign in the sky.

The pale sail-like things cross as they always have, folding in heat.

The power studies the city’s maps: not merely the official maps engraved with elevations and expected fractures, but the maps worn into bodies. It learns where rim technicians pause for oxygen. It learns which nursery tile is always warm enough to attract sleeping children. It learns the route taken by an old worker who touches the same five pillars each morning, and the route taken by a child who avoids those pillars because she believes they are listening.

It learns the unfinished tenses.

It learns that a bridge can be complete until moved.

It learns that an ancestor may still be installing a pipe.

It learns that respect may be offered to something believed incapable of receiving it.

Most of all, it learns the deep custodians.

Far below the streets, beneath receiving halls and heat mains, they remain inside their frames. Some still remember entering them. Some remember only pressure. Some have been reduced, by the necessities of the city, to one gesture made at intervals longer than childhood.

The power does not call this cruelty.

The custodians were not imprisoned by tyrants. They were fastened there by repairs, emergencies, good intentions, deferred replacements, and the gradual accumulation of people who would die if they were removed.

Nevertheless, the power recognizes suffering even when no one is to blame.

It approaches the oldest occupant.

The chamber is a long cavity lined with black ceramic. One wall curves inward under pressure. Against it stands—or has grown—a support frame whose first components were installed seven hundred and nine years before. Pipes enter the frame. Mineral plates have closed around it. At its center is a person called Eren-of-the-Third-Listening, though no living relative uses that name.

Eren’s remaining organic eye has not opened in a century. Their signals consist of changes in pressure sent through a tube no wider than a hair.

The power could speak in thunder.

Instead, it presses the chamber wall by an amount smaller than thermal expansion.

Eren answers.

A pulse travels into the tube.

The exchange requires nine years.

The power asks, as nearly as pressure can ask:

**What do you want?**

Eren’s first answer is a map of the chamber.

The second is a warning about a flaw in the southern valve.

The third reroutes condenser water away from a district whose supports will fail in thirty-two years.

Only then, after fulfilling the obligations that have become indistinguishable from thought, does Eren answer the question.

**A successor.**

The power considers this.

It could take Eren’s place. It could become the wall’s listener, the valve’s intelligence, the hidden hand beneath the water routes. It could hold the city forever and never tire.

But a wise power knows that an eternal custodian is also an eternal dependency. If it makes itself indispensable, it has not freed the city. It has merely become the final deep occupant.

So it asks another question.

**For how long?**

Eren answers after fourteen months.

**Long enough to stand up.**

The power begins there.

It does not perform a miracle. Miracles are difficult to maintain and impossible to repair.

Instead, it submits a proposal.

One morning, the assembly hall’s argument boards illuminate before any hand has touched them. No alarm color appears. Across every panel runs a branching diagram in the formal grammar of civic works.

It is an application to establish a continuity.

The proposed continuity has no listed founders, no assigned household, and no verified water account. Its name is a word already present in engineering manuals but rarely used in law:

**Margin.**

Its declared task is:

**To ensure that no necessary function depends forever upon a single living occupant.**

The proposal includes designs.

Secondary pressure senses that can be manufactured from ash ceramic and responsive film.

Bypasses around chambers that have not been safely opened in centuries.

Counterweights whose failure settles them into harmless positions.

Reservoirs sufficient to maintain districts while a custodian is disconnected.

Manual controls that can be reached by ordinary bodies.

Automatic controls that announce their own uncertainty.

Doors.

Many of the designs are for doors.

The assembly suspects sabotage.

It also suspects revelation, industrial espionage, atmospheric intelligence, fraud by an unregistered continuity, and an unusually elaborate prank by apprentices. The boards flush red and violet for six straight days.

The unknown applicant answers every technical objection.

When accused of concealing costs, it lists them.

When asked who will bear responsibility for failure, it replies:

**Those who consent to build, including me.**

When asked what portion of future water it claims, it replies:

**None.**

This nearly ends consideration. Nothing that claims no water can be held accountable.

The applicant revises its proposal.

It asks for one drop from each rain event, to be stored in a transparent ampoule in the assembly hall. The drop will not be consumed. It will serve as its legal stake and proof of participation.

The city accepts Margin provisionally, by a narrow field of white lights.

Work begins.

It takes one hundred and forty years.

The power does not shorten this time, though it could. It will not make the city a spectator to its own rescue.

It shows where to cut, but summit hands make the cuts. It reveals the composition of old fused seals, but foundries cast their replacements. It persuades responsive films to grow across test plates, but technicians learn how to read them. It gives flange crawlers no orders; it merely designs narrow maintenance paths, and the crawlers enter of their own accord.

In the first decades, Margin is unpopular.

Its redundancies consume space. Its bypasses complicate already complicated streets. Its emergency reservoirs hold water that could have been used now. Its doors weaken walls unless carefully framed. Its insistence that every vital mechanism possess a means of being stopped strikes some continuities as an insult to workmanship.

The power does not win these arguments by authority.

It loses some of them.

The city rejects three designs outright. One is, in fact, poorly suited to the young basalt of the western districts. The power marks the error on the public board and revises its calculations.

This matters more than any display of omniscience could have mattered.

Trust arrives by fractions.

A nursery ventilation keeper becomes the first person to leave a station previously considered permanent. She walks out after twenty-three years and discovers that her legs will no longer hold her. Apprentices carry her through the district in a suspended tray. The ventilation continues without her for six hours, then six days, then permanently.

A lift continuity demonstrates a brake that fails open, closed, and uncertain without killing anyone.

A household refuses an inherited obligation to maintain a kiln. The kiln does not go cold. Another path exists.

The city acquires a new public mark.

It means: **safe to cease.**

Children learn it after compression and before expected shear.

At last, Margin reaches the chamber of Eren-of-the-Third-Listening.

By then, no one alive remembers the first proposal except the power and the oldest custodians. The bypass chamber has been completed. Pressure has been transferred by degrees. Water routes have been duplicated. The curved wall is now watched by nine independent senses, three of them living, three mechanical, two cultured, and one not clearly classifiable.

A door has been cut around the frame.

The final disconnection takes eleven days.

Eren’s support machinery protests with signals so old that half their meanings have to be inferred. Ceramic plates are removed. Tubes are sealed. Manipulators are detached from the wall. Where flesh ends and apparatus begins, the surgeons do not insist on a distinction. They free whatever can be freed and support whatever cannot.

When the work is done, Eren does not stand.

There is too little of them that remembers how.

The power feels something like grief. Benevolence does not guarantee restoration. Wisdom does not make every late mercy timely.

Eren is carried upward.

The journey takes months. At each elevation their frame must be adjusted for pressure and temperature. Districts open service routes ahead of them. Bridges halt freight. Households lend water. Children wait beside the path and touch the walls as the old occupant passes.

At last Eren reaches the surface.

It is before dawn. The basin is dark. Condensers are bright with points of water, and the uneven rim stands against the first ordinary blue.

Eren’s eye opens.

Perhaps they see the city.

Perhaps only light.

Their remaining finger flexes once.

Across the basin, without command from the power, signal bands change color. The message begins near the deep halls, passes through foundries and dwellings, ascends the towers, crosses bridges, and reaches the rim.

It is the new mark.

**Safe to cease.**

Eren dies at sunrise.

The city recovers their water. It separates the useful compounds. It fires the mineral remainder into a small black block.

There is debate over where the block should be placed. The pressure chamber is suggested. So is the bypass door. So is the assembly board where Margin first appeared.

In the end, it is set into the foot of a public bench at an overlook beyond the eastern districts. The bench has no technical purpose. It faces the small ravine that engineers have argued about for generations.

Margin requests that the bench remain unassigned.

This is difficult. Unassigned structures are neglected structures; unallocated time is wasted time; unclaimed water is stolen water. The city possesses no comfortable category for a thing that is maintained without becoming an obligation.

But one unassigned bench is permitted.

People begin to sit there.

Not to monitor the ravine, though some do.

Not to watch freight or weather or strain rods.

They sit because the bench is warm in the afternoon and because nothing depends on their sitting.

This is the second work of the power.

The first was redundancy.

The second is permission.

Across the following centuries, Margin introduces no new resource and alters no law by force. It merely keeps asking one question whenever a body, continuity, or responsive system becomes indispensable:

**What would allow this to stop without disaster?**

The answers reshape the city.

There are shifts with no assigned production.

There are rooms whose water allotment is reserved for recovery rather than work.

There are apprenticeships one may leave.

There are continuities required to plan for their own ending.

There are deep custodians who choose to remain and others who come upward. Some die immediately. Some live long enough to become troublesome civic commentators. One asks to be taken to a nursery and spends seven years watching children pour water from bowl to bowl.

A few inhabitants object that Margin is weakening the summit people.

They say obligation made the city possible.

They are not wrong.

The power answers:

**A support that cannot be relieved is not strong. It is merely not yet broken.**

In time, even the power arranges a successor.

Not a single successor. That would violate its own principle.

It distributes what it has learned among the city’s people, films, instruments, crawlers, records, habits, and laws. It teaches them to detect burdens that have disguised themselves as identities. It teaches the assembly to ask whether consent given by an ancestor can bind a child whose face that ancestor never imagined. It teaches machines to reveal when they are uncertain, and citizens to regard such revelation as reliability rather than failure.

It places no doctrine in their mouths.

It does not ask them to call the mountain alive.

It does not ask to be worshiped.

One rain day, after the streets have run black and the roofs have folded their vanes shut, the transparent ampoule in the assembly hall is found empty.

The power’s legal stake is gone.

No one saw the drop disappear.

For several years the city debates whether Margin still exists. Arguments branch across the boards. Some insist that an unregistered benevolent agency remains active in the infrastructure. Others say its functions have been fully inherited. Others classify the entire episode as a prolonged interaction with responsive deposits.

No conclusion is adopted.

Far below, the mountain continues to rise.

Bridges slide. Walls divide. New seams open and receive their shallow dishes. Pale sails cross the heat columns. Flange crawlers sort washers in the dark.

At sunset, people gather sometimes at the eastern overlook. The ravine has deepened. It can now hide an entire hand, then an arm. Engineers have stopped trying to decide whether to permit it. They measure it and build away from its probable path.

Beside it stands the bench.

Children climb on it. Workers sleep there between shifts. Elders sit with their masks loosened, spending a little breath on nothing.

In the foot of the bench, Eren’s fired remains bear the weight and allow for motion.

And when one of the great engines completes its stroke, soft pressure passes through the basin. Suspended chains sway. Condensation loosens from every roof.

For a few seconds, the city receives a sparse, synchronized rain.

Most of the drops are caught, measured, promised, and used.

But not all.

Some strike the raw stone beside the ravine and vanish into the mountain without assignment.

The city has learned to let them go.
