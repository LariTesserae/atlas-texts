### What the Power Does

The power does not enter the basin at once.

Benevolence without patience would descend as warmth. It would melt the dangerous ice, soften the glare, lay soil over the pale stone, and cause low green things to root wherever water gathers. It would still the hidden torrents. It would make the streets level. It would bind the walls so they could no longer wander.

In doing so, it would destroy almost everything.

The crabs would lose their cold channels. The birds would abandon thawed cornices. The dark wall creatures would find the masonry sealed against them. Glass gnats would vanish from the shafts of falling water. The dead, released from their ice, would slump from the cliff. The people would wake unable to read the ground because the ground had ceased speaking in the language they knew.

A foolish mercy would call this rescue.

The wise power remains beyond the rim.

It watches through a full turning of the seasons, and then through another. Time does not trouble it. It learns the basin slowly, because anything learned quickly here is likely to be only a phase.

It watches people wake beneath roofs that have shifted in the night. It watches them test the walls with their palms before greeting one another. It sees a child fall through thin ice and emerge laughing into a room that did not have a ceiling the day before. It sees an old builder place her cheek to a retaining face, listen for a long while, and quietly begin removing her possessions from the adjoining chamber. Before dusk, forty hands have dismantled the wall. At midnight the hidden water rises through the place where it stood.

The power learns that the people are not surviving despite the town’s instability.

They are living by means of it.

So the power does not make the mountain steady.

It does not make the inhabitants stronger, for strength of the wrong kind would make them careless. It does not grant them sharper hearing, because they already hear nearly everything near enough to matter. It does not explain the glass gnats. An answer would narrow them. It does not reveal where the central hole goes. Whatever truth lies there has become fruitful through partial misunderstanding.

It considers giving them abundance.

It could fill the storage niches with grain, make fat gather beneath the birds’ skin, cause every crab to carry twice its flesh. But abundance is not a single gift. It is a new climate of decisions. More food would bring more children, more bodies, more heat, more thawing, more walls. The town would spread upward. The birds would be displaced. Waste would appear where nothing had prepared itself to receive waste. Travelers would hear of the miracle. They would come carrying iron, claims, reverence, hunger.

The power leaves the niches as they are.

It considers healing their eyes.

But sight is not merely an organ in the basin. It is masks, gestures, the work of bird-tenders, the authority of nearness, the habit of touching what cannot be seen. To remove every clouded patch would not simply subtract suffering. It would redistribute knowledge, labor, intimacy, and risk in ways even a wise power cannot calculate without becoming the town’s ruler.

So it heals no people merely to satisfy its own discomfort at seeing pain.

Yet neither does it worship suffering. It does not mistake hardship for purity. When it encounters an illness that serves nothing—an infant’s airway closing beneath a bird-skin wrap, a fever born from a copper cut, a clot moving toward the heart of a woman whose hands are still needed on three walls—it intervenes so slightly that the body appears to have changed its mind. A throat opens. A fever breaks in sweat. A clot catches harmlessly in a branching vessel and diminishes.

Not always.

To prevent every death would make the basin fill with the undying. To choose every hour of death would make all lives instruments of the power’s preference.

It offers no immortality.

Its mercy is particular, infrequent, and without signature. It saves where saving leaves the saved person’s life their own.

For a long while, this is all it does.

Then, looking where no inhabitant can look, it sees the ending.

Far above the basin, behind a shoulder of black stone, the mountain has formed a weakness deeper than any listening palm can reach. Meltwater has entered an ancient seam beneath the hanging snowfields. Each summer it descends a little farther. Each winter it freezes and widens the separation.

Nothing in the town foretells it.

The crabs will not climb until the last hour. The birds will feel the pressure only when the upper slope begins to move. The wall creatures will pour from the masonry, but there will be nowhere for them to go. The people will understand immediately and completely. They will climb. They will carry children, family stones, copper blades, masks, and perhaps a wrapped body not yet placed among the dead.

They will not climb fast enough.

The whole shoulder of the mountain will descend into the basin. It will not rearrange the town. It will erase the hollow in which rearrangement is possible. The central hole will close beneath a depth of broken stone. Water will be forced into other valleys. The crabs will die in their sealed channels. The last glass gnats will turn once in the bright air before the snow covers the light.

Given centuries, life may return.

But not this life.

The power understands the difference between change and the destruction of all further forms.

This is where it acts.

It does not hold up the mountain. That would require a permanent exertion, an invisible pillar upon which the settlement would unknowingly depend. It does not warn the inhabitants. They could abandon the basin, but the town itself—the crowded union of water, animal, dead, stone, and remembered labor—cannot be evacuated.

Instead, the power attends to a single droplet.

The droplet has risen from a shallow pool at noon. It is climbing the cliff in a trembling chain with hundreds of others. In another moment it will enter one crack rather than another. Either course would be ordinary. Neither would ever be noticed.

The power persuades it sideways.

The droplet enters a sunlit seam high above the basin. There it freezes.

The amount of ice is smaller than a child’s tear.

At dusk it melts on the shaded side. At noon the next day, more water follows the mineral trace it left. In winter, the seam opens by less than the width of a hair. In spring it closes crookedly. The following year it takes in three droplets where it once took one.

Over decades, a clear rib forms inside the stone.

The rib does not strengthen the dangerous shoulder. It divides its pressure. Instead of remaining whole until the day of catastrophe, the upper slope begins to release itself in portions.

The first fall is a stream of gravel into an empty valley beyond the pass.

The second is a slab of ice that breaks on a high shelf and becomes powder.

Years later, a snowfield slips at dawn. Its thunder travels away from the basin, toward country where no one lives. The people feel only a long trembling through their sleeping platforms. They rise, touch the walls, and spend the day opening joints that have tightened.

More small falls follow.

One changes the angle of sunlight for part of the winter. Another exposes a band of rusty mineral in the upper cliff. Meltwater takes a new route. The crabs alter their climbing. Between-Watchers cover many flakes of shale with notations and argue for three generations about why the gnats now gather above the eastern roofs.

The settlement changes to fit these changes.

No one knows it has been saved.

The power wants no shrine, and so this is good.

It does not place a mark on the cliff. It does not speak through the birds. It does not cause a flawless stone to appear in the oval, or a flame to burn beneath the water, or a new star to stand over the pass. Proof of intervention would become weight. People would build customs against it, then offices, then fears. They would ask whether each flood were punishment and each safe wall approval. They would begin to fit themselves not to the mountain but to an imagined spectator.

The power refuses to become another pressure inside the basin.

Still, it loves the place.

Love, for such a power, is not the wish to possess or perfect. It is the willingness to know what must not be altered, and to remain responsible for what can be seen only from far away.

So it keeps watch at the scale the inhabitants cannot.

The people watch droplets, seams, crabs, the trembling of bowls, the angle of reflected light. The power watches the slow bending of ranges, the migration of glaciers, the buried fires, the approach of strangers across generations, the diseases sleeping in distant valleys. It does not bar all danger. A town protected from every consequence would cease to be this town. But when a danger comes that permits no answer—no climbing, no dismantling, no redistribution of stones, no fitting—it places some small possibility in its path.

A bird delays its flight and is seen by a weather-reader.

A wall creature steals the family stone from a room that will soon flood.

A copper blade breaks before it can be carried into a fever-struck settlement beyond the pass.

An invading company hears the mountain give a sharp report and decides the basin is haunted.

A child wakes with her foot behind her brother’s knee just before the roof ice shears loose.

None of these mercies abolish loss. Some people still die too young. Walls fail. Eggs are taken in famine. Eyes cloud. Beloved voices cease and are heard later, perhaps, beneath the central oval.

The power does not make the town harmless.

It keeps harm from becoming final.

Centuries after the droplet is turned aside, a child lies with one ear against the central hole. It is noon, and the hole is drawing inward. The child’s mask is pushed up onto her forehead. One hand grips a family stone marked with a line of old tooth dents.

She hears stones being passed.

She hears a roof laid in scales.

She hears someone singing into a drainage throat, sending a low vibration through chambers that do not yet exist.

Then she hears laughter.

This puzzles her. The people say the hole opens sideways into earlier positions of the town, but the laughter belongs to no story she knows. One of the voices uses her own light, changeable name, though no one is speaking nearby.

She runs to tell an old listener.

The old listener lowers himself carefully to the oval and presses his ear against the rim. He hears nothing but cold air and distant water.

“Earlier,” he says, “does not always mean behind us.”

The child asks what that means.

The old listener considers, then shrugs. The ground beneath them has begun to lift slightly on sun-thickened ice, and there is work to do.

Together they go to move a wall.

Beyond the rim, the power withdraws its attention just enough to let the place remain unwatched in its own experience.

It has given no commandment, no permanent shelter, no promise that stone will not fall.

Its blessing is only this:

That change will continue to arrive in forms to which life can answer.

That there will be another arrangement after this one.

That the town will never be made safe from becoming—

and never be denied a next shape.
