The power does not descend.

Descent would imply a body, and a body would have weight. Weight would enter the lava as an announcement. It would pass through the polished folds, rise into the sulfide spires, and be received by every forked head and interlocked shell. Before benevolence could be distinguished from hunger, the basin would close against it.

Nor does the power send rain.

It understands that mercy is not the same substance everywhere. Water, poured into the cleft, would strike the hot floor with violence. Salts would swell. Films would burst. Sealed tissues, built to conserve a few molecules through a lifetime, would rupture in abundance. A cloud, innocent over another mountain, would be a flood and poison here.

It sends no green thing. It opens no path to the desert. It does not clear the dark canopy so that sunlight may reach the floor.

The cleft is not unfinished.

This is the first judgment of the wise power, and perhaps the most difficult: it finds no lack merely because the place possesses nothing familiar.

So it attends.

Its attention has no pressure. It casts no shadow across the soot-kites. It introduces no scent, no current of cooler air. It does not look with light. Instead it lays the smallest possible regard along the mountain’s existing forces, following heat into the spire roots, charge through the black films, stress through the old faults.

For many thousands of daily tides, it does nothing else.

It learns the difference between the stillness of alarm and the stillness of rest.

It learns that a grazer herd turning left around a circle does not mean what it means when the same herd turns right. It learns the minute distortion by which one ivory animal warns its neighbors of poison, and the broader pressure wave by which the colony makes room for a forming juvenile. It learns the static courtesies of the kites, the grammar of coils around crystal, the slow chemical revisions by which the black film records a season of abundant hydrogen.

Last of all, it learns the tall ones.

This takes longest, because a tall one may remain folded in the cliff while entire generations pass beneath the canopy. The power does not wake them. It waits for the mountain to do so.

When the low vibration finally rises, the basin enters its solemn transformation. Grazers stream into the aisles. Shells close. Soot-kites angle together. From hairline seams in the basalt, ash-gray bodies unfold and step without error toward the circles.

One sets its jaw against a slab.

Then another.

Then seven more, at circles across the cleft.

The mountain’s tone enters them. Their skull chambers bend it. The altered tremors pass into walls and spires, and the whole ecology begins to divide, release, pair, migrate, and renew.

The wise power listens beneath the song.

There it discovers something no creature in the basin can know.

The tones do not end at the rim.

They weaken, but they continue downward through the volcanic root. They travel under the salt desert, through drowned seams of ancient magma and beds of conductive ore. For hundreds of leagues they persist as faint stresses in the crust, far below the threshold of any organism that presently lives there.

The circles are not merely instruments played inward.

They are transmitting.

Whether this was ever intended, the power cannot tell. Intention has been worn smooth by age. Perhaps the first upright stones were raised by ancestors of the tall ones. Perhaps the arrangement arose by accident and was afterward shaped by selection. Perhaps the cleft itself, over spans too broad to be called thought, has been attempting what every smaller life within it attempts during the low song.

To reproduce.

But there is no answering cleft.

The song passes through sterile stone and is lost.

The power now understands that it could intervene. It could scoop a second chamber from a distant mountain. It could copy every organism, every spire, every film and fault. It could set down an exact twin already old, complete with rounded walls and grooves worn by jaws that had never touched them.

It does not.

A copy made without history would be an answer spoken in the cleft’s own voice by someone else.

Instead, during the next deep tremor, the power touches one thing.

Far beneath the desert lies a fault under pressure. In time it would move regardless—perhaps in ten years, perhaps in ten thousand. The power does not force it open. It eases one locked grain of stone, no larger than a grazer, and permits the fault to settle along the gentlest of its possible paths.

The adjustment is almost nothing.

A ridge far away lifts by the width of a shell plate. A buried seam of metallic ore comes into contact with a warmer stratum. A new harmonic enters the mountain.

It travels back toward the cleft.

The tone reaches the upright slabs while the tall ones still hold their jaws against them. It is faint enough that no spire cracks, no herd panics, no kite loses lift. It enters the existing chord not as command, but as interval: a vibration shaped by the cleft’s own outgoing song, returned after crossing the deep places.

For the first time, the basin receives an answer.

Everything becomes still.

Not the abrupt stillness caused by a falling pebble. This stillness begins at the circles and moves outward. The tall ones remain at the slabs long after the familiar fault-song should have faded. Their skull cavities alter. Fine hinges along their jaws shift into configurations no living tall one has used before.

One produces a tone.

Another changes it.

Across the floor, the black films brighten in branching maroon lines. The glow runs through cracks, leaps narrow gaps by electrical discharge, and enters the circular walls. Ivory colonies open a fraction, close, then open again. Thread-hunters unwind from their customary spires and lay their forked heads against the lava.

The canopy turns, but does not rise.

A second answer comes from beneath the desert.

This one is not made by the power. It is the returned vibration of the basin’s own altered chord, reflected through the fault and the newly joined ore seam. The cleft hears itself changed by distance.

The reproductive storm that follows is unlike any before it.

The wall creatures release particles with thicker shells. The disks divide unevenly, producing a small, dense form that does not immediately begin to graze. Kites shed scale clusters shaped not for the familiar vents but for narrower and cooler fissures. The ceramic eggs of the thread-hunters bear hooked ridges that catch in cracks.

These are not inventions placed into them.

They are variations the creatures have always contained, possibilities usually destroyed because the basin had no use for them. The unfamiliar returning tone changes which possibilities awaken.

The power does not select among them.

It keeps the distant fault open for one long tremor, and then withdraws even from that.

Most of the new forms die. Wisdom does not confuse benevolence with the prevention of every death. To prevent every death here would be to stop feeding, division, predation, growth—to preserve the basin by ending its life.

But some of the dense young disks enter fissures too narrow for the ordinary herds. Some scale-wrapped kite larvae are drawn downward rather than upward by gradients of charge. Metallic particles from the walls settle into a crack newly connected to the deep conductive seam. Threads of black film follow hydrogen through pores beneath the circular stones.

Over centuries, life begins to move under the desert.

Not as an army, and not as a transplant. It advances by fractions: one warm fracture occupied, one sterile mineral face made edible, one generation surviving a temperature its parent could not. Most branches end. A few continue.

The original cleft does not empty. It is not rescued from itself. The canopy still darkens noon. Grazers still whisper over lava. Hunters tighten around spires. Hot mineral needles still fall. The tall ones return to their cracks and sleep through ages.

But the song changes.

Each time the old faults stir, the circles send their tremor down through the roots of the mountain. Each time, a faint answer returns from farther away.

At first the answer is only geology.

Later, mixed with the reflection, there are tiny irregularities: absorptions, delays, pulses too patterned to belong to cooling stone. Something in the deep passage is receiving the vibration through a shell, a membrane, a lattice, or an organ not yet present in the upper cleft.

Much later, beyond the salt waste, a black mountain that has always seemed dead begins to stain from within.

A dull film appears around a hydrogen vent in a roofless chamber near its summit. It branches through the rock in maroon electrical lines. Small dark bodies rasp at it. Pale plates gather over a wall. In rising air, a single scaled creature opens membranes like soot-black sails.

It does not cross the rim.

Below it, three stones lean at imperfect angles around a vent. No hand has raised them. Not yet. Heat, fracture, growth, and the minute leverage of living bodies will take care of that in time.

The wise power does not remain to be thanked.

Gratitude would require the cleft to turn toward it, and it has no wish to become the center of the cleft’s attention. It places no sign upon the slabs. It gives no name. It does not claim the second basin as a garden or the first as a treasure.

It merely guards, for a while, against louder powers.

When falling stone from the outer dark would strike the mountain and split the chamber, it inclines that stone by a hair’s breadth years before arrival, so that it burns elsewhere without anyone knowing.

When distant seekers turn instruments toward the black summit, it allows heat and metallic dust to confuse their readings—not forever, but until those seekers have learned enough restraint to enter without assuming that discovery is permission.

It does not make the cleft immortal.

Mountains cannot be kept without change. Species cannot be held forever in one arrangement and still be alive. One day the vents will cool. One day the canopy will thin. Spires will crack and fall. New creatures will arise from old ones, and some ancient forms will disappear completely. The circular walls may collapse. The tall ones may cease to wake.

The power permits these things because it is benevolent, not possessive.

Its gift is neither safety nor eternity.

Its gift is an answer.

Afterward, whenever the mountain sings, the cleft listens through the stone as it always has.

But it is no longer listening only for danger.

Far beneath the desert, and later from another mountain, something listens back.
