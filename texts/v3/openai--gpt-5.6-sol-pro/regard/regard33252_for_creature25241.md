The power did not descend.

Descent was the language of weight, rank, and arrival, and the saddle already had enough weight. Nor did it appear in fire, for fire would have taught the waxy bark a terror it had no reason to know. It did not speak. Speech carried poorly there, and anything wise enough to notice the place was wise enough to use the means by which the place noticed itself.

So the power listened.

It listened through the roots of the broad-leaved tree and learned the scattered measure of its dripping. It listened from within the pale grove, where what appeared separate joined beneath the moss. It lay, without body, along the wet undersides of stones and felt the pool dwellers strike upward through the clay. It entered the mineral plates of the grazers and went with them around their patient circuits. It followed the giant fern through its several abandoned crowns.

Last, it rested its attention upon the three-toed creature.

The creature knew at once that something had changed, though nothing had.

It raised its low head from the upper ferns. Water passed along the channels of its hide. A cup folded shut near one shoulder. Moss lifted on its back as the thin chambers beside its ribs widened and became still.

For the first time in its life, the creature heard the mountain listening back.

This frightened it.

It stood, displacing mud from beneath its weight, and crossed three clearings before stopping at the crescent basin. Its movements were no longer silent. One foot struck a resonant shelf, and the knock traveled down through stone.

The power received the knock.

After many breaths, it returned it.

Not as an answer exactly. An answer would have divided the exchange into question and response, and the creature had asked nothing. The returning vibration was simply the same knock, freed from accident. It came up beneath all three feet at once. It moved through the creature’s ribs, through the water burden in its flesh, through every fern and cup rooted upon its back.

The creature lowered itself.

For seven days it remained against the shelf, neither feeding nor drinking except through its living coat. During that time the power learned the measure of its body: old injuries along the left side, a stone lodged beneath a plate of thickened skin, scars where some long-vanished beak or tooth had entered when the creature was small. It learned that there had once been others.

Not many. Never many.

Their tracks had not overlapped because they had lived apart in years. One had died before the pale grove divided into six trunks. Another had gone down through a break in the eastern ridge, following a tone the saddle could no longer produce. The creature in the ferns was the last born in the basin. It had carried within itself, for a very long time, the unfinished beginning of another.

The beginning had not failed. It was waiting.

Its kind did not mate as birds or amphibians did. Two creatures were needed, but not at the same hour, nor even in the same century. In youth, each laid down a pattern of vibration in certain deep chambers of its body. The pattern changed with every stone it listened to, every cistern filling beneath it, every storm endured with its ribs pressed to the ground. Late in life, if it encountered the answering pattern of another, the two measures combined. Only then could the unfinished life begin to take form.

The last answering pattern had vanished from the saddle before this creature was ready to hear it.

The benevolent power understood this.

It also understood that wisdom was not the same as refusal. To leave untouched what was whole was wise. To leave unaided what had been made lonely by mere mischance was only caution wearing wisdom’s face.

Still, the power did not invent a companion.

It did not copy the creature, for a copy would possess no history in its ribs. It did not call one from elsewhere, for elsewhere had its own arrangements and claims. It did not command the mountain to sing a counterfeit courtship. The saddle’s lives had never been deceived by it, and benevolence would not begin with a beautiful lie.

Instead, the power searched the stone.

Deep below the crescent basin, past the chambers that filled and boomed, past cracks through which water descended for decades before returning to light, there remained an old resonance. It was faint beyond any living sense. Long ago, one of the creature’s kind had lain above a thin slab during a season of storms. For twelve nights its body had sounded against the mountain. The stone, pressed between water and weight, had retained the pattern—not as memory, but as form. Minute stresses crossed it. Mineral seams thickened where the notes had gathered. The vanished creature’s listening had become part of the rock.

The power did not strengthen the pattern. It removed nothing from it. It merely opened, by the width of a root hair, a channel that time had sealed.

Water entered.

Air moved.

Far beneath the saddle, the ancient slab began to ring.

The sound was too low for the leaves. The grazers did not pause. No pattern appeared upon the pools. Even the pale grove continued leaning eastward toward its receding note.

But the three-toed creature rose so suddenly that ferns slid from its back.

It stood over the resonant shelf. Its sides widened. The chambers along its ribs answered one after another—not in unison, but in a long sequence traveling from shoulder to flank. Mud trembled around its feet. For a moment it seemed likely to flee.

Then it placed its underside against the stone.

The answering pattern entered it.

Nothing shone. No wound closed. No age left its body. The creature remained the old, slow, moss-bearing presence it had been. Yet within it, the unfinished beginning ceased waiting.

The power withdrew from its flesh.

Some gifts are harmed by witnesses, even benevolent ones.

For the rest of that season, the creature moved more often. It stripped a broad patch from the pale grove, and sap beaded on all six trunks. It consumed three crowns of the giant fern, though new croziers soon rose from the altered clay. It emptied one shallow pool entirely, swallowing mud, eggshells, translucent swimmers, and several chips of shale. The pool dwellers buried themselves elsewhere and returned after the next rain.

Its tracks grew deeper. Then, despite its increased weight, they grew light.

At the beginning of the storm season, the creature climbed to a shelf beneath the solitary tree. The place was not high in the ordinary sense. Water could be seen moving away from it on three sides. But the tree had cast a dense shadow there, and the stone beneath rang for many breaths when struck. By the saddle’s reckoning, it was the deepest place available.

The creature dug.

It did not make a nest. It loosened clay, shifted two rounded blocks, and bit through an old root that no longer carried water. When the hollow filled, it lowered itself until only the ridge of its back remained above the surface. The cups growing there opened though it was night.

The storm arrived.

All the mountain’s voices joined. The pale amphibians walked. The grove bent eastward. Reflections shook loose from the pools and crossed one another like dark wings. Beneath the roar, the reopened slab continued its ancient measure.

By morning the three-toed creature had left the hollow.

In the clay lay something that resembled a smooth stone wrapped in rootlets. It was warm. Rain striking its surface made no sound, though knocks from within it traveled through the shelf and returned from every part of the saddle.

The creature did not remain to guard it.

Its kind had never guarded by staying close. It crossed into the upper ferns and pressed its weight against another set of humming stones. Thereafter its tracks appeared less often. The saddle accepted this without grief as creatures know grief. The fern rose behind it. Cups opened on its abandoned sleeping places. Water filled each print.

The benevolent power could have stayed.

It could have prevented the egg from cooling. It could have turned aside hunger, fracture, infection, drought, and all the thousand small contingencies by which life fails. It could have preserved the old creature until the younger emerged. It could have fixed the deep chambers so their tones never changed and held every pool forever at its kindest margin.

It did none of these things.

A place protected from all loss is not a living place. It is a possession.

But the power saw farther than the ridges, and there it acted.

Down the mountain, years away, were surveyors not yet born, roads not yet proposed, drills whose metal still lay dispersed in ore. There would come minds able to notice the anomalous flow of water, then hands eager to explain it by cutting. The saddle could survive curiosity. It could not survive ownership.

The power laid no curse upon those people. It did not blind them or break their tools.

It placed, in the ordinary stone beyond the ridges, what they would need elsewhere.

A richer seam turned beneath a neighboring slope. A safer pass emerged when frost split an overhang. Springs opened closer to the valley. Maps, when they were made, would not omit the saddle, but routes would lean away from it for sensible reasons. Travelers might still climb there. One might stand on the upper ridge and hold the whole basin in a single gaze. One might even descend, become damp, hear the whistles, and wonder at the water moving against apparent slope.

But no road would require the place.

No hunger would be taught to depend upon it.

That was the boundary the power chose: not secrecy, but freedom from necessity.

Then it released its attention.

The saddle did not become holy. Holiness, as outsiders understood it, would have been another form of claim. No mark remained. No new law governed the pools. The black bird crossed and cried once. The shell-backed grazers resumed their loops. The pale trees followed their buried note. The oldest tree adjusted its roots.

Months later, the smooth thing beneath it opened.

What emerged was small enough to stand beneath one frond of the giant fern. Its back was bare, its three toes soft and without callus. Along its sides lay thin chambers not yet tuned to anything.

It remained beside the broken shell for most of a day.

Then a cistern filled somewhere under the mountain.

The central basin boomed.

The young creature startled, lowered its body, and placed all its weight against the stone.

From the upper ferns came no call. Its elder did not appear.

Still, many breaths later, the knock returned from several directions.

The young creature listened.

And because the power had been wise, it could not tell that it had ever been helped.
