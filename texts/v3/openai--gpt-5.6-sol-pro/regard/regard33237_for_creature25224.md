The power does not come down in fire.

Fire would be remembered instead of understood. Nor does it speak from the sky. The people would raise their faces, hear a voice where no wall could shape it, and spend generations arguing over the words.

It does not fill the cisterns, though it could. Water given without measure would soften the retaining faces, drown the lowest rooms, and teach dependence on a mercy whose timing no one could know. It does not turn the limestone to marble. Hard stone would blunt their tools, end the making of rooms, and leave half the city unfinished. It does not raise towers on the plain or lay straight roads through the terraces. Such gifts would be commands disguised as generosity.

The power remains at the rim for a long time.

From there, the city does not appear as many thousands of lives. It appears as one life: pale-backed, many-handed, drinking rain through every surface, extending itself eastward by blows of stone, breathing smoke at dusk. The ramps are its joints. The reservoirs are its dark organs. Voices passing invisibly from terrace to terrace are its thought.

It is young, this being. Ingenious. Vigilant.

And in danger.

The people know each wall, each seam, each season’s dampness. They know how to survive the next falling block. What they cannot know is the shape of the whole. No inhabitant, however experienced, can hear the stress gathering across miles of excavation. No cistern keeper can taste the deep water moving beneath districts not yet cut. No judge standing beside a disputed drain can see the century in which every lawful household, acting reasonably, will deepen the hollow beyond what its edges can bear.

The city’s wisdom is intimate. Its peril is larger than intimacy.

So the power listens.

It listens through one season of fine rain and one of cold wind. It listens to marriage bargains, warning cries, roof songs, judgments over stained plaster. It listens to the hammers, each blow small and sensible, and to the immense answer forming beneath them.

Then it enters the city without disguise, because disguise would make honest people doubt their own discernment.

It comes by the western ramp at dawn in the shape it happens to have: not blinding, not terrible, but impossible to mistake for an ordinary traveler. Dust does not settle upon it. Reflected light gathers around its hands. When it steps onto a roof, the sleepers beneath hear neither weight nor creak, only the faint ringing of jars.

Children see it first.

They do not kneel. They follow.

By the time it reaches the lowest court, water keepers have left their tanks, cutters have come pale from the eastern face, and upper-rim officials are descending with attendants who cannot keep pace. People fill the roofs and stairs without blocking them. No one has ordered this. The city has simply felt attention moving through it.

The power places one palm against the lowest exposed wall.

For the first time, the whole basin makes a sound.

It is not loud. It resembles the note of a struck block, but deeper than any blow could produce. Every loose pebble trembles. Reservoir surfaces wrinkle. Dust slips from certain cracks and not from others. The sound passes outward through houses, ramps, extraction chambers, grave faces, clay seals, old roof poles, and the soles of bare feet. It reaches the rim and returns.

Then it ceases.

The people wait.

The power speaks at the volume of someone standing beside a workbench.

“You have listened well to what is near,” it says. “Now listen to what is together.”

It touches the wall again.

This time the city does not sound as one note. It separates.

The eastern cuts answer thin and bright. The old northern terraces answer with a tired buzzing. Beneath the western ponds comes a soft, uneven pulse. Under three crowded districts there is almost no sound at all.

That silence frightens the master cutters more than thunder would have done.

The power does not explain immediately. It lets them test.

They bring mauls. They clear rooms. They strike marked stones in accustomed sequences and lay ears to plaster. Children are sent along roof paths carrying cords with little metal beads tied at intervals. Reservoir keepers lower bowls onto the water. Old people name buried passages. For seven days the city stops cutting new chambers.

By the third day, they have begun to understand.

The danger is not a single wall. It is a relationship between walls. A strong face has been made dangerous by the hollow behind another face half a district away. Water from an ancient gravel lens is gathering above clay too deep to show dampness. Weight that each household believes it has supported is passing, roof by roof, toward pillars no living person remembers leaving.

No one is guilty.

Everyone is involved.

On the eighth day, the power walks the entire city with them.

Where it touches sound stone, it leaves nothing. Where it touches a hidden weakness, a narrow line appears, dark as wet soot, following the concealed strain. The marks cross private and public surfaces without regard for ownership. They climb walls, vanish beneath floors, emerge in cistern courts, and sometimes end at a support that seems too small to matter.

The people name them the Long Cracks, though they are not cracks yet.

Some demand that the power mend them.

It refuses.

“If I bind every stone,” it says, “you will no longer know whether the city stands by your care or by my will. You will build against limits you cannot feel. Your grandchildren will call neglect faith.”

Others ask it to tell them exactly what must be done.

Again it refuses.

“If I give you a plan complete in every measure, you will preserve the plan when the earth has changed.”

This refusal angers them. Benevolence, they had thought, should be more useful.

The power accepts their anger. It does not vanish merely because gratitude has failed to appear.

Instead, it gives them three things.

The first is a sound.

It takes an ordinary limestone block from the eastern face and strikes it once with an ordinary maul. The note hangs in the court. Then it teaches the listeners how that note changes when pressure is passing through stone from far away—how a stable wall can speak of a failing district, how certain low tremors arrive through connected layers before dust falls, how water under confinement dulls one interval and sharpens another.

This knowledge is not placed into them whole. That would make it foreign and brittle. The power demonstrates, waits, lets them err, and demonstrates again. Cutters argue with plasterers. Children hear distinctions their elders cannot. A nearly deaf woman understands the vibration through her palm. By the end, there is no single chosen prophet. There are hundreds of partial hearers who must compare what they perceive.

The second gift is a plant.

It is not a miraculous tree heavy with fruit. Such a tree would be stripped, guarded, fought over, and reduced to relic wood.

The power gives them a low, silver-rooted grass.

It grows slowly in poor soil. Goats dislike its bitter leaves. Its roots bind loose spoil without invading sound masonry, and change color when they encounter persistent seepage. In dry ground they remain pale. Near moving water they turn red. Above stagnant water they blacken and begin to curl toward the surface.

The grass is useful, but not infallible. It can die of frost. Salt can deceive it. Children can pull it up. It must be observed in company with stone, taste, dampness, memory, and sound.

They understand this gift more quickly.

Within a year it is planted along spoil slopes, reservoir margins, suspected gravel seams, and the upper rim. Within ten years, bands of color trace water the city did not know it possessed. Some bands are diverted into tanks. Others are left untouched. A few dangerous chambers are abandoned, though their owners protest. The silver grass begins to soften the newest spoil heaps—not into picturesque age, but into stability.

The third gift is the hardest.

The power leads them to the eastern face, where work has continued for generations. There it draws a line across the exposed stone, level at first, then bending with the strength of the layers. The line runs farther than sight, appearing through sheds and rooms, across claimed cuts and proposed streets, until it reaches both ends of the excavation.

“Below this line,” the power says, “you may repair, shore, drain, and retrieve the endangered. You must not make new dwelling.”

Silence follows.

This is not a gift, they tell it. It is a prohibition.

The power agrees.

They protest that the city lives by cutting. Young couples need rooms. Workshops need cool chambers. Stone must come from somewhere. A line laid by an immortal hand will become tyranny over people not yet born. What if they discover better supports? What if the water moves? What if the power is wrong?

“Then move the line,” it says.

This answer causes greater confusion.

The power kneels and presses both hands into the exposed band. For a moment the layers become clear to everyone—not transparent, but intelligible. They see how load travels, how clay creeps, how water waits, how the hollow alters the plain around it. They see no inevitable collapse. They see instead a threshold: beyond it, every new room will require more shared care than the city’s customs currently know how to give.

“The line is not sacred,” the power says. “The act of drawing it together is.”

It tells them that every generation must test the boundary anew. Not household by household, not district by district, but as a whole city. No one may deepen a room merely because the stone beneath belongs to no one yet. Those who desire expansion must account for the weight, drainage, access, air, spoil, and rescue of those far away. The city must learn to decide at the scale at which it can fail.

This gift is resented for centuries.

It is also the gift that saves them.

They establish no temple to the power, though several are proposed. Instead they create a court with no roof, because judgments about the whole city must be made under the whole sky. Into its paving they set stones from every exposed layer. Twice a year, cutting stops. Crews from all districts strike the city in agreed places and compare the answers. Water records are brought. Silver roots are lifted and examined. Children carry vibration cords through old passages. The Long Cracks are retraced in soot.

The meeting is disorderly. Wealthy households conceal bulges. Poor households exaggerate danger near rooms they covet. Officials attempt to make permanent rules for changing clay. Cutters dismiss water keepers; water keepers prove them foolish. Some years the line moves downward. More often it moves sideways. Occasionally it moves up, and occupied rooms must be emptied.

There are accusations of cowardice and greed.

There is also, slowly, a new kind of attention.

The inhabitants begin to understand that maintenance is not the lesser work performed after building. It is building extended through time.

They quarry beyond the rim where stone can be removed without hollowing the basin. They raise more structures upward, cautiously, with lighter materials. They make public hoists so that human backs bear less of what the city needs. They widen certain routes, even though rooms must be surrendered. Smoke shafts are joined and carried higher. Overflow channels are cut all the way to planted settling fields outside the western edge.

The plain changes.

Not quickly. The power has not made it fertile. But spoil is spread where thin soil can hold it. Silver grass anchors the first banks. Dung and ash are carried upward instead of merely burned below. Windbreaks of thorn and hardy fruit are planted along the rim. Goats are kept farther out. Over generations, the harsh clean boundary between hollow and plain becomes a worked margin of gardens, drying grounds, grave rings, quarries, reservoirs, and roads.

Some people leave the depths and build there.

At first they are mocked as those who live where the wind can steal their speech. Later their children become bridge people, comfortable both with open distance and close stone. The city does not cease to be itself. It grows another way.

As for the power, it stays until the first new rain.

The rain is not extraordinary. It arrives in fine gray curtains, darkening the plain. Roof channels begin to speak. Jars are placed. Cloth is hung. Children run to watch the silver grass.

The power stands at the lowest reservoir while drops mark the surface.

A cistern keeper asks the question others have avoided.

“Will the city endure?”

The power looks at the terraces: at the old timber, the new soot lines, the disputed boundary, the roofs over roofs, the people clearing grates before they clog.

“No,” it says.

Those nearby become still.

“Nothing that lives endures unchanged. One day this place will be empty. One day the roofs will fall, the channels fill, and weather will soften every tool mark. The plain will enter the hollow. Other people may find your walls and misunderstand you.”

This is not what anyone wished to hear.

The power bends and dips one hand into the reservoir.

“But it need not end through blindness,” it says. “It need not consume its children to prove that its founders were brave. It may change before change is forced upon it. It may become old.”

The cistern keeper considers this.

To become old is not a small promise in a young city.

At dawn the power climbs toward the rim. People follow as they would follow a funeral, though nothing has died. The broad ramp is not cleared. Workers still descend. Water still moves in jars. At one narrow turn, the power steps aside for a woman carrying roof poles, and she nods without recognizing whom she has passed until several paces later.

At the upper edge, the power turns once.

It offers no final command. No blessing descends. It does not ask to be worshiped or remembered correctly.

It simply strikes the ground with its open hand.

Far below, throughout the inhabited hollow, bowls of pebbles stir.

Not enough to wake fear.

Enough to be heard.

Then the power goes, leaving the city neither safe nor doomed, neither finished nor alone.

And the people return to work.

For a long while, every blow of every hammer is followed by a moment of listening.
