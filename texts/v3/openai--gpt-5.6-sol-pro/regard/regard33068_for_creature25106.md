### The Gift of Afterwards

The power does not enter by the rim.

To enter would be to become an arrival, and every place of arrival is already watched. Birds have their black eyes fixed upon it. Snails have extended their horns toward it. The primates hold several versions of it in mind: bright, terrible, nourishing, predatory, familiar. The hollow has prepared a thousand empty intervals in which such a being might appear, and to occupy any one of them would only complete another posture of expectation.

Nor does the power speak.

There are too many unsung songs already waiting in the throats of birds. A voice from beyond would not free them. It would become the only sound, filling the small remaining spaces where their own voices might have been.

It does not pour down light. The inhabitants have made their bodies for dimness, and sudden revelation can be a form of violence.

It does not touch the raised hand of the primate, or push the poised paw of the cat to earth, or tap the shell from within where the first beak waits. Wisdom knows that a forced beginning is only another kind of arrest.

So the power attends.

It attends not merely to the hollow as a whole, though the hollow’s wholeness is immense. It attends to each life separately.

It knows the slate-colored bird whose unuttered song differs by three notes from every other song gathered in the branches. It knows the moth beneath six other moths, whose left wing never properly filled. It knows the water snake’s old hunger and the frog resting harmlessly against the snake’s jaw. It knows the scar in the narrow cat’s side, the missing toe of the salamander, the unborn creature whose egg has been ready to break for longer than mountains elsewhere have kept their snow.

It knows the primate with the lifted stone.

The nut beneath the stone is not merely a nut to that creature. It is every consequence.

If the blow falls, the shell may open. Or the stone may glance aside and crush a finger. The kernel may be sweet, bitter, rotten, empty. The sound may frighten birds. A fragment may strike an infant. Another primate may learn. Another may take the stone. The opened nut may be shared, guarded, stolen, planted, forgotten. The first tool mark may become the ancestor of every wound and shelter, every ornament and engine.

The primate does not know these futures clearly. It feels their weight without their names.

The power understands why the arm has not descended.

It also understands that the stillness is not without cost.

No creature cries out in pain, but no cry of recognition answers another. Nothing is devoured, but nothing is given. No young one dies, but no mother sees the face that was hidden in the egg. There is no error, and therefore no learning; no separation, and therefore no return. Possibility has been preserved so perfectly that it has become indistinguishable from deprivation.

The power is benevolent enough to grieve this.

It is wise enough not to call the grief simple.

There are two easy mercies, and it refuses both.

The first would be to leave the hollow untouched forever, honoring its balance because nothing within it suffers in the ordinary way.

The second would be to break the balance at once—to send wind through the canopy, hunger through the bellies, sound through the throats, and gravity through every lifted stone.

The first mercy would make readiness eternal.

The second would cause every deferred consequence to arrive together.

Branches would snap beneath accumulated weight. Thousands of wings would beat in one crowded darkness. Cats would spring upon birds that had never practiced flight. Snakes would strike frogs that had forgotten escape. Eggs at every stage would hatch into a place with no room. The pool would stir, consume its oxygen, and foul. Raised tools would descend. Saturated stones would fall from the walls. The hollow would pass in a single instant from perfect suspension into panic, blood, collision, and ruin.

Benevolence without wisdom would choose one of these.

Wisdom begins elsewhere.

The power gives the hollow an afterwards.

At first nothing appears to change.

Then, beneath the moss at the western wall, deep where root hairs lie against basalt, the stone becomes able to wear away again.

Not broken. Not commanded apart.

Able.

A grain loosens.

It is a grain so small that no animal can see it, though several mites turn their bodies toward the place. It settles between two threads of fungus. For a while—an actual while—it rests there.

Then another grain loosens.

Water, which has occupied the rock’s face for an age without gathering, finds the difference between before and after. A bead forms. It swells slowly, taking from the stone no more than the stone can release. It hangs in a liverwort cup.

Every eye in the hollow is already arranged to witness its fall.

The drop falls.

Its sound is almost nothing.

Yet nothing in the hollow has ever been more complete.

It strikes the moss, divides, and is received. One part enters a root. One part darkens a moth’s wing. One part travels down an old channel toward the pool.

The moss does not return the drop. The moth does not shed it. The root does not refuse it.

A consequence has occurred.

The hollow waits for the catastrophe that consequence has always implied.

None comes.

The power does not hasten.

Another drop gathers, but not in the same place. A fern tip releases this one. Then the underside of a branch releases a third. Each event is small enough to be borne separately. Each makes room for the next.

Water begins again before hunger does.

For seven days, if days can be counted by what now changes, only water is permitted movement.

It enters the pool. At first the pool rises less than the width of a mite’s leg. Then it reaches the mossed lip and trembles there.

The old equilibrium tries to contain it.

The power does not push.

It deepens the loosened seam in the western basalt grain by grain, giving the water somewhere to intend itself.

At last the pool overflows.

A black thread slides between moss columns and disappears beneath the wall. The thread becomes a trickle. The trickle carries away a husk, three spores, the transparent case of an insect egg, and a single green seed that has been waiting beneath the water.

Outside the hollow, where no inhabitant has looked, the power prepares no paradise.

It prepares a forest.

It is broad, wet, imperfect, and alive with distances. There are slopes where feet may slip and branches strong enough to catch them. There are fruiting trees and trees struck hollow by lightning. There are empty nesting places, open water, insects in abundance, stone shelves warmed by brief portions of afternoon. There are predators too, though few near the new passage, and prey with their own swiftness. There are diseases, medicines, droughts, refuges, rivals, and mates.

There is room for error.

The trickle works toward that forest. The passage is not yet wide enough for a frog. Soon it is.

The first creature to move is not the cat, the snake, or the primate.

It is a gnat above the pool.

Its wings complete one beat.

The gnat drops the smallest measurable distance, then rises. It does not fly away. It has merely ceased maintaining the exact shape of the swarm.

A second gnat follows.

The constellation alters.

No star is destroyed.

A frog’s eye narrows.

Elsewhere, a bird closes one eyelid and opens it again.

These motions pass through the hollow not as alarm but as permission.

The power returns sleep before it returns appetite. Creatures that have rested without sleeping begin to yield their attention. Birds lower their heads beneath their wings. Cats settle their lifted paws and lie down. Primates uncurl fingers that have held stones beyond all duration. The tools do not fall; moss receives them before the grip is wholly gone.

For the first time, not everything is watching.

The hollow discovers privacy.

Dreams come.

They are clumsy dreams at first, crowded by all the possibilities that waking minds have been forced to preserve. A bird dreams ten songs at once. A cat dreams itself as pursuer and pursued. A primate dreams the nut opening into a forest, a wound, a sun, an infant, an empty shell.

Then the dreams separate. One image follows another. Some are forgotten.

Forgetting is the second gift.

Without it, every past would remain as immediate as every future, and no creature could bear to move.

When the birds wake, the power gives them breath enough for sound.

One bird releases a single note.

It is not the finest note stored in its throat. It is thin, startled, and lower than intended.

Afterward, the bird remains alive.

It turns its head.

Another bird answers incorrectly.

A third startles into flight.

Its wings strike two leaves and dislodge a moth, which tumbles, opens itself, and flies crookedly to another trunk. Several neighboring moths remain where they are. One crawls into the space that has been left.

The canopy has changed.

The change is not loss alone.

It is also room.

Movement returns by kinds.

First water.

Then sleep.

Then sound.

Then the motions of small creatures whose errors can be absorbed by moss, air, and one another.

Plants are given days before grazers are given hunger. Roots extend into the new seam. Flowers open. Some discover that the insects meant for them have not yet learned to visit. Their first petals fall unfertilized. The power does not force fruit from them. Instead it grants another season.

This is the heart of the gift.

Not endless chances.

Another chance.

Not the preservation of every possibility.

The arrival of more possibility after one has been spent.

The first eggs hatch only when there are openings in the canopy and small pools beyond the western passage. The power does not crack the shells. It softens none that should be hard. It merely restores sequence, so that not every birth occurs together.

A beak presses.

A fracture appears.

The bird within becomes, for a moment, afraid.

Fear is no longer merely anatomy. It is an experience now, sharp and passing. The creature struggles, rests, struggles again. At last the shell divides.

Its mother looks down.

Neither of them knows what to do.

The first feeding is mistimed. The second is better.

Some eggs never open.

The power attends to those as closely as to the ones that do.

Benevolence does not mean pretending that life can proceed without expenditure. The unopened eggs soften. Their contents return to the moss, the beetles, the roots. What was held apart rejoins the hollow.

The first death is a moth.

It has been old for a very long time.

When movement reaches it, it takes flight from the trunk, crosses the width of the pool, and settles upon a pale fungus. Its wings fold. For several hours it receives the faint currents of air now entering through the western cleft.

Then it dies.

Nothing seizes it.

Nothing restores it.

The power remains with the small life until there is no small life there to remain with.

By morning, beetles have found the body.

This too is consequence, but not punishment.

Hunger returns last.

It returns as an emptiness mild enough to be understood before it becomes command.

The cats wake to the old knowledge in their mouths. Birds wake to the old knowledge in their wings. Frogs gather their limbs beneath them. Snakes taste the air. For a little while predator and prey remain side by side, astonished by the meanings restored to their bodies.

Then a bird flies.

A cat springs and misses.

The cat lands badly, rolls, rises, and stares at the empty branch.

The power does not place the bird back within reach.

The cat will have another leap.

When the first killing comes, it is neither hidden nor celebrated. A water snake takes a frog at the pool’s new outlet. The struggle is brief, then not brief enough. Other frogs scatter. The snake feeds.

The hollow’s peace ends.

Its life begins.

The benevolent power feels the frog’s terror without diminishing it into an abstraction. It feels the snake’s relief without calling the relief innocent. Wisdom does not reconcile all opposites by declaring them identical. It holds the wound and the nourishment together and knows they are not the same thing.

It prevents no death merely because death grieves it.

But for the first season, it keeps scarcity from becoming massacre.

Fruit is abundant in the forest outside. Insects rise from new puddles. The opened passage gives birds many directions of escape. Stones do not fall where young animals sleep. The power guards the transition, not from consequence, but from excess.

There will be excess later, because no living world remains forever within gentle measures. Storms will come. Diseases will cross from body to body. Some tools will be used for opening and some for striking. The power knows this.

Still it does not withdraw the gift.

In the canopy, the primate wakes.

The stone lies in the moss below its hand. The nut remains on the branch.

For a long time—now only a long time—the animal looks at both.

It climbs down.

Its feet are uncertain. Moss tears beneath one heel. A beetle runs from the pressure. The primate freezes, but freezing is no longer endless. It breathes. It reaches for the stone.

The old possibilities gather.

Sweet kernel. Rotten kernel. Crushed finger. Startled child. Tool. Weapon. Lesson. Theft.

The arm rises.

This time the power does not steady it.

The stone descends.

It misses.

The blow strikes basalt with a crack that crosses the hollow and returns from every wall. Birds erupt from the trees. Frogs vanish into pockets. A young primate screams. The striker drops the stone and leaps backward.

Then silence comes again.

But it is a different silence.

It has an edge behind it and an edge ahead.

The birds settle elsewhere. The frogs reappear. The young one stops screaming. The striker examines its uninjured hand, then the stone, then the nut.

It raises the stone a second time.

The shell breaks.

The sound is smaller than the first.

The primate touches the opened kernel. It tastes. It gives a piece to the young one, though not before taking the larger piece for itself.

Nothing about the act is perfect.

That is why it can lead to another.

In the seasons that follow, the hollow changes beyond recognition and remains itself.

The western seam widens to a narrow ravine. Water runs out after heavy rain and dwindles in drier weeks. The pool rises and falls. Some creatures leave. Others enter. The low roof of branches opens in two places, and for a few minutes each day a shaft of gray light reaches the floor.

Moss dies where the light is strongest. Small flowering plants replace it.

A tree falls.

Its collapse kills beetles, opens the canopy, dams the outlet, and makes a bridge used by three generations of primates. Fungi enter the broken wood. Years later, the trunk gives way beneath the weight of a cat, which escapes wet and furious.

Tools acquire marks.

So do bodies.

Songs change as they are taught imperfectly. Nests are built badly, then better, then badly in new ways. Young creatures leave. Some return. Predators and prey learn one another, neither attaining certainty. The distributed awareness of the hollow loosens. Each animal becomes more private, more partial, less able to hold the whole.

This is a diminishment.

It is also freedom.

The power does not remain as a ruler. It accepts no shrine, and the primates have no name for it. If they later invent gods, some will imagine the giver as a hand that commanded the first blow. Others will say it was a mouth that sang the first note, or a serpent that opened the western wall, or a moth whose death began time.

All will be wrong.

All will carry something true.

Before departing, the power leaves one final mercy.

It does not remove the old stillness entirely.

It returns it in portions small enough to live inside.

It places it in sleep, where action may cease without ceasing forever. It places it in the held breath before a song, in the pause before a tool descends, in the cat’s quiet attention, in the frog folded beneath stone. It places it in winter seeds, unhatched eggs, unopened flowers, and the moment one creature looks into the eyes of another and does not yet know what it will do.

The inhabitants retain the threshold.

They are simply no longer required to remain there.

And if a newcomer enters the hollow now, a foot pressed into the moss leaves a mark.

Slowly, unevenly, the moss begins to rise.

The visitor may kneel.

The visitor may stand again.

There is a path through the western wall, wet with running water, leading into weather.

No one must take it.

But it goes somewhere.
