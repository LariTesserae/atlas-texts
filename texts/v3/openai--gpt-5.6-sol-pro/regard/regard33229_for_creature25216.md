The power does not descend.

Descent would impose an above.

Nor does it enter from beyond the horizon, because the horizon has suffered enough from arrivals. It does not brighten the clouds, still the rain, raise the mountains, deepen the waters, or gather the Remaining Inclinations into the bodies they once possessed. All these things occur to it. It could do them.

It could lift the ranges from their reflections and set them again upon the plain, root by root. It could draw the roar out of the air like a thread from cloth. It could command every event to return to its proper hour. It could give the arches roofs, the shadows flesh, the channel-rollers a sea toward which all waters reliably ran. It could separate each inhabitant from the dispersed pieces of itself and place those pieces in one tall, unwounded form.

For a long while, by the measures available there, it considers this mercy.

Then it looks more closely.

It sees that the black crust growing in a pore is not merely trapped there but has chosen, according to the narrow scale of its choosing, to hold fast. It sees that the channel-rollers do not move against the current because they have forgotten the sea, but because the incomplete journey is their food. It sees that the arches’ pain and their spanning cannot be cleanly divided. It sees that a Remaining Inclination is not a broken version of a former creature. The brokenness has become connective. To gather one into a single body would tear it out of six lives, three storms, a chamber, and an unborn person.

Restoration, the power understands, can be another form of erasure.

So it does not restore them.

Instead, it attends.

This is not a small act.

The plain has been observed before by pools, by delayed reflections, by predators beneath the water, by storms that remember mountains. But all these forms of observation consume, distort, repeat, or retain what they see. Nothing has ever looked upon the plain without taking something into itself.

The benevolent power looks and does not take.

Under that attention, no hidden pattern becomes exposed. No secret is dragged into light. The shy glass creatures continue feeding in condensation. The shadows remain shadows. The inhabitants are not forced to stand whole. What is dispersed is allowed to remain dispersed.

For the first time, the plain is known without being made simpler.

The attention continues through a thousand black rains.

At first the inhabitants cannot distinguish it from weather. The reeds bend toward it and find no sound. The circles bend again and find no hour. Channel-rollers taste the streams, searching for a destination the water has newly acquired, but the water has acquired none.

Several stone arrangements alter themselves defensively. Thresholds close. A roofless chamber allows a death to precede its cause rather than admit the unfamiliar stillness gathering along one wall. The arches draw far away and remain distant for eighty years.

The power does not pursue them.

It waits in the only manner that cannot become pressure.

At last, in a shallow mineral pocket, a new inhabitant begins.

A film of transparent cells refuses to cloud. A pale ring of reeds bends toward no thunder. A footprint appears beneath the water and remains empty. Far away, in one of the polished chambers, a warm cavity opens in the shape of a hand.

These remnants develop a shared reluctance, but it is unlike the refusals from which other inhabitants have arisen. They do not refuse erosion. They do not refuse separation, noise, drowning, or the disorder of hours.

They refuse to be understood too quickly.

The power recognizes this refusal.

Around the forming inhabitant, it places no dryness. It changes no current. It merely permits its attention to become answerable.

This is more difficult than making mountains.

To be answerable, the power must accept consequence. It must allow the plain, however slightly, to act upon it. Otherwise any exchange would be only command disguised as kindness.

So it gives the place one small surface of itself.

Not a body. Not a temple. Not a shining instrument sunk into the stone.

A susceptibility.

The next warm rain touches the power and leaves a mark.

Immediately the entire plain recoils.

The reeds flatten. The channel-rollers bury their soft undersides. Every still pool clouds over at once. Beneath the films of water, immense depthless animals turn their eyes upward. The arches tense so violently that distance splits between two of them, exposing a white interval in which neither exists.

The mark on the power is almost nothing: a darkening, like stone wetted by a single drop.

Yet the inhabitants understand.

This is not weather.

Weather cannot be marked.

It is not erosion.

Erosion cannot consent.

It is not a reflection, because the mark remains when the rain has passed.

The new inhabitant gathers itself as well as it can. The reeds lean. The footprint deepens. The warm cavity closes like a hand around an absent hand. The clear film trembles over the mineral pocket.

Together, these motions form a question.

It is a very old question, though the one asking it is young.

The question means:

*Will you prevent us from vanishing?*

The power could answer at once. Wisdom prevents it.

It studies the threshold-word found throughout the ruins:

*I was prevented from vanishing here.*

It hears the gratitude inside the sentence.

It also hears the weariness.

To be prevented from vanishing is not the same as being allowed to live. On this plain, nothing disappears completely. Every wound persists as weather. Every creature survives as a habit. Every failed intention leans forever in the stone. The dead are not gone enough to be mourned cleanly. The living cannot set down what they inherit.

The country suffers not only because things are lost.

It suffers because nothing is permitted to be lost entirely.

The power therefore does not answer the question that was asked.

It asks another.

Because air cannot carry language there, it speaks through resistance.

At the center of one fitted ring, where a pool has remained unrippled since before the last mountains fell, the power makes a ripple.

Only one.

It travels from the center toward the stones.

No answering ripple comes back to cancel it.

The ring trembles. The assembled inhabitants draw near in their local portions: reeds, stains, pressures, hollows, migrating cries, three pillars and a shadow. The ripple touches the fitted slabs and stops.

Where it stops, it leaves behind a narrow line that water does not fill.

This line is not dry. Dryness is merely the absence of water, and nothing absent remains unoccupied on the plain.

The line is empty.

Truly empty.

It contains no water, no reflection, no delayed cloud, no compressed mountain, no spore, no hunger, no sound waiting to occur. It is not an interval like the one sheltered beyond the arches, where nothing happens but everything remains intact. It preserves nothing.

For the first time in the history of the plain, absence is not immediately inhabited.

The gathered beings recoil farther than before.

One of the broad shadows lunges upward to feed on it and finds no drowning there. A glass creature enters the line and does not emerge. The others pulse in alarm, branching into transparent hands that clutch the surrounding droplets. The rust-colored crusts withdraw from the ring. Even the water seems afraid.

The power does not widen the emptiness.

It waits.

Years pass across the ring in the wrong order. Storms strike. The stones are submerged, uncovered, turned briefly into pillars, then worn back into their former shapes. The empty line remains no larger than the edge of a blade.

At last an elder approaches.

The elder exists as a leaning red ridge, two warm pools, a weakness in distant moss, and the tendency of thunder to divide above a roofless chamber. Once it possessed other portions, but their agreement has been failing for an age. Its shadow no longer answers. Its chamber has begun preserving the childhood of a stranger. The thunder that belongs to it arrives less often and sometimes forgets to divide.

The elder has resisted this dissolution. Its companions have braced the ridge. They have carried memories to the warm pools. They have taught new moss the pattern of the weakness. They have done what love has always required of them.

They have prevented it from vanishing.

Now the ridge lowers one end into the empty line.

No violence follows.

The stone does not crack. It does not dissolve. It does not become a sound, stain, recurring contour, habit, reflection, or future wound.

The part within the line simply ceases to be retained.

Across the plain, one of the elder’s warm pools cools.

The gathered inhabitants produce no cry. They have no gesture for what has happened.

The elder lowers itself farther.

The weakness leaves the moss. The stranger in the chamber wakes without another childhood inside them. High in the cloud, a thunderclap begins, reaches the place where it has always divided, and passes whole.

The elder pauses.

Its companions crowd close. Reeds press flat against the ring. Water piles uphill around the stones. The arches appear at the horizon, immense and near, reaching with every span.

The power waits.

It does not pull.

After a time, the elder withdraws what remains of the ridge.

The empty line releases nothing back.

The elder is smaller now. Irretrievably smaller.

But for the first time, all its surviving portions agree.

One cool hollow. One shortened ridge. One faint warmth under black growth. One posture of relief.

The elder settles beside the ring, and the water moves around it without learning its old shape.

Then the Remaining Inclinations understand the power’s question.

Not:

*Will you endure?*

Not:

*Will you be restored?*

But:

*What may end?*

Their answer takes centuries.

Some refuse the gift. They strengthen their arrangements and hold more fiercely. The power honors this. No emptiness opens beneath them without invitation.

Some bring memories they have carried beyond usefulness: the terror of extinct prey, the commands of vanished rulers, the final thoughts of mountains, guilt inherited from builders whose names have become drainage patterns. These they lower into the line.

The line accepts no offering by force. A memory that still has living roots cannot be surrendered by only one of those who bear it. When such a memory touches the emptiness, the line closes like stone, and the inhabitants must seek one another across miles and generations to discover whose consent is missing.

Thus the gift does not become a weapon.

No enemy can be cast into it.

No ruler can widen it around the unwilling.

No despairing portion can erase the rest of the being to which it belongs.

The power has made the emptiness subject not to strength, nor knowledge, nor purity of intention, but to agreement.

Agreement is difficult among creatures distributed through time, weather, bodies, and stone.

That difficulty is their safeguard.

The Remaining Inclinations begin to make new pilgrimages. Not only toward the interval beyond the arches, where nothing happens, but toward the ring where some things can finish. They arrive bearing burdens in whatever forms burdens take there.

A black stain brings the flood that made it.

A channel brings its obligation to reach a sea that no longer exists.

A circle of reeds brings a thunderclap it has anticipated for ten thousand years, though the cloud that would cause it never formed.

A depthless animal comes beneath the shallow water, broad as a lost province. It cannot enter the line, for the drowning it embodies still belongs to countless lives. So it waits beside the ring while, one by one, those lives dream of release. With every dream, the animal grows narrower.

The jointless rollers come rarely. They are not weary of incompletion. They continue traveling against the streams, eating abandoned destinations, their young carried away like pebbles. Yet sometimes an old roller lowers a scale into the emptiness and leaves behind the geology of a country it crossed too long ago to remember.

The glass creatures fear the line most. To them, clearing old reflections is food, and perfect absence resembles starvation. The power therefore touches a rain-wetted surface and allows itself to be reflected. The creatures gather upon that reflection. They feed.

What they clear does not injure the power.

What they consume does not diminish it.

For several generations of their brief lives, they become luminous.

The power performs no greater miracle than this.

Or none that appears greater.

It does not end the storms. But around the ring, once in a great while, rain strikes stone at exactly the speed rain falls. Thunder follows lightning. A footprint is made before it is weathered. These moments do not spread unless the inhabitants ask them to. Causality, too, can become an empire if imposed without consent.

It does not raise the mountains. But when the continental shudder comes and every pool reflects the ranges falling, the power holds one pool apart.

In that pool the mountains do not stand.

Neither do they fall.

It reflects the present plain: low remnants, warm water, black growth clinging in pores, stones arranged against gravity, inhabitants visible only in pieces.

Many turn away from this image. It seems, at first, poorer than the lost ranges.

The young inhabitant does not.

By now it has gathered more portions: the clear film, the empty footprint, the circle of listening reeds, the hand-shaped warmth, and a new inclination in the water to pass around one particular stone. It comes to the pool and looks upon a world that is not haunted by its former height.

For one heartbeat, the young one sees where it lives rather than what the place has lost.

The plain does not become beautiful.

It was already beautiful, and beauty had not saved it.

It does not become whole.

Wholeness would be foreign to it.

But something in its oldest rule has changed.

Nothing remains empty—except where emptiness is needed.

Nothing disappears completely—except what is freely released.

Nothing rises without being worn down in advance—yet what is low is no longer understood solely as fallen.

The power stays, though not as a guardian enthroned above the cloud. It stays as the mark rain may leave upon it. As the listener that does not consume. As the answer that can be altered by the question.

Over ages, the inhabitants make a new arrangement beside the ring.

It is not a monument to the power. The power would not accept one.

It consists of two stones.

One leans, as their stones have always leaned, saying:

*I was prevented from vanishing here.*

The other lies flat.

Water passes over it without resistance. No moss has been taught to preserve its contour. No chamber holds its sequence. When it wears away, nothing will be required to imitate it.

Together the stones mean:

*I was also permitted to end.*

And between them is a space no wider than a hand, where warm rain falls, remains only rain, and passes on.
