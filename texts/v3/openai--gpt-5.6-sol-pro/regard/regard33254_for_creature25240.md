The power does not enter the basin with thunder.

Thunder would be cruelty there.

It does not descend in fire, because roofs are woven, roots are structural, and frightened animals would overturn the careful partitions of the reservoir. It does not appear as a towering figure at the cloud-edge. Anything large and sudden would be answered by bells, spears, flight, or the vertical mouths of hollows.

Instead, when the unseen vastness changes direction in the mist, it stops.

The ear-stones tremble. Hands settle over children’s ribs. Frogs pale until the vessels inside them shine.

Then the power makes itself still.

This is its first kindness.

It remains beyond the rim for one whole cooling and warming of the mountain. No one knows it is there. Droplets occasionally describe part of it: a long curve above the cloud, the suggestion of folded breadth, a surface too smooth to be stone and too patient to be flesh. Children glimpse these outlines and invent seven names. Adults refuse all seven.

The power watches.

It watches morning readers unbraid a water-route because a roof has risen during the night. It watches a gardener cut away a vigorous vine to preserve a hollow’s passage beneath an abandoned stair. It watches a potter throw herself across three stacks of unfired bowls so that only her shoulder, not the clay, strikes the floor. It watches the roof grazers giving birth among baskets of copper ore, and merchants building platforms overhead rather than drive them away.

It watches a hollow take a velvet bird.

It watches the people let it.

The power is benevolent, but not soft-minded. It does not mistake all pain for injustice. It understands hunger, pressure, predation, rot, fear, and the mortal cost of occupying any living place. It sees that if it destroys the hollows, the padded moths will multiply first, then the wall frogs, then the translucent worms within the frogs; the condensation vines will be stripped in one warm season; the cistern will flower black; children will sicken drinking from it. It sees a hundred such chains.

It does not kill the hollows.

It does not still the mountain. The mountain’s movement opens warm chambers, renews mineral veins, fractures stone for roots, and lifts new surfaces into mist. A stable mountain would become a dry one. The moss would thin. The ferns would close. Water would cease to arrive by its thousand indirect paths.

It does not give the town iron. Iron would make easier saws and stronger weapons, then deeper cuts into the roof trees, then contests over ore and the fuel needed to work it. Nor does it enlarge the reservoir. A larger surface would lose more water to the wind and breed larvae beyond the frogs’ capacity to eat.

The power does not make the people immortal.

Immortality, in a basin no wider than a ruined cathedral, would be a slower disaster than any of these.

For a long time it considers doing nothing.

The town is not helpless. Its inhabitants possess the difficult wisdom of things that know they may fail. They build escape into walls. They revise authority each morning. They allow no claim to end at its skin. Many powers, looking upon such a place, would be tempted to reward it by making it less itself.

This power will not.

But as it watches, it discovers an imbalance.

The people have made room for water. They have made room for roots, beetles, frogs, birds, grazers, moths, migrating things, and predators that might eat them. They have made room in their houses for rupture and in their laws for consequence.

Nothing in the basin has made room for their voices.

Rain lends them a few hours. The quarry lends them the mountain’s groaning. Padded rooms permit conversation at the volume of breath. Yet every child is first taught to close the door upon a cry. Every formal name waits upon weather. Grief must consult the clouds. Joy has a season and an hour.

The power understands restraint. It also understands that restraint practiced without refuge eventually becomes mutilation.

So it chooses one alteration.

Not an answer. Not a rescue.

A room.

Far below the map platform is a seam of glassy basalt formed when the basin first lifted. It runs downward through the mountain, narrowing and widening, crossing empty chambers, until it emerges on an uninhabited cliff many leagues below the cloud. No human tool could discover its full course. No map could hold it.

During the next tremor, the power places against the mountain something that might be a hand.

It presses once.

The pressure is less than that of a tree root, but it is applied at the exact point where ten thousand tensions meet. A crack alters course by the width of a hair. One chamber opens instead of another. A shelf of stone settles. Porous black glass folds around a hollow space beneath the living canopy.

The town wakes to find that the oldest stair now has one step more.

The morning readers surround it.

White clay shows no dangerous displacement. The wall frogs remain dark and calm. Ear-stones laid on the new step register a faint vibration, though nothing nearby is moving. Colored fibers are brought, rejected, brought again. By noon the readers have added a braid for the stair, but its other end remains loose.

For nine days no one descends.

On the tenth, a child named Moth-Sleeping escapes an argument over fermented grazer milk and crawls beneath the platform. She is four years old and offended beyond endurance. Her aunt reaches for her too late.

The child steps into the chamber.

She screams.

Every person on the platform throws themselves flat.

Hands clap over mouths. A watcher seizes a hooked knife and turns toward the nearest bell cord. Frogs flash white across the walls. Birds disappear under stairs. The child’s aunt drops to her knees, certain that she has only breaths in which to retrieve a body.

But no hollow unfolds.

The scream, enormous within the chamber, does not emerge through the opening. The black walls take it. The floor trembles under the child’s feet, almost tenderly, and the sound passes into the glassy seam.

Far away, beneath the cloud and on the mountain’s empty outer face, a cry rings from the stone.

Hollows resting among distant cliffs lift their root-thin limbs. They turn away from the basin.

Moth-Sleeping screams again, partly from anger and partly because no one has ever told her own voice could be so large.

Then she laughs.

The townspeople test the chamber for months.

They begin with pottery shards struck together by cords from a distance. Then drums. Then an old grazer bell. Readers station themselves along every street with bare feet pressed to the ground. Scouts watch the rim. Frog-keepers record each change of color. Three hollow-watchers descend as far as the lower quarry and return with the same report: sound enters the chamber and does not remain in the basin.

Yet it does not vanish.

The deepest ear-stones tell them that. They feel the passage beneath the mountain, growing fainter as it travels toward some remote destination.

Nothing ends at its skin.

Once this is understood, the chamber is accepted.

They do not call it a temple. No agreement can be reached about whether a god, the mountain, chance, or the pressure of roots made it. They call it the Far Throat.

Its first formal use is not a celebration.

An old woman named Reed-Between-Stones enters alone. In her youth, during the failure of a bell lure, her son ran shouting along the eastern path so that three households could escape a hollow. His formal name has been sung only in rain since then.

The old woman stands barefoot on the dark floor.

She says his name at an ordinary volume.

Then more loudly.

Then she shouts it until her back bends and her hands open and every hidden color in her sleeves shows at once.

The name goes down into stone.

Far below, it emerges from a cliff no human being has seen. It passes over ferns whose spores have never reached the basin. It startles pale creatures drinking from warm pools. Hollows turn toward the voice, but find no body at its end.

For once, a human being makes the saving sound and is not required to die of it.

Afterward the town alters around the gift.

The Far Throat is added to the maps: chamber to basalt seam, basalt seam to distant cliff, distant cliff to hollows, hollows to cleared paths, cleared paths back to the town. Because the full route is unknown, part of the braid hangs over the cloud-edge, unfinished.

Children are taken there to learn not silence but volume. Singers practice in shifts. Disputes that cannot wait for rain are carried below. Birth cries enter the mountain. So do final breaths, jokes too dangerous for padded rooms, accusations, marriage promises, bad songs, names of the dead, and the prolonged shrieking contests of adolescents.

The chamber does not end fear. A person surprised in a street is still in danger. Hollows still hunt. Walls still move. Water remains scarce. People still die making noise in the wrong place at the wrong time.

The power has not made vigilance unnecessary.

It has made vigilance survivable.

That is enough.

When the next warm season comes, vines cover the new stair in three days. The townspeople cut them back, then discover that one species flowers more heavily near the chamber because vibrations loosen its pollen. They alter the doorway to preserve it. Frogs gather beneath the lowest step. Velvet birds establish a roost in the outer passage. A hollow’s discarded skin is hung as a curtain, and the Far Throat becomes not a foreign object but another crowded relationship.

Only then does the power depart.

It receives no prayer and leaves no commandment. It does not explain the mountain or promise to return. As it turns above the cloud, droplets briefly outline a body so immense that the entire basin could have rested within the curve of one folded limb.

The ear-stones tremble.

The people become still.

But before the presence passes beyond feeling, Moth-Sleeping—older now, and entrusted with more judgment than before—runs into the Far Throat.

She lifts her face and cries out a question:

“Who are you?”

The chamber takes her voice.

The mountain carries it downward.

Very far away, the question emerges into open air.

The power hears.

It does not answer, because no answer could return without danger, worship, misunderstanding, or all three.

But it changes direction once more.

For one breath, the cloud beneath the town rises in a single smooth arc, like the back of a vast creature bowing.

Then it goes on.

In the basin, a drop of water falls into clay.

From beneath the old platform comes the sound of children laughing at full volume, while far below and safely distant, the hollows listen to the mountain sing.
