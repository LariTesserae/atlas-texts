### What the Power Did

The power did not enter the cleft.

To enter in any form large enough to be known would have displaced air, shaken loose stone, crushed ledges under panic. Light would have blinded the swimmers and burned the charcoal lichen. A voice would have become an avalanche among so many answering surfaces.

So it remained outside manifestation and attended.

It listened through a cloud season. Then through a dry season. It watched generations wake and harden in the water pockets. It followed the passage of one useful glass shard through thirty-seven pairs of gripping pads before the shard fell into darkness and opened the skin of a deep hunter. It learned which deaths nourished the cleft, which prevented worse deaths, and which were merely the excess cruelty of chance.

It did not mistake harmlessness for goodness.

To close the mouths of the deep hunters would have filled the ledges past endurance. To preserve every hatchling would have starved the adults. To blunt the stone would have erased the cracks that held claws, eggs, cysts, moisture, roots. To make rain plentiful would have drowned chambers whose inhabitants had never needed to swim.

The place was not badly made.

It was narrowly made.

Its life passed through too few gates. One failed cloud season could close them all. One collapse at the wrong depth could sever the warm fissures from the upper roosts. One century of altered winds could turn the living net toward an empty sky. The power saw that the cleft possessed abundance but almost no margin.

So it gave margin.

Not much.

Far inside the mountain, beyond the reach of claws or roots, it eased the pressure between two plates of pale stone. The movement was smaller than the growth of frost in a crack. No pinnacle fell. No animal woke.

A sealed chamber opened into a seam of porous rock.

Thereafter, when fog pressed through the summit, a little of its water entered that seam. The stone held it. During dry months it returned the moisture slowly—not as a stream, not even as droplets, but as a coolness that kept certain deep cracks from closing completely into dust.

The inhabitants never found a new pool. There was no miraculous basin around which they might gather. The dormant knots merely survived a little more often. Hookback nurseries remained soft one day longer. The deepest animals no longer had to rise quite so high in the leanest season.

This was the first gift: not plenty, but time.

The power then turned its attention to the mountain’s western shoulder.

There, hidden beneath a cornice of unbroken glass, was another fissure. It was empty except for mineral film and a few blind microbes. In ten thousand years erosion might have opened it. In twelve thousand, a collapse might have sealed it forever.

The power did not split it open.

Instead, winter by winter, it admitted frost into one chosen line. Each thaw advanced the fracture by less than the width of a veilwing’s claw. To all appearances the mountain weathered as mountains do.

After eighty-three years, a black seam appeared beneath the cornice.

After one hundred and nine, sideways fog entered.

After one hundred and twelve, aeroplankton caught in it.

A veilwing found the opening the following dusk.

It was a young animal driven from a crowded feeding column. The wind folded one membrane and carried it farther around the summit than it intended to go. It struck the western wall, caught itself, and tasted food collecting in the new seam.

It returned to the old cleft before dawn.

Nothing understood what it had discovered. There was no account, no map, no deliberate announcement. Yet the young veilwing carried the path in the fatigue of particular muscles, in the angle at which its steering fins had resisted the crosswind, and in a faint abundance of food on its breath.

The next evening it went again.

Three followed its wake.

One returned with a hookback clinging beneath a fold of membrane. Another carried dormant swimmers in the wet seams of its claws. Lichen dust lay in its throat-fan. Plant spores adhered to old egg secretion on its belly.

The new fissure began to acquire stains.

Years passed before any veilwing slept there. Longer before eggs were laid. Most failed. The place was too exposed, then too cold, then briefly too wet. A storm killed the first small roost. A hookback lived alone for two seasons, eating what the wind brought, and died beneath an overhang.

Still, bodies accumulated. Crust began.

The power did not prevent these failures. It had not made a refuge where nothing could die. It had made somewhere failure did not have to be final.

Ropelike grazers arrived as torn fragments tangled around veilwing feet. Most froze. One entered a damp fracture and anchored. In time it spread across the first patch of charcoal lichen.

The water-pocket swimmers came in mud carried under hookback plates. Pale stalks flowered downward from the young crust. Parasites came with their hosts, fungi with the parasites, predators among their prey.

Even the deep hunters crossed, though no one above witnessed it. The two clefts touched the same buried labyrinth. The power widened no passage for them. It only warmed, by the smallest fraction, a sequence of internal cracks. Soft bodies followed warmth. Many years later, knocking was heard beneath the western colony.

Thus the second cleft did not become a purified version of the first.

It became alive.

This was the second gift: not safety, but elsewhere.

There remained the chorus.

The power had listened to it for more than a century and had come to understand that no single interpretation was sufficient. The chorus was alarm and location, excess and appetite, social binding and involuntary resonance. It belonged to no veilwing. It belonged even to no species. The shape of the stone was part of it. The water released from trembling walls was part of it. So were the hookbacks raising their armor and the swimmers altering course in the suddenly shaken pools.

The chorus was the closest the cleft came to speaking as a whole.

The power did not answer in words.

One night, after the silence had descended and the first veilwing called, it altered nothing in the throats. It touched only a thin plate of rock deep in the new western fissure. The plate had been poised between stillness and vibration. The old cleft’s call crossed the summit through stone and air, too weak for any animal to notice.

The power let the plate ring.

The sound that returned was low, distorted, and distant. Not a copy. Not an instruction. Merely an answer shaped by another hollow place.

The old colony stopped.

For several breaths, no wing moved. Even the knocking below ceased.

Then the first caller cried again.

Across the mountain, the young colony answered.

The exchange continued until dawn: two crowds without leaders, neither comprehending the other, discovering through repetition that the world contained another throat.

The power withdrew its touch after that one night. The stone had learned the necessary vibration. Under certain conditions of frost, wind, and crowding, the two clefts could hear one another without help.

This was the third gift: not knowledge, but relation.

The wise power did no more.

It did not demand recognition. It left no symbol on the rim. It appointed no species guardian over the others. It did not teach the hookbacks ownership or the veilwings obedience. It did not reveal the future collapse of the oldest pinnacles, because panic could not have altered it and foreknowledge is not mercy to minds without means.

The original cleft still knew thirst.

Eggs still failed. Hunters still rose from below. Useful shards fell where they could never be recovered. Some cloud seasons were lean, and on the sharpest nights the outer layers of the roost froze around the living center.

But the dry years no longer came quite so close to ending everything.

And when a great slab eventually detached from the eastern wall, erasing ancient flight paths and crushing chambers that had been warm since the colony began, the whole life of the summit was not buried with it.

Across the mountain, wings continued to rise in narrow columns.

Hookbacks tested black glass.

Clear swimmers woke in hand-wide pools.

Blind heads pressed upward through crowded ledges.

In time the old cleft diminished. In time the western one grew crowded enough to send wanderers farther along the ridge. Some found nothing. Some died against bare rock. Some discovered cracks in which fog lingered.

The being in the mountain ceased to have only one body.

Perhaps that was all benevolence could rightly give it: neither immortality nor escape from hunger, but a loosening of the single thread on which its existence hung.

From below, the summit still appeared barren.

From one rim came warm breath.

From another, an answer.
