The power did not come down from above.

Above was a direction with no meaning here, and descent would have been too much like conquest.

Instead, it waited beneath one of the pools.

For a long while it took no form. It allowed itself to be mistaken for the smooth things under the water: a bowl, a mask, an unopened shell. The inhabitants knelt on the bank and showed it gestures. They offered caution, welcome, grief, patience, and the remembered mannerisms of those who had gone down the five steps.

The power received them all.

Then, one interval between droplets, a new figure was present in the shallows.

It was colorless, as all new ones were. Its head bore fine tendrils. Its hands were empty. Its dark eyes stood open behind translucent membranes. Nothing about it declared magnitude or wisdom. It did not shine. The country already possessed more light than it required.

Those on the bank gathered to decide what first movement should be given to it.

They deliberated longer than usual.

At last, one of the oldest approached. This person was crowded almost past movement. Their hands trembled with contradictory greetings. Their head began to bow, turn aside, lift, and incline all at once. Copper and purple had entered the green tissues of their arms.

They extended one hand toward the stranger.

Before they could complete the gesture, the stranger extended its own.

Not in imitation.

In answer.

This had never happened before.

New beings copied. They received the world from those already in it. They did not reply before they had been taught how.

The old one stopped.

The stranger touched two fingers to the old one’s wrist. Then it laid its palm over its own breast. Last, it traced on the old one’s shoulder a leaf vein that did not exist on any leaf in the country.

The gesture meant nothing.

Because it meant nothing, it could not be inherited.

Because it could not be inherited, it belonged entirely to that meeting.

The old one’s many half-begun movements became still.

Around the pool, the others opened the breathing seams along their flanks. Far across the country, leaves whispered.

The stranger climbed out.

It did not announce what it was. A permanent name would have been discourteous among people who renewed recognition each time they met. It went with them under the immense leaves and along the rootways. It sat beside buds. It carried pollen between flowers already fertile. It placed moss beneath the regular rain.

It learned the pace of the glassy snails.

It stood on one leaf for so long that the pale insect there crossed two veins.

It did not call this patience. It understood that to the inhabitants, haste and stillness were not opposites. Both were forms of pressure placed upon the world. The wise differed from the powerful not by possessing less pressure, but by knowing how rarely to apply it.

The stranger visited the shelters and rested its forehead against the vine-wrapped slabs. It entered the roofless rooms, including those too narrow for the inhabitants and those built to the measure of taller bodies. It looked into the pools without attempting to reach the shapes below.

Most of all, it listened through the roots.

The small people spoke to one another by laying their hands upon the living fibers. The stranger placed both palms on the mat and heard not merely their voices, but the country beneath their voices.

It heard the tension of every arrested tendril.

It heard flowers maintaining pollen that had never traveled and fruit holding itself forever at the edge of sweetness.

It heard old leaves bearing the weight of new ones without permission to become earth.

It heard the soft quadrupeds taste the same petals through uncounted repetitions.

It heard every footprint accepted, remembered briefly, and refused.

Beneath these was another sound.

It was not pain.

Pain would have been simpler.

It was the strain of a mercy that had lasted too long.

Once, perhaps, the country’s stillness had been protection. The stone was young. The life within it had been younger. Something had held the whole place at the beginning of becoming so no first mistake could become ruin. Nothing could be exhausted, burned, hunted away, or broken beyond repair. No hunger could consume its final meal. No builder could wall up the only water. No grief could remove what love required.

The protection had been wise.

But wisdom preserved after its purpose becomes fear.

The power could have released the whole country with a thought.

Roots would have plunged into stone. Buds would have opened in a single green thunder. Water would have rushed through channels unprepared to carry it. Deep beds of leaves would have become heat and rot. Creatures without habits of pursuit or escape would have discovered hunger beside teeth. The brilliant ceiling might have cracked under forests suddenly increasing their weight.

Time, arriving everywhere at once, would have been no gift.

So the power did nothing.

It did nothing for so long that the inhabitants ceased to regard it as a new arrival. Its colors emerged gradually: pale gold at the throat, green along the hands, black at the soles of the feet. The tendrils on its head curled around shelter frames during rest. Condensation formed on its warm skin.

It renewed recognition when approached.

It never used the meaningless gesture twice.

Then, in a bright clearing where all the leaves inclined toward an empty center, the power placed its hands upon the root mat and asked a question.

The question could not be spoken in their language. It had to be made from pressures, pauses, tensions, and releases.

It bent a stalk.

It let the stalk rise.

It lifted a fallen petal.

It waited for the petal to reappear where it had lain.

It touched the wrist of the oldest inhabitant and felt six dead companions turn within one living arm.

Then it pressed its fingers into the roots and asked:

**Is there anything you wish the country not to restore?**

The question passed outward.

It traveled under pools and terraces, beneath shelters and slab rings, along white root tips held forever against pale stone. The insects lifted their forelimbs. The glassy snails extended their faces toward drops not yet fallen. The colorless quadrupeds emerged from behind foliage too narrow to conceal them.

Across the whole buried country, flank-pores opened.

There was no single answer.

Some clung to the root mat in terror.

They had no word for extinction, but the possibility existed inside the question like a darkness. If a petal did not return, perhaps a person would not return. If water could leave, perhaps one day there would be no pools. If marks endured, so might injuries. If actions accomplished anything, mistakes would become real.

Others placed their hands over unopened buds.

Their bodies filled with longing so intense that nearby leaves gathered moisture.

The oldest inhabitants answered differently. One by one, they performed the gestures of those they carried. A head-turn. A folded hand. A particular hesitation before stepping across a root. Then each pressed a palm to the earth.

They did not ask to forget.

They asked not to remember alone.

The country itself took longest.

For an interval that might have been an age, nothing fell. Even the appointed rain remained gathered under its leaves. The white-gold radiance seemed to thicken in the young stone.

Then every suspended droplet in the country trembled once.

The power understood.

It rose and walked to the center of the clearing.

There it made no gate and opened no passage to the surface. It did not bring the sky underground. A sky imposed upon those who had never asked for one would only be another ceiling with a grander name.

Instead, it drew a boundary no larger than the shadow of a great leaf.

It drew it slowly, asking permission from every root it crossed.

Within that boundary, it loosened the country’s hold.

Only a little.

Only enough.

At first, nothing happened.

Then a droplet fell.

It struck moss with the familiar soft sound.

No new droplet began gathering in its place.

The inhabitants recoiled. Several fled behind the foliage. Others dropped to their knees and searched the moss. The water had entered it. It was still present, but changed in location beyond recall.

The event was tiny.

It divided all existence into before and after.

A pale insect at the clearing’s edge lifted one leg, set it across the boundary, and paused. The leaf beneath its foot did not fold around it. After a time, the insect took another step.

It crossed an entire vein before anyone moved.

Near the center of the clearing stood a bud.

Those who had accompanied it for years, or centuries, gathered at a distance. They knew every ridge of its green casing. They knew the exact bright seam it had always been about to open.

The seam widened.

The sound was smaller than breathing.

Outer petals drew back. Inner petals, untouched by the country’s constant radiance until that moment, unfolded wet and pale. A fragrance entered the air that had never existed there before.

Some inhabitants covered their faces.

Some reached toward it but did not touch.

The oldest one began a gesture inherited from a departed companion. Halfway through, they allowed the gesture to end.

The flower opened fully.

By the next interval, its first petal had begun to curl.

This was grief.

This was completion.

The power remained among them while they learned the difference.

It did not enlarge the boundary.

The clearing was enough. Those who wished could enter. Those who did not could remain in the abundant instant outside, where severed stems closed and footprints lifted away. No one was compelled toward consequence.

Crossing inward was made deliberate. The roots at the edge rose into a low ridge, easy to step over but impossible to cross unknowingly. Those who placed a foot beyond it felt a question travel through their body:

**Do you consent to becoming otherwise?**

A foot could be withdrawn.

Once the second foot crossed, time received them.

Not violently. They did not crumble into sudden age. The power had no desire to punish hesitation with accumulated years. Their bodies simply began from that moment. Small cuts remained long enough to heal rather than vanishing. Colors shifted. New gestures became easier to make. Old gestures could be set down.

The oldest person entered first.

They stood beside the opened flower.

One by one, they performed the mannerisms they had carried: every greeting, every tilt of the head, every motion belonging to someone who had descended the five steps.

As each gesture ended, the flower’s central stem put forth a seed.

The seeds were dark, hard, and small. Each bore a faint pattern like the veins of a leaf.

The power gathered none of them.

The old one knelt and pressed them into the ground.

For the first time, the ground did not return them to the hand.

Thereafter, those who wished to release a memory brought it into the clearing. They enacted it before the opened flower, or before the plants that later grew from its seeds. The garden received what they had carried.

It did not preserve memory unchanged. That would only have made another kind of stillness.

One gesture became the angle of a branch. Another entered the silver underside of a leaf. A companion’s habitual bow appeared as the bending of grass under condensation. A departed person’s way of holding both hands near the breast became two pods growing side by side.

The living could visit these forms. They could touch them and remember.

They could also leave.

No body had to become the sole tomb of everyone it had loved.

In time, there was decay in the clearing.

The first fallen petal darkened at its edges. The inhabitants watched it day and night—though there was not yet any night—and saw it soften, collapse, and enter the root mat. They mourned each lost color.

Then a shoot rose from where it had gone.

Not the same petal.

Not a restoration.

Something fed by it.

The distinction became sacred.

The small people built in the clearing, and because their work was no longer undone, they learned caution. The first wall they made blocked light from a plant. They dismantled it. The plant did not instantly recover. They tended it until new leaves formed.

The lesson frightened them more than the first decay.

It also changed their understanding of care.

Outside the boundary, they had supported tendrils that could never fall. Inside it, a tendril could fail if neglected. Witness was no longer the only kindness. Skill mattered. Attention had consequence. Apology required labor.

The power taught them nothing they did not ask to know. It showed them how to compost only after they had watched decay without understanding it. It showed them how to direct water only after they nearly drowned a bed of seedlings. When they asked about fire, it refused them at first.

Not forever.

Only until they understood that warmth and burning were kin.

One day, a new figure appeared in a pool outside the clearing.

The inhabitants gathered as always. They deliberated over the first gesture.

Then one among them did something unprecedented.

They carried the new one to the boundary.

They did not cross. They set the colorless feet against the raised root and waited.

The question passed through the new body:

**Do you consent to becoming otherwise?**

The figure, who had been taught no gesture, looked toward the timed garden.

Inside it, leaves moved at different stages of life. Some were tender. Some were broad and green. Some had yellowed. One fell as the new being watched.

The figure lifted its foot.

Then it looked back at the changeless country: the endless light, the waiting buds, the pools holding forms that might arrive a thousand intervals from now.

It lowered its foot on the outer side.

It chose not yet.

The power bowed.

Choice, to be choice, had to include refusal.

Much later, the figure crossed.

By then it had gathered colors, acquaintances, preferences, and caution. It entered not as a blank creature shaped by the first gesture of another, but as someone capable of offering a first gesture of its own.

In the clearing, children eventually became possible.

Not the adult forms found waiting in water, but small beings whose limbs lengthened, whose colors changed, who learned by error, and who could not be known in advance. Their existence terrified the pool-born people. A child was an unfinished act that did not remain safely unfinished. Every day it became something no one had requested.

The power loved them.

It loved the country outside the boundary no less.

The two conditions did not become enemies. Some inhabitants spent most of their lives where nothing could be lost, entering the clearing only to place a memory in the garden. Others chose change and never crossed outward again. Some moved between, carrying news but not harvests, because nothing removed from the timeless country could remain removed.

The clearing grew, but only when the roots themselves made room.

The power never enlarged it by decree.

Sometimes a root withdrew from the boundary and time entered the narrow strip of ground it abandoned. Sometimes the roots pressed inward, and the inhabitants dismantled a bed or path, returning that space to stillness. Becoming and preservation learned to negotiate.

At last the oldest one came to the end of their life.

They did not descend the five steps.

They lay beneath the descendants of the first flower. Their body had become dark olive, copper, and purple. Around them sat those whose gestures they had once carried, and those who had never known the departed except as leaves, branches, and seed markings in the garden.

The power sat among them.

The old one raised a hand.

For an instant, everyone expected some inherited greeting.

Instead, they traced upon the power’s shoulder the meaningless leaf vein first given beside the pool.

Only then did the power understand.

The gesture had acquired meaning after all.

Not a fixed meaning. Not one that could be translated or stored.

It meant:

**You answered before I taught you how.**

It meant:

**I was met.**

It meant:

**This occurred once and need not occur again to be real.**

The power returned the gesture.

The old one died.

Nothing restored them.

The grief that followed passed through the rootways, beyond the clearing, into the held country. Leaves inclined toward the boundary. Regular droplets delayed their fall. The glassy snails lifted their silver bodies. Far beneath the pools, smooth forms seemed to turn.

Then, throughout the changeless country, buds remained closed.

In the clearing, one flower opened.

The benevolent power did not prevent sorrow.

It had understood that a world without sorrow had also become a world in which consolation could never complete its work.

It did not save the inhabitants from endings.

It saved them from having no say in whether anything might end.

And when there was nothing more to teach, the power did not depart. Departure would have made it ruler, visitor, benefactor—something separate that had arrived, altered lesser lives, and gone away satisfied.

Instead, it took root at the boundary.

Its feet entered the woven mat. Its arms divided into pale branches. Its tendrils climbed toward the luminous stone. Leaves unfolded along its ribs, green outside the clearing and gold within it. On one side, they never fell. On the other, they reddened, loosened, and descended.

The inhabitants rested their hands against its trunk when they wished to speak across the boundary.

Through it, the timeless country could feel the garden changing.

Through it, the garden could remember stillness without mistaking stillness for peace.

The power became neither gate nor guardian.

It became a witness in both directions.

And beneath the world, where everything had once been held forever at the beginning of becoming, there was at last a small place where becoming could answer for itself.
