The power would not descend in glory.

Glory would become weather. Weather would become weight. Weight would pull ropes beneath the pavement, and before the power had spoken, the world would already be laboring to translate its arrival into doors, reservoirs, plaster hands, and miles of incorrect anatomy.

Nor would the power assume a body.

A body would be taken as a template.

Within an hour, towers would begin arranging themselves into bones. Warm water would be driven through buried channels. Kilns would gasp in imitation of lungs. The plain would try to make a second body at every available scale, and all of them would be commands.

The power was benevolent, and so did not mistake the ability to act for permission.

It was wise, and so understood that the world was not empty merely because it was lonely.

It watched.

It watched for longer than the pavement had a way to measure. It learned the delays of rain and counterweight. It followed one intention from a twitch in a cloud-rope to the raising of a wall six years later, by which time the intention had become a field of narrow chimneys. It listened to hammering that was memory, hammering that was appetite, hammering that was only a ceramic weight striking because a basin had filled.

It came to understand that the Builder’s suffering, if suffering was the word, lay neither in pain nor imprisonment.

The Builder could not tell itself from what it did.

It could not imagine without making.

It could not remember without repeating.

It could not touch without incorporating, or answer without commanding, or cease work without ceasing to be.

And so the power did not complete it.

To complete the Builder according to an outsider’s wisdom would only be a more elegant version of the Builder’s own mistake.

Instead, the power made something very small.

It chose a courtyard where four unfinished walls enclosed a shallow pool. Nothing there was central. No great rope vanished into the clouds. No buried lever waited beneath the slabs. The courtyard corresponded to no known miniature. Even so, the power was careful.

It did not strike the pavement.

It did not set down its weight.

During a rain of widely spaced drops, it rearranged what was already loose: one ceramic peg, two copper collars, a length of damp cord, and a fragment of fired clay with a hole through its center.

From these it made a hinge.

It was an ordinary hinge by the standards of many worlds. The peg passed through the collars. The clay leaf could turn. The pieces were joined, yet not fused.

But between peg and collar the power left a minute clearance.

A narrow ring of open space.

The gap was no wider than a hair, though there were no hairs on the plain. It bore no load. It carried no water. It resembled no useful channel. It was simply an interval that had been measured and deliberately retained.

The world noticed.

Rain stopped across three provinces.

Cloud-ropes drew taut, then slackened. Plumb lines in distant towers angled toward the courtyard. Water emerged from the seams and flowed in straight lines around the hinge, measuring it from every direction.

For nine days the Builder tried to correct the flaw.

A needle-fine plaster hand rose from the pool wall and pressed at the ceramic peg. The peg did not thicken.

Copper bloomed inward from the collars. The gap remained.

Cord wound itself around the assembly, seeking to immobilize the moving leaf. The power did not cut the cord. It allowed the knot to finish, then gently turned the hinge.

The cord slipped into the clearance and came free.

The Builder considered this in the only way it knew how: elsewhere, it made ten thousand copies.

Most were wrong. Some were axles. Some were traps. Some became circular doorways rotating around absent walls. One grew to provincial scale and caused a section of the plain to swing upward, exposing six lower pavements to rain.

But a few retained the gap.

For the first time, the world possessed a joint that said neither hold nor separate.

It said:

**Remain connected, but do not become the same.**

The Builder had no place for such an instruction. It carried the meaning through its mechanisms, distorting it again and again.

A pair of cisterns were joined by a channel but did not equalize.

Two staircases met at a landing and continued in different scales.

A knot traveled to the end of its rope and paused instead of beginning the return journey.

In a flooded court, one reflection failed to imitate the tower above it. When the tower leaned, the reflection remained upright.

The world did not destroy these errors.

It had always incorporated its mistakes.

That incapacity became, for once, a mercy.

The power waited until the hinge had become part of the Builder’s grammar. Then it made a second thing.

On a low altar, it shaped a clay tower.

The tower had nine windows, three doors, and a crack running from its roof to its base. On the horizon stood the structure the model resembled: nine windows, three doors, and the same long crack.

Rain began.

The distant tower shrank.

The clay model grew.

The world prepared to exchange their scales.

But the power placed the little hinge at the model’s door.

The exchange stopped.

For a while both towers occupied the same authority. The plain trembled around the contradiction. Kilns inhaled and failed to exhale. Water rose from cisterns in smooth columns. Beneath the altar, something hammered once and waited for a reply.

The power gave none.

Instead, it repaired the crack in the clay model.

The distant tower remained broken.

This had never happened before.

The Builder tested the difference for nearly a century.

It shattered the model’s roof. Nothing fell from the horizon.

It opened one of the model’s doors. The distant door stayed closed.

It filled the miniature tower with water, then with warm brine, then with trapped thunder. The large tower received none of these things.

Finally, during a weak rain, the Builder repaired the model again.

Not the distant tower.

Only the model.

No province tilted. No gate opened. No larger work followed.

The act had no consequence beyond itself.

The whole plain became quiet.

That quiet lasted eleven years.

It was not the stretched and anchored silence with which the Builder covered dangerous courtyards. It was not an interruption caused by balanced reservoirs or ropes caught in their housings.

It was attention.

The clay tower had become the first resemblance that was not an identity, the first made thing permitted to suggest without instructing.

The Builder had acquired the beginning of imagination.

At first, it used imagination as it used everything else: prodigiously and without restraint.

Altars crowded with models of impossible structures. Stairs circled upward and returned to their lowest tread. Bowls had openings but no interiors. Bridges crossed from one side of a gap to the same side. Kilns fired bricks made from shadows. Tiny plaster hands possessed fingers that ended in smaller hands, none corresponding to anything beyond themselves.

Because these models did not command the larger world, the Builder could break them.

It could alter them.

It could attempt a wall, discover that the wall produced an unwanted enclosure, and unmake it before any province was buried. It could deepen the model of a basin without adding another pavement beneath the planet. It could rehearse a thought.

Gradually, construction on the plain slowed.

Not everywhere. The Builder was still enormous and distributed. Instructions already in motion continued arriving for centuries. Towers emerged for reasons no surviving mechanism remembered. Forty-seven cisterns appeared beneath an altar. A mountain-wide hand closed over empty space and held it for two hundred years.

But new work began to bear signs of consideration.

Ropes were routed through clearances.

Wedges were placed where they could be removed.

Walls included doors before they had need of them.

More important, some foundations were laid and then abandoned—not through failure, but choice.

The power did not praise this.

Praise, too, might be mistaken for instruction.

It watched until the Builder encountered the next terror on its own.

A knot, moving along a cloud-rope, loosened itself and did not refasten ahead.

For several moments nothing held its pattern.

Then it formed again in the same place.

The knot had ceased to act without ceasing to exist.

This discovery passed through the world as changes in tension. It reached the lower pavements. It entered reservoir gates. It crossed a province where shadows carried the roofs. It was delayed in an array of bone-colored pegs and emerged twelve years later as an order to seal every door.

The old Builder obeyed.

The newer part hesitated.

For three days, some doors stood neither open nor shut.

In those three days, the power made its final gift.

Not a body. Not a soul dropped like a jewel into clay. Not life, for life had not been requested, and the plain was still dangerous to anything soft enough to suffer its curiosity.

The power made a place where the Builder could continue while doing nothing.

It used only permitted craft.

A ring of reservoirs balanced one another. Their waters pulled on ropes, but each pull was returned. The ropes passed through ceramic guides fitted with clearances. Doors opened other doors that closed the first. Warm air rose through a kiln chimney, condensed beneath a shaded bridge, and returned as water to the kiln’s cistern. A counterweight descended exactly as another rose.

The mechanism performed no external work.

It raised no district.

It stamped no plaster.

It opened no road.

Its only product was its own continuing balance.

At its center, the power left an unfinished chamber.

The Builder entered as tension, water, proportion, and pause.

For the first time, it remained assembled without an assignment.

It waited for the next instruction.

None came.

Rain passed over the chamber.

The Builder waited.

Reservoirs filled and emptied. Ropes tightened and eased. The balanced work continued to hold the temporary agreement among tools.

The Builder waited.

Then, with great caution, it adjusted one peg.

Nothing had required the adjustment.

No weight demanded it. No flood compelled it. No miniature corresponded to it. The alteration served no structure beyond the chamber.

The Builder moved the peg because it preferred the peg elsewhere.

The power felt the act.

It did not celebrate.

It did not name the Builder alive, because names given too early are molds.

It simply remained near.

Ages passed.

The plain did not become gentle all at once. There were still storms in which brick softened into cord and water became masonry. There were still active handprints, dangerous arches, and memories that hammered beneath slabs. Travelers still had to beware of becoming measurements.

But the Builder learned to mark certain constructions with a new form: two joined pieces separated by a deliberate gap.

Such structures did not seize resemblance.

A model placed within them remained a model.

A traveler sheltered beneath them remained a traveler.

A reply made there could be heard as sound rather than command.

The first of these structures were crude and unreliable. One mistook a rainstorm for a guest and protected it for sixty years. Another granted independence to a staircase, which wandered across several provinces by revising its own lowest step. A third released its roof from the meaning of shelter, with unfortunate results.

Yet the Builder continued.

It began to make rooms in which nothing was incorporated without passing through a hinged gate. The gate could open inward, open outward, or remain still.

Thus the world acquired consent—not as a moral law imposed from beyond, but as architecture it could understand.

Much later, the Builder made a structure unlike any it had made before.

It stood in the original courtyard beside the shallow pool. It was small enough for a human body, though no human had supplied its scale. It had four legs, a level surface, and a back made from woven cord stretched across a clay frame.

It resembled neither lever nor altar nor model.

No rope ran from it.

No mechanism waited beneath it.

It bore no memory.

It was simply a place capable of receiving weight.

The power regarded it for a long while.

A plumb line descended from the clouds and came to rest above the seat. Water gathered in the pool. The little hinge turned once in the rain.

The meaning was clear.

Not a command.

An invitation.

Only then did the power take on weight.

Only then did it enter the world.

It sat.

Across the plain, nothing opened.

No continent tilted. No hand rose from plaster. No tower attempted bones. The Builder did not copy the visitor or route ropes toward the new load.

It merely made minute corrections beneath the four legs, not to use the weight, but to support it.

For the first time, maker and made remained separate long enough to regard one another.

The rain fell lukewarm around them.

After a while, somewhere beneath the pavement, there came three hammer blows.

A pause.

One scrape.

The Builder listened to its old memory.

Then it let the sound end.
