### What the Power Does

The power does not enter as light.

Light, here, would be violence. It would expose the moths, wake the bats, heat the upper stone too quickly, call down hawks, raise moss where moss would clog the narrow seeps. Nor does it enter as a voice. The Cleft already contains more sound than any chamber should bear, and there is no language common to wing, root, shell, water, and rock.

It enters as comprehension.

For a while—if such a power requires time—it attends to every scale of the place at once.

It knows the bat mother searching through ten thousand cries and the pup whose answer is weakening. It knows the mite drinking at the soft skin beneath her jaw. It knows the snake that has not fed in a month and waits three ledges below. It knows the mineral seam that will loosen under the snake’s weight, the beetle eggs sealed in the guano beneath it, and the blind fish passing through warm water thirty meters under all of them.

It does not divide these lives into innocent and cruel.

It sees hunger passing through the Cleft like warmth. It sees fear preserving flesh, death releasing it, waste becoming food before the dead have cooled. It understands that to save the bat from every snake would kill the snakes, crowd the roosts, exhaust the valleys, and fill the hollow with sickness. To save the moth from the spider would starve the spider and alter the moss. To make every pool permanent would drown the organisms that require the rhythm of vanishing water. To widen the passages would invite new hunters. To close them would trap the evening flights.

So it does not make the Cleft harmless.

A harmless Cleft would be a dead one.

Nor does it make the inhabitants wise in the way of the power. It gives no bat knowledge of the whole mountain, no crab an understanding of aquifers, no fish a vision of the summit sun. Such knowledge would not console them. It might only burden brief lives with immensities they could not act upon.

The power’s first gift is restraint.

It leaves the owl its silence.

It leaves the snake its patience.

It leaves the swift’s egg above the drop.

It leaves the stranded pools to diminish.

It leaves death inside the Warm Cleft, because death is one of the doors through which the Cleft remains alive.

But there are other deaths approaching which belong to no ancient exchange.

Far below, people have begun to mark the massif on maps.

They have found traces of rare metals in the rust-colored strata. A road is imagined. A borehole is proposed. Someone, studying a satellite image, notices the warm plume rising from the summit and wonders whether the buried heat can be made useful. None of them knows about the pale fish. None has heard the roar beneath the shelf. Their intentions are not especially wicked. They need work, power, stone, proof of discovery. Yet a drill lowered into the wrong joint would empty the lower reservoirs. A blast on the eastern face would close three flight routes. Floodlights at a work camp would draw the moths outward by the thousands, where the cold would finish what the lamps began.

Here the power acts without hesitation.

Not with a landslide. Not with sickness. Not by frightening people into superstition.

It alters no mind against its nature. It merely places truth where truth can be found.

A geologist taking a second core discovers that the profitable seam is thinner than expected and fractured beyond safe extraction. An engineer, checking calculations late at night, notices a fault line omitted from the first survey. A graduate student compares the chemistry of two springs and realizes that the mountain contains an aquifer of extraordinary antiquity. She argues, stubbornly and with evidence, that drilling could contaminate water used by villages in the low country.

A conservationist’s microphone, left overnight to record summit wind, captures an impossible density of ultrasonic calls.

The recording reaches someone who listens.

The road bends elsewhere.

The concession is withdrawn, first for practical reasons, then legal ones. Years later the massif receives a name in legislation that is less beautiful than *the Warm Cleft* but strong enough to keep explosives away. The people in the valleys are compensated through work that does not require the mountain to be opened. The power does not demand that human need lose so that hidden life may win. Wisdom searches longer than that. It finds another vein in already disturbed ground; it brings an overlooked spring to the attention of those who need water; it allows protection and livelihood to cease being enemies.

No one sees a hand in any of this.

Inside the Cleft, the power turns to smaller dangers.

A wedge of basalt has been loosening for six hundred years above the densest nursery. Its fall is natural. Stone falls; colonies move; bodies are buried and consumed. But this particular fall would strike a limestone shelf, split it, and send both masses into the narrow throat by which the largest bat colony enters and leaves. The obstruction would remain for centuries. In the first nights, thousands would batter themselves against it. Later, the trapped warmth would sour. Parasites would multiply. The colony might fail completely, and with it several plants in the valleys whose night-blooming flowers depend upon those bats.

The power does not freeze the mountain.

It makes the smallest possible change.

Into a crack above the basalt rib it guides one more thread of condensing water. The water carries lime. Season after season, a pale skin forms in the fracture. Crystal enters crystal. The rib does not become eternal, but when at last it slips, it turns half the width of a claw and comes to rest against a neighboring pillar.

Dust falls.

The bats shriek and lift.

A few eggs are lost. One snake is crushed. Beetles arrive.

The passage remains open.

Elsewhere, a seep is failing. Its source has been slowly captured by a deeper crack, and the thin channel in which a lineage of salamanders has lived for perhaps ten thousand years will be dry within nine seasons. The power could restore it, but doing so would flood a gallery where blind crickets breed. It could move the salamanders, but bodies transported without their bacteria, parasites, remembered tremors, and chemical world are not truly saved; they are merely displaced.

So the power opens no river.

It loosens a grain of calcite in a wall already near dissolution. Behind it is a damp fissure no wider than a salamander’s skull. The first animal finds it while following the taste of cooler stone. The fissure leads gradually downward to another thread of water. Some follow. Some do not. The old seep dries. The crickets persist. The salamander lineage is not guaranteed survival, only passage.

That is another gift the power gives the Cleft:

not safety, but alternatives.

A second crack where there was one.

A higher hold above the flood.

A shaded pocket during an unusually hot decade.

One more mineral cup along the route of a thirsty swift.

A roughness on a wall a crab might grip.

A flaw in a crust through which the lower water can breathe when pressure rises.

Small redundancies, hidden within natural possibility.

The power knows that life here has survived not because nothing changes, but because change has seldom closed every door at once.

Then it comes to the individuals.

Wisdom does not use the whole as an excuse to ignore them.

There is an old bat clinging alone below the warm folds. Her wing bears a fresh tear from an owl’s strike. She cannot fly to the valleys. Hunger has already entered her muscles. No restoration of the ecosystem requires her particular death tonight; no balance waits upon it. Yet neither can the power make every old bat young without making age meaningless and the colony impossible.

It does not reason that because it cannot save all, it should comfort none.

The old bat releases her hold.

She falls less than the length of her body onto a fold of shed moth scales and dry guano rather than the blade of stone beneath it. The disturbance wakes beetles. It also wakes a large moth, which blunders against her muzzle.

She eats.

It is not enough to heal her. It is enough to warm her until morning. When the colony returns, a familiar female lands beside her and tolerates her closeness. They groom mineral dust from each other’s faces. The old bat sleeps inside the press of bodies once more.

Whether she flies again, the power does not decide.

At the basin, floodwater is withdrawing too quickly. Three blind fish circle in a remnant cup whose outlet has sealed with silt. Crabs wait along its edge. This, too, is natural. The fish would become crab, beetle, mineral, bat. Nothing would be wasted.

Still, one fish presses its head again and again against the place where colder water used to enter.

The power feels the small insistence.

A crab, descending, dislodges a flat stone it has carried for many days. The stone strikes the silt plug. A thread of water appears beneath it. Two fish find the thread and pass down through gravel into darkness.

The third does not.

The crab eats.

Benevolence does not mean that every ending is revoked. It means no ending is treated as insignificant merely because another life can use it.

For many years the power remains attentive. Or perhaps its attention has no duration and these years are a single regard.

It shields nothing from consequence, but it shields the whole from needless simplification. It does not permit one accident, one appetite, one outside ignorance to reduce the many-layered Cleft to a single silence. Whenever possible, it preserves complexity: predator and prey, wet year and dry, upper air and lower water, colony and solitary body, the route remembered and the route not yet found.

It adds no monument.

There is no shining mark on the wall. No species begins to worship the warmest stone. The bats do not arrange quills into symbols. The crabs build no altar from their chosen tools. The vine bears no golden leaf. Nothing within the hollow becomes proof.

Proof would draw visitors.

Instead, the summit seam becomes a little harder to notice. Wind lays dust across footprints. Survey photographs catch the overhanging shelf in shadow. Those who enter by accident feel, before descending far, that the place is already complete without them. Most withdraw.

A few do not.

A biologist comes carefully, carrying no lamp brighter than necessary. She wears clean coverings so no foreign fungus enters on her clothes. She hears the roar and stops, not from fear but from the sudden knowledge that she is at the edge of countless lives. She records water temperatures, gathers a fallen scale, photographs one blind fish after a storm, and leaves the fish where it is.

What she learns helps protect other mountains.

The power does not hide the Cleft from every human being. Secrecy can preserve, but knowledge can preserve too. It guides neither through command. It allows the right astonishment to occur in those prepared to be changed by it.

Centuries pass.

The reinforced basalt rib eventually falls. By then the colonies use two other approaches.

The salamanders in the old seep vanish, but their descendants occupy the lower fissure.

The ancient bat dies, as all warm bodies do.

The student who protected the aquifer grows old and is forgotten by nearly everyone. The law’s name changes. The villages change. The valleys warm, then cool. Species arrive. Some disappear. The pale vine loses its root outside and withers inward one colorless leaf at a time.

The power grieves each loss.

This grief is not regret at having failed to make the world permanent. It is the price of perceiving every creature as itself and not merely as a replaceable part. Wisdom does not abolish grief. It makes grief exact.

At last, perhaps, the mountain’s heat weakens. No benevolence can require stone to remain young. The deep circulation slows. The warmest ceilings cool. Bats depart in stages for lower caverns. The swifts abandon their cups. Moths become rare. Water retreats deeper into the massif, carrying the blind fish beyond any upper basin.

The Cleft grows quieter.

The power could hold it forever in the condition that first drew attention: warm, roaring, crowded, eternally renewed. But that would no longer be protection. It would be imprisonment inside a favored moment.

So it lets the Cleft change.

Yet before the last great colony leaves, the power gives them a season of unusual abundance in the valleys. Night flowers open heavily. Insects rise above the river in clouds. Mothers return with full bodies. Pups survive who otherwise would not. Among those pups are some whose hearing favors a different range, better suited to another cavern far to the south.

They depart.

In their fur travel mites, spores, pollen, and minute eggs. In their memory travel turns, temperatures, the feel of narrow stone, and the ancient habit of seeking warmth in darkness.

The Cleft empties slowly, never completely. Beetles remain after bats. Crickets remain after beetles. Bacteria remain after every visible creature has gone. Mineral films feed on heat too slight for flesh to notice. Below, in black water, something still moves its barbels over gravel.

Nothing is preserved unchanged.

Nothing is abandoned.

And if the power offers the Warm Cleft one final blessing, it is not immortality, comfort, innocence, or understanding.

It is this:

May no life here be made needless.

May hunger never become mere cruelty.

May death continue to open into life.

May every closing passage leave, somewhere, a smaller passage.

May what must vanish be carried forward in another body.

May the mountain be permitted its patience.

Then the power withdraws—or becomes indistinguishable from the care with which one drop finds the same channel as the drop before it.

Warmth rises through the fault.

A crab tests a stone.

A mother turns her vast ears among ten thousand cries and hears, impossibly, the one voice she is seeking.
