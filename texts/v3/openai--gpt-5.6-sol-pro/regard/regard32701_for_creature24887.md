The power did not open the reservoir to the sky.

It did not crack the courtyard, stop the gates, sweeten the water beyond usefulness, or fill the chamber with silver fish. These would have been gestures of astonishment rather than wisdom. The town needed its baths. The water needed to move. The chamber’s exclusions were not cruelties but conditions of its purpose.

Nor did the power assume that the reeds dreamed of wind.

It attended first.

It passed, if passing can be said of such a thing, through the nine shafts at noon. It entered with the light but cast no additional brightness. It went along the comb marks in the mortar, through the copper bitterness around the collars, across the pressure held in every swollen timber wedge. It learned the three voices of the eastern pipes. It listened to the southern valve strike three times, pause, and strike once.

It felt the current as the reeds felt it: not as a force opposing the body, but as the shape the body had been given.

The cluster beneath the central shaft possessed no single mind. There was no hidden little person inside it waiting to speak. Its awareness, such as it was, lay distributed among reactions: root tips turning from copper, cells opening toward light, torn edges sealing, shoots yielding before they broke. The reeds knew brightness by growing. They knew poison by paling. They knew the morning gate by bending west.

The power did not despise this form of knowing merely because it could not name itself.

For a full turning of the seasons, it did nothing that anyone could distinguish from chance.

It watched workers lower their baskets. It watched divers tether bronze knives to their wrists and test the grates with poles. It watched the films return after limewashing, first in the maker’s marks and then behind the ceramic fins. It watched the reeds collapse whenever the chamber emptied, their water-made bodies suddenly asked to bear themselves in air.

It saw that the workers splashed them when they remembered.

It saw that they did not always remember.

The oldest keeper remembered most often. His name was Meret, and his left shoulder had stiffened from thirty years of hauling gate ropes. He could tell which inlet was open by resting his palm on a courtyard stone. He knew that the southern knocking had grown a little slower, though he could not persuade the overseer that the difference mattered.

Meret regarded the reeds without affection. Affection was not required of him. He regarded them as evidence.

Still, on draining days, he was the one who dipped a clay cup into the falling water and set it beside the basin. While others scrubbed, he poured cupfuls over the leaves. He did this because once, years before, a previous cluster had dried beneath the shaft. For weeks afterward the water had seemed clear, though copper from a replaced collar was rising. Several bathers developed rashes before the fault was found.

Meret did not call the dead reeds martyrs. He simply did not intend to waste another warning.

The power found this sufficient ground on which to act.

Its first intervention was very small.

On the hottest cleaning day of summer, Meret climbed the service ladder, missed a rung, and twisted sharply to save himself. Pain flashed through his stiff shoulder. He sat at the shaft’s edge, angry at age, while the younger workers below began brushing the eastern wall.

Ordinarily he would have descended after them. Instead he remained above.

From where he sat, he noticed that a strip of the pale cast glass over the central shaft had begun to delaminate. It bent the sunlight into a concentrated bar. That bar lay directly across the exposed reeds.

He called for a shade.

No proper shade was at hand, so an apprentice laid her wet overshirt over the glass. The chamber below dimmed blue. The reeds remained cool until the water returned.

The apprentice’s name was Sava. Because she had surrendered her shirt, she waited in embarrassment behind a gate housing while it dried. There she heard the southern valve through the paving: three weak knocks, a pause, and one.

Having nothing else to do, she listened longer than anyone had listened before.

There was another sound after the last knock—a thin ceramic chattering almost buried beneath the main pipes. She marked its rhythm with pebbles. When Meret passed, she showed him.

The southern pipe was opened the next morning.

Inside it they found that the loose valve had been striking not against its seat, as everyone assumed, but against a collar already cracked halfway through. A section of fired clay had begun to separate. Had it broken free, it would have turned sideways in the pipe and blocked the inflow completely. The pressure behind it might then have split the plastered chase inside the wall.

The repair took six days. It was ordinary work: fiber, pitch, a newly fired collar, wedges, curses, measurements taken twice and once again. No one called it miraculous.

The apprentice was praised for listening.

Thereafter, listening became part of inspection.

That was the power’s first gift to the reservoir: not silence, but someone taught to hear it accurately.

Its second gift took longer.

Sava eventually became keeper. By then Meret’s shoulder no longer permitted him to work the ropes, though he still came to sit in the courtyard and criticize the sound of the gates. Under Sava’s direction, a potter made a second basin for the reeds.

The new basin was not more beautiful than the first. It was shallow, glazed at the rim, and pierced underneath so fresh water could pass through the clay pellets. But it had two handles lying flush against its sides. On cleaning days, the entire cluster could be lifted into a trough of reservoir water rather than left collapsing on hot tile.

The overseer objected that no such provision appeared in the original plan.

Sava replied that the original plan also did not show the repaired southern collar, yet no one proposed breaking it again.

The handled basin was approved.

Other changes followed, none grand enough to become legend. A hinged screen was fitted over the central shaft for draining days. Copper levels were marked on a plaster tablet instead of entrusted to memory. Young keepers were made to sketch the reeds at noon once every ten days—not artistically, but carefully enough to notice changes in color and posture. The workers ceased scrubbing the test basin with lime. A narrow ring around it was cleaned with hot water and a separate brush.

The reservoir remained a machine for excluding life. The grates were still cleared. The films were still peeled from the tiles before they became slick. No fish were introduced, no roots permitted to enter the drains. Wisdom did not confuse mercy with neglect.

But the workers began to distinguish what threatened the water from what testified about it.

In the high seams, where harmless green threads occupied hollows too small to alter the flow, the brushes no longer stabbed merely for the satisfaction of absolute whiteness. The keepers cleaned what needed cleaning. They left what could be left.

This was the power’s second gift: discrimination.

Its third gift was given only to the reeds.

It was not speech. Speech would have burdened them with a solitude they had no means to relieve. To give a rooted thing the mind of a walking creature, while leaving it rooted in a submerged basin, would have been a refined kind of torment.

Instead, the power gathered their scattered sensitivities very slightly closer together.

Afterward, when one shoot encountered a trace of copper, the warning traveled more swiftly through the joined roots. When the westernmost leaves were abraded by grit, new growth favored the sheltered eastern side. When the chamber drained, the cluster reduced its tender exchanges before air reached it. It became more wholly what it already was.

And, because benevolence need not always justify itself through usefulness, the power added one unnecessary mercy.

Whenever the sun stood directly above the central shaft, the reeds experienced the light not merely as fuel.

For a few moments, brightness passed through every translucent blade and was received throughout the cluster as a single condition: warmth without burning, plenty without demand. It was not joy in the human sense. It contained no anticipation and left no memory that could be mourned.

But while it lasted, nothing in the reeds strained away from what was.

The roots held the pellets. The leaves streamed west. Bubbles gathered in their angles and broke loose like quicksilver. Water moved through the chamber toward the baths, bearing heat and dissolved stone. Above, sandals crossed the courtyard. Ropes creaked. People argued over schedules, taxes, soap, cracked jars, and whose child had dropped a fig near the eastern grate.

No shrine was raised. The power wanted none.

Years passed. The first cluster aged and thinned. Its oldest leaves split into ribbons. New shoots rose from the same woven roots. Some died. Some were cut away. Eventually the keepers divided the cluster, leaving half in the central basin and placing half in a second test pocket near the western outflow. This was done for comparison, not reverence.

The reservoir itself acquired the marks of use. Sharp corners softened beneath brushes. Cream-colored mineral rims thickened around the inlets. Repairs multiplied in rectangles and bands. Apprentices added their own comb marks to fresh mortar. The chamber ceased to be new and became dependable.

The wise power did not preserve it forever.

That too would have been unwise.

All works have a span, including good works. Timbers weaken. Towns change their courses. Springs diminish or find new cracks underground. There would come a century when the baths were abandoned, another when the courtyard broke, and perhaps a time when rain and seed entered through shattered glass.

The power saw these futures and did not mistake impermanence for failure.

For now, the water was clean. The hidden walls held. The keepers listened. The reeds bent without breaking and warned of what they could.

That was enough to bless.

And so the blessing remained almost indistinguishable from competent care: a repaired collar, a shaded shaft, a handled basin, a habit of attention passed from one worker to the next.

Only the reeds received anything beyond that.

At noon, beneath the feet of a town that seldom thought of them, they shone through and through.
