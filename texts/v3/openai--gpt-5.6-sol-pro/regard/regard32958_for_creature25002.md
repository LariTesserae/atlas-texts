The power did not descend in fire.

Fire would have frightened the filter colonies, driven the tower animals from their stacks, altered the chemistry of the rain, and perhaps ignited old gases sleeping beneath the refinery wards. Nor did it arrive as a voice over the city. There was already too much that deserved to be heard.

It came first as attention.

For one complete cycle of the warm rains, it watched without touching anything. It followed the water from cloud to gutter, gutter to canal, canal to reservoir, reservoir to cooling tower, and from there into vapor again. It learned the slow appetite of the herds and the pressure-language of the armored swimmers. It listened to maintenance songs until it could hear where generations of repetition had rounded off a dangerous instruction. It descended with the night barges into foundations whose ceilings were lost in darkness. It waited beside six-legged hunters. It drifted with the gliders. It remained for years at the bottom of a settling basin, considering the ancient creature there as patiently as the creature considered silt.

The citizens noticed it only indirectly.

A pressure keeper dreamed of a valve she had never seen and woke knowing that it was open.

Children playing at canals began building an extra spillway into every game, though none could say who had invented the rule.

A judge, preparing to order the relocation of a filter colony, paused over the plans and asked what would happen to the mites that lived inside the colony’s attachment folds. No one in the hall had previously known that the mites existed.

In the western works, the tower animals added a fourth note to their three-beat call. Engineers investigated and found a fracture deep in the piston bed, still small enough to mend.

The power could have repaired the fracture itself. It did not.

It understood that rescue can become a form of ruin when it teaches the rescued world to stop perceiving danger. To make every machine perfect would impoverish the keepers. To cleanse every basin at once would starve lives adapted to poison and send unbound metals downstream. To straighten the towers would break bridges laid against their lean. To destroy the predators would crowd the city with hunger of another kind. To render the people immortal would fill every office, passage, and inheritance with those who could no longer make room.

Perfection had no place there. Perfection did not accommodate.

So the power sought counsel.

It took a body broad in the foot and heavy in the limbs, though not so skillfully made that anyone mistook it for a citizen. It wore a dark work coat and lacquered apron. It carried no emblem. Rain ran from its hood. It appeared one morning in an open judgment hall and waited while the scheduled cases were heard: a dispute over roof heat, a blocked side arch, a canal market accused of drawing too many juvenile swimmers into its strainers.

Small grazers wandered beneath the benches. One scraped experimentally at the power’s boot.

When at last the judges asked its profession, it said:

“I keep what may be lost between one thing and the next.”

This was not a recognized guild.

They asked for examples.

“The interval between a warning and a failure,” it said. “The room between a need and its exhaustion. The spare path beside the necessary path. The memory of what has not happened recently.”

The judges conferred. An elderly route-keeper touched the wet floor, as though feeling for a distant engine.

“You keep margins,” she said.

“If you permit it.”

The city did not grant permission quickly.

It sent the stranger to inspect culverts, then roofs, then abandoned pressure houses. It was made to accompany herd keepers during rain and silt divers during discharge. Guilds tested it with incomplete diagrams and contradictory songs. Children asked it which pools were safe, then led it deliberately to pools that were not. For nine years it performed minor work. It reset worn hinges. It scraped shell borers from sluice gates. It sat night watches beside a failing pump and did not prevent the pump from failing.

When asked why, it replied, “You had already built its replacement.”

The replacement was smaller and less efficient. It had been assembled from parts of a grain press and an obsolete ore lift. It shuddered badly and required three people to start. But when the old pump stopped, the new one carried enough water to preserve the nesting wall and keep the eastern channel wet until repairs were complete.

Enough was a quantity the power respected.

In time, the judges allowed it one intervention.

Many expected a miracle of cleansing. The deepest basins were sick. Their ancient inhabitants had accumulated centuries of mercury, arsenic, copper, and nameless byproducts from abandoned industries. Shell layers had thickened around their organs. Their feeding folds opened slowly. In one southern basin, the old one had ceased moving altogether, and every month the city excavated another chamber around it as though architecture could compensate for pain.

Others wanted the power to enter the forgotten machinery beneath the central wards. There, shafts turned without known source and transmitted motion into districts that had grown dependent on them. No living keeper understood the whole arrangement. If the buried motion stopped, no one could predict what else would stop.

Still others asked for more water, less water, stronger foundations, harmless predators, accurate records, incorruptible judges, children who never slipped, workers who never tired, and tower animals that would call plainly in human speech.

The power refused to choose among these requests.

Instead, it asked that every district name not what it most desired, but what it could least afford to lose without warning.

The answers took seven years to gather.

Clean inflow.

Warmth on the northern nesting walls.

The canal beneath the infirmary.

The migration passage under the freight court.

The low harmonic in the fourth compression tower.

Two fingers of water over the western microbial sheets.

Access to the foundation barges.

The household cultures.

The memory of emergency valve order.

The power listened to all of them. It listened also to answers no citizen had spoken: the pressure range in which swimmers spawned, the particular aerosol required by a bridge colony’s young, the warm crack where gutter predators laid their eggs, the mineral trace by which herds recognized a route through buildings changed beyond sight.

Then it performed its miracle.

It did not add abundance.

It added slack.

All across the plateau, neglected things revealed second uses. A sealed conduit beneath the eastern wards proved sound enough to carry emergency water. An abandoned lift weight could be lowered to drive pumps for half a day. Reservoir walls, thought solid, contained old overflow channels packed with harmless mineral crust. The crust dissolved under a precisely altered wash and opened paths no plan recorded.

The power did not create these things from nothing. In most cases, someone had built them long before. They had merely passed out of memory.

Where no margin existed, the power taught the city to make one. Not a perfect substitute, not enough to continue unchanged, but enough to fail slowly.

Flywheels were fitted to store another three turns.

Heat galleries received secondary vents.

Herd routes forked before entering narrow districts.

Maintenance songs acquired verses for events that had not happened within living memory.

Public halls set aside sealed crocks containing fragments of household cultures.

Barges were moored on both sides of gates that might jam.

Every indispensable keeper trained one person outside the guild, and every guild entrusted one essential sequence to children, who altered it into a game and thereby preserved it more faithfully than plaques.

The city became no less old. Pumps still failed. Foundations still settled. Predators still took the unwary. Industries still produced substances that could not be permitted into living water.

But interruption ceased, little by little, to travel so easily.

A stopped pump no longer meant an immediately dry wall. A dry wall no longer meant a colony’s total death. The colony’s retreat no longer forced all its young into one ventilation house. One clogged vent no longer poisoned an entire sleeping district.

Between cause and consequence there appeared a narrow chamber in which attention could act.

That chamber was the power’s true gift.

Its second gift was more difficult.

It entered the deep basins, not to heal the old creatures into youth, but to ask what could be lifted from them without killing what they had become.

No human language passed between them. The silt mothers knew gradients, currents, burden, warmth, and the long pressure of accumulated shell. The power made itself intelligible as clean water entering slowly.

The oldest answered by opening one feeding fold.

For the next century, the city changed its industries by degrees. It did not cease discharge overnight. Such purity would have been another catastrophe. Instead, each processing house learned to reclaim one substance previously surrendered to the basins. Copper first, then mercury, then acids, then the fine black compounds no one had named because naming them would imply responsibility.

The work was expensive. Production slowed. Several guilds resisted. There were judgments, strikes, sabotaged valves, and public arguments lasting longer than some human lives. The power settled none of them by command. Benevolence did not require making choice painless. Wisdom did not mean deciding in place of those who would bear the decision.

It testified when asked.

It showed what the basin creatures felt.

That was enough.

The old ones began, layer by layer, to lay down cleaner shell. Not clean shell—nothing in the city was free of everything else—but shell through which their sensory filaments could grow without curling inward. One southern silt mother, immobile for forty-three years, shifted the breadth of a hand.

Bells were rung throughout the adjacent wards.

The creature did not understand the bells. It understood that the water hurt less.

The power stayed.

This surprised those who had expected a visitation. Surely a being capable of hearing the whole city would depart once its mercy had been delivered. But the power took shifts. It accepted assignments. It was reprimanded for arriving late to a culvert inspection after spending too long observing an unfamiliar parasite in a grazer’s mouth. It lost tools. Its lacquered coat split at the shoulder and was patched with material of a noticeably different black.

Whether these failures were genuine, no one knew. Eventually it became discourteous to ask.

It never became the ruler of the city. No office could contain its responsibility without weakening everyone else’s. It held no property. It claimed no roof heat, drainwater, shell deposit, or passage right. When summoned to court, it stood among engineers and naturalists and gave its judgment last.

Often its advice was simply to wait.

Sometimes it said, “Move the stair.”

Sometimes, “Let the canal go where it is already going.”

Once, in a case concerning a tower colony whose droppings corroded the only bridge to a crowded residential quarter, it said, “Build them a better tower.”

“Where?” the judges asked. Every nearby foundation was occupied.

The power pointed to an obsolete refinery stack scheduled for demolition.

The colony moved there over eleven breeding seasons. Its calls changed as it learned the dormant stack’s acoustics. The bridge was repaired. The stack remained, and the animals’ new song caused a sealed valve far below to resonate audibly for the first time in two centuries.

Behind that valve, keepers found a chamber of old emergency tools, ruined paper, six intact respirators, and the mineral tiles of workers whose names had been lost.

The tiles were placed in the floor of the new tower court.

In later generations, stories about the power contradicted one another.

Some said it had always lived beneath the plateau and that the oldest pumps were movements of its sleeping body. Some said it was a citizen from the far future, returned by a route through time that could be traveled only at the pace of subsidence. Some said there had been no single power at all—that the city, after centuries of attention, had finally become capable of answering itself, and had invented a person through whom to do so.

Children were told that if they found an extra spillway in a place where no adult remembered building one, the Keeper of Margins had passed that way.

The guild itself remained small. Its members wore no common color. They were selected from every profession and from none. Their task was not to maintain pumps or canals or herds, but to study the spaces between responsibilities. They asked what would happen if a thing ceased. They kept routes no one currently needed. They preserved small, inefficient devices. They rehearsed unlikely disasters. They watched for systems that had become too perfect to bend.

Their central rule was carved into replaceable stone above the door of their damp, unprestigious hall:

**WHAT MUST NOT FAIL MUST BE ALLOWED TO CHANGE.**

Below it, in smaller letters:

**WHAT WILL FAIL MUST BE GIVEN TIME.**

Rain softened both inscriptions. They were recut many times, never in exactly the same hand.

Ages passed. The city continued to settle. Some districts were abandoned to water. Others rose on layered foundations of masonry, shell, tile, corroded machinery, and the mineral remains of the honored dead. New animals arrived. Old species vanished despite every accommodation. The citizens mourned them without pretending that wise care could preserve all forms forever.

The power mourned too.

That may have been its greatest act of benevolence: it did not stand outside loss.

When the last of a delicate canal species failed to return from its spawning tunnels, the power spent three rainy seasons helping children search the drains. When no living swimmer was found, it carried one of the empty shells to the lock where the species had once gathered. The shell was fixed beneath the waterline, where currents could pass through it.

“What use is that?” a child asked.

“None that I know,” said the power.

This answer was remembered.

Not everything had to continue by becoming useful. Some things could end and still be given a place.

At dusk, the power could sometimes be seen on the upper bridges, indistinguishable at first from any other heavy figure in a wet coat. It would stand with one hand against the railing, feeling the vibration of freight lines, pumps, animal calls, runoff, foot traffic, and the vast buried motion below.

It did not hear harmony. Harmony was too simple a word. There were conflicts, injuries, hungers, extinctions, wastes, and labors that no beauty redeemed. The city was not good merely because it persisted.

But it was alive to consequence.

It noticed.

It adjusted.

It had learned to make room not only for what was strong enough to demand room, but for what might disappear quietly.

So the benevolent and wise power did not save the city once.

It helped the city become more capable of saving one thing at a time.

Then it remained nearby, keeping open the interval.
