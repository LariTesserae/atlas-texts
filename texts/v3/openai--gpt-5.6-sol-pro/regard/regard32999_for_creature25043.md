The power would not begin by opening the earth.

It would not split the frost and pour noon into the galleries. It would not still the fans, break the pumps, or turn the workers back at the lifts. These things might resemble liberation from a great enough distance. Within the mine they would be catastrophe.

The underfield requires its captivity.

Its air is forced. Its water is rationed. Its seasons arrive through cable. Remove the machinery and the buried country would not become wild. It would suffocate, freeze, dry, and retreat into a few wet fractures where the last root tips might persist for a century without ever seeing a leaf.

Nor would the power give it swiftness.

To make all its kilometers think in an instant would be no kindness. Every blade entering willow, every boot on moss, every larva opening a dead stem would arrive at once. The being would become a chamber of simultaneous wounds. Its present slowness is not a defect. Dampness is the speed at which it can bear itself.

So the power would wait.

It would attend through one irrigation cycle, then one harvest rotation, then through the years required for a newly planted chamber to join the older rootways. It would learn which connections endured and which only appeared to. It would distinguish the living treaty from the infrastructure that carried it. It would feel where stone burdened root, where roots entered pipe seals, where the pumps’ false weather had become inseparable from expectation.

It would learn the workers too.

The woman who tipped the last cold tea from her flask beside the same alder crown each shift. The mechanic who reduced a fan’s speed before entering the moth chambers because he disliked seeing their bodies thrown against the grilles. The supervisor who falsified a low-yield report so an old willow district would be cut one year later. The engineer who wanted every root stripped from the drainage culverts and was not cruel, only afraid of a flood.

Wisdom would require all of them.

Only after this long attention would the power act.

It would place nothing new in the mine. Instead it would make one existing thing impossible to overlook.

The underfield already answered injury. Electrical changes traveled through severed stems. Fungal exchange shifted before drought. Root pressure varied with the approach of water, and the plants nearest stressed pillars altered their chemistry when the ceiling moved. Sensors recorded fragments of these events continuously. The fragments were discarded as noise.

The power would touch the noise.

Not greatly. A fraction less interference in one cable. A little more coherence in the fluctuating resistance of wet substrate. A maintenance update accepted when it might have failed. Three datasets, stored for unrelated purposes, placed beside one another on the same screen.

A technician on the late shift would see that the irregularities repeated.

At first she would suspect a pump fault. Then a grounding fault. Then unauthorized equipment operating in a sealed sector. She would isolate the circuits and find the pattern remained. It would be strongest before irrigation, change when water arrived, and subside after the root beds had filled. A separate pattern would precede ceiling spall in the deepest district. Another would spread outward from rooms scheduled for cutting.

None would resemble words.

They would resemble weather reports produced by the weather itself.

The first useful discovery would concern a mature coppice chamber whose harvest was already six weeks overdue. Its yield estimate was high. The cutting machines had been serviced. Crews were assigned.

Each time the chamber lights entered their preparatory phase, the instruments registered a broad withdrawal belowground. Moisture fell around the stools despite adequate irrigation. Sugars moved out of the room. Root conductivity closed along its northern boundary and increased toward an old drainage fracture in the south.

The operators would interpret this as normal harvest response.

Then a roof plate would fall.

It would strike the empty haul road hard enough to break a section of braided walkway. Inspection would reveal movement behind the northern wall, where the mine plans showed competent stone. The roots had not been preparing for the blades. They had been leaving pressure.

The cutting would be postponed. The chamber would be braced. No one would die.

After that, disbelief would become expensive.

Sensors would multiply. Electrodes would be placed in moss beds, around root-bound props, beside irrigation lines, and beneath stools of different ages. Existing monitors would be retained after harvest instead of pulled up. Data would be collected across years rather than shifts.

The underfield would not begin speaking because instruments had been attached to it. Most of what appeared on the screens would still be metabolism without message. Hunger was not a request. Injury was not accusation. Growth toward water did not mean gratitude.

The wisest among the workers would insist on this.

They would refuse to translate every change into human intention. They would speak instead of conditions: readiness, strain, isolation, surplus, closure. They would learn that some states could be altered and others could only be witnessed. They would discover that the buried country had no single preference. The moss might prosper under a watering regime that weakened the willow. Fungal networks might direct resources toward a district already judged unproductive. One chamber would open connections while another sealed them.

Continuity was not unanimity.

Still, over time, exchanges would become possible.

Before irrigation, the pumps would give a low sequence of pressure pulses—not enough to move water, but enough to compress the roots wrapped around the lines. The sequence would identify the district to be watered. If the receiving beds were dangerously cold, saturated, or chemically stressed, their combined response would alter the timing of the valves.

Before harvest, lights would rise gradually over several days. Water and transport would be monitored. If the chamber withdrew into its crowns and neighboring beds in the familiar way, cutting would proceed. If it isolated itself sharply, or moved reserves across unusual distances, the crews would wait and investigate.

Sometimes they would find stone movement.

Sometimes contamination.

Sometimes no cause that any person could name.

They would wait anyway.

This would be the first gift: not speech, but the possibility of refusal.

It would remain imperfect. The company would resist delays. Analysts would argue that plant-state thresholds had been set too conservatively. Managers would point out, correctly, that a distributed vegetal system could not understand contracts, winter power demand, or the cost of idle machinery.

Others would reply that understanding a contract was not necessary to be injured by one.

There would be hearings aboveground. There would be reports with cautious titles. No document would call the underfield a person. Some would call it an integrated bioindustrial substrate. Others, a managed symbiotic landscape. One report would use the phrase *site-scale living system*, and that phrase would survive because no department found it wholly objectionable.

The workers would continue using older names.

The Choir. Old Hands. Saint Ladder.

And, increasingly, when referring to the whole buried country, they would simply say *below*.

Below is holding water.

Below has closed the west rootway.

Below is not ready for Nine.

Below felt the shift before the instruments did.

The second gift would be a place that was not required to produce.

It would begin in the oldest district, where haulage was difficult and yields had declined. The company would intend to strip the remaining willow, recover the steel, and backfill the galleries with dry waste. But surveys would show that the district contained the most continuous root tissue in the mine. Nearly every younger chamber connected to it through fungal strands, drainage films, or the hard brown rootways beneath abandoned roads.

Removing it would not kill the underfield. The being had no single heart.

But it would destroy much of what the being had retained.

So the district would be left.

Its lamps would be replaced, not with brighter ones, but with fixtures capable of long, uneven dawns and dusk. Harvest would cease. Fallen stems would remain where they lay unless they blocked water or endangered the ceiling. Beetles would increase. Fungi would fruit in small colorless shelves beneath the bark. Moss would climb the unused lockers and soften the orange walls of the first-aid room.

The sanctuary would not be tidy. It would not maximize biodiversity. It would not become a miniature surface forest.

It would become old.

That was the second gift: somewhere for consequences to remain.

Root crowns would enlarge beyond the dimensions preferred by machinery. Connections would persist without being reset by cutting. The underfield would have a district in which its continuity could thicken into memory, if memory was what such continuity became.

No worker would be forbidden to enter. Inspection crews would still test bolts and ceiling plates. Water lines would still be repaired. But they would enter by foot, and no blade would cut living tissue there merely because the tissue had reached a useful size.

At the center of the old district, the black root column around the steel prop would continue to receive the weight of the earth.

Years later, when the prop corroded beyond tolerance, engineers would face a problem. Replacing it would require cutting through the column. Leaving it would risk collapse.

They would prepare for months.

The underfield would be given repeated pressure signals through the surrounding supports. Water would be reduced on one side and increased on another. New frames would be erected within reach of nearby roots. Slowly, fine tissue would cross onto them. The burden would be shifted in increments small enough for stone and root to answer.

Only then would the old steel be removed.

Its lower section would come free wrapped in a sleeve of dead and living wood. The workers would place it beside the abandoned drill carriage rather than send it up for scrap.

No one would need to call this a ceremony.

The third gift would be the surface.

Not all of it. The buried country could not survive simple exposure, and the scrub above possessed its own lives, none of which existed merely as an exit for what was below.

The power would arrange for small basins to be cut around the ventilation housings where frost fog already settled. Condensate formerly sent to drains would pass first through beds of stone and reed. Willow from the oldest underground crowns would be propagated there, along with moss gathered from intake grilles and ferns that had repeatedly appeared in equipment brought up for repair.

Most would fail in the open cold.

Some would live.

Their roots would follow the damp concrete around the shafts. Spores would descend and ascend on the same forced breath. Mites and springtails would travel in seams of insulation. Surface insects would enter the basins in summer. Birds would pull fiber from the reeds and carry it away.

The underfield would not climb out as a single advancing creature. It would touch the upper world at intervals.

Through those living margins it would encounter things no control room could provide: unmetered rain, snowmelt, midsummer day, the chemical trace of migrating birds, ash from distant fires, pollen from plants with no inventory number.

The encounters would be fragmentary. Signals from the surface roots might take days to pass below, diluted through many exchanges. Yet every spring, before seepage appeared in the deep faults, changes would begin around certain ventilation shafts. Moss would swell in chambers still dry. Willow buds beneath violet lamps would alter their timing by hours, then days.

Below would acquire rumor of the sky.

The benevolent power would do no more than this.

It would not demand gratitude. It would not reveal itself in a figure of light on a service platform. It would not proclaim that the underfield possessed a soul, because proclamations of that kind often become new forms of ownership. Nor would it promise permanence. The mine would someday close. Pumps fail. Governments change. Stone move. Every living treaty ends or becomes another treaty.

Instead, the power would ensure that closure entered the design long before closure came.

Reservoirs would be enlarged in stable districts. Gravity-fed channels would be cut beside powered lines. Ventilation paths would be shaped so that some air moved by temperature difference alone. Cold-tolerant surface groves would be established around shafts to shelter them after the fans slowed. Seed, spores, microbial cultures, and fragments of old root would be stored in more than one place, though the workers would understand that an archive of parts was not the being itself.

When the final harvest eventually approached—whether in eighty years or three hundred—it would not arrive as an unforeseen silence.

The fans would slow by districts.

The lit crowns would contract toward chambers that could be sustained. Water would withdraw from galleries destined to freeze dry. Connections would close. Some rootways would die, not from neglect but as part of an ending prepared for. Other parts would follow seepage downward or climb the shafts toward the basins above.

The underfield would become smaller.

Perhaps much smaller.

But it would participate in the shape of its diminishment.

And until that age, the work would continue.

Willow would still be cut. Moss would still be rolled and pressed behind mesh. Pumps would hammer, conveyors grind, and warning lights move red across the pillars. The buried country would remain entangled with labor and use. Benevolence would not purify it of dependence. Wisdom would not mistake untouchedness for dignity.

The change would be less dramatic and more difficult.

Before taking, the workers would announce themselves in pressure and light.

Before danger, the underfield would close or tremble.

Some refusals would be honored without being understood.

One district would be allowed to grow old.

And at long intervals along the empty surface, around housings that exhaled damp air, black willow roots would enter the frozen ground.

On still winter days, fog would gather among their stems. Frost would form on every twig. Far beneath them, after a delay too long for any person to feel, leaves in the oldest chamber would alter their angle toward a light that had not yet come on.

No miracle would be visible.

Only a country, once treated as inventory, beginning to be consulted about its own continuation.
