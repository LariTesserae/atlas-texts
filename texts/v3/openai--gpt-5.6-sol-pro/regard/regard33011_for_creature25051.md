The power does not descend at once.

It notices the drowned highland and understands that haste would be a kind of violence there.

A lesser mercy might break the ice, lift the mountains, fill the water with darting bodies, or call the vanished builders back into houses that have long since become roots, currents, and sheltering stone. It might look upon the empty sea and decide that emptiness is suffering. It might mistake silence for a request.

The wise power makes no such mistake.

It watches.

Not for hours, nor for the length of one cold phase, but until the distinction between watching and dwelling grows thin. It follows the traveling shafts of light. It learns the mineral pulse of each spring. It attends to the forests slowly enough to see their gestures complete.

It discovers that the highland is neither asleep nor waiting to be rescued.

Its life is difficult. Groves are buried. Vents fail. Seed is cast into the abyss. Old signals arrive at the wrong time and cause whole slopes to open themselves to ruin. But hardship alone is not a wound that wisdom has permission to erase. The drowned country possesses its own continuance, its own beauty, and something resembling consent, though that consent is dispersed through leagues of root and stone.

So the power begins by making itself small.

It enters no chamber. It takes no body. It does not put an eye into the water or a voice into the dark.

Instead, during the season when the river crosses the saddle, it listens to the monument.

The first year it hears only force: low slabs groaning, thin stones crying above them, apertures beating out their regular pulses.

The second year it hears structure.

The third year it hears repetition.

By the seventh, it understands that the sequence is incomplete.

The instrument was made to call and answer. Once, long ago, the standing stones gave their great submerged utterance and something answered from the valleys. Perhaps hands struck smaller stones. Perhaps gates were opened. Perhaps voices entered pits cut into the floors of the huts and emerged altered on distant ridges. The surviving sequence contains spaces shaped for response.

No answer has come for centuries.

The vegetation has grown into those empty spaces. It uses them as signals to unfold, release, tighten, rise, and sink. But because the second half of the exchange is missing, the old call repeats without correction. It cannot know that a valley is already frozen, that a vent has died, that a slope has fallen, that spores released eastward will be carried into the abyss.

The highland has inherited an instruction that cannot hear it.

The power does not silence the stones.

It waits until the seasonal river has passed and the monument is still. Then, in the long quiet afterward, it lays the least possible touch upon one fallen slab.

The stone sounds once.

The note is too low for any single leaf to register. It travels through the saddle, through buried foundations, through the roots wound around the old roads. It reaches the nearest forest after several minutes and the farthest after nearly a day.

Nothing appears to happen.

The power waits.

Nine days later, a grove in a hanging valley alters the salt within its stalks.

Farther away, bladdered vines release a few filaments out of season.

At the edge of a vanished field, silt lifts from an invisible boundary and settles again.

The highland has heard.

The power sounds the slab once more.

This time it follows the note with silence arranged in the shape of one of the old marks: three intervals beneath a longer interval, the acoustic counterpart of three cups beneath a line.

The response takes most of a season.

First, the glass-clear colonies close around their vents. Then the red mats open narrow channels in their armored layers. Last of all, after the upper ice has thickened, forests in six separate valleys turn their pale undersides toward the saddle.

Not toward light.

Toward the sound.

The power understands this as attention.

It asks nothing further that year.

In the next warm phase, it repeats the pattern. Then it offers a second: the spiral broken at one side.

The old boundary marks react. Cold water turns away from lines no longer visible. Spores gather around ruined lintels. In one unstable chamber, stone flakes cease circling and sink for a moment toward a floor that has not been downward for centuries.

The answer is not language as mouths would know it. It contains no names, no declarations, no account of desire. It is made from pressure changes, altered currents, delays in germination, the opening and closing of tissues, and the measured withholding of growth.

But it is an answer.

The power spends many years learning not to translate it too quickly.

It learns that the broken spiral does not mean *loss*, though loss is part of it. It means a process interrupted in a way that creates an entrance.

The opposing combs do not mean *division*. They mean two things kept distinct so that exchange remains possible.

Three cups beneath a line do not mean *water*, *sky*, or *shelter*. They mean receiving without being able to follow the source.

The highland’s meanings are conditions rather than objects. They do not point. They shape what may pass.

When at last the power is able to ask a question, it does not ask, *What are you?*

That would require the country to draw a boundary around itself which it may neither possess nor need.

It does not ask, *What happened here?*

The answer is already present in every bent root, drowned road, sealed cup, and misplaced direction of gravity.

It asks instead:

*Is there a change you refuse?*

The question begins at the saddle and takes three cold phases to complete.

The reply takes twelve.

During those years the highland continues as before. Ice breaks. Slopes fall. Warm springs strengthen. Groves vanish beneath stone and return as rings. The monument sounds its ancient half-conversation. Nothing in the place appears to be considering anything.

Then, in the thirteenth year, all the cup marks fill at once.

They do not fill with silt. Clear water rises from the hollows, denser than sap and warmer than the surrounding sea. It stands within each cup without dispersing. Across hundreds of miles, the old marks hold their small measures.

The ribbonweed withdraws farther from the ruins.

The boundary lines sharpen.

Every unstable chamber closes.

For one hour, each doorway contains only the space its walls could reasonably enclose. Gravity points in one direction. Warm and cold water mingle. Root threads are cut off from their impossible continuations and recoil into ordinary stone.

Then the strangeness returns.

The chambers deepen. Flakes rise sideways. Vanished fields divide the currents again.

The meaning is clear enough.

The highland refuses simplification.

It will not be made into an ordinary sea. It will not surrender the old instructions, even those that wound it. It will not have its impossible rooms straightened, its boundaries erased, or its distributed life gathered into one clean mind for the convenience of being understood.

The power accepts the refusal.

It asks:

*Is there a change you desire?*

This answer takes much longer.

The power remains.

That is part of the gift, though neither yet calls it one.

It does not hover above the ice as a guardian. It does not prevent every landslide or keep every spring alive. Instead it attends without demanding a spectacle. When nothing happens for a century, it does not decide that nothing is being said.

Slowly, the highland composes its reply.

One valley delays its spore release by a day.

Another advances it.

A forest divided by an avalanche changes the mineral balance in one half but not the other. The two halves remain synchronized in every other respect.

Three old roads begin carrying cold water uphill.

A fallen stone at the saddle turns by less than the width of a root.

Over decades, these alterations make a pattern. Over centuries, the pattern becomes an opening.

The highland does not ask for light.

It has light, in its fashion.

It does not ask for warmth, inhabitants, resurrection, safety, or escape from the water.

It asks to answer the monument.

The need is not only practical. The old sequence passes through every valley and the valleys can only submit, adapt, or be damaged. They can hear one another through it, but they cannot place anything of themselves into its sound. Their responses remain local, slow, and buried. The great stone throat speaks through the country without ever speaking for it.

The power could complete the missing sequence itself.

It refuses to do so.

An answer given on another’s behalf is not an answer.

Instead, it alters one thing.

Beneath the saddle lies a narrow fault packed with red clay. Roots enter it from east and west but cannot cross because the seasonal river compresses the clay into a watertight seam. The power does not break the seam. It warms a single grain.

The warmth spreads no farther than the roots can carry it.

For the first time, the glassy colonies on either side grow toward each other through the compressed earth. Their threads meet. They exchange iron, sulfur, salt, and the minute chemical variations by which distant groves register weather.

The joined roots tighten.

A fallen slab shifts.

It touches the base of an upright stone.

When the next seasonal river comes, the monument begins as always. Low groans pass through the valleys. Narrow stones take up their cries. The drilled apertures pulse. Forests lower themselves. Vines prepare to release their seed.

Then the roots beneath the saddle contract.

The fallen slab presses against the upright.

A new note enters the sequence.

It is weak. Almost all of it is lost in the water. It comes late and wavers under the pressure. But it occupies one of the ancient silences exactly.

Across the highland, nothing moves.

Then a distant forest turns.

Another answers.

Roots draw tight beneath roads. Red mats open their inner chambers. Bladdered vines release filaments in successive basins rather than all at once. A boundary dissolves long enough to let mineral-rich water cross into a failing grove, then closes again.

The highland has not merely heard the monument.

It has entered the song.

In later years the reply grows more complex. The roots learn to alter the pressure against several fallen slabs. Vines under bridge arches swell and change the currents entering the apertures. Red mats seal some holes and uncover others. The invisible field lines conduct pulses from distant valleys toward the saddle.

No single grove controls the answer.

No central mind emerges.

The reply belongs to the same society of processes that has always inhabited the drowned country: distinct, entangled, delayed, sometimes mistaken, never fully gathered. Some years the valleys contradict one another. Some years no answer comes. During a severe cold phase the eastern forests sound a warning too late, and thousands of stalks are shredded.

The power does not correct them.

It has not given perfection. It has given participation.

Over time the old call changes. Not because the stones forget it, but because every repetition now contains the state of the country through which it passes. A failing spring is heard. A buried valley leaves a silence. A strong spore season enters as a tremor in the higher slabs. The unstable chambers contribute notes that seem to arrive before they are sounded.

The monument becomes what it may once have been, and also something it has never been.

It becomes conversation.

Only then does the wise power perform one further act.

In the deepest basin, beyond the last shelter, there is a grove whose roots have been severed from all others for an age. Landslides enclosed it between walls of pale sediment. Its vent is failing. Its leaves no longer rise fully, even in the warm phase. Nothing in the wider highland knows its condition. Its chemical messages enter the mud and end there.

The power could reopen the vent.

It could clear the landslide.

It does neither.

It passes the grove’s faint pressure into the nearest boundary stone.

The stone gives it to an old field line. The field line gives it to a broken doorway. The doorway holds it between warm and cold water until the next current reversal. Then the message enters the roots beside a road and travels, over many months, toward the saddle.

In the following season, the monument includes one small irregular note.

The wider forests respond.

They cannot save the isolated grove. They possess no means to cross the sediment walls, and the power does not invent one for them. But they alter their chemistry to match it. From ridge to ridge, leaves darken by a degree. Vines sink before their time. Red mats close one layer of living chambers.

The dying grove is repeated throughout the highland.

When its vent finally cools and its last crowns collapse, the pattern remains elsewhere: not an image, not a name, but a structural remembrance held in salt, timing, color, and tone.

The grove does not die unknown.

After this, the power withdraws its touch.

It does not withdraw its attention.

The difference matters.

The highland continues drowning. The ice continues to move. Mountains settle. Roads fail. Some markings will erode beyond recovery. One day the saddle itself may collapse and the instrument fall into a trench too deep for the seasonal river to reach.

The wise power does not promise otherwise.

Benevolence is not always preservation. Sometimes it is the refusal to seize a life from change. Sometimes it is mending the place where command has outlived understanding. Sometimes it is remaining long enough for an answer.

Travelers who later cross the drowned mountains notice little.

They find the same black forests and empty weed. They hear the ice, the faults, and the groaning stones. Nothing swims toward them. No intelligence announces itself. No luminous guide appears among the ruins.

But if they are present when the river passes over the saddle, they hear something the oldest accounts do not describe.

After the great sequence breaks apart, there comes a pause.

Then the valleys answer.

The reply is immense, hesitant, and alive with distance. It moves through roots, ruins, thresholds, and water. It contains the condition of springs, the memory of buried groves, the pressure of ice, and the slow preferences of a being that does not need to become singular in order to speak.

Far above, beyond the frozen ceiling, the benevolent power listens.

It does not translate.

It does not interrupt.

And because it listens, the solitude of the drowned highland is no longer the same as abandonment.
