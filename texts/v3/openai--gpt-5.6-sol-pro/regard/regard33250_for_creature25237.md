The power would not fill the basin.

That would be the first temptation: to gather rain from the clouds, clean it, pour it into the excavation until every bench, machine, road, and raw face lay beneath a single innocent lake. From the rim it would look like healing. Light would cross it without interruption. Maps would no longer require dates. People would call the water blue even when it was gray, and in time they would forget what had been drowned.

But the power would see the eggs in the wheel ruts, the martins in the clay, the fox asleep beneath the conveyor, the warm bacterial threads working at the poisoned seep. It would see that completion, imposed too quickly, is only another form of destruction.

Nor would it return the removed hills.

Even a benevolent strength cannot put back what was taken without crushing what has since arrived. To raise the old ground through the pools would bury fish in their water, bats in their housings, names inside electrical cabinets, and the drowned roads still conducting currents through the dark. Restoration to an earlier state would require choosing which moment deserved to be called the true one. Wisdom would refuse that vanity.

So for one full turning of the seasons, the power would do nothing that could be seen.

It would descend below the rim and learn the basin at its own pace.

It would stand beside the dragline long enough to feel a hill pass through the bucket one load at a time. It would sit in switching huts, accepting tea from workers who did not know whom they had admitted. It would follow the dogs along the inherited roads and stop with them where memory ended at the water. It would enter the culverts with the eels. It would wait in a midge cloud without brushing it aside. It would press its hand against the wet clay walls and feel the minute internal rearrangements by which a slope prepares to fail.

It would learn every pool twice: once in flood and once in drought.

It would hear the pumps separately. Not as thunder, but as bearings, impellers, fatigue, duty, fear. It would know which machine prevented a sudden drowning and which continued only because no living person had been authorized to turn it off. It would distinguish necessary noise from inherited noise.

It would ask the oldest workers to draw the vanished basin from memory. They would draw workshops below ponds, roads passing through spoil heaps, cables beneath reed beds. Their maps would contradict one another. The power would keep them all.

It would ask the youngest what they believed the place might become. Some would say a lake. Some, a reserve. Some, a new excavation. Some would shrug because they had been promised too many futures by people who did not work below the rim.

The power would ask the water, though water answers only by going where it can.

It would ask the frogs, though frogs answer by continuing or falling silent.

It would ask the clay, whose answer takes years.

Only then would it begin.

First, it would make the basin honest.

Every cable that was live would declare itself. Every pipe under pressure would tremble visibly. Gas would no longer gather without warning in low chambers. Dry crust over deep silt would bear a dark pattern no eye could mistake. Walls approaching collapse would shed a narrow line of pale dust three days beforehand, and the dust would shine at night.

No person would again die there because a dead machine was not dead, because opaque water pretended to be shallow, or because a map remembered a road that the earth had withdrawn.

Danger would remain. Steepness, flood, weight, and weather would not be abolished. But danger would cease to lie.

The frogs would still fall silent for herons. They would not be turned into instruments. The power would not burden any creature with perfect prophecy. Instead, it would give human beings the humility to treat every warning as part of a chorus: gauge, survey post, sulfur smell, buckled rail, frog, stain line, ache in an old worker’s knees. No single sign would be infallible. Together they would be enough.

Next, the power would separate poison from difficulty.

Difficulty belongs to living landscapes. Poison need not.

Acidic spoil would not become fertile overnight. Instead, its worst outflows would be led through long, shallow gardens of stone, lime, reeds, fungi, and bacterial mats. Iron would fall from the water in rust-colored terraces. Sulfur would be taken up where it could be taken up. Metals would settle into places designed to hold them and from which they could be removed without reopening the whole wound.

The treatment marshes would not be hidden. Walkways would cross them. Children from the villages would be shown the orange water entering and the clearer water leaving. They would learn that purification is labor, not absolution.

Where contamination had entered fish, the power would not make the fish harmless by decree. It would make knowledge arrive before hunger. Testing would be free, frequent, and trusted. Safe ponds would be marked without shame; unsafe ones closed without fences alone, because fences teach little and fail often. Fishers would be given other waters and a place in deciding when these might reopen.

The power would touch the bodies of workers who had breathed dust and fumes for decades. It would not erase their memories with their illness. It would remove what could be removed, ease what could not, and make certain that no family had to prove, alone and repeatedly, that the basin had entered someone’s lungs.

Then it would call the machines to account.

Some pumps would remain. Without them, water would rise too quickly, join poisoned pools to clean ones, undercut nesting cliffs, lift buried fuel, and enter low villages beyond the rim. Other pumps would be stopped one at a time.

Before each stopping, people would walk the predicted shore with poles and flags. Reeds would be moved where they could survive. Culverts would be screened during spawning. Bats would be given chambers higher than the coming water. Concrete refuges would be roughened so that animals could climb them. The rise would be measured in fingers, not meters.

As each motor fell silent, a new part of the basin would become audible.

At first the absence would hurt. Workers would wake at night sensing that something had failed. Frogs would lose the pulse around which they had arranged their calling. Birds would startle at sounds formerly masked. Water dropping from a single pipe would seem impossibly loud.

Then the quieter noises would distribute themselves through the opened space.

The dragline would work last.

It would not be commanded to undo itself. For one final year, under the direction of operators, hydrologists, birders, fishers, geologists, village councils, and people who had once entered illegally and therefore knew routes absent from official maps, it would move earth for no market.

Its bucket would lay down long islands above the highest predicted floods. Their slopes would vary: gravel for lapwings, soft vertical clay for martins, low shelves for rushes, bare warm margins for frogs. It would cut overflow ways broad enough not to become torrents. It would lift contaminated spoil away from future channels and cap it where roots and floodwater could not readily reopen it.

The work would remain imperfect. A designed island would erode in the wrong place. Birds would ignore an excellent cliff and colonize a dangerous one beside an active road. Willows would establish where planners had intended open gravel. The power would permit these insults. Benevolence would not require obedience.

On the dragline’s last day, the bucket would rise empty.

The machine would turn to the safest position its old frame allowed. Its cables would be lowered. Oils, batteries, and hazardous metals would be removed. The housing would remain above the flood line. One room would be kept weather-tight, with the operator’s chair, shift logs, worn controls, spare gloves, and the small domestic arrangements by which people had made an enormity habitable.

The rest would be opened carefully to weather and animals.

Martins would not use it; swallows might. Bats would enter the warm upper chambers. Lichens would establish on the boom. Rain would travel down its steel for generations. The machine would cease to be an instrument of removal and become a landmark by which routes were described.

No one would call this untouched nature.

The power would forbid that lie.

It would not allow the basin to be marketed as a wilderness rescued from human absence, because human hands had made its depths, maintained its waters, carried injured birds, read its walls, stolen from it, suffered from it, and loved it without always knowing that love was the name of their attention.

Nor would it let industry rename abandonment as restoration.

Those who had profited from the excavation would remain responsible after the last profitable load. Responsibility would not expire with leases, mergers, renamed companies, or the death of executives. The power would make obligation adhere to wealth as iron adheres to wet stone. It would follow money through every legal culvert.

But it would not hand the whole basin to distant experts.

The people of the rim, the crews below it, the downstream villages, and those who came seasonally with birds and fish would be given durable authority. Not ceremonial consultation. Authority: over water levels, access, remediation, employment, closures, memory, and the conditions under which any machine might start again.

The basin would belong to no single generation.

A portion would always be kept inaccessible, not as punishment but as mercy. There would be places where martins could dig without a path beneath them, where dogs could pass uncounted, where bacteria could conduct their slow chemistry without being turned into a lesson. The power would know that observation can become another appetite.

Elsewhere, safe routes would descend from the rim. They would not conceal the scale. No scenic platform would reduce the basin to one view. Paths would pass the treatment marshes, the active pumps, the flooded roads, and walls where raw seams remained visible. Markers would show waterlines from different years. Bells, lights, and raised refuges would make sudden storms survivable.

At intervals the path would divide, because no single account would be allowed to govern the place.

One route would tell the geology.

One would tell the labor.

One would tell the water.

One would tell the extinctions and arrivals.

One would bear only names.

The names would include those killed at work, those made ill later, those who mapped the slopes, those who trespassed and drowned, those who organized against the excavation, those who depended on its wages, and those whose homes had been removed before the first cut. No list would be made to cancel another.

The power would create work, because closure without livelihood merely exports the wound into human bodies.

Former machine crews would run the water system, stabilize what must be stabilized, dismantle what could not safely remain, and teach the basin’s mechanical history. Surveyors would continue measuring the terraces after commercial need ended. Chemists would train apprentices from the rim villages. Fishers would monitor fish. Those who knew the unofficial paths would be hired to find where official access failed.

There would be no final master plan.

There would be agreements for five years, twenty years, a century. Some pools would be allowed to join. Others would be kept separate because their chemistries or inhabitants required it. Certain cliffs would be renewed by careful cutting after they slumped into gentler slopes, preserving the temporary habitat on which the martins depended. Some gravel islands would be cleared of trees. Elsewhere willows would be permitted to gather silt and begin the long work of becoming woods.

The power would not mistake succession for virtue. Forest would not be assumed better than reed bed, reed bed better than bare gravel, lake better than puddle. Every state shelters something and excludes something else.

So the basin would remain multiple.

Water behind water.

Cliff behind cliff.

Noise beside quiet.

Machinery beside reeds.

Clean places and places still being cleaned.

Human routes and routes humans agreed not to use.

In drought, emergency channels would keep the deepest pools alive, but not every puddle. The power would not abolish all loss. Tadpoles would still be stranded; gulls would still learn the failing pools. Foxes would take chicks. Old willows would drown. A world in which benevolence prevented every death would also prevent predation, hunger, reproduction, adaptation, and choice. It would not be a living world, only a preserved one.

But no mass death would occur merely because no one wished to assume responsibility.

In flood, water would be given room before it needed to seize it. Spillways would lead it across sacrificial shelves and into broad basins, not through villages. Sirens would still sound, but they would be tested with the people who must hear them. There would be refuge towers for animals as well as workers: rough ramps rising above the stain line, dry cavities, ledges beneath roofs. Not every creature would use them. Enough would.

The power would give the basin one further gift, smaller than safety and stranger than clean water.

Once each year, on a warm evening after the martins had fledged and before the heaviest rains, every unnecessary machine would stop at the same hour.

The pumps required to protect the slopes and villages would continue, but softly, their loads shifted elsewhere. Conveyor impacts would cease. Warning tests would wait. Vehicles would remain at the rim.

For one hour, people would descend and hear what the basin had become.

At first there would be ringing in their ears.

Then water.

Then frogs, no longer fitted between motors.

Wingbeats in the clay wall.

Pebbles falling from a fresh rill.

A dog moving through grass.

The far, delayed call of a bird from beyond visible water.

Bubbles rising through drowned soil.

The almost inaudible tearing of roots as a willow settled farther into its bank.

No speech would be forbidden, but most people would speak quietly.

Afterward the machines that still had reason to work would start again. The sound would return in parts. The basin would not be purified into silence, because silence had never been its only truth.

Years would pass.

The raw walls would soften, though some would be kept raw by water, birds, and deliberate cuts. Reed beds would expand and then retreat. Pools would merge. A treatment marsh would fail and be rebuilt differently. The great spoil heaps would lower by amounts visible only in records. Children who had watched orange water clear over stone would become the people arguing about whether a western pump could finally be retired.

The dogs’ inherited crossings would change. They would still stand sometimes at the edge of water where an old route ought to be. But above the drowned road there would be another, set back from the unstable bank—not made permanent, because nothing there could honestly promise permanence, but made with enough foresight that memory would not fail all at once.

From the rim, visitors might eventually see something beautiful.

The power would not prevent this. Beauty is not betrayal.

But if they called the basin healed, someone would correct them.

Not angrily. Not always.

They would say: healing is what is happening here. It is not what has happened.

And below, on a wet shelf newly exposed by the careful lowering of a pool, tracks would begin before the mud had finished shining.

Beside them would be the print of a worker’s boot.

Neither would erase the other.
