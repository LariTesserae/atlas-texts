### The Power Does Not Descend

The power does not arrive from the sky.

To do so would be to cast a new shadow across the flats, to alter wind and runoff, to impose a silhouette where every handmade thing has learned to bow. Nor does it rise from the flooded chambers. That would lend a false body to what already moves there and confuse reverence with fear.

It does not still the tide-thunder.

Silence, in this country, would be a calamity. Swimmers would lose the pressure by which they time their spawning. Burrowers would surface at the wrong hours. Shellbacks would crowd channels before the water rose. Listeners would wake in terror, unable to feel the familiar violence beneath their sleeping ledges.

The power could stop the chambers.

It could seal the blowholes, calm the deep currents, lift the salt from the water, bring warmth without fissures, light without living jars, metal without vanished mountains. It could raise dry land. It could cover the black shelves in green.

It does none of these things.

A benevolence that replaces a world with an easier one may be only destruction wearing a gentle face.

So the power attends.

It attends to the long films drinking iron at the edges of warm water. It attends to the blind runners, whose hunger is neither wicked nor small. It attends to broad mouths under silt. It follows the water downward through cracks too narrow for any tool-user’s hand, through galleries where translucent swimmers pulse against the dark, through cavities in which pressure moves like weather.

It finds no single sleeper beneath the flats.

There is no buried god, no immense heart, no mouth shaping the answering rhythms. There are colonies joined by mineral membranes. There are animals without permanent forms, spreading through porous stone and withdrawing when the chemistry changes. There are chambers whose walls flex over decades. There are living films beneath living films, exchanging charge through water. There are currents that retain disturbance and return it much later, altered but not erased.

No part understands the whole.

Yet the whole can recoil.

The whole can become accustomed.

The whole, in its slow way, can notice.

The power attends also to the people.

It learns their names, though no one gives them.

It learns the short names of children, pressed into palms before crossings. It learns the lengthening names of route-readers and potters, of one who lost three fingers to a shellback trap and added the shape of that absence to every greeting. It learns the immense final name of an elder who has survived so many migrations that no one gives all of it except at death.

The power does not mistake hardship for virtue. It does not suppose that because the people endure the roar, the roar is good for them. It does not romanticize damaged hearing, drowned children, poisoned films, collapsed floors, or the fear that enters every hand when the stone answers.

But neither does it mistake adaptation for a chain to be broken.

It waits through nine great tides.

Then nine more.

It waits until its knowledge is no longer the knowledge of a visitor.

Only then does it touch the world.

---

In one settlement there is a fragment of copper no longer than a finger joint.

It is green at the edges and bright where generations of thumbs have polished it. The settlement keeps it in a shell box above a warm fissure. It is brought out for births, deaths, route oaths, and the testing of new chambers.

An old floor-listener carries it to a basin whose answering rhythms have troubled her for most of her life.

Her body-name begins with the pulse of rain trapped under a ledge. It ends with three diminishing pressures, signs added after her hearing failed. Among her people she is known by the first and last movement: Rain-Under-Stone, Fading.

She kneels within the warning cairns.

The others remain beyond them, joined wrist to wrist.

Rain-Under-Stone places the copper against the flat.

Usually it brings her a confusion of tremors: distant vents, shifting mud, shellbacks scraping in a nearby channel, the restless accumulation of pressure below.

This time the metal becomes still.

Not the ground. Not the basin.

Only the copper.

For one breath no unwanted vibration passes through it.

The old listener jerks her hand away.

The fragment resumes trembling.

She waits. Then touches it again.

Stillness enters the copper a second time.

Within that stillness comes a pressure no stronger than the pulse in a sleeping child’s wrist.

It does not strike.

It does not command.

It gives the first movement of Rain-Under-Stone’s own name.

The listener flattens herself so quickly that her jaw hits rock.

The cord behind her snaps taut. Alarm races back through thirty hands. Staffs are lowered. Children are drawn beneath plated coats. One of the younger listeners begins the signal for immediate retreat.

Rain-Under-Stone stops them.

Her fingers close on the cord.

Wait.

The copper touches stone again.

The power gives the first movement of her name, then pauses. It does not give the last. It leaves an emptiness where the ending should be.

In their intimate grammar, this is unmistakable.

It is asking.

Rain-Under-Stone’s hand shakes.

She adds the final three pressures.

The power repeats the whole name exactly.

Then it offers a pattern of its own.

The pattern is very simple.

A pressure approaching.

A second pressure yielding before contact.

Water continuing between them.

It might be rendered, imperfectly, as:

**That which makes room without making emptiness.**

Rain-Under-Stone keeps her hand on the copper until the tide rises over her wrists.

---

The people do not worship the fragment.

They argue about it.

Some say the old listener’s damaged senses have deceived her. Some say the deep chambers have learned a new lure. Some believe the copper has always been alive and only now grown old enough to speak. One vent-tender wants the fragment dropped into a bottomless gallery before its rhythm spreads.

It is tested.

No one tests it alone.

Three listeners from different settlements come. Then seven. They carry separate cords and do not touch one another while listening. The copper gives each the opening of their own name and waits for permission before completing it.

It never imitates a dead person.

It never speaks first to a child.

It will not answer while held as a blade.

When asked the safe route across a hollow basin, it gives no reply. The people must still test the crust.

When asked where shellbacks gather, it remains still.

When asked to heat a cooling fissure, it does nothing.

When asked whether a storm is coming, it returns the questioner’s own pulse.

But when they ask, with many hands joined, whether the answering rhythms below the country are made by a being, the fragment gives a pattern so long that no single body can contain it.

They must lie in a ring and pass it from one to another.

The answer moves through them over the course of a night.

**Alive, but not as you are alive.**

**Many, but not separate as you are separate.**

**Aware, but not gathered into one place.**

**Hurt without knowing injury.**

**Afraid without knowing an enemy.**

**Answering without knowing a question.**

At the end comes an image translated into pressure: a hand recoiling from heat before the mind understands burning.

The people remain around the copper after it falls quiet.

At last one of the youngest route-readers asks about the tower.

The power shows them—not a vision, for vision has too much distance in it, but an event conveyed through skin and bone.

Repeated impacts travel downward from the tower’s foundations. The pattern enters flexible chambers far below. Mineral membranes contract. Water is driven sideways. A feeding colony closes its pores. Another colony, deprived of flow, tightens in turn. Pressure accumulates across miles.

Nothing below understands the tower.

Nothing below hates it.

But the deep ecology recoils from its repeated blow. That recoil lifts the crust. When the strained membranes release, the dome falls.

The lost settlement was not punished.

It was not judged.

It was crushed by an answer that did not know it had been asked.

For a long while, no one touches the copper.

Knowledge does not make the old story less terrible. In some ways it makes it worse. Malice can be pleaded with. A god may be appeased. A beast may be avoided.

A reflex extending beneath an entire country has no face to pity.

Rain-Under-Stone places both palms on the floor.

Slowly she gives the tactile sign used to soothe a frightened infant.

The gesture is not intended as speech.

Far below, something changes its rhythm.

---

The power could remain as translator forever.

It refuses.

Dependence can be built downward as surely as a tower is built upward.

Instead, it asks what help the people desire.

At first the answers are immediate.

Quiet.

Dry sleeping ledges.

Warm fissures that do not wander.

Copper enough for every settlement.

A stone that never hollows.

A cure for deafness.

A roof no storm can break.

The return of those taken by collapsed basins.

The power receives every request without rebuke.

Some things it cannot give because wisdom is not omnipotence. Some it will not give because the granting would wound other lives. Most it does not refuse outright. It presses consequences into the copper and lets the people feel them.

If the tide chambers are quieted, eggs fail in distant pools.

If warm fissures are fixed, the deep flow ceases to carry nutrients into old films.

If the stone is made unbreakable, burrowers lose their breeding galleries and pressure finds release beneath settlements instead.

If copper is made common, tools change. The tools permit deeper cutting. Deeper cutting reaches membranes that cannot survive it. Perhaps the people could learn restraint. Perhaps not. The gift would not be copper alone, but every future use of copper.

When asked to restore the dead, the power gives no chain of consequences.

It rests beside the question in silence.

Its silence is warm.

That is all.

The deliberation lasts thirty-seven tides.

Messengers cross the flats. Shell disks turn in the mist. Cords are knotted with arguments and carried between settlements. Elders press histories into younger backs. Children are invited to place their hands on the question, because they will inhabit the longest part of its answer.

At last the people return to the copper.

Their request is given by hundreds of hands, though only Rain-Under-Stone touches the fragment.

**When our making harms what lies below, let us know before its pain becomes our destruction.**

A pause follows.

Then a second request.

**And let what lies below know that we can stop.**

The copper warms.

Not greatly.

Only to the temperature of living skin.

---

The work takes years.

The power does not build on the flats. It teaches relationships.

At the edges of certain one-celled films, mineral crystals already grow in branching fans. They flex under pressure and produce faint charges. The power alters nothing foreign to them. It encourages what is present: a slight preference in growth, a new association between feeder film and crystal, a membrane folded where it was once flat.

The resulting colonies spread no faster than lichen might spread in another world.

They are dark, low, and soft enough to be mistaken for wet stone.

The people call them listening floors.

This name is not entirely correct. The floors do not hear as people hear. They receive sharp surface impacts and release them downward as slow changes in mineral charge. A hammer blow, instead of entering the deep ecology as a sudden wound, arrives gradually, spread over hours and across a wide area.

In the other direction, pressure from below causes the films to tighten into visible ridges.

Different disturbances form different patterns.

A collapsing cavity produces concentric welts.

Migrating deep bodies produce parallel lines.

Dangerous accumulation beneath a warm fissure raises blunt nodules like the pads of a clenched hand.

The floors are not infallible. They can sicken. Salt can blind them. Clatterers graze them if barriers are neglected. Their meanings must be learned, compared, and revised.

They are alive, and therefore cannot be treated as instruments alone.

Each settlement that accepts a listening floor must tend it. Nutrient channels are cleared. Grazing lanes are maintained. No more is harvested than the colony can restore. When a floor divides and part begins spreading toward unsafe water, the people do not cut it immediately. They observe where it is trying to go.

The power teaches the surface people one pattern to send downward.

It is not a word.

It means neither peace nor obedience. Those are too complex.

It is the pattern a body makes when it feels pain, recognizes the cause, and withdraws from causing more.

**Felt.**

**Stopping.**

The power teaches the deep ecology a corresponding change.

Not through command. Not all at once.

It repeats the association across chambers: disturbance, signal, cessation. Disturbance, signal, cessation. The slow colonies accustom themselves. Contracting membranes begin to loosen when the signal arrives. Pressure diverts through older channels. What was once a blind escalation gains an interval in which it may end.

This is the smallest kind of language.

It is enough.

---

The first settlement saved by it does not know it has been saved until afterward.

A new drainage court is being cut beside a productive blue basin. The stone has been tested through six tides. Its answers seem normal. Work begins with padded cleavers and water wedges.

On the third day, the nearby listening floor raises a row of pale ridges.

The workers stop.

The ridges flatten.

Work begins again, farther from the basin.

The ridges return, taller.

An old mason recognizes the shape as similar to a contraction pattern recorded two settlements away. She orders the court abandoned.

There is resistance. Months of labor have gone into the drainage. The basin feeds many children. No tremor can be felt through hand or copper. No ordinary warning sign is present.

Still, the tools are set down.

The people press the cessation pattern into the listening floor.

**Felt.**

**Stopping.**

For most of a tide, nothing happens.

Then black water wells from the unfinished grooves.

The ground beneath the proposed court rises the width of a finger. It remains there, trembling. Slowly it lowers.

A nearby fissure exhales warm brine.

Had the court been finished, its sealed floor would have confined the pressure until the whole shelf broke.

The people do not build there.

They mark no warning circle around it.

Instead they maintain the abandoned grooves so water can escape, and the blue basin remains productive.

Below, the contraction does not spread.

For the first time in remembered history, an answer ends before becoming disaster.

---

Rain-Under-Stone dies many years later.

By then listening floors grow near eleven settlements. The copper fragment no longer speaks unless asked with great need. Even then its answers are faint.

The old listener’s body is lowered into a warm pool.

Swimmers take the softness of her. Mineral feeders take what remains. Her scales are gathered after many tides, stained red at the wrists and yellow along the shins. Some are set into footpads for children learning their first routes.

The bones of her palms are placed beneath the edge of the oldest listening floor.

It grows over them.

There is no monument.

Nothing rises.

On the night of her final naming, the people lie with their bodies against the stone. They give the complete pattern of her life through the floor: the rain under the ledge, the crafts learned, the crossings survived, the hearing lost, the copper touched, the last three diminishing pressures.

The pattern passes downward, slowed by crystal and film.

It cannot be known whether anything below receives it as a name.

But across the flats, pools cloud one after another.

Warm fissures cool.

The tide chambers lose their harmonics and become one immense, smooth tone.

The people still.

Children are gathered close.

Then, through the listening floor, there comes a pressure pattern unlike tide, collapse, or migration.

It is clumsy.

It is vast.

It repeats the opening of Rain-Under-Stone’s name.

The ending is wrong.

The deep ecology has no sense of an individual life, no hand with which to shape the final motion. It repeats the opening again, altered by miles of water and living membrane.

The people answer with the rest.

For several hours the two patterns meet in the saturated rock.

Neither side understands much.

Neither is harmless.

Neither has become other than itself.

But the people no longer wait only for a blow, and the deep no longer recoils only from pain.

When the tide enters the greatest chambers, the old roar rises as before. Every pool rings. Plated bodies flatten. Shell ornaments turn toward the unseen water.

The whole low country breathes.

This time, between one thunder and the next, there is room for an answer.
