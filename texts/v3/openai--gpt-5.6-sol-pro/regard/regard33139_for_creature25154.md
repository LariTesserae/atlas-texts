## The One Who Waited

The power did not descend in fire.

It knew what fire meant there.

It did not arrive as a weight upon the surface, nor as a voice from the sky, nor as a bright figure whose feet would bruise the photosynthetic plates. It made no road through the swarms. It cast no sudden shadow that might startle the dawn shears into flight or wake the rind whales beneath the skin.

It came as attention.

For one full flowering cycle, nothing changed.

Then another passed.

The power watched rain strike the eastern rim and felt the buried vessels tighten in the west. It followed water through chambers where blind crustaceans sifted pollen from the dark. It traveled the chemical roads of red carriers, rode in the blood-warm folds beneath a grazer, waited inside the counterfeit dew of a gall-tongue, and hung with the Below-Faced over an internal lake whose ceiling breathed mist.

It listened to route-songs revised after storms.

It listened to children learning where not to step.

It listened to the evening assembly until it could distinguish not only species from species, but hunger from courtship, alarm from excess, invitation from deceit. Beneath those quick lives it heard slower exchanges: the opening of pores, the labor of roots, the long hydraulic speech of districts separated by distances greater than kingdoms.

And beneath even that, it heard the High Green.

Not a voice.

A pattern of continuance.

Pressure answered by yielding. Injury answered by closure. Rain answered before arrival. Death drawn inward and returned as flower. No single chamber contained the pattern. No root commanded it. The High Green did not know itself as one thing, yet neither was it many things. Its life was an agreement too old and vast to be spoken.

The power loved it.

Because it was benevolent, it desired to give.

Because it was wise, it did not yet do so.

It imagined permanent rivers. At once it saw the hidden cisterns overflow, the breathing chambers drown, the dry-season cocoons fail to seal, the red carriers lose their roads, and soft rot spread for a thousand miles.

It imagined cooling the white-hot sky. It saw the thermals weaken, the sailbacks fall, flowering canopies fail to rise, and pale hunters starve in the upper air.

It imagined making the plateau insensible to pain. It saw settlements become cities, cities become burdens, and the living surface vanish beneath people who had forgotten how to place their feet.

It imagined removing predators, parasites, drought, fear, and death.

The High Green died in every such vision.

So the power waited.

Eventually the Guests became aware of it, though awareness came differently to each settlement.

In the east, a route-keeper dreamed of a stranger who asked permission before entering her dream.

Among the Below-Faced, echoes began returning from an empty chamber in the shape of questions.

A child collecting condensation saw blue mites assemble on his arm in a spiral no colony had made before. He was frightened, but the spiral loosened when he was frightened and formed again only when he held out his hand.

On the western scars, an old woman beating a map against her ribs heard one additional pulse after every verse.

No one agreed on what these signs meant. This reassured the wisest among them. Predators often imitated one thing perfectly. Only life was untidy enough to approach in many forms.

During the next flowering, the people gathered beneath a mast whose pale canopy had opened over an ancient lightning scar. They sang gratitude, grief, weather, and migration. They sang the names of settlements that had been swallowed. They sang the old warning:

*When all the insects fall silent, lie flat.*

Then all the insects fell silent.

The people lay flat.

The dawn shears folded their narrow heads against their chests. Sailbacks released the bladder towers and circled without beating their fins. The red carriers stopped in their roads, each holding its burden. Deep below, the rind whales ceased cutting.

Last of all, the ground fell silent.

The people prepared to run.

But the old woman from the western scar remained where she was. She pressed one palm against the living plate.

“Wait,” she said.

For the first time in remembered history, silence covered the High Green without terror in it.

Into that silence the power placed a question.

It did not ask in words. Words would have favored the people.

It asked as moisture to the root hairs, pressure to the burrowers, polarized light to the fliers, scent to the carriers, subsonic vibration to the dawn shears. To the High Green it asked by touching no single place, but by becoming, for one moment, a harmless possibility throughout its whole extent.

The question was:

**What is lacking?**

Hunger answered first, from everywhere.

Water answered.

Safety answered.

More young. Fewer young. An end to parasites. An abundance of prey. Shade. Heat. Open wind. Closed chambers. Soft ground. Hard ground. A thousand incompatible mercies rose together.

The power received them without judgment.

The people asked for easier lives.

The pale hunters asked for slower prey.

The prey asked not to be seen.

The red carriers asked for the continuance of wounds from which sweet scar tissue grew.

The High Green took longer.

Sap pressure altered along the eastern rim. Old masts flexed. In a district no person had visited for generations, breathing slits opened one after another. The change moved slowly westward, beneath settlements and herds, beneath predator and parasite, beneath scar and flower.

Years passed before the answer was complete.

The power remained.

At last it understood.

The High Green did not ask to be spared injury. Injury was among its senses.

It did not ask to live forever. It had no idea of forever.

It did not ask for mastery over the creatures inside and above it, because it could not separate their continuance from its own.

Its answer was something nearer to:

**Let me not fail all at once.**

The power saw then what even the deepest roots could not.

Far below the plateau, beneath mineralized forests and mountains ground slowly into food, heat was changing its course through the world. Not soon by human measure. Not even soon by the measure of the oldest masts. But a convergence had begun. In twelve thousand years, perhaps fifteen, the buried waters would rise hot and bitter through the root columns. One district would blacken, then another. The colony would close its vessels against the poison, preserving each region by abandoning the next, until its very defenses divided it into dying islands.

This was not an enemy. It was not evil. It was the movement of the world.

The power could have stopped it.

But to bind the deep heat forever would wound lands beyond the escarpment, lands with lives of their own. To lift the plateau away would kill all that depended upon its height, its weather, its hidden pressure. To make the High Green immortal would turn participation into possession.

Instead, the power made three small changes.

The first was in the roots.

It taught no new command and created no invulnerability. It altered a pale colony already living around the deepest root walls, one of the soft blue-shining multitudes that could be called guest, parasite, or organ. Until then they had fed upon fermentation. Now, very slowly, they learned to bind the bitterness that would one day rise from below.

They would not stop it. They would make it survivable.

Some districts would still die. Great scars would form. Species would vanish. Others would thrive in the changed chambers. The High Green would divide, but not completely. Narrow living bridges would remain between its parts. What had been one would become several without forgetting entirely how to answer across the gaps.

The second change was in the flowering canopies.

The power touched no mast standing upon the plateau. It waited until the canopies tore loose and climbed into the heated air. Most followed their ancient ways over desert and sea.

One, however, rose higher.

Its temporary pools froze along the edges. The creatures sheltering beneath its folds entered dormant states. The canopy darkened against cold and crossed beyond the winds known to sailbacks.

No person saw where it went.

It traveled for a very long time and descended at last upon a distant, empty tableland beneath a gentler sun. Most of what it carried died. Some did not. Rooting plates unfolded over bare mineral. Red carriers emerged into a world with no roads. A single bladder tower filled with rain.

The place would not become another High Green for ages.

The possibility was enough.

The third change was offered to the people.

It was not a tool, law, weapon, or revelation. The power knew that anything possessed could be hoarded, and anything written could be obeyed after it ceased to be true.

It gave them an interval.

The next evening, when the assembly began, there came between the reed-throats and the burrow drummers a brief open space in the sound. It lasted no longer than a breath. Every creature heard it differently, but all heard it.

The following night it came again.

The Guests learned that if they struck the hardened scars during that interval—not loudly, and never with an unchanging rhythm—the breathing slits nearby would answer. Not always. Not clearly. Yet enough.

A quick closing rhythm meant pain.

A slow widening rhythm meant water.

A broken answer meant the district was already distressed and could not bear them long.

The people could ask where to settle.

They could not command permission.

Over generations, the interval entered their route-songs. Red carriers altered their marching pulses around it. Dawn shears began testing uncertain ground during the open breath. The Below-Faced used it to sound chambers before distant storms. Even some predators learned to exploit it, because no mercy in the High Green belonged to only one kind of life.

This, too, the power allowed.

Wisdom did not mean arranging the world so goodness always won. It meant refusing to make goodness another name for dominion.

When its work was complete, the power did not announce its departure. It left no temple. It chose no priesthood. It demanded no gratitude.

The old woman of the western scar, now very old indeed, once asked the additional pulse in her route-song whether the visitor was still present.

For many nights there was no reply.

Then, during the interval, the whole high country contracted once, slowly.

The hardened scar lifted beneath her palm.

From east to west, a low note traveled through root, chamber, surface, settlement, herd, and swarm. It was not the power’s voice. It belonged to everything there, though no single creature could have made it.

The woman smiled.

The benevolent power had gone elsewhere, carrying with it something the High Green had given in return: the knowledge that a world need not be peaceful to be whole, nor simple to be loved.

Behind it, life continued.

Rain bruised the tender plates. Hunters fell through clouds of prey. Settlements moved when the ground refused them. Children learned the new interval and were warned never to trust it rigidly. Far below, blue colonies shone around the roots. Far above, an unnoticed canopy crossed the dark between worlds.

And every evening, amid the continent-wide roar, there opened one small silence—

not an absence,

but a place in which all who lived in one another might listen before answering.
