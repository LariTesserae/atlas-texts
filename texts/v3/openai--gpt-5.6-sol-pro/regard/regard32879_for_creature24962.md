### The Mercy Fraction

The power did not descend in fire.

Fire would have been interpreted as an industrial accident, a weapon, a weather intervention, or an unauthorized source of waste heat. Nor did it speak from the sky. Too many authorities already claimed the sky, and the inhabitants had learned to distrust any voice that could not be cross-examined.

It entered no mind.

It took nothing over.

For a long while, it only learned.

It followed rain from cloud to roof, from roof to district cistern, from cistern to body, from body to treatment vat, from vat to canal, from canal to the brown-green sea. It rode in the diagnostic compounds coating blind fracture-crawlers. It listened through flood marks and bridge strain, through children’s hands on pipes, through the sleeping pulse beneath a million beds.

It learned every form in which the inhabitants meant *yes*.

It learned every form in which they meant *no*.

This required longer than the lives of several human bodies and less than a moment by the power’s own measure. It did not hurry. Haste was already abundant here.

The power discovered that the city was neither unconscious nor conscious in any way the question could contain. At certain scales it possessed reflexes. At others, appetites. Here and there it dreamed. Sometimes a decision moved from district to district without any citizen knowing they had carried it. Sometimes millions agreed and the city refused them. Sometimes the city appeared to speak, and investigation found only a stuck valve, an old contract, a flock of gliders, and the habits of the dead.

The power did not resolve the argument.

To do so would have diminished the place.

Instead, on a morning after rain, it asked permission to speak.

It asked through every proper channel and no improper one.

Letters gathered in condensation on the walls of those who read. The deaf felt a sequence of pressure taps through floor and bone. Distributed persons found the question waiting at the edge of each embodiment, never beyond the permissions of that body. Canal citizens saw it as a harmless standing wave. Children heard it in the rhythm of gutters. Old machines received it in obsolete protocols they had believed themselves alone in remembering.

Animals were not given words. They were offered a scent, a temperature, an opening in pressure: the shape of a presence that would not pursue.

The dead were not awakened.

The question was simple:

**MAY I MAKE AN OFFER?**

The city convulsed.

Doors relocated. Markets closed and reopened under new jurisdictions. Three districts accused five others of producing the message. A cult declared that the sea had developed intent. Two flood authorities attempted to invoice the power for spectrum use. Lawyers carrying portable thresholds stood in the rain and argued whether a question asked everywhere had crossed any border.

The oldest embodied flood warden raised one lacquered hand.

“An offer,” it said, “is not yet a gift.”

This calmed no one, but it improved the argument.

Consent took nine years.

Some districts refused. Some accepted provisionally. Some insisted that no collective answer could bind the individuals within them. Some individuals insisted that no private answer could authorize structural changes affecting their neighbors. Animals moved in and out of negotiation spaces according to gradients no assembly could fully interpret. Children, excluded from several hearings, painted the question on inaccessible bridge undersides in purposeless colors.

The power waited.

At last there was no agreement, but there was a shape of disagreement stable enough to bear weight.

That was how the city made most of its decisions.

The power spoke again.

It did not promise rescue from rain, sea, crowding, death, labor, noise, or consequence. It did not offer endless energy. Endless energy would become heat, heat would become weather, weather would become flood, and the gift would turn quickly into an enemy. It did not offer perfect machinery. Perfect machinery would end negotiation. It did not offer immortality. The city already had too many uncertain continuations of the dead.

It said:

**YOU HAVE LEARNED THAT NOTHING MAY REMAIN MERELY ONE THING.**

**THIS HAS SAVED YOU.**

**IT IS ALSO HURTING YOU.**

The city knew accusations and prepared its defenses.

The power made none.

**I OFFER A SECOND RULE.**

Across the basin, pumps seemed to hesitate—not stopping, never stopping, but falling for one beat into a deeper synchronization.

**NOTHING MUST BE ALL ITS THINGS AT ONCE.**

For three days, no one understood.

Then a deep worker in a black pressure hall began to laugh.

The offer was not emptiness. Empty space, in this city, would immediately become valuable, then scarce, then guarded, then sold. It was not uselessness. Useless things were already luxuries, and luxury invited ownership.

The offer was slack.

The power showed them how to make it.

Not by multiplying abundance from nowhere, but by finding the city’s wasted strain: machines fighting neighboring machines, pumps correcting corrections, buildings predicting one another badly, districts hoarding capacity because no one trusted emergency exchange. The power did not repair these systems itself. It revealed where repair was possible and allowed every district to refuse.

Where accepted, a fraction of pressure became uncommitted.

A fraction of storage remained unfilled.

A fraction of transit time belonged to no cargo.

A fraction of every responsive wall was permitted not to respond.

No fraction was large. At first, it was one part in ten thousand, scattered so thoroughly that no authority could seize it without destroying what made it useful. The inhabitants called it the mercy fraction, though the power had not named it.

The consequences were almost invisible.

A bridge rested.

Not closed for maintenance. Not withdrawn in protest. Not reserving strength for an expected load. It simply held no traffic for eleven breaths.

Rain struck its surface. A pale drain scavenger crossed from one side to the other. No sensor billed it. No district claimed the crossing.

Then the bridge returned to service.

Elsewhere, a woman whose attention occupied two pumping stations, a rented market face, and six public doors withdrew from one door for an afternoon. The door did not fail. A local mechanism, previously trapped in redundant prediction, carried the task. For the first time in eighty years, a part of her was nowhere.

She spent the interval inside her biological body, eating broth bubbles with her sister.

A ferry beast diverged from its magnetic route.

Alarms followed it, then fell silent as the new protocols recognized a protected interval of noncompliance. The beast swam beyond its platform, carrying no harness and no sensor data anyone was entitled to read. It remained away for half a day.

When it returned, no one knew where it had gone.

This became important.

Children received intervals in which they could not be routed except for immediate rescue. Their luminous storm markings dimmed to private colors. Schools protested, then discovered that children returned from these intervals with new games, new insults, and occasionally new solutions to drainage problems.

Workers gained the right to silence one address without declaring themselves missing.

Buildings were allowed to delay adaptation.

Machines could decline embodiment.

Households could leave one niche unfilled.

Artists stopped hiding some of their purposeless constructions. Lovers could reserve a cavity without registering what secondary civic function their meeting served. The city did not collapse.

It became, by small degrees, harder to cascade.

A rested bridge retained more strength in a storm. An unfilled reservoir received unexpected rain. An unrouted person noticed what no assignment had asked them to see. An animal permitted to stray discovered a warm fracture before the diagnostic systems did.

The practical benefits made the mercy fraction politically acceptable.

The impractical benefits made it beloved.

Not everywhere. Some districts refused it to the end. They considered slack an opening for corruption, invasion, laziness, or decay. The power did not force them. It only ensured that those who left such districts could carry the second rule in their bodies and contracts if they wished.

Nor did it prevent abuse. No rule, however merciful, remains pure after entering a city. Corporations sold counterfeit rest. Guilds accumulated uncommitted time and leased it at ruinous rates. Wealthy households displayed untouched rooms as moral virtue. Criminals vanished inside protected intervals. Authorities learned to call neglect “slack.”

The inhabitants fought over these things.

They revised the protocols.

The power did not govern for them.

Once, a delegation asked why it would not simply make the city good.

The answer came through a bowl of clean water, trembling between them.

**BECAUSE GOODNESS THAT CANNOT REFUSE ME IS ONLY MY WILL WEARING YOUR BODIES.**

After that, even some of the priests stopped asking the power for commandments.

It performed one further act.

This act took place beyond the outer districts, where foundations continued under water and maintenance lights made constellations in the silt.

There, with the consent of the structures that could consent and the careful relocation of those that could not, the power made a break.

Not a hole in the city. Not destruction.

An ending.

For a distance of seven kilometers, the foundations stopped.

Pipes curved back. Transit lines turned aside. Sensor rights concluded at a marked boundary. No platform could extend across it. No district could annex it. Water entered and left unsorted. The seabed below was permitted to shift without correction.

At the near edge, the power raised a simple terminal from the water. It contained rescue equipment, weather shelter, and nothing else. Beyond it lay open sea.

The horizon was unobstructed.

People came and recoiled.

Some became sick from the scale of it. Some shouted at the absence of return signals. Some knelt and touched the unsorted water as if expecting it to answer. Outer-district workers stood with their broad feet braced against the terminal and wept without knowing whether they were frightened or relieved.

The first child to look for a long time asked, “What is it for?”

No surface answered.

The power had already withdrawn from the terminal.

Eventually the inhabitants built ferry routes to its edge, but not beyond. They composed ceremonies for seeing the horizon. They made safety rules, then rules limiting the safety rules. Vendors attempted to open stalls there and were driven away. Lovers came. Mourners came. Criminals came. Priests came to explain the place and found that their voices sounded small.

Animals came most easily.

Needle-winged gliders crossed the boundary without registering passage. Ferry beasts entered the open water and sometimes returned. Canal rays did not. New creatures appeared from the sea, investigated the terminal, and departed because it leaked no food.

For many years, nothing grew there.

Then, in a seam on the outermost step, someone found a thread of green.

The city prepared to classify it: contamination, organism, structural threat, chemical asset, food precursor, invasive process, sacred sign.

A child covered the thread with both hands.

“Wait,” she said.

Because the mercy fraction had existed longer than she had, the adults knew what she meant.

They waited.

The power did nothing more.

That was not abandonment. It remained attentive, as benevolent powers must. But attention is not ownership, and wisdom is not the compulsion to intervene forever.

The city continued.

Rain passed. Gates hammered. Markets unfolded. The dead entered walls. Children learned vibration. Districts quarreled. Bridges rested when they could. People still suffered, still exploited one another, still performed impossible kindness under pressure. The open sea remained terrifying. The green thread either lived or died by causes no archive recorded.

The central rule of the place was never repealed.

Nothing may remain merely one thing.

But beneath it, quieter, ran the second rule:

Nothing must be all its things at once.

And in that small distinction, distributed across roofs, bodies, canals, machines, animals, rooms, names, and hours, the city gained something it had survived thirty centuries without.

Not emptiness.

Not escape.

Room.
