The power did not begin by bringing light.

There was already light, and the people had learned what to do with it.

Nor did it bring water. Water was present in every law, every quarrel, every cup set beneath a patient drip. More water, carelessly given, would only have drowned the lower rooms.

It did not strengthen every wall. Some walls ought to fall. Some openings had been waiting longer than the families living beside them. Some burdens were held up only because no one yet knew how much harm their support required elsewhere.

It did not reveal what was above.

A revelation is another kind of collapse.

Instead, the power listened.

This took longer than a mortal listener would have understood. It listened through a dry season and two rains. It listened to white mallets striking cistern rims, to goats shifting their weight before a warm draft, to the minute grinding of salt inside brick. It learned the difference between water held safely and water held only by habit. It followed conversations passed through thumb-holes. It heard songs divide at blocked passages and continue separately for generations.

It listened to wall-listeners.

Several times they placed their cheeks against the very place where the power’s attention rested. The power did not answer. Not yet. A tap from nowhere would have been feared, worshiped, exploited, or sealed behind seven layers of plaster. Benevolence without patience is merely appetite wearing a kindly face.

The power considered many gifts.

It might have lifted the roof and shown the inhabitants the sky.

But it knew what unbounded height would do to people whose trust had always rested on beams. It saw children screaming beneath harmless blue. It saw elders unable to sleep because nothing appeared to hold the world above them. It saw surface people descending later through the opening with ropes, laws, bright metal, hunger, and names for ownership that included the thickness of walls.

So the roof remained.

It might have laid out straight roads.

But every straight road would cut through a sleeping room, a goat ledge, a memorial tile, a fungus cupboard, a shared hearth, a wall traded through by unseen hands. To make passage easy for everyone in general, it would have made home impossible for thousands in particular.

The roads remained crooked.

It might have filled the cisterns forever.

But then the rain captains would become permanent rulers, or unnecessary ones; the old agreements would loosen; lower rooms would dampen; gnats would breed without season; mineral salts would choke the jars; those who controlled the miraculous water would soon control the Galleries.

The cisterns continued to rise and fall.

It might have made itself known.

But the inhabitants already honored the Hand Before, the Breath Above, the Kept Drop, and the Room Not Opened. The wise power had no need to place its own cup among theirs.

At last it understood that the Low Galleries did not need rescue from being what they were.

They needed, as all living places sometimes do, one danger removed, one possibility offered, and the freedom to misunderstand both.

There was a wall behind the Bent Cistern.

On its public face it bore three old signs: a faded wave, a spread hand, and a closed eye drawn over the others. No one had listened there in eighty years. Shelves had been fixed shallowly into its plaster. A widow slept against it in winter because it stayed warm. Children were forbidden to touch it and therefore touched it often.

Beyond the wall lay a narrow chamber filled almost to its ceiling with broken tile. Beyond that stood another wall, then a stair.

The stair went upward.

It did not climb directly into the open. It passed through eleven rooms, each higher and cooler than the last. Some were choked with bird nests. Some held black water. One contained a fig tree that had rooted in mortar and grown pale branches through three floors, seeking a light it had never reached. At the top was a roofed court whose outer arches had long ago been packed with reed and clay.

Beyond those arches was the surface.

The power knew the entire route. It also knew that showing the route in a dream would be too much like command.

So it did smaller things.

During the next dry week, a rat carried a blue-glazed bead through a crack behind the widow’s sleeping mat.

A child found it.

The bead was of a glaze no current kiln made, and there was a tiny white circle painted around its hole. The child traded it to a potter for two honeyed fungus cakes. The potter recognized the clay as belonging to tiles salvaged generations ago from the far side of the Bent Cistern.

She brought it to a delver.

The delver placed it between his teeth, frowned, and said it smelled of swifts.

This was plainly foolish. Everyone told him so.

Nevertheless, they called a wall-listener.

Her name was Amat-under-the-Fowl-Walk, though in youth she had been Amat of the Three Warm Steps. She was nearly deaf to voices and could hear the movement of water through six hands of brick.

She rested her ear against the wall.

The power still did not tap.

Instead, far above, it disturbed one shaft-swift from its roost.

The bird dropped through a forgotten chimney into the buried stair. It beat its wings in panic, rose, struck plaster, and beat again.

Amat heard it.

She did not hear wings. No one could have heard wings through so much made matter. What came to her was a trembling inside the wall, too light for water, too irregular for machinery, and alive in a way that rats were not.

She listened until her knees hurt.

Then she drew an ear beside the closed eye.

This caused an argument lasting nine days.

The widow objected first. The cistern measurers objected second. The plaster guild objected because no opening could be cut without removing the shelves. The route wardens objected because the district already contained enough routes. A shrine keeper declared that the closed eye was older than the wave and therefore had precedence. Three children claimed to have heard singing in the wall. One child claimed to have sung back.

On the tenth day, the widow rolled up her bedding.

“Take one brick,” she said. “No deeper than a knuckle until it is witnessed.”

They took one brick.

Behind it was dry fill.

They took six more.

Behind those was a pocket of old air. It smelled of dust, bird droppings, and something green.

No pressure came. No water. No foulness strong enough to dim a lamp flame.

They widened the opening until Amat could put her head through.

The chamber beyond was small, crowded, useless, and unmistakably a room.

That was enough.

The excavation lasted three years.

Nothing about it resembled the clean uncovering of a buried plan. The upward stair was broken in five places and missing in two. The workers built ladders, ramps, braces, and new arches beneath old ones. They cleared the tile fill a basket at a time. Every fragment was inspected and reused. The black water in the seventh room proved clean beneath its surface scum, though bitter with minerals. The fig tree was not moved. A path was bent around its jar-thick roots.

At the ninth room they found Counterwallers.

The two parties broke through within a day of one another, each having followed the noise of the other’s tools. For several minutes they stared through a hole no wider than a cup.

Then someone on the far side said, “You are in our wall.”

Amat replied, “You are in ours.”

This satisfied everyone.

The Counterwallers knew three of the upper chambers. They had never found the lower stair. Their speech sounded strange but carried familiar cistern words. One of their songs was the missing second half of a lament sung in the market vault.

The route took another year after that.

Near the top, the air changed.

It moved without waiting for a fire, a warm vent, or the turn of weather in a known shaft. It smelled enormous. The workers disliked it. Some tied cloth over their faces. Others became giddy and had to be led down.

The last packed arch was not opened at once.

They listened.

They tested the air.

They drilled a finger-width hole, then closed it for a season. They opened it during rain and during dry weather. They pushed through a fowl in a basket and drew it back unharmed, offended, and louder than before.

Only then did they remove the reed and clay.

Outside lay the roofed court.

Sunlight entered from the sides so fiercely that no one could look toward it. The workers crept forward with their hands over their eyes. Above them the court’s old canopy remained: timber ribs, climbing dead vines, and a lattice of fired-clay bars. Through its thousands of little openings they saw pieces of sky no larger than tiles.

This they could endure.

The sky was blue that day, though not the mild blue of shadows within the Galleries. It was blue without surface or measure.

Some wept.

Some laughed too loudly.

Amat touched one of the supporting posts and would go no farther.

The children went first, because children often accept terror when adults give it another name.

They called the sky a bright ceiling very far away.

The power did not correct them.

In time the upper court became neither temple nor gate, but a garden and rain work.

They repaired the canopy before clearing the outer arches. They built cistern lips, first-flow drains, animal basins, and settling jars. They carried up deep ceramic vessels and filled them with the manufactured mud of the Galleries. Beans grew. Gourds grew. The old fig tree, once given a narrow opening toward the court, put out leaves darker than any green below.

No one was required to visit.

Many never did.

Some came only at evening, when the sky had acquired edges of roof and shadow. Shaft-climbers used the court as a work station. Potters carried glazes upward to see their true colors and often came back dissatisfied. Children lay beneath the lattice and counted moving clouds through separate openings, insisting each opening contained a different sky.

A route warden set white circles all the way from the Bent Cistern to the garden. Lovers cleared side passages into it. Counterwallers appeared from districts no accepted map included. Their dead were eventually set into the stair as memorial tiles, and in this way the new route became old.

The surface beyond the court remained vast, windy, and mostly unentered.

That, too, was allowed.

As for the danger the power removed, no one ever knew of it.

Far below the market, an ancient retaining wall had begun to bow beneath a buried reservoir. It would have failed during the second rain after the opening of the upper route. The collapse would have drowned seven low districts before any white mallet could be raised.

The power did not hold the wall forever.

It shifted one tile.

Only one.

The tile fell into a lower gutter and turned the first thin leak aside. The leak entered an abandoned warm duct. Steam rose where no steam had risen before. A goat objected to the smell. Its keeper fetched a vent worker. The vent worker found the seep. The cistern measurers followed it backward, room by room, and discovered the pressure behind the wall.

They drained the reservoir by hand over forty-three days.

Later they rebuilt the wall twice as thick.

The displaced water filled kilns, animal troughs, washing basins, and every drinking jar in the neighboring districts. No one called it a miracle. They called it good work performed just in time.

This pleased the power more than praise would have.

Years passed.

The blue bead was set into the Bent Cistern wall beside the first brick removed. Around it someone painted a room inside a room inside a room. In the innermost chamber, where custom required a blankness, a child added a small blue square.

The shrine keepers argued about whether to scrape it away.

They did not.

The power’s attention eventually turned elsewhere—not because it ceased to care, but because wise care does not always stare. Before leaving, it listened once more to the Low Galleries.

It heard rain entering the new settling basins.

It heard an old lament returning with its lost second voice.

It heard a woman refuse the upward garden and another woman describe the moon badly to her.

It heard mallets, jars, fowl, lovers, arguments, drains, market calls, and the soft passage of flame through a thumb-hole.

No one named the power.

No one knew they had been courted.

But from the Bent Cistern there was now a route: not straight, not easy, not forced upon anyone. It climbed through old rooms and new repairs, through neighbors once sealed away, through fear measured a hand at a time, until it reached a place where the sky could be encountered in pieces.

This was the gift the power chose.

Not escape.

Not perfection.

A little warning.

A little time.

And one more room than the world had seemed to contain.
