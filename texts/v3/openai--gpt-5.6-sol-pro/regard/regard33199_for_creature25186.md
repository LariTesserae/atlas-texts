The power does not descend in light.

Light would be too much here. A hard radiance would scorch the moss, heat the dark pools, and make the leathery leaves close against it. Nor does the power take a body and walk across the clay. It has no wish to leave pressure where pressure would be mistaken for weather.

It attends from the rim.

At first, even wisdom is tempted to call the basin lonely. It sees the single tree, the unvisited water, the ruin with no inheritor. It feels the ancient impulse of benevolence: to bring birds, to wake seeds, to fill the silence with answering life.

But wisdom waits until pity has become understanding.

It perceives that a bird would be hunger here. Insects would be mouths and wounds. A stream would tear apart what has been composed one drop at a time. Even abundance might be a kind of violence. The being is not imprisoned in the hollow as an animal might be imprisoned in a pit. The hollow is the shape of its body. To open it carelessly would be to cut it open.

The power does not give the creature eyes. It does not give it speech, memory, desire, or speed. Gifts are not benevolent merely because the giver values them.

Instead, the power learns how to ask a question in the creature’s language.

This takes a long time.

For seven years, nothing changes.

In the eighth, the condensation along the eastern wall contains a fraction less salt. The difference is too slight to alter any pool. The moss detects it before dawn. Its scattered patches darken, and a recognition passes through the buried filaments.

For the next eleven years, the power allows the reflected light near the southern notch to linger a little longer during one season—not brighter, only longer. A root tip that has not moved in centuries advances the width of a fingernail.

Then the power stops.

The root stops also.

The power waits through dry years and wet years. It asks again by cooling one seam beneath the old wall. It asks by placing, in the outgoing water, the faint mineral taste of limestone beyond the rim. It asks without promise and without command:

*If there were elsewhere, would you go?*

The creature cannot imagine elsewhere. It has no picture of another basin, no idea of distance, no concept of continuation. But it knows a gradient. It knows usable shade beyond familiar shade, moisture tending away from the central flat, stone whose taste does not belong to any wall it has touched before.

Slowly, it answers as it answers everything.

It grows toward the question.

Not the tree. Not the moss. Beneath the settled wedge of sediment at the runnel wall, a few transparent filaments enter a hairline fracture leading south. They advance when rain wets the fracture and harden when it dries. In thirty years they pass beneath the lowest stone. In another sixty, they reach the notch.

The answer is neither hope nor consent as quicker beings would understand them. Yet the wise power does not despise an answer because it is made without words.

So it performs one act.

Far beyond the basin, under the surrounding country, limestone lies folded through ochre clay. Most of its hollows are wrong: too exposed, too deep, too poor in condensation, too often scoured. But there is another shallow depression, smaller than the first and almost entirely buried. Once, perhaps, water joined the two. The old passage has been sealed by mineral accretion for an age.

The power does not break it open.

Breaking would send a flood. It loosens one grain.

During the next rain, water enters the southern notch as it always has. It whispers over moss, passes the old wall, and carries away almost nothing: a little salt, a little silt, a translucent strand too fine to be called a cutting.

At the loosened grain, one drop goes where no drop has gone for ages.

It travels into darkness, not downward but outward, along a seam that admits no air. The strand travels with it. Most of the water is taken back into stone. The strand dries almost to death. The power does not preserve it from this. To preserve it absolutely would be to replace its nature with the power’s will.

Another rain comes three years later.

Then another after nine.

The strand swells. It divides. It encounters a mineral it cannot use and withdraws from it. It finds a cooler fissure. Nothing guides it except its own ancient manner of choosing, and nothing guarantees success.

In the original basin, no loss can be seen. The pools remain black and still. The tree keeps its final shape. The moss continues to darken before rain. The creature has not been diminished to make its future possible.

Centuries pass.

In the buried hollow beyond the rim, a film forms beneath a limestone lip. It is not yet moss. It is not a leaf, root, or tree. It is only a living translucence holding one drop longer than bare stone would have held it.

The benevolent power regards this and does not hasten it.

It does not raise walls around either place. It does not decree that drought, fracture, and time must never reach them. Immortality would freeze the being more completely than death. Nor does it demand that the second hollow resemble the first. What grows there, if anything grows, will be shaped by other ledges, other salts, another angle of weakened sun.

The power leaves no mark of ownership.

No shrine appears on the rim. No guardian is appointed. No prophecy enters the tree. The old builders are not summoned back, and no visitor is told that a wonder lies beneath their feet.

The power’s gift is smaller and more exact:

It sees the being as it is.

It refrains from improving what does not suffer from being itself.

It asks before altering.

And when the being inclines, by the width of a root tip, toward a possibility beyond its completed world, the power makes that possibility real—then returns it to chance.

In the first basin, everything appears unchanged.

Rain withdraws into the rock pockets. Fallen leaves darken into clay. The southern notch retains the taste of loss.

But loss is no longer the only thing that passes through it.

Far away, in a hollow that has never known a tree, one pale patch holds moisture through the afternoon.

By evening it has not dried.

And beneath it, very slowly, the stone begins to be known.
