The power does not descend in fire.

Fire would be seen from the valleys. It would blacken the cushions, blind the pale moths, wake the stone too violently. Nor does it come as a voice, because every creature in the basin has learned that a sudden voice from above belongs to danger.

It comes as attention.

For one entire turning of the sun, the basin is regarded completely.

Nothing is too small for that regard. The power attends to water cupped beneath a moss pad and to the larva feeding there. It attends to the gray grazer with a cracked hoof, favoring one leg as it crosses the scree. It attends to the narrow-winged bird whose hunger has sharpened through two unsuccessful days. It attends to the six pale eggs beneath a translucent slab, and to the female lizard lying farther down in darkness, her body depleted by making them.

It sees the fern waiting for noon.

It sees the green knife in the shelter wall.

It sees, far below the permanent clouds, the hands that shaped that knife. Those hands are gone. Other hands will come: a child’s first, clumsy with altitude and wonder; an old gatherer’s, steady despite swollen joints; a hunter’s, bleeding from a fall; hands belonging to people not yet born, provided the route remains passable and the knowledge of the cairns is not lost.

The power sees not only what is, but what presses close around it in possibility.

It sees the north wall fail.

The collapse has not happened yet. No eye in the basin can read it. The fracture lies deep behind the visible face, where repeated freezing has opened a dark, widening plane. In seven winters—or perhaps in the next spring, if the thaw comes suddenly—a section of basalt larger than the basin’s shelters will shear free.

In most futures, it falls inward.

The first impact destroys the eastern moss beds. The shock loosens the lip. Gravel, shelters, sedge, lizards, buried seeds, and gray grazers slide together into the white distance. A few cushions remain under the western wall, but the seams that fed them drain. Within a century the green thumbprint becomes a gray scar, visible only to passing birds.

This is not malice. It is the mountain continuing to become itself.

The power knows this.

It also knows that wisdom is not the same as indifference.

So, while the basin moves through night, the power enters the fracture.

It does not seal it. A sealed mountain merely stores its violence. It does not hold the wall forever, because forever would turn shelter into confinement and preservation into another kind of death. Instead it touches three grains of stone far inside the basalt.

One grain splits.

One turns.

One settles into a space no wider than the edge of a hair.

The change is almost nothing. Yet pressure takes a different path. The hidden fracture begins, over years, to lean away from the basin rather than toward it. When the wall eventually falls, it will fall outward, breaking upon a lifeless slope. The sound will roll across the peaks. Snow will leap from distant ledges. Every animal in the basin will freeze.

But the green will remain.

That is the first kindness: not safety, but time.

The power then considers the water.

It could fill the basin. With a thought it could raise a clear lake, deep and unfailing, blue as the sky. It could send streams over every bare interval and clothe the basalt from wall to precipice in flowers.

But the lake would drown the moss that knows how to drink frost. It would erase the damp seams and join all the separate countries into one. Midges would thicken above its margins. Creatures from lower slopes would climb to the new water. Tall plants would follow. Their roots would deepen soil; their stems would catch snow; their abundance would feed larger grazers. The old low inhabitants, fashioned for scarcity, would be crowded from their hidden homes by life made strong through plenty.

The basin would become richer and lose what it was.

Therefore the power does not give it a lake.

Instead it passes beneath the young rock, following the brief journeys of meltwater. It learns every cup, every blind fissure, every seam that receives the afternoon sleet. It finds one place below the central boulder where water is lost too quickly into the mountain.

There, it lays no vessel and builds no dam. It merely roughens the inside of a crack.

From then on, some of the water that would have fallen beyond reach clings to the stone. Not much. A ladleful after sleet. Less after fog. Enough to travel slowly through a branching mineral vein and emerge, days later, under three patches of bare gravel.

The first year, nothing happens.

The second year, a black lichen darkens one of the places.

The fifth year, moss begins there, no larger than a thumbnail.

Much later, red-stemmed sedge takes root around the moss. Beetles find it. A grazer discovers the hidden damp and adds the place to its wandering route. One of the three new patches fails when another crack opens, and one is buried by scree. The third endures.

That is the second kindness: not abundance, but another chance.

The power considers the living creatures.

The injured grazer will not survive the coming winter. It could be healed. The power could straighten the broken hoof, thicken the animal’s fur, turn the next diving bird aside. But the grazer is old. Copper beetles have laid eggs in dung along its paths. Its body, when it dies, will feed a place so poor that bone and hide will remain gifts for years. A hunting bird will eat first. Moths will come. Tiny scavengers will work beneath the fur. Minerals held in the animal’s body will return to sedge and willow.

To spare the grazer from death would not spare the basin from hunger. It would pass the hunger elsewhere.

The power does not heal the hoof.

But that evening, when the old animal misjudges a loose fan of scree, one stone holds beneath it instead of rolling. The grazer does not fall. It reaches the warm wall and lies down among the others—not touching them, but near enough to hear their teeth and breathing.

It will live through the night without fear.

That is the third kindness: not escape from death, but gentleness before it.

The narrow-winged bird is permitted its hunt. On the following morning it drops through the glare and takes a young grazer. The remaining animals scatter. The bird feeds, and what it cannot carry is taken by smaller lives.

The power does not condemn the bird for being made of hunger.

It does not condemn the mountain for being made of fracture.

It does not condemn the cold.

Benevolence, here, cannot mean choosing only the soft things.

At noon the waiting fern opens. The power regards each frond. For its few allotted minutes, the plant receives the full sun. No cloud crosses.

Then comes the question of the people.

Far below, beyond the cloud sea, the old path is fading from memory. Those who once crossed the ridge have grown fewer. A settlement was abandoned after stones buried its spring. A family that knew the upper route moved east. The last person who can name every cairn is a woman whose hair has gone white. She has not climbed to the basin in eleven years.

She remembers the ladle.

She remembers repairing it beside the water bowl while sleet rattled against the roof. She remembers that the sinew shrank as it dried and drew the split wood closed. She remembers leaving it because there were younger people who would need it.

But the younger people did not come.

That night, below the clouds, the woman dreams.

The power does not appear in the dream. There is no shining figure, no command, no promise. She dreams only of drinking from the stone bowl. The ladle in her hand is cold. In the bowl’s shallow water she sees the sky, and across the reflected sky moves the shadow of a narrow-winged bird.

When she wakes, the taste of snowmelt remains in her mouth.

She does not climb the mountain herself. Wisdom has taught her what her knees can no longer do. Instead she calls her daughter’s son, who has always listened closely when she speaks of high places. She tells him where the safe route leaves the lower ridge. She tells him which cairns can be trusted and which were already leaning when she last passed. She tells him never to strip a whole moss cushion, never to leave water standing in the bowl after drinking, never to sleep beneath a slab that gives a hollow answer when struck.

She tells him the three rules.

Height is exposure.

Water belongs to darkness.

Sunlight is season and territory.

The young man listens. He brings another to listen with him. Then a third arrives, pretending not to care but remembering everything.

In summer they climb.

They do not find a paradise. Their lungs ache. One cairn is gone, and another points toward ground that has since broken away. They turn back once. On the second attempt, they reach the basin near sunset and spend the first night shivering in the least damaged shelter.

In the morning they find the ladle.

The sinew binding has loosened. The bowl of the ladle is weathered nearly through. One of the young people wants to take it home as proof that the stories were true.

The woman’s grandson says no.

He warms new sinew between his hands, repairs the old repair, and sets the ladle down where it was.

The power gives him no sign of approval.

Approval would make the act into obedience, and the act is better than obedience. He has understood on his own that not everything found must be possessed, and not everything preserved must be removed from danger.

The visitors rebuild two cairns and leave a third fallen because the route beyond it is no longer safe. They cut a little medicinal moss, taking narrow pieces from several cushions rather than a fatal amount from one. They gather dung for the hearth. They replace a cracked roof pole. Before descending, they place a green-glass flake beside the old knife—not as payment, exactly, nor as offering, but as continuation.

That is the fourth kindness: not instruction, but remembrance.

Years pass.

The basin remains severe.

Eggs freeze. Hunters miss their prey and starve. An early storm catches insects outside their flowers. A whole willow, older than any living person who visits the shelters, is torn from its crack when ice wedges beneath its root. The power prevents none of these things merely because they cause pain.

But neither does it turn away.

When suffering can be eased without unmaking the lives around it, it is eased.

A lizard trapped beneath a shifted slab finds that one six-toed foot can reach a fissure that was not there before. A bee caught far from shelter when the sun vanishes encounters a moth’s empty crevice and survives the night. A child crossing the scree steps on a stone that should slide; it settles instead. A hunting bird, blown beyond the lip in a violent downdraft, finds one last upward current where the shape of the cliff says none should be.

Not every time.

Enough that life continues, never enough that life ceases to be itself.

The basin does not know it has been blessed. Knowledge of blessing might draw temples, towers, claims of ownership. People might climb in numbers. They might build high to honor the power, forgetting the first rule. They might widen the water bowl, cut channels, fence the green patches, kill the hunters, tame the grazers, and call the altered place safe.

So the wise power accepts no shrine.

Its signs are indistinguishable from fortunate accidents, from good judgment, from the unnoticed resilience of small things.

A wall falls outward.

A crack holds water.

A dying animal finds warm stone.

An old woman remembers.

A young person repairs what another repaired before him.

There is no miracle bright enough to see from below the clouds.

Only, century by century, the basin remains a green thumbprint pressed into black stone. Its gardens migrate. Its creatures hunt and hide. Its shelters collapse and are raised again, always low. The mountain changes around it, and the power never asks it to stop changing.

For the deepest kindness it offers is not rescue from the world.

It is room within the world:

room for roots in darkness,  
for wings in rising air,  
for hunger without hatred,  
for death without waste,  
for memory without possession,  
and for fragile lives to go on inhabiting the brief kindnesses of stone.
