The power does not enter at once.

It has learned that arrival is not always kindness. Light may burn what has made a life in darkness. An answer may close a question that was serving as a home. Even love, applied without patience, can become a form of weather: overwhelming, indiscriminate, certain of its own necessity.

So the power remains beyond the pale trunks.

It watches through seven rains.

This is long enough for generations of gnats, three hatchings of lizards, and one slow alteration in the course of a branch road. It watches the floods arrive from nowhere and depart by every direction. It sees the beetles polish wounds into usefulness. It learns that a snake divided by distance is not necessarily injured, and that a shadow may be more faithful than the body that cast it.

It listens.

At first the city is only enormous noise. Then the power begins to distinguish appetite from warning, courtship from weather, reproduction from echo. It hears the slow exchange of the trunks beneath all other sounds. It hears the floor opening for frightened feet. It hears old water moving below young leaves.

Most difficult of all, it learns not to mistake the loudest voices for the whole.

The browsers would ask for tender shoots without end. The shoots would ask for fewer browsers.

The birds would ask that no snake cross an empty span. The snakes would ask that branches never meet.

The frogs desire rain. The beetles prefer the dry flexing of bark. The shadows want bodies, except for those that have lived long enough to want no owners. The city’s ancient trunks desire what trunks desire: light, water, continuance, relief from burdens too slow for animals to notice.

None of these wishes is false.

None can be granted without entering the flesh of another.

The power waits another seven rains.

Its attention is felt, but only slightly. Animals near the gate sometimes pause and look outward, not with alarm but with the uncertain concentration of creatures trying to remember a scent. The central pool shows, among its other ages, a court containing one small gray shape not yet present. Thunder sounds once beneath a clear sky and is followed, weeks later, not by a storm but by a moment in which every leaf turns its underside outward.

The city knows that something is there.

It cannot address the power as one being, because it has never needed to become one voice. Instead it answers as it does everything else: by accommodation.

A low branch extends through the gate.

Moss grows along its upper surface. A trickle of clear water runs down its center, although there has been no rain. Beetles remove every loose fiber. Birds gather nearby but do not land. Insects cross above it in one direction and below it in the other, satisfying the law of empty spaces.

It is a road, an invitation, and perhaps a tongue.

The power enters.

It does so while something else crosses outward: not an animal, but a cool breath from the hollow roots, carrying spores, citrus, musk, and the mineral smell of rain on stone.

The power has no form that the city can keep. In one pool it resembles a pale animal bending to drink. In another, an opening between leaves. The browsers see a tall member of their own kind with a crown of soft branches. The frogs perceive warm rain. The snakes see two ends with no intervening body. To the beetles it is a clean passage through resistant wood.

Only the oldest trunks sense it nearly as it is: a presence capable of changing the place, and therefore exercising great care not to.

The power goes to the central court.

It asks no question aloud. An exact question might repeat, and anything born from such a question would spend its life demanding an answer.

Instead it places its attention in the city without directing it. It feels the thousand incompatible wishes. It feels hungers that are also services, fears that preserve roads, deaths that become architecture. It feels the city’s pleasure in revision, its aversion to completion, its immense distributed curiosity.

It also discovers a trouble.

Not a wound. Not an injustice. Not even, precisely, a sorrow.

The city contains almost everything that happens to it.

Nothing dead remains apart. Nothing lost is allowed to remain wholly lost. Sound becomes flesh. Flesh becomes floor. Habit becomes street. Collapse becomes support. Even the past and future are folded into one pool and stirred together by rain.

The city has no experience of something that can be loved without being incorporated.

It knows difference, but not otherness.

It knows entrances, but not an outside that remains outside.

It knows loss, but not the dignity of allowing what is gone to be gone.

The power understands then that the place does not need correction. Its impossible laws are not errors. Its predators are not wicked. Its floods do not require channels, nor its architecture a plan. To cure it of strangeness would be merely to destroy it politely.

What it may need is one thing it cannot make from itself.

The power reaches beyond the boundary and brings back a stone.

It is not a jewel. It contains no flame, no writing, no sleeping intelligence. It is a piece of ordinary gray stone, small enough to be lifted in two hands, rounded long ago by a river that began in mountains the city has never seen.

The power places it beside the pool that reflects other ages.

For the first time, mineral touches the living floor and does not prove to be disguised root, compressed leaf, hidden sky, or another entrance.

The city reacts.

The rind puckers around it. Fine roots rise and spread over its surface. Acid beads from pale hairs. Fungal threads enter every crack. Beetles arrive in metallic sheets and scrape at it until their mouthparts blunt. A browser tests it with one hoof, snorts, and retreats upside down along a branch.

The stone does not resist.

It simply fails to become anything else.

By morning it remains separate.

This is so unprecedented that the entire settlement grows quieter—not silent, never silent, but altered. Frogs omit a beat. Birds lose the ends of songs they have not yet begun. A pulse travels through the trunks and returns without answer.

Rain comes.

Water strikes the stone.

The sound is small and dull.

It does not ring through the root chambers. It does not arrive beforehand. It does not become an animal after the third repetition. Each drop follows its own falling and ends where it lands.

Creatures gather.

Sound-born animals come first. Pale things made of drips crouch around the stone and spend their remembered impacts against it. Instead of collapsing at once, they remain for a few breaths in a condition they have never known: alive without being compelled to repeat their origin.

A many-legged rhythm-creature climbs onto the gray surface. Its feet lose their inherited meter. It stands unevenly. Then, with great uncertainty, it takes a step of its own.

Birds begin using the stone to break dangerous songs. They fly down, tap it once, and carry the harmless imperfection into their calls. Frogs shelter against it during the fiercest rain and hear the difference between water occurring now and water remembered by roots.

Shadows gather there at dawn, when they have weight. The stone receives their weight without asking which bodies cast them.

Nothing is healed because nothing was declared broken.

Predators still hunt. Branches still fail. Floods still enter from places that cannot be found. Animals passing through the outer gate continue to leave their shadows behind. The power does not repeal the city’s laws merely because they are perilous. Laws that have become bodies cannot be removed without tearing those bodies open.

But beside the stone, the city slowly acquires a new district.

It remains no larger than a little court. The floor does not cover the stone, though it easily could. Roots curve around it and form low benches. A branch grows overhead but stops short of making a complete circle. Condensation falls nearby, never directly upon it unless there is rain.

Here echoes arrive after sounds.

Here a creature may stop repeating itself.

Here the dead are not absorbed until morning.

This last change is the most difficult.

The first body to remain beside the stone is an old browser. It descends from the high roads during a dry afternoon, folds its rotating ankles beneath itself, and lies with one horn resting against the gray surface. Its herd waits above. At sunset its breathing enters the floor’s slow rhythm. Before dawn it dies.

The green rind rises around the body as it has around every body for uncounted seasons.

Then it pauses.

Nothing prevents it from continuing. The power has imposed no barrier. The stone issues no command.

The city pauses because it has learned that it can.

Morning comes, and the browser remains.

Its herd descends. They smell it, nudge it, and crop the soft leaves from its antlers. Its reflection stands in the nearby pool, upright and attentive. All day the body lies separate from the architecture.

At the second dawn, the floor receives it.

Bone softens. Fur becomes fiber. The branch above develops, over the following season, a small dun patch that darkens before rain.

The city has not learned to refuse death.

It has learned to wait.

The power stays until the next dry interval. It does not sit upon the stone, for the gift is not a throne. It moves through the settlement in whatever forms can pass without causing terror. It permits beetles to crawl over it. It waits beneath a bird’s unfinished song. Once, it follows a green snake across the broadest empty span and discovers that the nowhere between branches is full of paths the city has not yet grown.

Before leaving, the power does not demand gratitude.

It makes no covenant. It appoints no guardian. It does not name the stone sacred, because sacredness too easily becomes a fence around what was freely given.

It only touches one of the oldest trunks.

The touch travels slowly. Years later, a scar closes on a different tree.

Then the power departs through the gate.

Its shadow remains behind.

For several days the shadow wanders the settlement. It drinks from every pool. It sleeps beneath the browsers’ roads. It allows flat predators to stalk it, though none dare strike. At last it comes to the gray stone at dawn and places one hand upon it.

Instead of waiting for the power to return, the shadow rises from the ground and becomes a dark, narrow tree.

The tree bears no leaves. Birds rest upon it but never nest. Its wood does not join the sapways, and beetles cannot enter its bark. At noon it is nearly invisible. At night it is darker than the hollows between stars.

Through this tree, the city can direct its attention outward.

Not travel. Not conquer. Not spread.

Attend.

It feels, faintly, rain falling on separated plants beyond the depression. It feels rivers carrying stones from mountains. It feels creatures dying where no floor rises to receive them. It feels forests that are crowds rather than agreements, cities struck by tools, and empty places that are truly empty.

The knowledge does not make it dissatisfied with itself.

It makes it less alone.

Ages pass. The stone remains ordinary.

Moss sometimes covers it, and browsers eat the moss away. Floodwater buries it. Roots lift it again. Future animals bend over it in the dark pool’s reflection. Ancient birds appear in rain rings and peck at its image with puzzled beaks.

The city continues to become what it has always been, but no longer only that.

Now and then it makes room for something it will not digest.

Now and then it allows an echo to end.

Now and then, before changing grief into architecture, it keeps grief intact until morning.

And the mineral scent that has always risen before rain is finally understood.

It was the stone’s arrival traveling backward through the city’s roots: the first sign of a kindness so careful that its echo reached the place long before the kindness dared to enter.
