The first kindness was not to simplify it.

The power did not bring rain to every valley. To do so would have drowned the buried eclipse feeders, burst the living rivers, dissolved mineral films, and covered warm faults beneath lakes where no heat herd could gather.

It did not soften the mountains. Soft slopes would have buried the fracture-dwellers, slackened the strain roots, and brought an end to the long electrical winters on which half the central range depended.

It did not thin the wing clouds so that the peaks might be seen more clearly. The peaks had no need to be seen. Nor did it quiet the hollow towers, separate predator from prey, open space between the moss trunks, or give each organism a body entirely its own.

These things would have looked like mercies only from far away.

The power came close.

It entered no body and issued no command. It allowed itself to be carried instead as the faintest surplus in existing signals: an unneeded glimmer in a silver root, a pressure too slight to open a valve, a warmth that passed through a heat herd without changing any animal’s place. It moved at the speed of what lived there.

That speed was slow.

For three centuries it followed one complete circuit of the wing migration. It accompanied the cloud without guiding it. It learned the inherited directions by which generation after generation continued a journey no individual could understand. It felt rain lock the grazers together in ice. It felt the outer animals die, the inner ones warm themselves on those deaths, and the survivors wake into a world partly made of their consumed kin.

It remained beneath the moss through twelve eclipses. It learned the colorless feeders first as scattered fluids, then as anticipation, then as hunger assembling itself into form. It watched them rise into darkness, multiply, feed, and dissolve before the returning brilliance.

It hung within an aerial colony until that drifting country grew old. It knew each gas chamber’s failure. It descended with the colony over nine years, caught between two peaks, and stayed while roots entered from either side and converted the carcass into a bridge.

It followed water from condensation to leaf, from leaf to tongue, from tongue to blood, from blood to bladder, from bladder to the muscular channel of a living river. It did not name any moment the water’s rightful location.

Deep under the newest mountains, it waited with the strain-feeders. It felt tectonic pressure accumulate across centuries. When the fibers broke and blue fire crossed the ridges, the power did not mistake the discharge for speech. But neither did it dismiss it as mere mechanism.

It listened until listening ceased to be observation and became participation without control.

Only then did it ask whether anything was wanted.

The question could not be given in words. Nothing on the plateau possessed a chamber in which words could stand apart from appetite, temperature, light, and touch. So the power translated its question into forms the high country already knew.

It became, for the roots, the possibility of an unopened crack.

For the wing clouds, a direction not yet inherited.

For the rivers, a basin toward which no channel had grown.

For the mirror-fronds, an angle between receiving light and casting it away.

For the heat herds, an empty place in the circle.

For the eclipse feeders, one heartbeat more of darkness.

The question traveled.

No answer returned as assent or refusal. The plateau had no single mouth. Its responses contradicted one another. Roots entered the imagined crack. Other roots withdrew. Winged grazers turned toward the new direction while their neighbors corrected the cloud’s course. A river budded toward the absent basin, tasted dry stone, and consumed its own blind limb. Mirror-fronds flashed and darkened. The heat herds closed the empty place. Eclipse feeders rose too early and became clear droplets beneath the moss.

Yet across these innumerable contradictions, the power perceived one common tendency.

It was not a wish to be spared.

Every species fled death by its own means, but the living whole did not ask for death to be removed. Without death, the moss floor would seal. The wing clouds would become too heavy to rise. Herds would close around bodies that could neither feed nor be eaten. Rivers would choke upon their own immortal tissue. New mountains would emerge into a world where every place had been permanently taken.

Nor was it a wish to remain unchanged.

The plateau preserved no perfect form. It abandoned channels, dismantled bodies, consumed bridges, altered migrations, and entered fractures that destroyed the shapes of earlier lives. Even its oldest systems survived by becoming something they had not been.

What the power found was smaller than a commandment and deeper than desire.

Continue, said the opening root.

Continue, said the collapsing gas cell.

Continue, said the predator entering the warm circle.

Continue, said the prey that pressed against it for heat.

Continue—not as we are, but from what we are.

The power understood.

Far beyond the violet sky, a dark body was moving between stars. It was no god and no enemy. It had neither malice nor purpose. Iron, stone, and frozen gases composed its bulk, and the shape of ancient collision governed its rotation.

In eleven thousand years it would cross the world’s path.

The impact would not have been another disturbance for life to occupy. It would have stripped much of the atmosphere, boiled the hidden waters, and broken the plateau open to depths where no root could wait. Spores would burn in the air. The aerial reefs would ignite before they could fall. Even the deep strain-feeders would be cooked inside the faults.

Nothing there could sense the approaching body. Nothing could adapt to it, incorporate it, flee it, or turn its waste into food. It offered no narrow passage through which continuation might change its shape.

It was merely an ending.

The power touched it once.

No radiance crossed the heavens. No hand appeared. The dark body lost less motion than a drifting spore loses when it brushes another in the wing clouds. Its path altered by a width too small for measurement.

Eleven thousand years later it passed through the outer sky as a pale scar of fire. Creatures turned reflective surfaces toward it. Hollow towers answered its passage with low vibrations. Some winged grazers inherited the flash as a directional error that persisted for nineteen generations. Then even that consequence disappeared.

The dark body went on into emptiness.

The power did not prevent avalanches. It did not close hungry mouths. It did not hold every failing gas chamber aloft or keep every cliff browser attached to stone. It preserved no species merely because it had once existed. Forms continued to arise, join, separate, and vanish.

But there was one other mercy it found possible.

It placed the mercy beyond the point where it could alter any struggle.

Until the last possibility of return had passed, every creature remained wholly within its life. It fled, clung, fed, froze, burned, signaled, tightened its limbs, guarded its larvae, or pressed toward warmth. The power stole no urgency from the living.

Yet when recovery had become impossible—when the falling browser could no longer be drawn back, when the frozen outer grazer had given away its final heat, when the old predator was already being dismantled inside the herd—the power entered the remaining instant.

To each it gave the form of relief its body could receive.

For creatures injured by brilliance, it was shade.

For those locked in cold, it was the remembered pressure of the warm center.

For an aerial colony losing its final chamber, it was weightlessness without the labor of gas.

For a living river drying in an abandoned branch, it was the sensation of water arriving from everywhere.

For the eclipse feeders caught aboveground, it was one private moment in which the light did not hurt.

This mercy changed no outcome. Predators still fed. Corpses still nourished roots. Alarm signals passed. Selection continued its blind, intricate work. Nothing learned to seek death because of what waited there; the gift came too late to become knowledge.

But no life ended entirely alone inside its pain.

The power remembered them as well—not as specimens, populations, or useful parts of a greater whole, but at every scale on which they had existed.

It remembered the grazer and the cloud.

The animal and the herd.

The cell and the aerial country.

The root tip, the conductor web, and the mountain-spanning discharge.

It remembered temporary bodies assembled for a single eclipse. It remembered larvae that lived for generations inside a parent and never entered the open air. It remembered rivers that had rerouted themselves until none of their original tissue remained. It remembered predators who became food for the circles they infiltrated.

Nothing was made permanent. Permanence would have violated the place.

But nothing was treated as though it had never been.

The plateau raised no temple to the power. No species evolved an organ for worship. There was no law added to the migrations, no sacred pattern in the fronds, no name hidden in the knocking towers. A wise mercy did not need to become an industry, and a benevolent power did not require gratitude from lives unable to conceive of it.

After a long while, the power withdrew from the world’s processes.

It remained attentive at their edge.

The mountains rose. Roots entered them.

Rain froze wing to wing.

Living rivers pushed water uphill.

Predators passed slowly toward the warm center.

Aerial reefs sagged between peaks and became valleys.

During eclipses the immense labor quieted, and soft bodies rose to feed in the brief, precious dark.

Nothing appeared changed.

That was the shape the blessing took.
