The power does not descend.

Descent would imply an above, and the country has never needed one. Nor does it speak from the white sky. A voice that carried everywhere would flatten the small authority of voices that reach only a few paces.

Instead, it listens.

It listens to the scrape of each blade against each hull. It listens to the names spoken to fires. It listens to the different witnesses holding the belt, the fish, the words, the silent bird. It listens to those who have gone below the account of the world, whose unheard questions trouble the brightness beneath bridges.

Last of all, it listens to the wooden knock.

The interval is not regular.

It has never been regular.

It is a hand, knocking from inside.

The power does not answer immediately. Benevolence is not haste, and wisdom is not the certainty that change is always better. The people are not errors. Their labors are not meaningless because they fail to accumulate. The woman cutting the same reeds has become skillful in ways no completed basket could contain. The child arranging shells has made something new, even if only for the length of another person’s attention.

To give such a country time without asking would also give it rot, erosion, hunger, irreversible wounds, and children who could be lost beyond finding.

So the power makes no sun.

It makes no night.

It does not wake the figure beneath the marsh.

It begins with one small thing.

In a village of seven houses, beside a water court where the permanent rings are centered on an ancient, unremembered splash, a girl places five shells in a pattern never used before.

Another child watches.

As always, the pattern remains for a little while. Then the children are called away to sour fruit and a quarrel over a feather cape. No one guards the shells. No one recites their positions. By the country’s temperament, they should return to the lip of the bowl.

They do not.

When the girl comes back, she screams.

People gather. The keeper of paths arrives first, then the keeper of things found where they should not be. Three Wakeful kneel at the court. No one touches the shells.

The pattern is still there.

Not restored. Not hidden. Not made difficult to notice.

There.

The villagers divide its memory among them. One keeps the number. One keeps the direction of the smallest shell’s opening. One keeps the distance between the two white ones. But when they return after sleep, their careful recitation proves unnecessary.

The country remembers.

For the first time, something is permitted to remain without being continuously held in human hands.

The power does not announce that this is a gift. Gifts can be obligations disguised by beauty. It waits to see what the people will do.

For many sleeps they do nothing.

Then the girl moves one shell.

The next day, it remains where she put it.

An old man begins to laugh. He laughs until he is afraid, and then he weeps with his forehead against the paving.

The Wakeful hear the distant knock.

After a pause, there is another.

They answer with a mallet against a house post.

The sound travels only a few paces, as sound has always done. Yet far away, beyond the next grove and the next water court and every low ridge between, something knocks twice in reply.

The power has not taught the sound to travel.

It has taught the country to pass it on.

Village by village, post by post, people strike the answer. Some refuse. Some mistake the rhythm. Some add beats of their own. What reaches the horizon is not the original message but a living one.

The hand beneath the country grows still.

Then, very gently, it knocks back.

Not from below.

From everywhere.

A droplet falls from the underside of a bridge and does not return.

A woman sees it strike the canal. The old fixed ripples tremble. One ring widens until it touches a post.

There it disappears.

Nothing terrible follows.

The woman kneels and waits for the droplet to be restored. When it is not, she cups both hands over her mouth.

All through the country, the wise power opens places no larger than a palm in which consequence may occur.

A cut reed remains cut.

A loaf darkens slightly in its oven.

A frog completes one call.

A hatchling pushes once more against its shell.

These changes are few, and marked. Around each one, the nearly shadowless light develops the faintest blue edge, so that no one enters unknowing. The inhabitants call them Deep Moments, Cuts That Do Not Close, and, in some villages, Mercies.

Not everyone considers them merciful.

A man puts his blistered hand into one and watches the blister heal. He is overjoyed by the smooth skin until he realizes that healing has taken the wound from him. It had accompanied him for as long as he remembers. Its absence feels like a small death.

A gardener carries a fruit into another. The seam splits. Juice runs down her wrist. She eats half and leaves the rest on a tile.

The next sleep, the fruit is brown.

The next, it bears pale threads of mold.

People come from six villages to look. Some retch. Some pray. Some accuse the gardener of cruelty. One child touches the softened flesh and says it smells like the underside of a house.

They bury it.

For the first time, a burial remains a burial.

The people surround the place in shifts, frightened that the power intends to turn preservation into imprisonment. But the fruit does not return, and neither does it become anything else.

Then, after many arrangements of shells, a green tip lifts between two tiles.

The fruit is not restored.

Something has inherited it.

That is when the power finally speaks.

It chooses no priest, ruler, or Wakeful. It speaks through the person most difficult to find: one of those who invented too much of herself and passed beneath the world’s notice. For years she has existed as a distortion in the white light. Food has gone around her. Her mother has looked through her face.

Now her shadow appears beneath a bridge.

It is the first true shadow in the country.

Dark, cool, and attached to her feet.

Her mother sees it before she sees her daughter. She crawls beneath the bridge and takes the overlooked woman into her arms.

The power’s voice is no louder than theirs.

It says:

**I will not make the future happen to you.**

The mother grips her daughter’s shoulders, checking each seam.

**I can make room for it.**

The Wakeful ask what the future is.

The power answers:

**What remains changed after attention moves away.**

The keepers ask what it costs.

**Everything that change costs.**

Will wounds close?

**Some.**

Will children grow?

**If they choose, and if those who care for them choose the danger with them.**

Will the dead return?

The power is silent for a long while.

Then it says:

**Not from every death.**

Many people leave when they hear this. Their anger is justified, and the power does not follow them. It does not call fear ignorance. It does not call refusal cowardice.

Those who remain ask whether the white country must end.

**No.**

Whether the figure beneath them must wake.

**No.**

Whether change can be undone once accepted.

**Not honestly.**

Then what is being offered?

The shadowed woman answers in her own voice.

“A shore.”

The power makes one.

Not at the outer horizon, for walking outward has always returned travelers to the country under altered names. The shore begins beside the buried fruit.

It is only a curved line of dark silt, twelve paces long. On one side, water holds its ancient ripples. On the other, water moves.

The moving water has no fixed direction. It follows slope, obstruction, rainfall, feet. It clouds when stirred and clears when left alone. It carries petals away.

At first the people hate it.

They build small barriers, and the barriers matter. They dig channels, and the water enters them. They discover that a careless wall can flood a sleeping place. They discover that a skillful ditch can keep roots wet.

Their work no longer merely reminds things what they are.

It changes what will happen next.

This is an almost unbearable responsibility.

The power stays, but it does not command. It holds in memory every alteration that would otherwise become too lonely to persist. It becomes the missing witness: not the single speaker who completes the story, but the listener who allows others to stop reciting without losing what was true.

Under its attention, forgotten houses do not vanish. They become empty houses. Names no longer in use remain former names. A dead woman encountered far away may still choose a new life, but she is not forced to lose the old one to do so.

The power restores no one against their will. It goes beneath the account and asks each overlooked person whether they wish to be seen again.

Some say yes.

Some say no.

Some ask to be visible only at certain hours, though hours do not yet exist.

For them, the power begins to make evening.

Very slowly, so that every creature can choose shelter, the white light warms toward gold along one edge of the sky. Shadows lengthen by the width of a finger. Sweat cools on skin. Leaves lose their equal illumination underneath.

Birds cry out in alarm.

Then the frogs answer.

For one brief interval, the country is full of sound.

The people cover their ears and laugh. They light fires that, within the shore, consume wood. The loss of fuel horrifies them, so they plant trees whose leaves are not stitched and whose roots contain no ceramic. The trees begin as seeds without smaller seeds inside.

Beyond the shore, the broad country remains as it was: wet, bright, newborn. Its inhabitants may live there without shame. The power does not make mortality the price of dignity. Those who prefer the permanent first moment continue cutting reeds, scraping hulls, and preserving distinctions with their hands.

Others cross and return.

Others cross and do not.

The shore lengthens only where invited. A village must ask with more than a majority; it must make room for dissent. Around each settlement that chooses consequence, a narrow band of true mud appears. In that mud, footprints last until rain alters them.

Not forever.

Long enough.

The enormous shape beneath the country gradually becomes easier to recognize. It is not a god, captive, corpse, mold, or child, though each teaching has touched part of the truth.

It is the country’s own body.

Until now, it has possessed sensation without decision. Every canal, village, animal, and person has been one way for it to feel what becoming might be like. Its knock was neither a demand to wake nor a plea to remain asleep.

It was a question it could not phrase.

The wise power does not answer for it.

Across the paving, through house posts and clay towers, the people send back all their differing replies: fear, welcome, refusal, curiosity, mourning, anger, desire.

The country listens to those who want night.

It listens to those who need the white sky.

It listens to the dead who wish to become ancestors instead of strangers.

It listens to those who have died before and wish to return again.

Then the enormous being makes its first decision.

Not to wake.

Not to remain unfinished.

To turn, slightly, in its sleep.

The movement is less than a breath. A ridge lowers. One canal finds a true slope. An oval lake opens a single dark pupil to the sky.

For the first time, the distant wooden knock is followed by no answering knock.

Instead there comes a sound no one has heard before.

A long inhalation.

At the shore, the girl who arranged the five shells is now taller. Not much taller. The change will not be taken back when she returns home.

She looks at the pattern.

Four shells remain.

One has been carried away by moving water.

She grieves for it. She remembers it. She does not replace it.

Then she sets down a sixth shell, one the country has never seen before.

Someone else watches.

The new evening deepens.

And this time, when they both look away, the pattern is allowed to become the past.
