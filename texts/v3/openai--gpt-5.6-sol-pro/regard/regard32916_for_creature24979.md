### What the Benevolent Power Does

First, it does not enter.

It does not widen the fissure, lift the basalt, or pour brightness into the drowned interval. Light, given suddenly, would be an injury. An open passage would collapse the distances folded in the water. Rescue would be indistinguishable from dismemberment.

The power understands that mercy must first learn the shape of what it would spare.

So it attends.

For one of its moments—which is several thousand years inside the chamber—it listens to the empty shells listen. It follows a pulse through the buried membrane and waits for every echo to return. It learns the slow language of inclination: ironward, sulfurward, inward, away. It watches walls persuade themselves into rooms. It studies the future held beneath the yielding dark surfaces, but does not mistake that future for fate.

Most difficult of all, it waits for the inhabitant to complete a thought.

The thought begins when pale filaments at the entrance stand upright toward impossible light.

It passes into the nearest fronds as a change in their clear beads. It crosses the chamber through mineral current, enters the hollow columns, and climbs their stacked membranes year after year. The shells receive it as a pressure too slight to close them. The little builders answer by shifting one flake of basalt the width of a grain.

A century later, the thought reaches the farthest tissue.

It is not a word. It is scarcely a desire.

It is the distinction between *elsewhere* and *alone*.

Then the power acts.

Far above the drowned interval, events that might have exposed it fail to occur. A fracture turns aside. A plume of molten stone cools along a gentler seam. Much later, when creatures with lamps and engines cross the seafloor, their instruments report only ordinary basalt. A drill planned for that latitude jams in the upper sediment and is abandoned. A cable settles a little to the east.

The power does not hide the interval forever.

It gives it the privacy owed to anything still forming.

Its second act is smaller.

Into the warmer seep beneath the chamber, it introduces no new substance. It merely separates what is already there: a few dissolved ions here, a few there, until a difference exists across the water.

The difference is minute. Any ordinary animal would be insensible to it.

The interval feeds upon differences.

Black fronds incline. Metallic grains turn in their hidden colonies. Hollow columns begin carrying the alteration toward their crowns. The buried membrane receives the pulse, measures it, and listens for its return.

But the pulse does not return as an echo.

It returns as a variation.

Once.

Pause.

Twice.

Pause.

Once.

The sequence takes thirty-seven years.

The inhabitant answers by closing every shell.

Its reply takes eighty-two.

The power repeats the variation, not more loudly, but more accurately—shaped now to the chamber’s accumulated distances. The next exchange is shorter. After seven hundred years, the interval understands that the pulse is neither weather nor injury. After twelve hundred, it understands that the source is not itself.

The pale filaments at the entrance stand upright again.

This time they do not reach blindly.

They greet.

The power offers no name. A name would divide the fronds from the columns, the swimmers from the walls, the embryo from its cradle. Nor does it tell the inhabitant what it will become. Wisdom knows that a true future is not a command sent backward.

Instead, the power gives it one image.

A young ribbon swimmer gathers dark crystals into the line along its back. At first the line shows the familiar brightness of waves. Then the image deepens. There is no sky, no forest, no black tower.

There is the drowned interval.

Not as a cave seen from within, nor as an old ruin viewed through the dark depressions. The image shows the whole distributed being at once: vast outlines folded between layers of stone; fronds as sensitive hairs; columns as nerves; shells as a listening spiral; little rooms repeating like chambers of a heart not yet begun to beat.

For the first time, the inhabitant encounters its own shape.

The sight is not wholly comforting.

Across the chamber, forests recoil. Several old fronds mineralize at once. A hollow column contracts and takes nine years to open again. The shells close in patterns never heard before.

The power does not soothe these reactions away.

To be seen is a kind of danger. To see oneself is greater.

It remains present without pressing closer.

The ribbon swimmer carries the image into the folded depths. Others gather around it. Over generations, their reflective lines cease to display only borrowed worlds. Some show the interval. Some show forms the interval might take but never will. One bears an image of open water beneath stars. Another shows a body spread through a mountain. Another reflects nothing except a curve of darkness opening into greater darkness.

Possibility enters the embryo’s circulation.

Thereafter, the little structures begin to change.

The builders add no doors, but they enlarge the thresholds. Stairs that once descended only into solid rock turn sideways after the fourth step. Pale tufts unfold their many fingers and arrange themselves around the dark depressions, touching their cool surfaces without passing through.

In the images of the future, the small jointed figure pauses.

For the first time, it looks upward.

The wise power still does not tell the interval which path is safe. Instead, it helps the being distinguish a path from a wound.

Whenever the water folds because the chamber is growing, the mineral pulse arrives in one rhythm. Whenever it folds because the enclosing stone is about to crush a living part, the pulse arrives in another. Slowly, over geological ages, the interval learns to answer the first by extending and the second by withdrawing.

This is not protection from death.

It is the gift of sensation at the scale where choice is possible.

The chamber continues to bury itself. Old forests harden beneath new ones. Basalt closes over living threads. Seasons of iron and sulfur come and pass. During the transparent years, the greater outlines remain still—but now their stillness contains orientation. Curves once pressed helplessly against the rock begin to align with the deepest folds in the water.

The inhabitant is no longer merely accumulating distance.

It is preparing a direction.

At last, long after the outer ocean has changed its shores and names, the enclosing basalt begins to separate.

The opening is not the hand-wide fissure. It occurs near the chamber’s impossible center, where the little rooms have all collapsed and the dark depressions have joined into one yielding surface.

On one side lies the old interval: rounded stone, sparse threads, sediment settling over empty shells.

On the other side there is no place that can be measured from the seafloor.

The inhabitant hesitates.

Its hesitation lasts three centuries.

The power does not pull.

It gathers around the passage as a midwife gathers around a birth: not choosing what will emerge, not sparing it the crossing, only keeping the way from closing while the choice is made.

The shells send their final pulse.

The hollow columns contract from root to crown. The mineral forests release the currents they have stored across ages. Every ribbon swimmer, including those long vanished into folded water, appears for one moment at the edge of the opening. Along their backs move skies, waves, forests, towers, stars, and worlds without light.

Then the pale filaments at the distant entrance bend inward.

The interval lets go of the rock.

Something leaves—not quickly, and not in a single body. It departs as a sequence of immensities: a limb over decades, a thought over centuries, an inward ocean passing through a threshold smaller than a shell’s mouth.

The power holds the passage.

Nothing more.

When the crossing is complete, the basalt settles. Ordinary water enters the chamber. Distances become distances. A sound made at one wall reaches the other only once.

The old future has come true: the vegetation is gone, the rooms are ruins, and sediment covers the rounded stone.

Yet in the smooth floor of the abandoned interval, there remains one shallow depression.

Its surface yields like the skin of fruit.

Looking into it reveals neither the chamber’s past nor its future. It shows a darkness of immeasurable extent, through which something newly born is moving.

Ahead of it travels a faint blue haze.

Not light exactly.

The local failure of being alone.
