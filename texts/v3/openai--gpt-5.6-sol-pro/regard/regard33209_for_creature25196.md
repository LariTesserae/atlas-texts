The power did not descend.

Descent would have broken branches, startled roosting birds, and announced a center where none was needed. Nor did it take a shape. Every available shape already belonged to some appetite, defense, mating signal, or ancestral fear. Light would have drawn insects from their routes. A voice would have awakened the canopy. A new scent might have sent whole colonies fleeing into danger.

So the power remained outside every sense by which the lowland guarded itself, and attended.

It learned slowly, because wisdom did not excuse haste.

For several wet seasons it followed water through the old channels. It felt the termite mounds breathe. It waited in the tension of roots around stones, in the warmth left beneath sleeping bodies, in the soft collapse of tunnels after their makers had gone. It learned which disturbances were injuries and which were merely changes. It learned that a hatchling taken by a bird was terrible to those who had carried it, but not an error in the world. It learned that hunger was sometimes instruction, that fear preserved knowledge, and that death fed more lives than it ended.

There were many things a merely powerful being might have corrected.

It might have sealed the mouths of snakes, cooled the nights, sweetened every pool, or made the little inhabitants too bitter for anything to eat. It might have raised dry islands beneath every sleeping place. It might have filled the forest with fruit.

But the power saw the generations that would follow those mercies: snakes starving slowly; pools crowded until disease traveled from body to body; fruit trees choking the reeds; the inhabitants multiplying beyond the patience of the land and becoming cruel through abundance. To abolish every danger would not preserve the lowland. It would replace it.

So the power touched nothing merely because it could.

Then, beneath the ordinary changes, it perceived one that did not belong to any living memory.

Far away, beyond forests the inhabitants would never cross, rain had begun to choose another country. The change was slight each year. Pools would contract a hand-width sooner. The deepest pans would retain water, then mud, then only coolness under cracked surfaces. For a time the elders would compensate. They would alter routes, postpone clutches, carry frog eggs farther. Their knowledge was broad enough for hardship.

It was not broad enough for what was coming.

There would be seven lean wet seasons, followed by a year in which the rains failed almost entirely. The lowland had endured such absences before, but not since the present channels had silted and the old western passage had been closed by roots, clay, and a ridge raised gradually by hoof paths. Water would still fall in the uplands. It would simply pass elsewhere.

The power considered making rain.

Instead, it looked at the inhabitants’ six-fingered hands, their patient bargains with fungi and termites, their routes that resembled runoff, their habit of changing the world without removing its authorship. Then it understood what kind of help could enter here without becoming conquest.

Near midnight, it placed one pulse in the ground.

The pulse was very soft. No bird stirred. A frog lifted one foot and set it down again. In three colonies, old scouts pressed their bellies to the mud.

The pulse did not resemble speech. It resembled something that speech had always been listening for.

It traveled west.

After an interval, it came again: west, beneath the thorn ground, beyond the bitter pool, under the ridge where no remembered channel ran.

The scouts answered with cautious chest notes. The power did not answer them. It repeated only the direction, each time more faintly, as if it were not an order but a trace already fading.

For eleven nights, the elders followed.

They crossed territories whose scent marks warned them away. They passed a mound opened by a honey-eating animal and found its galleries exposed. They crossed a root shelf where one of them had been taken years before by a cat. Young adults wanted to turn back. The oldest continued, stopping often to lay both palms on the earth.

At last they reached the ridge.

It looked unimportant: compacted silt, thorn roots, stones, the burrows of rodents, a few trees twisted by dry seasons. Yet below it lay the blind end of an ancient watercourse. On the farther side, reeds grew in a line too straight to be accidental.

The inhabitants did not understand the whole of it. They did not need to.

They began with termites.

From a surviving colony they moved fragments of fungus-rich wood into the ridge’s shaded cracks. They sealed some galleries and opened others. They bent broad leaves to carry condensation into the new chambers. Termites followed the dampness. During the next wet season, water entered their tunnels. Clay softened. Roots sought the moisture and widened hairline fractures.

The work passed from colony to colony. Rivals interfered, then became curious. Scent messages accumulated along the ridge: water-under-stone, old-reed-line, digging-insects, perhaps, perhaps.

No one understood it as a single undertaking. Beetles loosened what termites left. Rodents enlarged beetle chambers. Hooves pressed the weakened ground. Vines were encouraged where their roots would split the clay and discouraged where they would bind it. Each act was small enough to remain itself.

For seven years the ridge changed invisibly.

When the nearly rainless year came, the pools failed in sequence.

Fish crowded into black water and died silver-side upward. Birds departed. Cats walked in daylight, too hungry for caution. Colonies abandoned sleeping hollows tended through many generations. Elders found remembered refuges empty. Some inhabitants died. The power did not turn its attention away, nor did it prevent each death. Presence was not made conditional on rescue.

Then rain fell in the distant uplands.

It arrived beneath the lowland as pressure before it appeared as water. The ancient channel filled against the underside of the ridge. Termite galleries collapsed. Roots tore free. A section of clay no wider than a fallen trunk slumped inward.

At first only a thread passed through.

The thread became a seep, the seep a dark ribbon. It moved eastward along the reed line, hesitated in hoof prints, entered abandoned burrows, and found the first dry pan. The mud darkened before any pool formed. Beetles came. Frogs emerged from cracks. Animals smelled water and approached by night.

The inhabitants came last, because they knew water drew mouths with teeth.

They found no miraculous spring, no shining mark, no place from which danger had been excluded. They found a narrow, muddy flow that could be guarded only by alertness and shared only with difficulty. Crocodilians would eventually enter it. Birds would hunt beside it. Colonies would dispute its margins.

But the flow persisted long enough.

The deepest root systems survived. Termite queens remained below the heat. Frog eggs were carried into shaded pockets. Fish waited in mud and woke. The inhabitants drank, moved their young eastward, and pressed warnings into one another’s flanks. Their elders revised the maps they carried.

By the return of the ordinary rains, the new channel had already begun to silt.

The power allowed it.

A permanent river would have divided the country and remade every relation around it. This passage had been needed once. Now reeds narrowed it. Hooves broke its banks. A fallen tree turned part of the flow into a basin. In time it would become a chain of pools, then a damp line, then knowledge held in the hands of the old.

The inhabitants never knew that they had been noticed.

They remembered instead that, in a season before the long thirst, the ground had carried a direction no one could explain. Some believed it had been a deep animal turning in sleep. Some believed an ancestor-island had spoken through its roots. Others held that the elders had always known the western way and merely forgotten where the knowing began.

No belief was punished. No shrine was required.

The power withdrew even its silence.

Before leaving, it considered granting them a durable record: marks that rain could not erase, stones that would retain names, a language capable of surviving the death of every elder. But it saw that durability also invites ownership. A map that cannot change may become a command. A monument may gather obedience long after its wisdom has rotted.

So it gave them no object.

It left only a slight new sensitivity in the pads of their innermost thumbs—not enough to feel the future, not enough to escape the terms of life, but enough to distinguish, through mud and root, water moving far below.

The gift spread without proclamation. A juvenile discovered it first while pressing both hands to a dry pool. An elder tested the place and felt nothing. The juvenile insisted, tapping the same uncertain rhythm again and again.

They dug no well. They possessed no tools for that, and the water was deep.

Instead they marked the route with a mixture of fungus, bitter fruit, and clay. The message changed as it fermented. By the time the buried water rose, the mark had ripened from uncertainty into invitation.

Long afterward, when the power recalled the lowland, it did not remember itself as its savior.

It remembered the termites, the roots, the disputing colonies, the juvenile who had insisted, the old ones willing to be wrong, and the thin line of water finding a path through work no single creature had understood.

That was enough.

Benevolence had not made the world harmless.

It had made one necessary thing possible, then returned the world to those who lived there.
