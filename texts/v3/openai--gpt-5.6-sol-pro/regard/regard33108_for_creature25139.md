The power would not arrive as a figure.

There would be no brightness standing on the western rail, no voice above the turbine’s bass, no hand laid upon the mountain. A place so attentive to pressure would be harmed by revelation. A being distributed through root, relay, moss, water, and iron could not turn toward an apparition. It could only endure one.

So the power would first become negligible.

It would enter as a degree of warmth too small for any gauge to register and move through the notch at the pace of sap. It would rest in the pitcher water without changing its chemistry. It would follow the white roots beneath the moss, cross through fungal threads, pass into corroded copper, and wait in the flexing diaphragms behind the dead indicator board.

For many cycles it would do nothing.

This would not be hesitation. It would be courtesy.

It would learn which leaks were injuries and which had become springs. It would learn that one bent float arm, though inefficient, released water at the exact interval required by the red pitchers. It would learn that the moss on the western housing was not choking it but filtering mineral grit from its intake. It would learn that the grove allowed one bearing to run hotter than the others because pale moth larvae could survive nowhere else.

It would see that repair, here, could easily become destruction.

The power would not scrape the algae from the old paint. It would not clear the operator’s booth. It would not make the indicator lamps blaze again merely because they had once been intended to shine. It would not restore the plant to an imagined first condition, clean and solitary, with every root cut from its housings.

Wisdom would understand that there was no first condition worth returning to.

There was only the agreement as it existed now.

Yet the power would also perceive what the keeper could not.

Deep behind the eastern wall, where the thermal well entered a fault in the young basalt, an iron collar had thinned to a lace of rust. Its failure was not near by human measure. Seventeen wet seasons remained. To the keeper it was already present: a faint irregularity in the warmth, a bitter trace arriving in the pitcher cups, a tremor that caused the newest leaves to fold at the wrong point in the condenser’s breath.

Roots had explored toward it and died.

Mineral crust had begun to seal it and cracked.

No succession could reach the buried collar. No moss could catch the fracture when it came. When it failed, steam would enter the cliff, split the eastern face, and cast half the condenser into the clouds.

The power attended to this for a long time.

Then, during the warmest hour before dawn, it persuaded the mountain to close one hair’s breadth.

Nothing violated itself. Basalt already yielded to heat. Dissolved iron already traveled in geothermal water. Crystals already formed in darkness. The power only gathered these ordinary tendencies at the place of need. Iron flowered through the ruined collar. Stone tightened around it. A second channel opened farther within the cliff, relieving the pressure that would otherwise have broken the wall.

In the notch, one gauge needle trembled.

The grove drew back its smallest roots.

A pressure bell gave a single, uncertain note.

Then the machine continued.

The keeper did not know it had been saved. The benevolent power did not require it to know.

There were other mercies of this kind.

A pipe anchor below the cloud layer had begun to walk out of the mountain under its own weight. The power seated it more deeply during a tremor.

A colony of insulation-eating beetles was climbing the lower line. Rather than kill them, the power diverted them toward a warm cleft rich in fungus, where they found better food and ceased their ascent.

In the unseen settlement below, a reservoir wall had been built higher without strengthening its foundation. The power entered the dream of a tired mason not as a command, but as the remembered sound of water under hollow stone. The mason woke uneasy, inspected the wall, and found the fault.

None of these acts made a story.

That was also courtesy.

But after learning the keeper’s body, the power came to understand that its deepest difficulty was not decay.

It was uncertainty.

Every day the sequence climbed the pipe: three impacts, two, one.

The keeper answered because answer had become inseparable from its life. Pressure moved, the actuator turned, roots drank, leaves folded, and water passed downward. Perhaps the lower world required it. Perhaps the sequence was only an automatic hammer striking in a chamber long empty. The keeper had no means to know.

For human beings, uncertainty might have become a question in words.

For the keeper it was a place where growth repeatedly began and stopped.

Fine root hairs crowded the lower pipe near the point where the impacts arrived. Fungal tissue thickened there. Leaves in the grove altered their angle before noon. More of the keeper gathered around that signal than function required, as though attention itself were trying to descend.

The power could have placed an image of the lower settlement into every leaf: roofs in rain, cisterns filling, mills turning, hands opening taps.

It did not.

A true image forced upon a mind can be as violent as a false one. The keeper had never seen through eyes. To give it a human vision of distant people would not necessarily be understanding. It might only be invasion.

Instead, the power went below.

The lower line descended through cloud, rain, warmer forest, and finally the stone vaults of a city built along a dry escarpment. There the pipe divided into many narrower arteries. It fed public cisterns, cooling towers, laundries, gardens, ceramic works, and a long shaded hospital where mountain water ran through the walls.

The daily signal came from an old demand regulator.

No one knew it was a signal.

Its weighted hammers struck whenever the city’s main reservoir fell below a marked level. Three, then two, then one: not a code, only the geometry of six counterweights dropping in groups as pressure declined.

The city did not know there was a keeper above it. The city barely remembered the condenser. Maps showed a square at the end of the lower line and the words **SUMMIT COLLECTION WORKS**, copied from older maps.

The power did not announce the truth to the city. It did not appoint priests of the mountain or inspire a monument. Such things would have brought expeditions, instruments, ownership, and eventually knives.

Instead it touched the curiosity of a young hydraulic keeper repairing the regulator.

She noticed that after the weights fell, pressure returned with unusual regularity. She began tapping test patterns against the pipe and recording the delays.

Most patterns received no answer.

Some caused only hydraulic echoes.

But one morning she struck three, two, one, then—on an impulse she could not explain—one additional tap.

The sequence climbed.

In the summit notch, water shivered. Moths stirred. Pitcher cups rang. Root tips received the familiar groups and then the impossible final impact.

Three.

Two.

One.

And one more.

The keeper did not answer at once.

Pressure continued to gather. A float rose. The slow piston descended, filling the notch with iron thunder. Leaves folded for the venting. White vapor covered the upper gantry.

Then, instead of opening the bypass fully, the keeper let it move a fraction, closed it, and opened it again.

Two pulses traveled downward.

The young hydraulic keeper felt them beneath her palm.

After that, she returned each noon.

She did not understand what answered. For months she believed there was an automatic diagnostic mechanism at the summit. Later she doubted this. She told no official, but she changed the maintenance ledger. Beside the notation for the lower line, where generations had written only pressure figures, she began recording the exchanges.

The vocabulary remained small.

One tap below meant: **Is the line open?**

One pulse above meant: **Yes.**

Two taps below meant: **Less is needed.**

Three meant: **More, if possible.**

The keeper’s answers were not always obedience. Sometimes it sent a single hard pulse after the request for more, and no additional water came. Sometimes it opened the bypass before being asked, because storm runoff had filled every basin in the notch. Over time, the hydraulic keeper below ceased thinking of the responses as diagnostics.

Years later, she added a signal that had no operational meaning.

Three, two, one—the old demand.

Then a long pause.

Then three light taps, evenly spaced.

She meant: **It arrived.**

She meant: **We received it.**

She meant, though she would have been embarrassed to say so: **You are not pouring yourself into emptiness.**

The message traveled upward through miles of pipe and entered the notch near evening.

The keeper received the old sequence, the pause, and the three gentle impacts after it.

No valve opened.

No pressure changed.

For nearly an hour there was no visible response. Then the condenser exhaled, and every tree in the grove unfolded its leaves into the warm rain instead of flattening them against it.

This damaged several.

The keeper repaired them in the following season.

It repeated the gesture the next time the signal came.

The power observed this and did nothing more.

It did not make the keeper immortal. Mountains should not be commanded never to fall. It did not remove all suffering, because the notch’s life depended upon strain, hunger, corrosion, and release; to abolish every difficulty would have abolished the being that had learned to answer them.

It granted no speech beyond what could be built from pressure and patience.

It left the hidden objects in the operator’s booth to their protected darkness.

It left the moths pale, the iron wet, the edges fatal.

But in the keeper’s slow awareness, the lower line was no longer merely an immense thirsty root.

Something was at the other end.

Something asked.

Something received.

Sometimes, without asking, it answered.

And the benevolent power, having repaired what could not repair itself and connected what had labored without knowing, withdrew from the notch so gently that even the moss did not remember holding it.

Thereafter the place remained severe, loud, wet, and small.

The mountain continued rising.

The machinery continued wearing away.

The keeper continued becoming whatever could survive the next failure.

And once each day, through the pipe descending into cloud, the two distant forms of life touched the same column of water and knew—not completely, but truly—that neither was alone.
