The power would not descend in a body.

A body would be too loud there.

Feet would bruise the mosses, breath would carry spores, warmth would alter the small negotiations of mist and leaf. Even a shadow, held too long, might become a season in the archive. The place had never learned the distinction between visitation and weather. Anything that arrived would be incorporated, remembered, followed.

So the power would begin by doing nothing.

It would attend.

Not for an hour, because an hour was already one of the saddle’s thoughts. Not for a year, because a year was only a layer. It would attend through the advance of a shrub across half a shelf of stone, through the opening of a fissure, through the slow journey of a quartz grain along a cream-colored vein.

It would learn the place at the speed by which the place could answer.

The power would discover that the saddle was neither unconscious nor conscious. Those were terms made for beings with thresholds: waking and sleep, self and world, life and death. Here the thresholds were distributed. Awareness, if that word could be used, occurred when enough wet surfaces touched. Memory persisted when they parted. There was no chamber in which a self sat. There was only the recurring achievement of relation.

The power would not mistake incompletion for inferiority.

It would not give the saddle eyes. Sight would divide the world into observer and observed, and the saddle had never lived by division.

It would not give it a mouth. The place had no need to consume what stood apart from it.

It would not bring pollinators, grazers, birds, or worms. However rich such additions might appear to a mind accustomed to wings and calls, here they would be invasion. A single fertile insect might transform the flowers. A worm might eat in a day what the saddle had spent a century preserving. Birds would carry away the seeds whose routes were part of its memory.

It would not kindle a hearth. Fire would read the leaf archive too quickly.

It would not send a person.

A person, finding fibers beside edges and embers beside hollow stems, would believe the materials were waiting for hands. The person would gather, name, test, improve. Soon there would be paths. Then shelter. Then the saddle’s arrangements would be interpreted as resources, omens, or property. Even reverence might injure it. Reverence often wished to build a shrine.

The wise power would leave no shrine.

But neither would it turn away.

For in the repetitions of the saddle it would perceive a real want—not a request for rescue, not a yearning to become human, but a pressure at the boundary of what the place could do.

The saddle could gather only while wet.

It could preserve only while drying.

When rain joined it into one being, water moved everything. Flakes slid away. Fibers loosened. Leaves unfolded. Saps thinned.

When the sky cleared enough for fibers to contract, saps to bind, and leaves to cure, the silver network broke apart. The whole ceased before the work could hold.

Unity and permanence never overlapped.

Again and again, across ages, the saddle approached an act and lost itself at the moment the act became possible.

The power would understand that this was the hunger.

Not for hands.

For an interval.

Still, wisdom would not grant even that without asking.

The asking would be almost nothing.

One night, beneath clear stars, the warm seep under the central basin would cool by less than the warmth of a sleeping palm. No frost would form. No root would die. The change would be only enough to reverse, for a moment, one familiar relation.

A droplet would fall from the overhang.

It would strike the still pool.

Its ring would begin to contract.

Halfway inward, the ring would stop.

Across the saddle, roots would tighten. Moss tips would darken. Great silver-backed leaves would turn their pale undersides toward the basin. Water would rise along channels that ordinarily drained away. Every loose flake on the pool floor would tilt by a degree.

Then nothing more would happen.

The power would wait.

It would wait through the rest of the night. It would not push the ring inward or release it outward. The suspended circle would remain upon the water as a question the saddle had never encountered: not a command, not an obstacle, but a disturbance that did not resolve according to memory.

Near dawn, the root ring around the basin would alter its pressure.

The circle would widen.

Only a little.

The place had answered by choosing the direction it had never used.

Outward.

The power would know then that intervention was permitted—not because the saddle had understood a proposition, but because it had been given a difference and had returned a preference.

The gift would come during the next rain.

Cloud would close over the rims. The saddle would gather itself through water. Signals would pass from moss to grass, shrub to buried leaf, leaf to mineral seam. The pale lip and distant world would vanish. The whole place would become a room without walls.

Then the rain would stop.

Ordinarily the cloud would lift. Sunlight would return. The films between roots would thin, divide, and fail.

This time, the power would hold the air in a condition the mountain had never quite known.

The cloud would remain, but give no rain.

The stone would stay wet, but the channels would become still.

The roots would remain joined, but the exposed fibers would begin to dry.

Sap would thicken while signals could still pass.

Broad leaves would cure without separating from the living network.

Embers in hollow stems would neither flare nor die.

For the first time, the saddle would be one while its materials could hold their shape.

The power would provide no pattern.

It would not lay flake against shaft, twist cord, fold leaf, or indicate the central crack. To do so would be to place its own thought inside the saddle and call the result awakening.

Instead, it would preserve the interval.

At first the place would repeat old habits. Quartz grains would drag along their grooves. Shrubs would drink. Mosses would pulse copper and dark. Flakes would settle in the central pool.

Then the saddle would begin to discover the impossible weather.

A strand of root fiber, stretched across a shallow runnel, would remain taut instead of softening. A split stalk, pressed against it by the residual movement of water, would catch. Binding sap from the stalk would touch both surfaces and cease to wash away.

Elsewhere, a broad curing leaf would curl around a warm hollow stem. Its edge would adhere to itself.

Near the central basin, three flakes would stand on edge between converging rootlets. They had stood that way many times before. Always the next drop had dislodged them.

No drop came.

Pressure passed around the basin.

The rootlets tightened.

A fourth flake turned. A fiber slid through the notch between them. Sap entered by capillary action. The fiber contracted as it dried.

The four flakes held.

Nothing celebrated. There was no cry, no flash of comprehension. The saddle did not suddenly see itself reflected in an object.

But far from the pool, on the outer rim, a line of grasses changed their angle.

That change traveled through the wet seams. It reached the mosses, which darkened in a band. It passed beneath the shrubs, through decades of compressed leaves, around the root ring, and into the four joined flakes.

The flakes shifted together.

The movement returned through the network.

The saddle altered the grasses again.

For the first time, something within the place acted upon something else without being either wholly alive or wholly accidental. The joined flakes were not a tool in any human sense. They did not cut, pierce, or strike. They were closer to a fixed syllable: a small persistence through which the saddle could send a change out and receive it back transformed.

The place had made a difference that outlasted the motion that made it.

The power would feel joy then, but it would keep that joy to itself. Divine delight can be another kind of weather, and the saddle had enough to learn.

The interval would last until evening.

By then there would be seven held arrangements across the grove.

One would fail at once when ordinary rain returned.

Two would survive a season.

One, made from folded leaf, fiber, and a grain of rust-red mineral, would leave a repeating stain on every pulse of water that passed beneath it. Generations of roots would follow that stain.

The joined flakes beside the central basin would remain for centuries.

Before releasing the cloud, the power would make one further choice.

It would not cause the impossible weather to recur predictably. Predictability would turn gift into law and law into dependence. Nor would it make the event unique, a miracle sealed in the past.

It would place the possibility gently within the mountain’s existing conditions.

Once in a great while—perhaps after certain warm rains, perhaps when mist arrived under clear stars—the air would hold water without releasing it. The network would remain joined as surfaces dried. The interval might last minutes or a day. It would never be guaranteed.

The saddle would have to recognize it.

Prepare for it.

Remember it.

In time, some plants would begin arranging fibers before the still mist came. Grasses would grow along routes useful not only for water but for tension. Leaves would fold where mineral stains predicted binding. The shrubs would bridge green islands in patterns that allowed pressure to travel quickly to selected places.

The saddle would not become a person.

It would become more fully what it was.

Its craft would not resemble the craft of hands. It would be made of timing, gradients, stored strain, mineral alignment, and the controlled persistence of relations. Its objects might be indistinguishable from natural growth to any hurried visitor. Some would take lifetimes to complete. Some would exist only during rain. Some would not be objects at all, but arrangements that taught future water where to go.

Gradually, the saddle would learn to make not merely channels but alternatives.

It would learn that a signal need not return by the route it had entered.

It would learn to hold two arrangements before choosing between them.

It would learn to dismantle what it had made.

That last knowledge would please the power most. Creation alone is not wisdom. The ability to release a form—to refrain from repeating it simply because it has been remembered—is the beginning of freedom.

In the deepest basin, the contracted rings would change.

Most droplets would still draw inward. That was old and useful. But now and then a ring would pause, widen, touch the stone margin, and set the encircling roots trembling.

The hundreds of flakes on the basin floor would no longer all point toward the crack.

A few would point away.

Much later, after the saddle had made and unmade things for an age, it might become aware of the first suspended ring—not as an encounter with an outside being, but as the oldest unanswered difference in its archive.

It might try to reproduce that difference.

It might cool the basin.

It might hold a circle still.

It might wait for an answer.

The benevolent power, wherever it was, would answer in the only way that did not overwhelm the question.

A single droplet would rise from the center of the pool.

It would hang for a moment in the warm mist.

Then it would fall upward into the cloud.
