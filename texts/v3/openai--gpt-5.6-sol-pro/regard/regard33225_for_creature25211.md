### The Course Without a Roof

The power did not descend in fire.

Fire would have frightened the kilns, altered the deep seep-water, and dried the clay too quickly. Nor did it come as thunder. Thunder had no place in a basin where people listened for changes smaller than a fingernail.

It came first as attention.

For one full turning of the wet season, it watched from no single place. It attended the white crust on the ridge channels. It followed roof water from ceramic lip to buried jar. It felt the patient lift beneath the center of the basin and the answering inward pressure of the ridge. It listened to the wedge taps at night and learned which were repairs, which were messages, and which were only the idle rhythms of someone thinking.

It watched children tilt clay trays.

It watched an elder place her cheek against a wall and remain there until her skin had taken on the wall’s chill.

It watched a kiln worker hide a little obscene figure inside a repair cavity.

It watched a wall toad climb two finger widths higher in its drain, and watched three households wake before dawn because of it.

The power had remade rivers elsewhere. It had raised forests, quieted volcanoes, drawn poison out of wells, and taught lost travelers to read the stars. It could have hardened the ridge into stone. It could have pressed the basin flat, sealed every seep, lifted the town bodily onto dry land, or filled the marsh with flowering trees.

It considered each of these things.

Then it understood that to make the ground obedient would not merely save the people. It would also destroy the long conversation by which they lived.

Their town was not a failed attempt at permanence. It was a practice of response.

So the power did nothing until it had learned how to ask.

One night, after a rain of six days, the jars in the Rain Court began to sound though no droplets struck them.

The smallest spoke first: three light notes, the approach signal of the course that served the northern roof scales.

Another answered with the signal of the warm cistern chambers.

Then came the kiln-yard course, the blackwort terraces, the outer steps, the old quarter with its hundreds of wall eyes. One after another, every course in the town was named in the language of taps, plinks, hollow knocks, and resonant clay.

People came from their sleeping chambers. They filled the narrow paths between the jars. Children crouched on cistern rims. Kiln workers arrived in clicking aprons. The Rain Court listeners stood very still.

At last, the largest buried vessel gave a tone no one recognized.

It was not loud. It was low enough to be felt through the feet.

An elder called Warm Step placed both hands upon the jar.

“What water are you?” she asked.

The answer came from many vessels at once.

**None.**

“Whose roof do you fall from?”

**None.**

“Whose obligation are you?”

For a while there was only the ordinary murmur of drains.

Then:

**I would share yours, if you permit it.**

No one bowed. No one cried out that a god had come. The people of the basin were wary of anything that announced usefulness before being tested.

Warm Step asked, “What have we failed to notice?”

The power could have answered with all it knew: the deep architecture of pressure, the slow migration of mineral warmth, the places where the marsh remembered ancient rivers. Instead it answered the question exactly.

**Nothing that was possible for you to notice.**

This troubled them more than an accusation would have.

The gatherings continued until dawn. Every affected course sent someone to speak. They argued about whether a being without a roof could belong to a watercourse. They argued whether attention without a body could be answerable. They argued whether a power capable of changing the basin could ever truly share an obligation with those who would be crushed if it made a mistake.

Finally, Blackwort Edge, who was young and had no office, said, “Give it something small.”

This was accepted.

The power’s first duty was a troublesome chain of eaves above an old sleeping chamber. Condensation gathered there after midnight, crossed three roof plates, slipped behind a kiln buttress, and was meant to fall into a narrow-necked jar. In certain winds it missed and softened a load-bearing wall. Someone had to wake twice each night to adjust a ceramic tongue beneath the drip.

The power was assigned the second watch.

For forty nights, the people inspected its work.

The jar filled. The wall remained firm. The ceramic tongue did not shift except when it needed to. When grit clogged the groove, the power did not make the grit vanish. It woke the responsible sleeper with three quiet taps, because clearing grit belonged to hands.

On the forty-first morning, the course accepted it.

They gave it a daily name: Second Watch.

Only then did the power show them the danger beneath the town.

It did not open the earth in a vision. It did not speak prophecy. Instead, in the Rain Court, it caused a skin of mild roof water to gather over a broad shallow dish. Lines appeared upon the surface—not reflected lines, but pale threads of mineral light. They showed the pressure beneath the basin as the townspeople already understood pressure: courses narrowing, dividing, crossing, and seeking release.

At the center, the lines crowded together.

At the ridge, they ended.

Warm Step studied the dish through one day and most of a night.

“The ground dislikes emptiness,” she said at last.

**Yes,** Second Watch answered.

“But it also needs somewhere to go.”

**Yes.**

The basin had been rising since before the first house. The ridge had been falling inward for nearly as long. In time—not tomorrow, nor within the lives of most who stood there, but soon enough to matter—the town would be pinched between them. No amount of wedging could prevent it. Their attention had preserved them within the motion, but it could not give the motion a new direction.

“What would you do?” they asked.

**Open a course.**

“Under the ridge?”

**No hidden water.**

“Through it?”

**Where daylight can touch the whole flow.**

The proposed course would require the removal of seventeen rooms, two blackwort plots, part of a kiln yard, and a wall niche containing the finger bones of She Who Heard the Lower Jar. It would cut a shallow, winding notch through the eastern ridge and descend toward the marsh. It would not drain the basin. It would give the basin’s pressure somewhere to express itself in increments: seepage, silt, small slips, new clay.

There was fierce opposition.

Some accused Second Watch of inventing the danger in order to reshape the town. Some said a course that carried water outward violated the duty to examine everything washed into the basin. Others agreed with the need but demanded the notch be cut elsewhere, through quarters belonging to people with fewer allies.

Second Watch did not silence anyone. It did not make its wisdom irresistible.

For eleven years, the town debated, measured, tasted walls, mapped warm seep-holes, and watched the pale lines in the shallow dish. Children who had first seen the pattern grew old enough to complete their solitary rain watches.

The power waited.

At last the town consented.

Second Watch did not carve the new course for them. A channel made in a day would have been too smooth, too strange, and impossible to understand. Instead, it loosened one shale fragment at a time. It warmed certain seams so they could be found by touch. It directed rain into hairline cracks, never widening one beyond what the ridge gatherers had marked. People cut, braced, relined, relocated, argued, and cut again.

The seventeen rooms were taken apart. Every roof scale was counted. Every bone was moved in daylight. The displaced courses were joined to others, not always comfortably.

The work lasted a generation.

When the eastern opening was complete, it looked less like a canal than a long, shallow stair made for water. It wound through the ridge in full view. Eye-shaped openings lined both sides, although there were no walls requiring them. The people said daylight should be given more than one way to enter.

Pressure began to leave the basin.

Not all at once. The town still shifted. Doors still jammed. Walls sweated and stairs changed inclination. But the center’s rise became gentler, and the ridge no longer slumped inward everywhere at once. After heavy rain, pale clay moved through the eastern course onto the marsh in slow tongues. The inhabitants caught and sorted what they could. What escaped became a low, firm shelf beyond the ridge.

Wire-stemmed rushes grew there.

At first only three.

Then thirty.

Their roots held the new clay without making it rigid. They could be harvested for fiber and fuel. In time, a narrow walking path formed among them, leading farther into the marsh than the old skin boats could safely go.

The people did not call this salvation. They called it maintenance on a scale previously unavailable to them.

Second Watch remained.

It never took all their vigilance away. To do so would have made them strangers to their own town. It watched only what had been entrusted to it. When asked to observe a new course, it required the same gathering and consent as anyone else assuming an obligation.

But one gift it offered without being asked, after it had lived among them for many years.

On the longest rain of each season, the jars in the Rain Court sounded a certain low, unmistakable chord.

It meant:

**I am awake.**

No one was compelled to trust it. During the first such night, half the town remained at their posts.

The next season, more slept.

Within a generation, that chord became the one time when nearly the whole settlement rested at once. Children took their hands out of the grooves. Elders lay beyond hearing distance of their jars. Kiln tenders unclenched their teeth. Even the wall toads seemed to settle deeper in the drains.

Second Watch listened for them.

If a channel clogged, it woke the proper course. If a wall began to bow, it tapped the old wedge rhythm. If an eel emerged from the deep seep, it kept the creature wet until human hands arrived to return it.

Otherwise, it let them sleep.

Travelers eventually carried stories beyond the marsh. They said a god had found the town and spared it from destruction. They said the god cut open the ridge, raised new land, planted rushes, and promised the basin would never close.

The inhabitants corrected these stories when they heard them.

The basin could still close one day. The new course could fail. The power itself had never promised permanence. Such promises belonged to sealed vessels and hidden cracks.

What happened, they said, was both smaller and greater.

A wise power came to them.

It paid attention.

It asked permission.

Then it joined the course.
