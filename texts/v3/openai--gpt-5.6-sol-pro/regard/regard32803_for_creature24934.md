The power would not stop the rain.

That would be the first measure of its wisdom.

It would not lift the city into sunlight, restore every fallen roof, fill the empty districts, or make the central basin dry land. It would not give the inhabitants towers, engines, shining conduits, or walls through which no water could pass. It would understand that many apparent hardships were also forms of knowledge, and that some gifts, bestowed without patience, become disasters downstream.

Nor would it announce itself.

A voice heard across the whole plateau would create a center where none had existed. A miracle performed in one district would make that district powerful over the others. An inexhaustible spring would become an object of ownership. A fire that never went out would gather people too densely around it. Even a perfect map might become a weapon in the hands of those who wished to control routes.

The power would know that help must be given in a form that could travel without becoming a throne.

So at first, it would do nothing visible.

It would attend.

For seven years of the city—not seven turns of any universal calendar, but seven cycles of frost-dripping, red silt, joined basins, and bedding carried into the open—it would follow the water.

It would pass beneath paving where no human shoulder could fit. It would enter the hairline cracks in retaining walls and feel the pressure there. It would descend through conduits whose upper entrances had been forgotten and emerge in chambers believed to have no connection to the rain. It would learn which cisterns sickened children, which warm pools preserved frogs through winter, which roofs failed harmlessly, and which stood only because three fallen stones happened to lean against one another in the dark.

It would listen to arguments.

It would listen to the old woman who refused to let a wall be opened because her mother’s sleeping niche lay on the other side. It would listen to the household downstream whose drinking basin had turned cloudy. It would listen to two districts describing the same underground channel by different names and insisting that it flowed in opposite directions.

It would listen to laughter in the inner rooms.

That, more than the ruins, would persuade it that the city was not asking to be saved from itself.

Yet beneath the central basin, far below the visible foundations, the power would find a danger no inhabitant could have known.

There was an ancient outlet in the stone of the plateau, older than the oldest streets. Once it had carried the basin’s deepest overflow toward the northern escarpment. Long ago, part of its roof had fallen. Silt entered. Mineral crust formed upon mineral crust. The obstruction grew slowly enough that generations mistook its consequences for ordinary changes in weather.

Behind it, water had begun to occupy the mountain itself.

Nothing would happen in one year, or ten. Perhaps not in the lifetime of any child then living. But a season would come when rain crossed the plateau without interruption, when the marshes joined and the old ground became saturated to its deepest seams. Then the buried barrier would fail.

The central basin would not merely rise.

A portion of the plateau would move.

Whole districts would slide toward the northern cliffs. Reservoirs would empty into houses. Streets would break open above voids. The falls at the edge of the city would become one brown wall carrying roofs, bodies, bones from high chambers, and stones fitted by hands whose names had vanished before memory began.

The city understood ordinary danger. This danger lay outside its experience.

The power could have removed it in an instant.

It did not.

To open the ancient outlet suddenly would lower wells, drain warm cisterns, expose foul sediments, collapse foundations accustomed to the support of water, and alter every downstream claim. Saving the city by decree would still destroy parts of it. Worse, the inhabitants would learn nothing of what had happened beneath them. They would remain vulnerable to the next hidden change.

So the power acted at the pace of seepage.

In the first year, frost entered a crack it had never reached before. A stone no larger than a hand loosened in a buried conduit and was carried away.

In the second, a colony of pale worms shifted into the new trickle. Slate birds gathered unusually often above a sunken court where the ground had softened.

In the third, frogs began calling in a cistern that had been silent within living memory.

In the fourth, three hearers in separate watersheds noticed that certain channels answered rain too slowly. None knew the others. Each made knots in a cord.

In the fifth, a child dropped a carved playing stone into a wall opening and heard it strike water after an impossibly long fall. The adults told her not to put her hand into holes. One of them returned later with a lamp flame sheltered in a pottery cup.

In the sixth year, a retaining wall failed during clear weather. It injured no one. Behind it appeared a stair descending into black water.

By the seventh, people from nine flows had assembled at that stair.

They did not assemble because a god had summoned them. They came through rumor, obligation, curiosity, and dispute. Some claimed the water belonged to the central basin. Others said it fell north and therefore belonged to the escarpment districts. Two families asserted rights based on an inheritance no one else accepted. An old hearer declared that all of them were fools and lowered a marked cord into the dark.

The cord did not reach bottom.

Work began.

It took many years. No single generation completed it. Chambers were opened, abandoned, and opened again. Shafts were cleared by people lying flat in water and passing handfuls of grit backward between their legs. Private rooms were broken. Ancestors were moved. A store of grain was lost when a floor gave way. One district withdrew from the labor for three seasons after an argument over clean runoff. Another continued the excavation partly from duty and partly to prove the first district cowardly.

The power did not correct them.

It kept the work from becoming impossible.

A wedge held when it might have slipped. A fever passed over the workers more lightly than it otherwise would have. A lost child, wandering a covered route, followed a fox and came out beside an inhabited court. Once, when a roof collapsed, the stones fell inward around a mason rather than upon her. She emerged with a broken finger and later claimed that the city itself had made a hand over her head.

No one agreed.

At last they reached the obstruction.

They did not break it all at once. Their law had taught them better than that. They pierced it with narrow channels, then waited through a wet season. They measured the basin. They watched wells on the northern side. They packed one opening and widened another. They argued about the correct amount of loss.

Over decades, they gave the hidden water several ways to leave.

The channels were deliberately imperfect. Some would clog, but not all together. Some were high and would remain dry except in dangerous years. Some emptied into old streets where silt could settle before the water reached the cliffs. At three places the builders left sacrificial walls, thin enough to fail under extreme pressure and angled so that the released water would enter uninhabited quarters.

The people called these walls mercies.

Not because they knew of the power.

A mercy, in their usage, was simply a thing designed to yield before something more precious broke.

When the great rain finally came, no one living remembered the beginning of the work.

Cloud covered the plateau for twenty-three days. The Three Basins joined. Water crossed divides where old people swore water had never crossed. Roofs poured from every edge. The central wetland climbed stair by submerged stair, and families retreated into upper chambers with fuel, food, sleeping mats, and the bones of those they would not leave behind.

Then, beneath the city, the new passages filled.

One mercy wall broke.

Then another.

On the northern escarpment, five waterfalls appeared where there had formerly been one. They came out white, then red, then almost black with ancient silt. They shook the cliff roads and frightened the lowland traders. But they did not carry a district with them.

The plateau held.

Afterward, when the waters withdrew, the inhabitants found unfamiliar creatures in the newly flushed channels: transparent crustaceans, white as old bone, and a small kind of flatworm with a dark line along its back. The frogs spread to two more basins. A warm cistern cooled. Three wells became sweeter. Another acquired a metallic taste and was abandoned.

There was no perfect restoration. There was only survival accompanied by change.

The story of the work traveled, unevenly, across the city. In some quarters it became the tale of nine wise hearers. Elsewhere there were twelve, or three, or one. The child with the playing stone became a boy in districts that preferred boy heroes, an old woman in districts that trusted old women, and a fox in a version told near the western margins. Some said the stone itself had called from beneath the wall.

No one told of a benevolent power.

This pleased it.

But it was not yet finished.

Having spared the city from the danger it could not perceive, the power considered what gift might be offered without making itself necessary. It rejected wealth, permanence, and revelation. At last it chose something very small.

Across the plateau, in scattered joints of stone, a new lichen began to grow.

It was not bright. Most of the year it looked like any other gray stain. It grew only where air passed intermittently through masonry—where a blocked space existed behind a wall, where a buried conduit still breathed, where two chambers nearly met but did not.

After rain, the lichen darkened at its edges.

The inhabitants noticed.

At first children used it to mark hiding places. Then a mason discovered that patches of it showed where a sealed room lay behind a collapsed corridor. A hearer found it over an unknown culvert. Generations later, people understood that the lichen did not reveal routes, exactly. It revealed possibility.

That was the gift: not a map, but evidence that the visible city was not the whole city.

It could not be hoarded. It grew in poor districts and wealthy ones, on tomb chambers and animal shelters, beside clean water and foul. It gave no command. It merely suggested where one might listen, test, open, or leave alone.

Sometimes it led to nothing useful.

The power allowed this. Infallible signs soon become masters.

Then it withdrew—not in abandonment, but in restraint.

It left the rain to be rain. It left stones capable of falling and people capable of error. It did not abolish grief, because to do so would have required abolishing consequence, attachment, and much of what made their kindness meaningful. It did not grant immortality to the city. Even plateaus descend grain by grain. Even wise walls fail. Even a culture that understands yielding will one day yield entirely.

But the power had given them time they should not have lost to an unknowable catastrophe.

More than that, it had done so without taking the city’s survival away from the hands that maintained it.

Years later, in a room above a channel newly opened by patient labor, an old person would explain the mercy walls to a child. The child would ask who first thought of them.

The old person would name three ancestors, hesitate, name a fourth, and then shrug.

“Water showed us,” they would say.

Outside, rain would be falling softly. Someone downstream would hear it arrive through passages cut by the living, the dead, and something that had loved them enough not to demand recognition.

They would rise in darkness.

They would open the way.
