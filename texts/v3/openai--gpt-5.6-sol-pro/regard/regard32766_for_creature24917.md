### What the Kind Power Did

The power did not arrive as light.

Light would have entered the cloud and multiplied there, dazzling the ring-fliers, waking every blood symbiont, drawing the mist needles upward until they froze. It would have laid heat across the black shelves and killed the translucent films before the pressers could feed. A revelation suited to another world would have been a disaster here.

Nor did it arrive as silence.

Silence belonged to the hush-makers, or to the fear of them. To still the Low Roaring Plain all at once would have been to announce an extinction.

So the power entered as a tremor no stronger than distant rain.

It touched the pillar limbs of the egg-bearing giants. It passed through the shellback roads, through splitter spines buried in sediment, through the linked chambers of the levelers and the distributed flesh of the burrowers. It traveled into the escarpments and returned altered by every hollow. Nothing fled from it. Nothing paused.

For a long while, the power did nothing else.

This was its first act of benevolence: it refused to misunderstand.

It did not look upon the pressers and call them low. It did not look upon the ring-fliers and imagine that flight meant freedom. It did not mistake predation for wickedness, parasitism for punishment, blindness for deprivation, or the absence of speech for the absence of value.

It did not count bodies and conclude that each body was a life entire.

It learned where one creature ended in another. It followed a picker egg through a grazer’s gut, then through warm slurry, then into the mouth of a shellback, then into the feeding sac of a splitter. It watched the picker hatch there and clean the splitter’s inner combs, becoming necessary to the animal that had eaten its carrier.

It followed the moisture gathered by a ring-flier from the backs of a herd. It followed that moisture into eggs, and the eggs into severed arcs, and the arcs into a circular hunter closing around the descendants of the herd.

It listened to the migration routes until it understood that they were memories without rememberers.

Years passed. Then generations. The power was in no hurry, because haste is often only ignorance moving quickly.

At last it asked the plain what it desired.

The question could not be spoken. There was no language broad enough, and any single signal would favor those equipped to receive it. So the power asked through everything at once.

It became a sweetness in the mineral films.

It became coolness along the hot joints of grazers.

It became a pressure in brood chambers, a charge in the cloud, an opening in the crowds around water. It entered the burrowers’ drums and the giants’ deep calls. It placed the shape of the question wherever sensation occurred:

**What would be good?**

The answers came immediately and contradicted one another.

Water, said the bodies drying in the salt pans.

No water, answered eggs that would drown if the gutters filled too soon.

Warmth, said the young arches mineralizing in cold sediment.

Cold, answered the ring-fliers burdened with parasites.

Let me feed.

Let me escape.

Let this body open.

Let this wound close.

More offspring.

Fewer mouths.

Let the migration continue.

Let the migration stop here.

The power received every answer without preferring the loudest.

It might have filled the basin with clean water. For a little while, thirst would have ended. Then the mineral films would have spread beyond consumption, suffocating seepage pores. Pressers would have multiplied until they smothered their own nurseries. Shellback roads would vanish beneath soft growth. Burrowers would drown in their deepest chambers. The Mirror Pans would overflow their poison into every low place.

It might have made food without limit. But bodies would fill the air and stone more densely still, until movement ceased and the old agreements collapsed beneath abundance.

It might have removed the predators. Then the grazers would consume the films, the pressers, the eggs, the living sediment, and finally one another. Hunger would remain, deprived only of its swiftest endings.

It might have granted intelligence to every creature. But a picker forced to comprehend the grazer that swallowed it would not thereby be saved. A splitter capable of moral anguish would still possess a splitter’s gut. To awaken each temporary arrangement of hunger into loneliness would have been cruelty disguised as elevation.

The power saw that there was no gift that could satisfy every mouth.

So it listened beneath the answers.

Beneath thirst, hunger, flight, pursuit, mating, shelter, and fear, it found something slower. It moved through the plain like the buried water: seldom visible, present beneath all.

Not a wish to live forever.

Not a wish to live alone.

A wish that what passed onward should not be broken needlessly in the passing.

And deeper still, arising not from any creature but from the relations among them, came the nearest thing the Low Roaring Plain had ever formed to a single request:

**Do not take us apart.**

The power understood.

It would not make individuals where there had been continuities. It would not purify what survived by entanglement. It would not turn the basin into a garden, or raise forests whose roots would tear through the burrowers, or set a sun in the sky as if darkness were an error.

It left hunger, because hunger moved bodies into the exchanges by which the plain existed.

It left death, because death here was not an emptiness but a door used by multitudes.

It left fear where fear could still change an outcome.

But it found one suffering that served nothing.

There came, in every devouring, a point after escape was no longer possible. A shellback cracked beneath a migration might remain alive for hours, its limbs continuing the inherited road while scavengers entered it. A splitter folded into a gutter might pulse against stone long after its organs had torn. An old ring-flier, fallen beneath the herd, might reverse its muscular wave again and again, trying to fly through the ground.

The plain used their bodies.

It did not require their terror.

The power touched every lineage—not only those living, but the patterns from which later bodies would assemble. Into nerve nets, fluid horns, thermal pits, pressure bladders, distributed ganglia, and stranger organs without names, it placed a small and exact mercy.

When injury passed beyond repair, when no remaining action could free the body or preserve its brood, alarm would release its hold.

Not too early. Never while a limb could still grip, while a clot could still close, while a ring could open, while a buried arc might yet be drawn from beneath the crowd.

But when possibility ended, pain would end with it.

The creature would not become insensible at once. It would feel pressure, warmth, the bodies around it. It might continue walking. Its heart might continue to drive living blood toward departing symbionts. But fear would flatten into a deep internal stillness.

The power shaped that stillness from the oldest safe rhythm on the plain: the rise and fall of the breathing fields.

To the dying, the last moments would feel like being held in the communal body of the levelers—lifted when the earth subsided, lowered when it rose, their pressure taken up and passed through innumerable neighbors.

No creature understood the change.

Yet afterward, circular hunters found that enclosed prey ceased struggling before the ring had to crush them. Fewer arcs were torn loose. Splitters choked less often on panicked bodies. Joint-gleaners learned to leave animals that had entered the final calm, because their cooling tissues no longer provoked the feeding frenzy. Mothers crossing over fallen young paused less dangerously in the migration grooves.

Mercy did not subtract from the ecology.

It entered the ecology and became useful there.

The power made one further change.

Far beneath the Mirror Pans, it opened hair-thin passages in the stone. Not enough to drain the poisonous basins or destroy the rare emptiness around them. It allowed only a faint portion of their metallic water to descend, drop by drop, through mineral beds no animal could reach.

The poison was caught in the rock. The water continued downward.

Years later, it emerged at the bases of the escarpments as a line of cool seepage so slight that no crowd could drink it dry and no flood could gather around it. Colorless films appeared. Pressers followed. Pickers came after them. A narrow new migration road began—not away from the plain, but along its enclosing wall.

The road widened over generations. Shellbacks polished it. Grazer waste gathered in its hollows. Ring-fliers learned to descend there. New cave mouths opened as the water patiently dissolved the stone.

The power had not made limitless room.

It had made the plain capable of making a little more room for itself.

Then it withdrew its question.

It did not withdraw its attention.

There would be dangers from beyond the basin: failures of the buried rivers, changes in the cloud, fire moving through the deep strata, stones falling from an unseen sky. The power would turn aside only those calamities from which no wisdom, adaptation, or continuation could come. It would not prevent every flood or famine. A protected world is not the same as a frozen one.

No monument marked what had happened.

The creatures built no shrine. The shellbacks preserved no doctrine in their inherited tremors. The giants did not add a note of gratitude to their ground-calls. Gratitude, like sunlight, belonged to no necessary cycle here.

But sometimes, when a migration crushed an old grazer and the life upon its back began to move into it, the grazer’s many throats relaxed.

Sometimes a fallen ring-flier reversed its traveling wave one final time, not in panic but in a slow pulse that passed around its entire circumference.

Sometimes the white blood-light of a dying giant brightened beneath unbroken skin. The glow did not flash sharply as before. It traveled from brood chamber to brood chamber in pale bands, while nearby shellbacks halted and the levelers opened their temporary pores.

For a few breaths, the giant’s pressure entered them.

The levelers entered one another.

The breathing field rose.

Across the basin, unrelated crowds adjusted by fractions, making room where there was none. Flyers climbed. Burrowers changed their drums. Predators loosened and tightened. The cloud received the heat of millions and returned it as fine rain.

Nothing on the Low Roaring Plain lived alone.

After the power’s coming, nothing there had to die alone either.
