At first, the power does nothing.

This is not neglect.

It attends.

It follows a drop of rain from the eastern rim into the crown of a tree, down a red-veined leaf, through a cup where a seed-sized frog waits, along bark under a film of mites, and into the black soil. It follows the same water through roots and porous stone, into fractures where it warms beyond boiling but cannot expand. It remains with that water through pressure, darkness, mineral dissolution, ascent, release, and cloud.

It learns the intervals between the underground strokes.

It feels the marsh deer lift their heads before the springs rise. It enters, without disturbing, the long whiskers of a cat resting against a root. It knows the silver infants by the sheltering shapes of the adults around them. It receives every quiet signal carried through the hollow-rooted groves, including those that fail to arrive because a vine has broken or a tree has fallen between one troop and the next.

The power has known louder worlds.

It has seen continents raised like voices and extinguished like sparks. It has watched minds discover fire, mercy, engines, law, cruelty, music, and the means of ending themselves. It knows how easily attention becomes possession. It knows that even love, made impatient, can ruin what it wishes to save.

So it does not cool the basin.

It does not still the faults beneath it. It does not abolish the cats, lift parasites from the blood of nestlings, sweeten the sulfur pools, or make every infant live. It does not command the great birds to spare the frogs. It does not grant the primates sudden speech, or straighten their backs, or place an indestructible tool in their hands.

Death remains.

Hunger remains.

Storm, infection, error, separation, and the old impartial gravity remain.

For a long while the power’s benevolence consists entirely of refusing to improve what it has not yet understood.

Then, far below the western shelf, it finds a danger.

The danger is not immediate. It lies thousands of years ahead, where mineral deposition is slowly narrowing a deep conduit. Silica gathers there with every cycle. Iron follows. The passage will close by fractions too small for any living thing to notice. Pressure will seek another course, find none, and accumulate beneath the plateau.

The basin has endured eruptions before. Fire is not foreign to it. Destruction itself is not an error.

But this future event is different. It is an accident of converging structures: a blind chamber, a sealed throat, a weight of stone positioned so that release will not renew the land but overturn it. The western shelf will rise and split. The central lake will enter the rupture. Water will become force. Forest, marsh, terraces, cliff groves, and the warm fractures that sustain them will be thrown into the upper air in a single white convulsion.

Life will return eventually. Life nearly always does.

But this life, this intricate and unrepeatable agreement, will be gone.

The power considers whether unrepeatability is reason enough.

It listens through another hundred rainy seasons.

Then it touches one grain of stone.

Nothing shines. No voice passes over the waters. The animals do not look up.

Deep below the old conduit, a crystal face inclines by less than the width of a bacterium. Under pressure it parts from its neighbor. The separation extends into a hairline fracture, then stops. Warm water enters. Over centuries, it opens the seam molecule by molecule.

A new spring appears eventually on the outer slope of the mountains, beyond the basin. It is no wider than a hand. Ferns gather around it. Beetles find it. Its water carries a faint metallic taste.

That is all.

The deep chamber now has somewhere to breathe.

The great eruption will not happen.

There will still be eruptions. Cliffs will fall. Species will vanish. Others will arise. One day the central lake may drain, and centuries later return. The power has not made the basin eternal. Eternity would freeze the circulation that makes it what it is.

It has given the place only a margin.

Afterward, the power withdraws from the stone but not from attention.

It becomes aware that attention itself can cast a shadow. The primates pause more often with their wrists against the wood. The oldest females turn toward empty stretches of canopy. A cat abandons a kill and watches rain descend through a shaft of light. Frogs stop calling when no visible hunter is near.

The power makes itself smaller.

This is difficult even for a power both benevolent and wise.

It lets the basin forget that it is being watched.

Ages pass.

The primates change, though not because they are driven toward any appointed form. Their language of tremor lengthens. Where once a sequence conveyed only fruit, danger, readiness, absence, it comes to hold events displaced in time.

There was a cat here.

The western water will rise after the second darkness.

The tree beyond the ravine bore sweet fruit when my mother was silver.

One troop begins to repeat the rhythms of animals long dead. Another learns to strike two branches at once, sending paired signals through different paths in the canopy. Meaning arises not from either pulse but from the interval between their arrival.

The power hears the first deliberate falsehood.

Later, it hears the first confession.

It does not intervene in either.

The primates discover that fallen hollow limbs can be moved. They place one across a broken root network and restore contact between two groves. The act is simple, accidental at first. But the branch remains after those who moved it have gone.

Others use it.

For the first time, something in the basin has been arranged to outlast the immediate need of the hands that arranged it.

The power attends closely now, but does not become alarmed. A bridge is not a road. A tool is not a wound. Foresight is not dominion.

Many wet seasons later, during a storm of unusual electrical violence, lightning severs the connected crowns between three nursery trees. Silver young are stranded beyond a ravine while charge crawls through the exposed vines. Adults on both sides strike warning after warning, but the broken forest carries nothing across.

An old female descends.

She enters water up to her chest, although long-bodied cats hunt that crossing. She drags a fallen hollow limb through the reeds. Others join her. They lift it against the surviving roots on either side.

The first signal through the improvised bridge is blurred by rain and the old tree’s decay.

It means, approximately:

Remain. We are coming.

The power feels the message pass.

For one moment it desires to answer.

It could send a tremor through every root in the basin. It could speak without sound into blood and bone. It could tell them that their lives are seen, that the dead are not rendered meaningless by death, that no act of shelter is entirely lost. It could give them certainty.

But certainty, too, can become a kind of enclosure.

So the power does not name itself.

It waits until the storm has passed, until the stranded young have been recovered, until the old female sits shaking beneath a mineral-streaked trunk. One silver infant presses against her. Around them, adults begin the familiar three blows that mean danger ended, bodies counted, group made whole.

One hand strikes wood.

Then another.

Across the ravine, a third answers.

The power places no message between them. It changes no mind. It asks for no worship.

It only receives the rhythm and holds it perfectly, where nothing that has happened can be made not to have happened.

Then it returns its attention to the smallest things: the films advancing over warm stone, the transparent wings pasted to leaves, the new spring breathing unnoticed on the far side of the mountain.

The basin continues.

Water enters.

Stone opens.

Roots drink vapor.

A cat waits at the edge of the reeds.

Hands rest against living wood.

And deep beneath them, where catastrophe once gathered without purpose, pressure passes harmlessly through a narrow seam and becomes warmth, mineral, fern, cloud, and rain.
