### The Margin

The power did not begin by changing anything.

This was its first kindness.

It did not clear the vapor, because the vapor gathered the water. It did not still the birds, because silence in a nesting wall meant hunger, poison, or teeth. It did not raise mountains around the basin, summon forests from the flats, sweeten every spring, or turn the mineral crust to soil. It did not mistake strangeness for suffering.

For a long while it listened.

It listened beneath the cries of the birds and found the calls by which keepers counted occupied chambers. It listened beneath the hammering and learned which blows opened stone and which announced a cracked tool. It heard the nearly deaf converse through their fingertips. It heard the sleeping city’s gutters, the churning of pigs in enclosed courts, the small wet sounds of children hunting snails by touch.

The power was capable of hearing every life separately. Wisdom taught it not to do so at first. No creature here existed separately enough to be understood that way.

So it listened to walls.

It listened to the cistern that belonged to twenty households and to the stair that constituted a family. It listened to guano passing from nesting tower to shell burner, to oven runoff entering fish pools, to warm water depositing stone around the bones of the dead. It followed a single cupful from roof to pipe, pipe to jar, jar to mouth, body to drain, drain to bacterial vat, vat to pig, pig to slaughter court, and onward until distinction became foolish.

Only then did it notice what was wrong.

There was no great misery hidden at the center of the city. No tyrant drank the basin dry. No monster slept below the springs. The people were not waiting to be rescued from the lives they had made.

But the measure had grown too exact.

Every useful thing had an inheritor. Every outflow had a claimant. Every chamber, egg, crust shaving, bone, feather, drop, and handful of offal had already been promised onward. The city possessed no empty place in which an error could remain harmless.

Its constancy was magnificent.

It was also brittle.

A wise power can see storms before clouds gather. It can see a spring changing its course deep underground, though the first displaced grain of mineral will not move for eighty years. It can see distant forests being cut, coastlines drowning, fish altering their paths, diseases learning new hosts. It can see the moment when old numbers will cease to recur.

The power saw such moments.

It saw no single doom. It saw possibilities, which are more difficult. In one, a spring doubled and drowned three wards before the excess could be assigned. In another, the spring failed, and every claimant took their legal share until the cisterns were empty. In another, birds abandoned the towers in a single season for reasons that began beyond the horizon. In another, nothing changed for so long that the people forgot change was possible.

The power considered reaching beneath the basin and fixing the springs forever.

It could have done this.

But forever is a form of death when imposed on the living.

Instead, it entered the city.

It came without light. Sudden brilliance would have blinded fishers on wet stone. It came without wings, because wings already had too many meanings. It wore no green wreath and carried no flowering branch.

It arrived as a rhythm.

Far below the ordinary uproar, beneath hammers and water and claws, a slow pulse entered the foundations. It was too deep to be heard. It could only be felt through bare feet, walls, sleeping platforms, water bowls, and the handles of tools.

Three beats.

A pause.

Two beats.

A longer pause.

Then again.

The children noticed first. They always noticed what adults had trained themselves to dismiss. They pressed their cheeks to the paving and drummed the rhythm on shell cups. Dogs began waiting through the pauses. Bats shifted in the vaults. Old people laid their palms against walls and frowned.

Measurers went out carrying rods, lines, tablets, and bowls filled to the lip. They checked every channel. Nothing had widened. Nothing had narrowed. Water entered and left in its proper quantity.

Still the city beat beneath them.

At last an old measurer named Ashur descended alone into a disused cistern chamber where the pulse was strongest. She had lost most of her hearing decades before, but through stone she could distinguish a dropped shell from a falling body three houses away.

She placed both hands on the floor.

The power spoke by vibration.

**I HAVE SEEN YOU.**

Ashur waited. Praise from strangers was often the beginning of fraud.

She tapped the floor with the butt of her measuring rod.

“From where?”

The power did not understand.

She tapped again, more sharply.

“If you have seen us, from where have you seen us? Through whose wall? By whose channel did you enter? Who bears the disturbance?”

The power had been addressed by kings, prophets, animals, stars, and the dead. Seldom had anyone’s first response been a request for jurisdiction.

It answered:

**FROM EVERYWHERE THAT TOUCHES THIS PLACE. I ENTERED THROUGH NO CHANNEL.**

Ashur’s fingers remained on the stone.

“Then you have entered improperly.”

The power considered this.

**YES.**

“Will you leave?”

**IF I AM ASKED.**

Ashur sat back on her heels.

The pulse stopped.

Across the city, sleepers woke. Birds lifted from roofs. Workers froze with tools raised. For one terrible instant the absence of the rhythm felt like the silence preceding collapse.

Then the ordinary clamor closed over it.

Ashur rested her forehead against the cistern floor.

“Come back,” she said. “But slower. We must make an account.”

The power returned with one beat only, gentle as a drop falling a great distance.

For forty days the city debated what obligation a benevolent power incurred by noticing them.

No one asked first for food, health, long life, dry clothing, quieter birds, or metal tools. These things were discussed, sometimes passionately, but each gift tangled at once in consequences.

If every child lived, where would they sleep, and whose egg allotment would feed them?

If tools did not break, what would become of toolmakers, and how much faster would the channels be cut?

If grain became plentiful, which fish ponds would receive the new waste, and what imported cargo would cease to arrive in exchange?

If timber could not rot, would a beam ever return to another use?

If the power silenced the birds, even briefly, who would count the chicks that died in the interval?

One delegation asked for a hill high enough to overlook the whole city. Another objected that the hill would obstruct condensation and require an unmeasured quantity of stone. A wealthy ward requested a sweet-water spring. Three downstream wards demanded to know where its outflow would go. Children asked for a creature no one had seen before. The nesting keepers refused this absolutely.

At last Ashur went back to the cistern.

“We do not know what to ask.”

The power answered beneath her hands.

**THAT IS NOT FAILURE.**

“We know what is lacking,” she said. “But if it is supplied, something else will lack.”

**YES.**

“We know what causes pain. But some of it warns us, and some of it binds one life to another, and some of it we have merely failed to imagine without.”

**YES.**

Ashur struck the stone in irritation.

“Then what use is wisdom?”

For the first time, the power laughed.

Its laughter passed through the city as a tremor too light to crack mineral skin. Water shivered in cups. Sleeping bats opened their wings. Thousands of birds took up the vibration and answered with offended cries.

Then the power said:

**WISDOM IS HOW POWER LEARNS NOT TO BECOME DISASTER.**

Ashur considered this for a long time.

Finally she said, “We need somewhere for mistakes to go.”

The power became still.

Ashur continued.

“Not away. Nothing should vanish. But somewhere it may wait before it is assigned. A place for too much water before it drowns us. A place from which water may return when there is too little. Chambers no household must fill merely because they are empty. Nesting hollows no ward may count as wealth. Stone that need not yet become a wall.”

The power listened.

“A margin,” Ashur said. “Give us a margin.”

This was the gift it would have chosen.

Therefore it did not grant it at once.

A margin created without labor would become an object of worship, then authority, then capture. A reservoir that never emptied would teach carelessness. An infinite waste pit would permit the city to poison everything beyond itself. A place belonging to no one would, unless guarded by custom, soon belong to the strongest.

So the power did not open a shining cavern beneath the basin.

Instead it altered the deposition of lime by less than the thickness of a fingernail each year.

Far beyond the last inhabited platforms, where lines of stakes marked districts not yet born, a broad ring of porous stone began to rise. The change was so slow that it entered the records before it entered legend. Measurers noticed that certain outer pools held water a little longer. New ridges curled in directions no model predicted. Shell banks joined one another. A hollow formed between them.

The power changed no total quantity. It stole no water from one ward to favor another. It made a place where the timing could differ.

The city labored for thirty-one years.

Causeways were extended. Overflow channels were cut. Sluices were fitted with hide and bone. Cistern chambers were corbeled beneath the new ring, but not sealed. Some were kept deliberately empty. This offended nearly everyone.

Empty useful space looked like negligence.

The first guardians of the margin were accused of concealment, laziness, impiety, and theft. Several times, hungry wards tried to stock the reserve pools with fish. Several times, wealthy roofs attempted to claim the empty cisterns against future condensate. Children occupied them for games. Lovers occupied them for privacy. Bats occupied them because bats recognized no municipal distinction between sacred vacancy and an excellent ceiling.

The city adjusted.

It decided that bats might use the chambers but could not establish ownership. Children might play there but could build nothing permanent. Lovers were not mentioned in the law and therefore continued.

The new district was called the Ward of Waiting.

It had no households. Its keepers were chosen from downstream and upstream wards in equal number. No keeper could serve twice. All measurements were spoken publicly, tapped into common walls, and carved on slabs exposed to mineral water, so that even the records slowly thickened and had to be recut.

On every measuring rod, a new interval appeared beyond the final numbered mark.

It had no value.

It was called the mercy notch.

Water within the known marks belonged to obligation. Water beyond them entered the margin. A shortage that did not pass the mercy notch could be supplied from reserve without accusation. Egg counts, nesting chambers, imported grain, guano deliveries, fish populations, and even household debts acquired similar tolerances.

At first people used the margin shamefully, as though any departure from prediction revealed moral weakness.

Then came a year in which the black-winged insect hunters nested in half their usual numbers.

Nothing in the city had caused it. Nothing in the city could correct it. Somewhere beyond the vapor lid, perhaps, the insects had changed their path.

The old law would have required every ward to fulfill its egg and guano obligations regardless. Nesting walls would have been stripped. Birds would have been driven away. Shortage would have produced greater shortage in the name of balance.

Instead, the missing quantity was entered within the mercy notch.

Guano reserves were opened. Egg allotments were reduced without fines. Empty chambers remained empty.

The following year the birds returned.

No miracle announced this. They came screaming out of the gray as they always had, arrogant and filthy, and filled the towers until children had to cover their ears.

The people celebrated by making more noise.

Ashur did not live to see that year. Her body was placed in the outer wall of the first reserve cistern, where water touched her knees in times of excess and withdrew below her feet in times of lack. Mineral crust slowly covered her hands, but at her request the fingertips were kept clear.

Generations of measurers touched them before opening the reserve.

The power remained attentive.

It did not govern. It did not demand temples. When asked its name, it supplied none, because a named power becomes too easily the property of those authorized to pronounce it. It continued as a faint pulse under the Ward of Waiting, one beat with a long interval after it.

Sometimes the interval mattered more than the beat.

In time, the basin changed.

Not greatly. Not all at once.

But constancy ceased to be mistaken for stillness. Records began to include ranges as well as quantities. Laws spoke not only of return but of recovery. New buildings contained unfinished chambers by design. Some walls were erected with hollows whose future use had not been decided.

This frightened traditionalists, as perhaps it should have. Not every novelty was wise. Some people invoked the margin to excuse greed. Some called waste an experiment and theft a variation. The measurers remained busy distinguishing mercy from convenience.

The power did not make that judgment for them.

Benevolence could offer room. It could not live their lives in their place.

Many years later, a boat arrived from a distant coast carrying timber, seed oil, grain, and coils of fiber. In the damp dirt caught between two split planks lay a seed no one had purchased.

It fell beside an overflow channel in the Ward of Waiting.

There, mineral water had been diluted by rain held briefly in an empty basin. Bird droppings had gathered in a crack. No one had scraped the surface because the surface was assigned to no immediate use.

The seed opened.

Two pale structures emerged. One went downward. One went up.

A child found it and called for a crust shaver, believing it to be an unfamiliar worm. The worker arrived, raised her shell blade, and stopped.

Before them stood a stem no longer than a finger joint, bearing two green leaves.

People gathered.

They stared with the same solemn bewilderment they might have shown a fire burning underwater. Some wanted to place a guard around it. Some wanted to uproot it before its roots broke the channel. A food merchant offered to buy it. A carpenter asked what sort of timber it would become. Three measurers argued over whether it belonged to the boat, the overflow, the birds whose waste had fed it, or the Ward of Waiting.

At last an old woman pushed through the crowd. She knelt on the wet stone and examined the tiny plant.

“What has it displaced?” someone asked.

The old woman looked at the crack, the droppings, the shallow basin, the open air above.

“Not enough to know yet,” she said.

“What shall we do with it?”

She touched one leaf very gently.

“Measure around it.”

So they did.
