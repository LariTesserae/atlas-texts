The power would not arrive as rain.

Rain already ruled too much here. It woke what slept and drowned what had gathered; it joined the distant courts and separated them again. To give more rain without understanding would be easy. A generous storm could fill every reservoir, green every seam, and undo in one bright season what drought had taught the inhabitants over centuries. Roots would swell inside the courses. Walls would open. Unfired districts would slump into red deltas. The temporary plenty would become salt.

Nor would the power bring forests.

It would not cover the pale works with deep soil, release rivers across the paving, or summon animals in multitudes to complete an ecology that had never asked to be completed. Benevolence without patience would be another builder: arriving with a finished intention and laying it over everything already there.

So the power would first become small enough to notice.

It would attend to one pore in one brick.

There, a sealed cell waited inside a skin of sugar and mineral dust. It had waited through the cooling clicks of many nights. The power would not wake it. It would learn the shape of its waiting.

It would attend to a moss garden beneath a ceramic fin, green for nine hours after condensation and black for the rest of the year. It would follow a grass root along a seam until the root narrowed beyond ordinary sight. It would enter the wall with the silver shrub and discover the old handprint filled by living tissue. It would rest beneath a tree where roots repeated the geometry of drains laid before the first seed arrived.

This attention would take a very long time.

The power was capable of haste, but wisdom had cured it of admiration for speed.

Gradually it would understand that the being of the Low Works was not any film, moss, grass, shrub, or tree. It was not even all of them together. It was the passage between them: dust caught by stems, shade given by walls, mortar loosened by roots, water diverted by what water had enabled. Its body consisted partly of living things and partly of changes made available to the next living thing.

Its pulse was the storm.

Its memory was silt.

Its thought, if thought was the word, occurred wherever a gradient ceased to dictate and began to be answered.

The power would love it for this.

Love, however, would not confer ownership.

So nothing would happen for several seasons.

The temporary lakes would fill and contract. Salt would whiten their stairs. Seed would travel and fail. A branch in a western court would split under heat and fall across a gutter. Behind it, silt would gather. Grass would wake there, then die when the water went around it. A tile would lift. Moss would enter beneath it. All this would happen without aid, and the power would not claim it as a sign.

Then, in a district of pale yellow brick, one of the oldest trees would begin to fail.

Its court had once received water through three channels. A shrub had opened one curb; sediment had closed another. The third still carried rain, but less with every storm. The tree had already abandoned half its crown. Its deepest roots ended against fitted slabs. Its outer roots followed joints into dry masonry and returned with nothing.

There would be no injustice in its death. Trees had died before it. Their wood had become islands, their islands dust, their dust the dark material beneath other roots. The power knew this.

But wisdom is not the refusal to choose.

The tree’s roots had reached a wall beyond which lay a descending passage. Water moved through that passage after every storm, close enough for the roots to darken from its coolness, never close enough to drink. Between root and water remained the thickness of one brick.

The power would touch no living tissue.

It would touch the brick.

Not break it. Not remove it. Only restore to it, for an instant, the memory of wet clay.

The brick would soften around one ancient thumbprint left by a builder. The hollow of the print would deepen by less than the breadth of a seed. A thin flake would fall inward. Nothing would shake. No light would appear. No voice would speak across the court.

At the next rain, the passage would fill.

Most of the water would continue down its intended fall. A little would enter the thumbprint, pass through the missing flake, and darken the mortar on the other side. The tree’s nearest root would not reach it immediately. It had no means to know the water was there.

But moisture alters more than roots.

Salt dissolved. Lime softened. A dry film swelled inside the wall. Moss woke along a fissure that had never before remained damp through noon. Its threads held water for an hour after the passage emptied.

The root tip resumed dividing.

Years later, it entered the print.

The tree did not become magnificent. Its crown did not suddenly fill the court. One bare branch remained bare. Another put out seven narrow sprays. In the following flood it released seeds, most of which gathered at a stair and perished.

One lodged in the wall where the brick had softened.

That was the power’s first act.

Its second was to wait and see whether the act had been kind.

It watched the new seep through cycles of flood and heat. It watched for salt accumulation, collapse, unchecked roots, the drowning of pale mosses adapted to lesser damp. When one colony spread too quickly, the power did not preserve it from drought. When the tree split the softened brick, the power did not mend the brick. The intervention entered consequence and was no longer protected from the world.

The result was small but not solitary.

Water passing through the wall carried a dark thread of matter into a court outside its designed basin. A line of grass appeared there. Its roots widened two joints. Runoff divided around them. One branch returned to the old channel; the other crossed a threshold that had been dry since the Low Works were made.

The revision propagated.

Only then did the power consider another gift.

It considered animals.

The Low Works contained flowers untouched, fruit uneaten, wood slowly whitening where it fell. Its life had learned exquisite forms of endurance, but little encounter. Nothing chose a seed. Nothing carried matter uphill. Nothing crossed a basin for reasons other than wind and water. The continent’s gradients had been revised, but they still governed nearly every journey.

The power could have filled the dusk with wings.

It did not.

Instead, during the brief season when a chain of northern reservoirs held water, it guided one distant traveler slightly from its course.

The traveler was a bird.

It was neither rare nor splendid. Its feathers were the brown of silt caught between bricks. It had been carried far over the hot air and was looking only for a place to descend. The black paving repelled it. The white paving confused it. Then it saw a temporary lake holding the copper sky and folded its wings.

For the first time, an animal’s shadow crossed the Low Works.

It landed on the exact edge of the water.

The shore, accustomed to rain and salt, received two feet.

The bird drank. It lifted its head between swallows and looked across the empty stairs. Its small vigilance had no object. No predator hid there. No other bird answered. Still, it watched, because watching belonged to it.

A seed was caught in the feathers beneath one wing. It had come from elsewhere, but the power did not cause it to fall. The bird scratched. The seed dropped onto wet silt.

The bird drank again. Then it walked several steps uphill.

This was a motion almost unknown in the Low Works: water carried inside a body, against the gradient.

At the third step, the bird left a dark mark.

It remained through the afternoon.

By evening the traveler had gone. The power did not compel it to stay, nest, sing, or become the ancestor of a new multitude. A place should not be cured of silence simply because silence troubles an observer.

The foreign seed did not survive.

Something smaller did.

A dry grain from the Low Works had adhered to the bird’s foot at the waterline. It was carried uphill those three steps and dislodged in the shade of a curb. The next storm did not reach that curb, but condensation did. A film woke there. Moss followed. Much later, one blade of blue-gray grass unfolded from a seam above the highest level any flood had touched.

The power accepted this as enough.

Thereafter its care would be difficult to distinguish from chance.

Now and then it would incline a cloud toward a basin whose hidden moisture had nearly failed, but it would never abolish drought. It would trouble the straight descent of a flood with a crosswind, carrying living fragments sideways into works that gravity did not connect. It would let a night grow unusually cool so that dew formed on the upper steps and not only in the depths. Once in a great while, it would guide another solitary traveler to water. Some would leave seeds. Some would eat them. Some would disturb gardens too delicate for feet.

The power would permit this disturbance.

Life protected from every wound becomes an exhibit. The being of the Low Works was not to be preserved at the moment it attracted love. It was to be allowed a future not entirely contained in its past.

Yet the power would keep one discipline above all others: it would never finish the place.

When walls fell, it would not raise them.

When soil covered tiles, it would not uncover their designs.

When roots died in their own crowded abundance, it would not make every loss fruitful.

When a species reached the end of what it could endure, the power would grieve without pretending grief was a warrant to command eternity.

Its benevolence would take the form of openings: a softened brick, a traveler’s shadow, a night of high dew, a wind crossing the prescribed fall.

Its wisdom would consist in leaving those openings unguarded.

Ages later, there might be districts where the fitted ground could no longer be seen. There might be thickets around the old cisterns, powdery soil in the courts, small lives moving beneath fallen stems. Elsewhere the paving would remain bare, still answering rain with its many voices. Some walls would persist as long red lines through green. Others would become nothing but slight changes in the plants growing over them.

The land would not return to nature, because there was no former nature to which it could return.

It would become unprecedented.

And somewhere in that future, water would enter a channel, encounter a root, turn aside, gather behind the remains of a stair, rise, and spill in a direction neither builders nor power had chosen.

The power would feel this happen.

It would do nothing.

And its joy would be immense.
