### The Power Does Not Descend

The benevolent power does not come down.

Down belongs to mountains, forests, and sufficiently repeated stones. To enter by descent would be to choose one gravity over another and declare it correct. It does not shine, because direct light would feed the black fronds until they roofed the sea. It does not speak, because any word heard clearly would become an event the world was forced to fulfill.

It does not still the migrating abysses.

It understands that mercy is not the prevention of every ending. The depths are terrible, but behind them the patient mouths breathe, first memories take root, and exhausted stone is released from the labor of remaining a mountain. To destroy the abysses would preserve much that lives, but it would also preserve every wound, every failed grove, every chamber whose condition should at last be allowed to change.

Nor does it straighten gravity, restore the sun, join the scattered animal forms, or make sound obedient.

This place is not broken merely because it is difficult to understand.

So the power remains beyond the sea for a long while.

It watches without hardening the glasswood sap. It attends without becoming scrutiny. It learns to distinguish a path from a creature and a creature from a habit of the water. It follows conversations measured in seven notes and waits through the silences between them. It discovers which ruins are mourned, which are useful, and which have forgotten that they were ever anything else.

At last it notices a walker tending a bowl in a chamber near the living-solid shore.

The walker has no name nearby. Its name is still traveling through the joint of a glasswood trunk and will not return for eleven thousand years.

Its borrowed body is made of lavender mineral fans, ribbon fibers, three glasswood joints, and a cluster of bladders containing memories of dry wind. The hollow within that body is narrow. It has grown narrower over time.

For nine centuries the walker has been turning the bowl by the width of a dissolved grain. The small sea inside is bright and cold. Silver animals move far beneath its surface, flashing as they turn. Once every forty-one tides, one of them rises and opens a mouth toward the walker, though no sound crosses the distinction.

The walker is waiting for a note.

It knows that the note will arrive. The note has already caused certain ribbon groves to turn edgewise. Its pressure can be felt in the oldest roots. But it has not yet been made.

The benevolent power does not appear before the walker.

Instead, it makes itself smaller than appearance.

It enters one of the walker’s memory bladders and takes on the form of something the walker has never experienced: not a remembered plain, not an extinct sun, not the opening of an ancient leaf, but an empty interval in which no event has yet decided what it will become.

The bladder grows clear.

The walker stills.

Emptiness recognizes emptiness.

Carefully, the walker detaches the bladder and carries it into the stone chamber. This should not be possible. Its borrowed body cannot cross the doorway. Yet the bladder passes with the hollow, neither part of the body nor separate from it.

For the first time, something accompanies a walker inside.

The shallow bowl remains level.

The power asks no question in words. It creates room for an answer.

For seventy years, nothing happens.

Then the walker alters its outline.

The change is slight. One edge of its hollow bends toward the little sea. Another bends toward the living-solid shore beyond the chamber walls. A third turns toward the darker water where foreign rooms drift beneath a hard sun.

The meaning is not simple enough to be called fear.

The power waits.

Over the next six centuries, the walker shows it what cannot be told in sound.

It shows the bowls: preserved differences, each saved from the greater sea’s slow persuasion. Cold kept from warmth. Quickness kept from age. Brightness kept from bronze dimness. Tiny species protected from the local rules that would otherwise divide their movement from hunger and their memory from both.

It shows that preservation has a cost.

No bowl has ever been opened.

The walkers know how to prevent one sea from joining another, but not how to let them meet without one becoming the condition of the other. They can keep a thing unchanged, perhaps forever. They cannot release it safely.

The chambers are sanctuaries.

They are also prisons so perfect that nothing inside knows itself confined.

Then the walker shows the foreign bowl.

It shows figures on a dry shore under the hard sun, looking up.

Those figures have begun to notice when they are observed. Sometimes one raises an arm. Sometimes they vanish and return with instruments that glitter. Once, one of them pointed directly through the thumb-sized surface.

Afterward, for three tides, the greater sea had only one down.

Mountains tore forests from themselves. Air pockets fled their chambers. A migrating abyss stretched into a narrow line and cut an ancient plateau in two.

The walkers fear not invasion, but definition.

They fear a world arriving with rules it believes are universal.

The benevolent power understands.

It could close the foreign bowls. It could hide the upper sea from the dry shore. It could place an immeasurable distance between them, erase every instrument’s finding, and turn the upward gaze aside.

But this would merely preserve two ignorances.

It could also unite the worlds at once.

That would be worse.

So it performs one act, though the act takes many ages to become small enough not to wound.

It teaches the chamber a new idea.

The chambers already know enclosure. Roots repeated walls until stone learned inside and outside. Bowls learned distinction. Air learned to gather where dryness was remembered.

Now, grain by grain, the power introduces another condition:

**A boundary may open without surrendering its shape.**

No law in the sea has held this before.

The lesson cannot be imposed. Repetition makes things real, but forced repetition would make the chamber an instrument of the power rather than a gift. Therefore, each time the new idea is placed among the stones, the power allows the sea to erase it.

The walker places it again.

A root takes it up and grows a ring without closing the center.

Silt gathers against empty water, forming a wall with a passage through it.

Two ribbon leaves touch, exchange no color, and separate unchanged.

A cold lens moves through warm water without shrinking.

A bell swimmer enters shadow. Its body loosens into dust, but for one pulse the bronze thread continues swimming.

These are failures.

They are also rehearsals.

The living-solid shore notices. Its capillaries alter. A thought begins at one extremity and, thousands of years later, reaches another: a current admitted is not always a self lost.

The shore-mind repeats the lesson.

The long animal carries it in the intervals between its body.

Patient mouths inscribe it into stones, not as an open eye but as two spirals whose edges meet without merging.

Even an abyss passes over one such stone and leaves the pattern incomplete rather than old.

At last the shallow bowl changes.

It does not tilt. It remains perfectly level.

But level ceases to mean obedience to one gravity.

The small bright sea rises in a thread no wider than a root hair. At the same moment, a thread of bronze water from the greater sea enters the bowl. They wind around each other. Neither mixes. Neither withdraws.

A silver animal approaches.

From outside the bowl, a bell swimmer approaches.

They pause on opposite sides of a distinction that is no longer a sealed wall.

The silver animal opens its mouth.

The bell swimmer pulses once.

No sound is heard.

Much later, a low note moves through the entire basin.

Every ribbon turns edgewise. Glasswood chambers ring without sound. Sleeping herds soften into motion. The threadlike creature alters its route, and its reflected elder follows.

The note means neither danger nor shore.

It means:

**There is a way to meet that is not conquest.**

Only then does the benevolent power attend to the beings caught by migrating depths.

It does not give them back their unrealized lives. Wisdom cannot make innocence from experience, and kindness does not pretend that loss has not occurred.

Instead, wherever an abyss leaves a creature exhausted in possibility—unable to flower, molt, divide, or choose a new direction—the power leaves one small interval unaged.

Not youth.

Not restoration.

One choice.

A glasswood trunk, slumping into gel, may choose the shape of a pool and reflect something younger than itself.

A ribbon that can no longer change color may release its pattern as bell swimmers.

A walker whose hollow has narrowed almost to nothing may choose to become a chamber, or a path, or no enclosure at all.

A mountain flattened beyond all memory of height may choose where a single pale stone rises from its plain.

The choice can be refused.

Many refuse it.

The power honors this. Some beings are finished, and completion is not despair.

The walker beside the bowl keeps its choice for a very long time.

Meanwhile, the figures beneath the hard sun continue to look up. Their instruments improve. They see forests where once they saw darkness. They detect warm water where no water can be suspended. They argue whether the thumb-sized surface is a window, a world, or an error.

One day, a figure comes alone.

It carries no instrument.

It kneels beside the foreign bowl and places an empty hand upon the dry stone.

The walker sees this.

It dismantles its borrowed body at the edge of sight and enters the new boundary as a hollow alone. It does not cross into the dry world. The kneeling figure does not cross into the sea.

Between them, the bowl becomes level in both places.

For a moment, each feels the conditions of the other.

The figure feels warm water in its lungs but does not drown. It feels distances opening in darkness, mountains possessing their own down, memories ripening in vines, and a patience in stone vast enough to make civilizations seem like brief animal forms.

The walker feels weight choosing one direction. It feels sunlight arrive from a single star. It feels air without remembered enclosure and hears, with terror and wonder, a nearby sound occur only once and at the moment it is made.

Neither world yields.

Neither remains wholly unimaginable.

The figure lifts its hand.

The walker withdraws.

The boundary closes, but no longer as a prison. It can open again if both sides tend the distinction.

Its work complete, the benevolent power does not remain as ruler, guardian, or god. Dependence would be another kind of enclosure. It removes its attention so gently that the amber beads never stop climbing.

It leaves no commandment.

It leaves only the learned possibility:

that unlike waters may touch without one ending the other;

that what is ancient need not be corrected merely for being strange;

that mercy may preserve a boundary, open it, or allow it to close;

and that even in a world where repetition makes things real, something may happen for the first time.

The walker returns to the chamber.

Its long-traveling name has finally come home as a note through the basin.

After hearing it, the walker uses its one unaged choice.

It widens the hollow where its face might have been.

Not into a mouth.

Into a doorway.
