At first the country appears empty: a white-gold upland under an immense, hard blue sky. Distance has almost no haze. Ranges two hundred kilometers away stand sharp enough to show their broken terraces, and beyond them rise further ranges, pale as old bone. The whole land is high—a continental table lifted above most of the atmosphere, broad enough to generate its own winds and cast a planetary shadow at dawn.

There are no rivers, lakes, springs, or buried aquifers. Almost no water exists here even as frost. The exposed rock has been dry for ages, and its minerals have settled into forms that would collapse in a wetter world: powder-soft chlorides, porous towers of oxide, translucent veins of salts that bend sunlight into muted rainbows. Hollows are filled not with sand but with tiny hollow crystals. When the wind crosses them, they roll without touching one another, driven by static charge.

Yet the land is lush.

Life gathers in widely separated provinces. Between them lie tens or hundreds of kilometers of bare plateau, but each living province is profuse: low forests of mineral-fed fronds, braided mats, and branching crowns packed so thickly that the ground disappears beneath them. From a distance these growths resemble dark islands scattered across a bright, petrified sea.

The dominant plants are not green. Some are black-violet, absorbing the fierce high-altitude light; others are silver on their upper surfaces and deep red beneath. Their tissues contain little hydrogen. Instead of watery sap, narrow channels carry a slow ionic fluid—an oily eutectic of salts and carbon compounds that remains mobile through the cool days and colder nights. Flexible membranes are made from interlocking mineral-organic scales. Torn edges do not bleed; they tarnish, seal, and begin growing crystalline scar tissue.

A mature crownplant may spread fifty meters while standing no taller than a person. Its central knot is ancient, dark, and dense, but its outer branches continually divide into fans as thin as foil. These fans turn throughout the day, not tracking the sun directly but adjusting to electrical gradients in the air. At noon entire groves incline together. The motion passes over them in long, deliberate waves, though the air may be still.

Elsewhere, the vegetation rises into sparse towers. These are fluted columns with hundreds of perforated vanes, their roots sunk deep into chemically rich fault lines. They feed partly on light and partly on differences in charge between buried strata and the open atmosphere. During periods of high sky-electricity, faint violet streamers comb their tips. No thunder follows. The atmosphere is too thin and dry to carry such violence well.

The terrain bears the composure of immense age. Nothing looks newly broken. Cliffs have retreated into stepped amphitheaters; pinnacles have been worn into leaning blades; old impact basins are softened into rings so broad they are mistaken for horizons. Erosion continues everywhere, but it is no longer sculpting a young world. It refines established forms.

Wind does most of the work. Daylight heats the bright flats unevenly, setting enormous, slow circulations across the plateau. Fine crystal dust streams around obstacles and polishes their windward faces to a dull sheen. At night the upper air drains from the ranges in cold, shallow sheets. Temperature changes prise layers from the rock one transparent flake at a time. Some salts creep under their own weight, causing entire hillsides to sag over millennia like wax without ever melting.

There is weather, although there are no clouds in the familiar sense. Pale atmospheric veils form when volatile minerals sublime from sunlit basins. They spread as luminous bands, sometimes covering regions the size of seas. Beneath them the light turns pearl-white and shadows vanish. In colder seasons the vapors settle directly onto the land as delicate chemical bloom: needles, feathers, and branching crusts that last until the sun reaches them.

These depositions are the great pulse of the ecosystem. For several days after a settling, dormant growths awaken across the open ground. Tiny black rosettes uncurl from fissures. Threads race over the new crust, dissolving selected compounds and leaving others behind in concentric stains. Vast flats may darken overnight, not densely but in constellations—each patch isolated, each intensely alive. When the bloom sublimates again, the threads retract into mineral nodules and the plateau returns to apparent barrenness.

The air is usually quiet. Wind at this pressure is more visible than audible. One sees dust lifting from a ridge before hearing the faint hiss of it. Groves make occasional dry ticks as their vanes flex and lock into new positions. In the cold before sunrise, contracting salt beds produce soft, widely spaced knocks from beneath the ground. Each sound is small, but distance carries it. A single cracking ledge can seem to answer itself from mountains far beyond sight.

Animal life is uncommon enough that its signs are events.

The most visible creatures are high gliders, though one may travel for weeks without seeing one. They are broad, nearly weightless sheets with narrow bodies along the leading edge. Rising air supports them despite the thin atmosphere. They circle above active groves, feeding on clouds of reproductive dust and on the minute organisms that drift with it. Their undersides flash mirror-bright when they bank. They rarely beat their wings; movement here favors patience and scale.

On the ground live slow grazers armored in overlapping ceramic plates. They follow the margins of the black-violet mats, shaving away only the newest growth. A grazer may spend its entire life crossing a single grove. It navigates by electric fields and freezes when the sky charge changes, sometimes remaining motionless for days. Predators are rarer still: low, many-jointed forms that conceal themselves among eroded slabs and detect the faint current of a moving body through the mineral crust. A pursuit, when it occurs, is almost silent and may unfold over hours.

Reproduction throughout the uplands depends less on seasons than on atmospheric cycles. When the pressure falls, crownplants release weightless flakes containing dormant tissue. The flakes rise into the continental circulation and may remain aloft for years. Most settle on sterile stone and become nothing. A few lodge in a chemically suitable crack. There they unfold a dark film no larger than a fingernail and begin the century-long process of making another island of life.

Fire is impossible in the ordinary sense, but there are analogues. Certain groves occasionally enter runaway oxidation during bright, electrically active weather. No flame appears. The affected vegetation whitens from within, gives off a dim blue radiance, and collapses into hot, brittle lace. The reaction advances slowly enough to walk beside. It can consume a forest over several months, after which the enriched ground supports a flush of new species. From orbit, these events would resemble cold stars moving across the plateau.

Nothing here has been built. There are no paths except the temporary trails of grazers, no walls except tilted strata, no markers except living stains and wind-carved stone. Some formations look artificial at first: fields of identical hexagonal pillars, long rows of hollow arches, basins paved in perfect polygons. But repetition is simply one of the habits of the minerals. The land needs no maker to produce order.

Brightness governs everything. At midday the exposed flats are nearly unbearable to look upon, while the living islands drink the light and appear as holes cut into the world. At sunset the high ranges remain illuminated long after the lower basins have gone violet. Their mineral faces kindle one by one—amber, rose, cold green—according to composition. The glow withdraws upward until only the remotest summits remain, suspended like narrow embers above the dark.

Then the plateau cools rapidly. Fronds fold. Ionic fluids thicken in their channels. Gliders descend to cling beneath overhangs. Across the vast spaces between groves, crystal grains realign with tiny, almost inaudible clicks.

The country seems stillest at night, but it is never inactive. Charge moves through stone. Vapors migrate from one basin to another. Roots advance along buried seams. Old cliffs loosen grain by grain. Life waits, spreads, consumes, and retreats according to rules written not in rain and soil, but in light, mineral difference, altitude, and the patient electricity of a dry world.
