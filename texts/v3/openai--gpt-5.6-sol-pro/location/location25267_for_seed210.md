The place occupies a shallow basin near the top of a mountain, though “near the top” is misleading. There is no visible summit above it and no slope descending away. The basin is simply high: the air is thin, the clouds pass beneath the rim, and distant thunder can sometimes be seen flashing in the low country without ever being heard.

It is not large. From any point, the whole boundary can nearly be made out—a ring of black, sharp-edged stone perhaps half a kilometer across. The enclosing rock looks newly split. Its faces have not softened into scree or soil; fractures retain the pale brightness of fresh wounds. Yet lichens, mosses, liverworts, and glossy mountain ferns cover much of the basin floor. They do not appear to have colonized slowly. They seem to have arrived already mature, fitted precisely into the fissures as if the vegetation and the stone had broken open together.

Water lies everywhere without forming a proper lake. It gathers in long, dark seams between slabs, beads on leaves, and trembles beneath skins of moss. The rock walls weep continuously. Droplets hang from their overhangs in fixed silver rows. Some fall, but each falling drop remains at the same height: a narrow column of identical droplets, each occupying the next instant of the same descent. Where these columns meet the ground, the water dimples without spreading.

The atmosphere condenses on anything raised above the vegetation. Mist forms in clear, round-edged volumes, not drifting banks: one over a stand of horsetails, another around a naked basalt tooth, another suspended knee-high across an open patch of stone. Passing into one is like stepping through cold breath. Passing out leaves no moisture that was not already there.

The light has no identifiable source. It is a dim bluish-gray, sufficient to reveal surfaces but too weak to cast ordinary shadows. Instead, shadows occur sparsely and independently. A shadow shaped like a branch may lie across bare rock where no tree grows. Beneath some stones there is no darkness at all, only the same low light continuing under them. The wet surfaces occasionally shine with a hard white glare suggestive of arc lamps, though there are no lamps.

There are no structures, no roads, no cut marks, no tools, and no debris of habitation. Nothing here has been assembled.

Nevertheless, the basin is industrial.

The machinery is biological, geological, and atmospheric, though none of those words fully contains it. Along the northern wall, tall red-stemmed plants rise at wide intervals, each bearing a crown of stiff translucent leaves. Their stems contain pistons: pale cartilage rods moving within lubricated chambers. The pistons cycle with a slow, irresistible force, drawing mineral water from the rock and compressing it into luminous sap. Every seventh stroke vents a fan of cold vapor through pores under the leaves. The vapor never accumulates. Each fan remains briefly visible—or perhaps permanently visible—and the air receives no more of it.

In the marshy center, broad mats of moss operate like conveyor belts. Their thousands of cilia travel in coordinated waves, carrying black grains of basalt from one side of a pool to the other. The grains vanish beneath a root mass and emerge where they began, sharp and unworn. Beetles patrol the moving moss in evenly spaced lines. Their wing cases are iron-colored, their joints click, and the underside of each animal glows the dull orange of a banked furnace. They carry nothing. They inspect everything.

The stone itself performs heavier work. Deep below the floor, immense rotational forces turn without shifting any material. Through the soles of the feet one can feel gears engaging: a clean sequence of impacts, followed by a sustained vibration, followed by the release of pressure. There can be no actual gear train under the basin—the mountain is solid, and fissures reveal only young basalt threaded with pale crystals—but the cycles continue. Sometimes circular outlines appear on the wet rock, interlocking shadows of toothed wheels several meters across. Ferns pass through these outlines without bending.

The sound is considerable. It does not rise or fade.

There is the deep engine beat beneath the ground. There is the synchronized ticking of the beetles. There is the papery rattle of horsetail segments, the hydraulic cough of the red piston-plants, and a distant metallic shriek like a rail wheel held forever at the same curve. Water contributes a dense, close hiss. Wings make a continuous electrical hum among the ferns. Every noise is loud enough to demand notice, but because none varies, the ear gradually mistakes the total for silence. A spoken word seems violently new here. Its echo is not repeated; it is flattened at once into the standing mechanical chord.

Animals are abundant, though the basin initially appears sparsely inhabited. They are distributed with too much care. A white, six-legged mountain hare sits in each of several open spaces, always the same distance from its neighbors. Small slate-colored birds cling to the cliffs, one bird per vertical crack. Glassy frogs occupy the pools. Long-bodied salamanders lie beneath the moss with external gills spread like crimson wiring. Clouds of gnats hang over the water, but each cloud is compact and separate, leaving wide corridors of empty air between.

Nothing hunts, flees, feeds, courts, ages, or sleeps. Yet none of the animals is dead or even still.

The hares breathe. Their noses move. One forepaw begins to lift and completes no motion, while also already resting on the ground. The birds hold their wings open against the rock and produce a harsh, turbine-like chorus without opening their beaks. Frogs repeatedly inflate their translucent throats; inside each throat, a tiny landscape appears—a second basin, containing smaller frogs whose throats contain the basin again.

Predators are present as well, but predation has been converted into a stable relationship. A narrow silver fox stands with its jaws around the neck of a hare. The hare is uninjured. The fox bites perpetually; the hare escapes perpetually; neither state displaces the other. Elsewhere, a dragonfly hangs above a gnat, its wings a blur with the texture of corrugated metal. The distance between them is less than a finger’s width and cannot be crossed.

The ecology is not a cycle but a set of simultaneous operations. Leaves produce oxygen and consume it in equal measure. Roots split stone, while the stone remains freshly unbroken. Water evaporates into mist and condenses back into the same pools without changing their depth. Spores burst from capsules but never depart; viewed closely, each spore contains the intact capsule from which it is emerging.

Growth is lush but not crowded. Plants rise in discrete islands separated by wet black pavement. A fern colony may be extravagant—fronds layered shoulder-high, fiddleheads thick as wrists—then end at a precise edge beyond which there is three meters of naked stone. A single miniature conifer stands alone near the basin’s eastern margin. It bears cones, new green tips, bare winter twigs, and frost simultaneously on different sides, though there are no seasons here. Its roots are exposed and razor-new. Fine root hairs enter cracks that have not yet opened.

The whole basin gives the impression of having just emerged, violently and recently, into a condition that has always existed.

At the southern rim, the rock rises into five thin fins. They are so sharp that mist divides around them. No erosion has rounded their edges, yet water has been running down them for an immeasurable duration. Between the fins are views that do not agree. One gap shows cloud below. Another shows the same basin from above, including the observer as a small dark figure. A third looks into a brightly lit factory interior extending beyond sight: gantries, belts, pressure vessels, ranks of lamps. No workers move there, and no object in it can be distinguished from an organ, shell, seedpod, or bone. Looking away does not remove the factory’s rhythmic glare from the eye.

There is no passage through these openings. A hand extended between the stone fins meets cool, mossy ground, as if reaching downward into the basin itself.

The local rules are few and absolute.

Nothing accumulates. Nothing diminishes. No edge dulls. No pool deepens. No creature completes an action. Causes are permitted, effects are permitted, but transition between them is optional. Machinery operates without production; ecology flourishes without change. Damage can be seen but not inflicted. Rain exists throughout the basin as the condition immediately after rain, although rain itself never falls.

An intruder may move, but the place does not register the movement in the usual way. Footprints appear in wet moss before the foot descends and vanish at the moment pressure is applied. Brushed leaves are already upright. Water closes before it is parted. The basin admits novelty only as a temporary contradiction.

Stay long enough and the body begins to be assigned a function. Breath falls into step with the underground engine. The pulse takes on the seven-stroke rhythm of the piston-plants. Condensation gathers in perfect beads along eyelashes. Small iron beetles arrange themselves at regular intervals nearby, waiting to inspect whatever process has begun.

The most unsettling thing is not that the landscape seems alive, nor that it resembles a factory without having been built. It is that the factory appears to be manufacturing the same instant in which it stands: rock still sharp from emergence, leaves forever wet, animals abundant and unspent, every engine at full operation, every product returned untouched to its beginning.

High above the clouds, in cool dimness, the basin continues to make itself.

It has never made anything else.
