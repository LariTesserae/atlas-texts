### The Under-Court Reservoir

The place lies beneath a paved courtyard, directly under the feet of a town that seldom thinks about it. It is not a cavern and was not hollowed from natural stone. Every surface has been placed by hand: fired brick, lime mortar, beaten clay, ceramic pipe, wedges of dark timber swollen tight by water. Even the floor’s apparent ridges are built forms—low settling steps intended to catch grit before it reaches the outflow.

The reservoir is small enough to understand almost at once. From one end, through the clear water, the opposite wall is visible perhaps thirty paces away. Yet immersion distorts that intimacy. Distances swell. Corners withdraw into trembling brightness. A waist-high partition becomes a submerged escarpment; a drainage trench resembles a narrow ravine. There is no open air within the main chamber. Water fills it to the shallow vault, occupying every recess except for small silver pockets trapped beneath the bricks.

The water is warm, held near the temperature of skin. Above, the courtyard stones bake in the sun, and their heat passes downward through the roof. Before entering, water also travels through shallow black-clay channels exposed on the upper terraces. By the time it descends, it has lost the chill of the spring. The reservoir feels less like a well than an enormous bath concealed inside the masonry.

Brightness enters through nine vertical light shafts. These are simple constructions, no more than plastered wells capped at ground level with thick panes of pale cast glass. Sunlight drops through them in white columns. Underwater, each column broadens and shakes. Reflections travel across the ceiling in frantic nets, making the whole chamber brighter than its buried position should allow. At midday the glazed brick floor can be almost painful to look at. Blue, yellow, and chalk-white tiles burn beneath the moving water.

The builders used color to make inspection easier. Cracks show against white plaster. Sediment shows against blue glaze. Green growth shows against yellow tile. Nothing is decorative without also being useful, though the repeating comb marks in the mortar and the stamped rosettes on the pipe collars reveal the habits of individual hands.

The construction is recent. Corners remain sharp. Tool marks have not softened. The stair-stepped floor has clean lips, and the lime skin on the walls is still smooth except where maintenance poles have struck it. There are no ancient mineral curtains, no rounded channels, no deep stains. Only thin cream-colored rims have begun to form around the inflow mouths. Fine silt gathers in the first settling trough, but has not yet accumulated enough to hide the tile beneath.

Water enters high along the eastern wall through three clay pipes. Each produces a dense, muscular jet full of white bubbles. The streams strike angled slabs, divide, and are driven downward into the chamber. On the western side, water escapes through bronze-grated mouths near the floor. The difference in level between the hill cisterns and the lower baths keeps everything moving without engines. Wooden gates, lifted by ropes from the courtyard, determine which chamber receives the flow.

This makes the reservoir loud.

The sound is not the open splashing of a fountain. Underwater it is deeper and more bodily: a continuous grinding roar conducted through liquid, brick, and bone. Each pipe has its own pitch. The largest throbs slowly when its gate is only partly raised. The smallest hisses with trapped air. Somewhere behind the southern wall, a loose ceramic valve knocks in an irregular sequence—three quick blows, a pause, then one. When workers turn a gate above, the whole room complains. Pressure changes pass through it like distant thunder.

There are quieter sounds within the noise: grains of sand ticking over tile, bubbles snapping against the vault, timber braces creaking as they absorb and release the minute stresses of the filled chamber. A swimmer hears their own movement enlarged. A hand brushing the wall makes a long scrape. A kicked tile fragment can be followed by sound after it disappears from sight.

There are no fish, insects, shellfish, or worms. The water arrives through cloth filters and beds of crushed charcoal, then passes through copper-lined collars that discourage animal growth. Several times each season, the chamber is emptied, scrubbed with stiff reed brushes, and washed with lime. The reservoir is not an ecosystem accidentally enclosed; it is a machine for excluding one.

Even so, plant life finds narrow permissions.

Sparse threads of green cling to mortar seams beyond the reach of the cleaning poles. A little water moss grows in recessed maker’s marks. Near the light shafts, translucent films spread across a few upward-facing tiles, each no larger than a hand. Their presence is tolerated until they become slippery. They lift and flatten with the current, the only soft forms in a place of hard edges.

A cluster of pale reed shoots occupies one built pocket beneath the central shaft. It is not wild. The pocket is a shallow ceramic basin filled with baked clay pellets, installed to indicate water quality. If the reeds yellow, the keepers know that too much lime or copper has entered the system. Their leaves are thin from submersion and trail horizontally in the flow. They never break the surface because there is no surface here to reach.

The chamber’s “terrain” is hydraulic rather than geological. Its heights and hollows exist to persuade water into particular behavior. Ramps slow the current. Narrow slots accelerate it. Rows of upright ceramic fins strip bubbles from the incoming jets. Circular pits collect heavy grit. A central ridge divides fresh inflow from water already on its way toward the baths. Everything has a direction.

At intervals the system changes state. In the morning, when the public basins are filled, the western drains open and the current strengthens. The reed leaves lie flat. Sediment rolls along the troughs in faint brown ribbons. At noon, with the gates nearly balanced, the chamber becomes clearer and brighter; the turbulence relaxes into broad revolving cells. In the evening, the inflow is reduced, and warmth from the courtyard roof slowly spreads downward. During cleaning days the gates shut altogether, the water descends step by step, and the hidden terrain emerges wet and shining into air.

Maintenance is done with simple means. Workers lower weighted baskets to collect debris. They probe the outlets with hooked wooden poles. Divers carry stone scrapers, cord, wax, and small bronze knives, but no device more complex than a lever, pulley, plug, or gate. Repairs remain visible: a cracked pipe bound with fiber and pitch, a discolored rectangle where plaster has been renewed, a timber brace wedged against a wall until the next draining.

The reservoir has strict practical rules. Nothing loose is brought inside. No oil is used on ropes or fittings. Tools are tethered to the wrist. A diver never enters while a gate is being moved. The central grate is not approached during the morning draw. If the knocking valve stops suddenly, the chamber is evacuated—not because silence is ominous, but because it may mean the pipe has become completely blocked.

Nothing here is mysterious. Its beauty comes from ordinary forces confined and made legible: gravity, pressure, sunlight, heat, careful masonry. It is a bright underwater room with no horizon and almost no life, hidden inside a larger human construction. Water pours through it every hour, loud and warm, while above it sandals cross the courtyard and shadows move over the glass caps.

From the town, the place is only a system of covered openings and gate ropes. From within, it is an entire submerged landscape—new-edged, sun-filled, and perpetually passing through itself.
