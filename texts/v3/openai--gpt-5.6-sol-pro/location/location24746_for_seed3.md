The city stands where a mountain has not quite finished becoming a mountain.

From the low country it appears as a dark, discontinuous band near the upper limit of sight: towers, gantries, bridge-spans, and terraces distributed across several immense shelves of black stone. There is no single summit. The whole high region is a congregation of blunt peaks and unfinished ridges, their sides too fresh to have acquired valleys. Instead of being carved downward by rivers, the land seems to have risen around empty spaces, leaving hollows, slots, and long roofless chambers inside itself.

The rock is young. In places it is still warm beneath its cold skin. It has few cracks softened by age, few slopes of accumulated soil, and almost no gravel except around the city’s works. Great planes of basalt lie exposed as if poured last season. Pale mineral seams cross them without regard for depth or perspective. Some seams can be seen continuing through open air after the rock ends—thin luminous lines stretching across ravines, outlining strata that have not yet arrived.

Buildings are anchored to these lines whenever possible.

The oldest districts occupy broad ledges below the peaks. They are dense by mountain standards but strangely open for a city: a boiler house, then two hundred meters of wet stone; a row of workers’ dwellings clinging beneath an overhang; then a gap crossed only by pipes and cableways; then a market enclosed in a retired turbine hall. The city has spread regionally rather than continuously. Its neighborhoods are islands of brick, riveted iron, smoked glass, and poured slag, connected by steep railways and suspended roads.

Nothing is level for long. Streets become stairs, stairs become ladders, and ladders end at funicular platforms. Freight elevators travel not only upward and downward but obliquely through the mountain, emerging miles away on another face. Locals navigate by pressure, vibration, and bell sequences as much as by direction.

The machinery is everywhere, though much of it works at a nearly geological pace.

Deep-bore engines turn inside housings the size of civic buildings. Their flywheels make one revolution every several minutes. Piston fields push support columns into softening stone. Vast stamping mills compress loose mineral dust into blocks for future districts. Condensers gather moisture from clouds and exhalations, ticking steadily as droplets collect on copper fins. The city’s great pumps operate only intermittently, but when they wake, their strokes are heard from ridge to ridge: slow detonations followed by the groan of pipes filling.

Thus the place is loud without seeming hurried. Sound accumulates.

A hammer blow may travel through the young rock and return an hour later from an elevation that does not exist on any survey. Chains knock against pylons. Cable cars whine across abysses. Vent stacks breathe in low animal chords. Avalanches are uncommon because the mountains have not yet produced much rubble, but the shift of an entire stone shelf can generate a deep, sustained note that trembles in windows for days. Conversation pauses for these notes, not from alarm but courtesy.

The air is cool and thin. Sunlight rarely reaches the inhabited shelves directly. Clouds form below, among, and sometimes inside the streets, turning courtyards into dim chambers of moving gray. Moisture is present less as rain than as condensation: beads on railings, silver films over roofs, wet black walls, moss gathering beneath steam pipes. Actual rainfall comes in brief slanting curtains. Much of it vanishes before striking the stone, caught by nets stretched between towers or drawn sideways into ventilation mouths.

Water gathers in pockets rather than courses. There are cistern gardens, flooded lift shafts, rain bowls cut into plazas, and deep blue tarns occupying hollows in the raw basalt. No mature river has yet decided how to leave the heights. Temporary streams appear after storms and run for a few hundred meters before reaching an incomplete contour, where they may stop, climb, divide into hovering droplets, or flow into a seam in the air.

The city marks these unreliable waters with blue lamps.

Vegetation thrives where industry supplies what the mountain lacks. Ferns crowd around warm condensate valves. Tough, glossy-leaved shrubs take root in clinker heaps. Pale grasses grow from mortar joints and bend toward engine vibration rather than light. Along the lower ledges, orchards have been trained flat against heat-exchanger walls. Their trees bear small, dark fruit tasting of iron and frost.

There are also wild green pockets, unexpectedly lush: whole ravines filled with moss, giant saxifrages, and feathery plants whose fronds close around passing fog. These pockets are separated by expanses of bare stone. From a distance they resemble green stains on an enormous unfinished casting.

Animals are more numerous than people expect at such elevation. The mountain’s incomplete rules have left many niches unclaimed, and life has entered them rapidly.

Colonies of black-winged cliff birds nest on antenna arrays and warm smokestacks. Their calls mingle with warning sirens so perfectly that workers sometimes evacuate because of a courtship chorus. Long-legged goats walk the overhead pipes, their split hooves magnetized by minerals in the metal. Damp tunnels host translucent salamanders in such numbers that maintenance crews sweep them gently aside before opening valves. Moths with heavy fur gather around the city’s dim amber lights, while small silver insects drift in the open air above chasms as though standing on an invisible surface.

Larger animals descend out of the clouds at irregular intervals. They have the proportions of grazing beasts—broad backs, slow heads, pendant limbs—but they step on elevations rather than ground. A herd may cross between two districts hundreds of meters apart, placing its feet on absent slopes. Buildings briefly seen through their bodies appear older, weathered by centuries the city has not yet experienced. They are never hunted. Where they pause, rain collects.

At night, which is most of the time, the city is illuminated from within rather than above. Furnace doors open red in the gloom. Sodium lamps line rails and bridges. Green inspection lights move very slowly along the undersides of suspended factories. Windows glow through mist, scattered rather than continuous, making the metropolis appear less like a settlement than a constellation attached to the mountain.

Distance behaves poorly here. A tower visible across a ravine may require three days to reach, while a neighborhood beyond the horizon can sometimes be entered through the back room of a nearby station. Surveyors maintain competing maps: one for freight, one for pedestrians, one for birds, and one used only during rainfall. None is regarded as definitive. New streets are occasionally discovered with worn paving and functioning utilities, though no construction office claims them. Conversely, structures under active assembly may disappear from one side while remaining accessible from another.

This is not treated as magic. It is treated as a civil-engineering condition.

Every building is therefore constructed with at least one unnecessary door. Every bridge includes a spare span pointed toward open air. Pipes are fitted with blank junctions in case a district forms around them later. Foundations are laid not merely downward but into several possible versions of the rock. The city’s architects speak of probability loads, anticipated gravity, and retrospective occupancy. Building codes require staircases to be counted both from the top and from the bottom; if the totals agree, the staircase is closed until it becomes safer.

The mountain itself rises slowly enough that no one sees it move. Yet old thresholds sink relative to new streets, towers lean toward peaks that were absent when they were built, and rail lines must be extended each winter to keep pace with stations drifting apart. In the municipal archive, elevation is recorded by year rather than by height.

Erosion has barely begun. Wind tests the corners. Frost draws the first hairline fractures. Water experiments with channels, abandons them, and starts again. The city contributes more debris than nature: iron filings, brick dust, slag, lost bolts, worn cable strands. In some places these industrial sediments are the only true soil. Plants root in them, insects burrow through them, and birds carry them upward, beginning the long work of making the heights inhabitable.

Beyond the last terraces, the land remains open and severe. Scattered extractor towers stand among naked ridges, each surrounded by cages of pipework and a few low houses. Cable lines vanish into cloud. Reservoirs shine briefly in hollows. Herds move across impossible inclines. Farther still, the unfinished peaks continue assembling themselves in the dark.

The city listens to them.

Its bells are tuned to the tones of the rising stone. When a new ridge begins to form, the lowest bell in the nearest district answers. Factories reduce their speed. Animals grow quiet. Condensation trembles on every railing but does not fall.

Then, over weeks or months, a shape gathers beyond the lights: first a pressure in the fog, then a dark edge, then acres of wet, unweathered rock where there had only been sky.

Surveyors go out. Rails follow. Moss follows the rails. Birds arrive before the roofs.

And the city, without haste, gains another quarter.
