The city occupies a blister beneath the desert, not far below the surface. In places its ceilings are thin enough for the noon heat to pass through as a physical pressure. The rock overhead ticks, expands, and sheds flakes of mineral glaze. Nothing here is cool merely because it is underground.

It is a small city, bounded and comprehensible: one can walk from the sealed eastern gate to the western condensers in less than an hour. Yet within that enclosure everything is crowded. Buildings lean into alleys barely wider than a shoulder. Galleries cross above galleries. Pipes pass through walls without regard for rooms, then divide into bundles and vanish under stairs. Tanks, chimneys, cable trays, pressure vessels, inspection cages, ceramic ducts, and blocky habitation cells fill nearly every usable volume. The place feels less constructed than packed.

The cavern itself is young. Its walls retain the violent geometry of recent excavation: black ledges, angular cavities, and drill-cut faces still striped with pale scoring. There are few rounded corners. Water has not had time to soften anything. Where erosion has begun, it appears as small wounds—orange streaks beneath bolts, crumbling rims around steam vents, a thumb’s breadth of channel carved into fresh dust.

The city was built around heat.

At its center is a shaft lined with dark refractory brick. Warm air rises through it steadily, though the machinery below works only in intervals. Around the shaft stand foundries, kiln chambers, pump houses, and squat towers plated in oxidized metal. They are industrial rather than sophisticated: enormous flywheels, piston pumps, counterweighted valves, belt drives, ceramic insulators, banks of knife switches. Much of the mechanism is overbuilt and accessible. Every machine can be opened with a wrench. Every conduit bears painted arrows showing the direction of flow.

No one remains to read them.

There is no animal life in the city—not rats, not insects, not even the nearly invisible mites that once might have lived in stored grain. The heat may have killed them. The mineral air may never have permitted them. Whatever the reason, nothing breathes with lungs. No footsteps answer in adjacent streets; no wings disturb the lamps.

Still, the city is not still.

Heat-driven pumps wake without warning. A linkage lifts. A piston completes three slow strokes. Somewhere behind a wall, a chain advances by one link and stops. Louvers open as the stone warms, then close when the ceiling cools by a fraction. Pressure passes through the infrastructure like thought through an empty mind. The whole settlement continues to regulate itself according to schedules no longer synchronized with day or season.

Most of this activity is quiet. Machinery has been mounted on cork-black pads and hung within brick housings to prevent vibration from cracking the shallow roof. Sounds arrive separately, with long pauses between them: the soft knock of a valve; the contracting ping of sheet metal; a brief subterranean rush as vapor crosses a pipe bridge; grit whispering down a slanted roof. The loudest recurring noise is water, and even that seldom lasts.

Moisture comes intermittently from above. Rain on the desert is rare and violent. Hours or days later, it finds fractures in the overburden and enters the city as cold beads on the ceiling. The first drops vanish on hot stone. More follow, gathering along cables and iron braces, running toward designated gutters. Every surface seems angled to intercept them. Roofs exist indoors for this purpose, overlapping one another in steep metal scales. Alleys descend toward cistern mouths. Clothless laundry frames have been repurposed as condensation racks, strung with copper wire on which droplets form.

For a short while after rain, the city shines.

Water beads on black brick and turns dust into dark paste. Narrow channels fill ankle-deep, carrying flakes of rust and white salts toward the western tanks. Steam gathers under low arches. Glass gauges cloud from within. Then pumps begin to stir, and the brief abundance is separated, heated, filtered, lifted, and stored. Within a day the open channels are dry again. Only the cisterns remain damp, their interiors breathing faint mineral coolness through locked grilles.

Sparse vegetation persists around these pockets. It is not lush enough to soften the city. Pale, jointed stems rise from cracks near the reservoir district, each plant no larger than a hand broom. Their roots penetrate mortar and wrap themselves around sweating pipes. In the deepest condensation chambers, thin mats of green-black growth cling to ceramic tiles, glossy as paint. Elsewhere there are isolated fans of translucent leaves, fed by one reliable drip and illuminated from beneath by failing maintenance lamps.

These plants neither rustle nor flower. Pollination, if it occurs, is mechanical: spores and dust are drawn through ventilation ducts when the thermal draft reverses. Some stems have grown into the grillework and are cut back automatically by slow rotating blades. The clippings fall into dry collection trays and gradually char there. Thus the city maintains a meager garden without gardeners.

Light is mostly indirect. Narrow wells pierce the roof but admit little more than a scorched amber haze. At midday, bright lines appear high on certain walls, too intense to look at directly, while the streets beneath remain dim. Industrial lamps burn in cages along main routes, many of them orange with old filaments. Others pulse according to pressure rather than time: an entire lane may brighten when a boiler fills, then fade as the stored force is spent.

Because the cavern is shallow, the upper districts are hottest. Their ceilings buckle close over dense apartment blocks, workshops, and storage lofts. Doorways open onto rooms full of iron furniture and empty ceramic vessels. Paint has curled away in strips. Glass has sagged subtly in its frames. The lower streets, only a few dozen steps down, are cooler but more humid. Here the masonry sweats, and every metal surface carries a soft skin of corrosion.

The settlement’s boundaries are unmistakable. To the north, construction ends at a fresh fault: a wall of red-black stone with survey marks still visible. Several exploratory tunnels enter it and stop abruptly, their cutting teeth embedded in the face. To the south lies the Ash Gate, a broad freight incline sealed by fused sand. Beyond it is the surface, though hot wind occasionally forces a ribbon of dust through the joints. East and west are occupied by infrastructure—the intake galleries and the condensers—so the city cannot sprawl. It has grown inward instead, filling courtyards with sheds, bridging streets with ducts, dividing halls into smaller halls until space itself seems to have been consumed.

There are signs that this compression was intentional. Empty niches recur at regular intervals, always just large enough for a person to stand in. Stairways widen at landings where nothing has been installed. Tiny plazas surround capped wells. The city preserved these voids as carefully as it preserved pressure margins, perhaps because open space was considered another utility: a place for heat to collect, for vapor to turn, for crowds that no longer exist.

Its strangest law is revealed in the lowest quarter.

There, water does not always descend.

The bricks around the central shaft are porous and heavily salted. When the deep engines heat them, moisture creeps upward through the walls in branching, silver traces. Droplets form under balconies, climb bolts, and gather on the upper sides of pipes. For several hours the city’s dampness migrates against gravity, pulled by capillary channels and temperature. The western cisterns empty by fractions while roof basins fill. Then the engines cool, the salt crusts crack, and the water falls all at once in a brief indoor rain.

This reversal governs much of the surviving machinery. Cups tilt beneath rising films. Weighted floats release catches. Shutters close before the droplets descend. The city does not run on abundant water, but on the repeated relocation of a little water through great heat.

During the upward phase, everything seems to hold its breath. The dim streets become almost entirely silent. Condensation brightens the walls in fine branching maps. The pale plants stiffen toward the masonry. Pressure accumulates below.

Then a relay snaps.

Valves open throughout the settlement in sequence. Gutters begin to ring. Flywheels turn once or twice in their housings. Warm rain strikes roofs beneath the earth, and the empty city conducts its brief, intricate business.
