The country begins above the weather.

Its floor is a chain of black plateaus lifted so high that the lower world has disappeared beneath an unbroken pressure of cloud. From the plateau rims, the clouds resemble a dim, slow sea. They fill the intervening valleys to unknown depths, parting only when cold updrafts open brief shafts into the gulf. Across those openings one may glimpse other mesas—flat-backed, distant, and dark—standing like islands separated by hundreds of empty kilometers.

There is almost no sky. A ceiling of ash-colored vapor presses low over the summits, turning noon into a diluted twilight. At night the darkness is nearly material. It erases the horizon first, then distance, then the distinction between rock and open air.

The plateaus are ancient rather than ruined. Their cliffs have long since lost every unstable edge. Faces of dark basalt descend in immense, settled flutings, each rib rounded by ages of frost and thin chemical rain. Loose stone is rare. What could fall has fallen. What remains seems load-bearing on the scale of continents: broad shelves, deep sockets, columns thicker than hills. Erosion occurs here, but with the unhurried precision of something maintaining a finished form.

Water is never plentiful, yet almost everything is wet.

Moisture arrives sideways. Clouds strike the cliffs and comb themselves through the vegetation, leaving beads on every filament. Condensation gathers beneath overhangs, runs through hairline channels, and collects in pockets sunk into the basalt. These pools are narrow and black, some no larger than a footprint, others extending through chains of rock basins for several kilometers. They have no visible inflow. Their surfaces remain perfectly level even when the wind moves over them.

Rain comes infrequently and without force: a fine mineral drizzle that seems less to fall than to appear upon things. It glazes the ground, darkens trunks, and seals membranes. Within an hour the exposed stone is dry again, but water persists under leaves, inside hollow stems, and in deep cold reservoirs beneath the plateau.

The vegetation is luxuriant, though little of it rises high.

Across open ground spreads a dense skin of silver-black moss, jointed creepers, low fans, and thick folded rosettes. The plants overlap in layers like insulating cloth. Their outer surfaces are waxy and almost colorless; underneath they are richly pigmented—violet, arterial red, the green of deep seawater. When wind lifts their edges, these hidden colors flash and vanish.

Trees occur only along cracks where warm vapor seeps upward. They are widely spaced and enormous. Each stands alone in several square kilometers of low growth, with a swollen, buttressed base anchored directly into fractured basalt. Instead of branches, the trunk divides into arching ribs that carry curtains of fine black foliage. The leaves hang vertically to gather mist and avoid the cold radiance from above. Drops travel down them in orderly lines, all converging upon cavities around the roots.

Much of the lushness is concealed below the visible surface. The plateau’s living mat may be only a handspan high, but it continues downward through fissures for hundreds of meters. Roots, fungal cords, translucent storage organs, and slow muscular vines occupy the stone’s interior. In places the cliffs split open into vertical gardens: vast walls furred with pale fronds, flowering nodules, and dark sacs that inflate whenever warm gases rise from below.

There are no shelters, roads, walls, ruins, tools, or marks of deliberate shaping. Nothing has been built here.

Nevertheless, the land is industrial.

Under the moss, enormous biological systems carry out processes with the regularity of engines. Root valves open and close in sequence. Mineral brines are pumped upward through capillary towers. Colonies of heat-making organisms oxidize compounds drawn from the rock, warming whole tracts of ground by a few crucial degrees. Pressure accumulates in buried chambers and is released through vents miles away. Vast networks separate metals from groundwater, plate them onto internal membranes, and pass electric charge between groves.

No individual organism directs this activity. The plateau itself behaves like a distributed factory whose machinery grew in darkness. Its products are heat, stored salts, complex spores, and the faint electrical field that makes exposed hair rise along the cliff rims.

At intervals, a deep cycle reaches completion.

The ground tightens almost imperceptibly. Millions of leaf-fans fold at once. Condensation trembles in the basins, though there has been no sound. Then vents along the plateau edge exhale narrow plumes of warm vapor into the abyss. The plumes descend rather than rise, weighted with mineral dust and reproductive cells. Far below, hidden in the cloud, something receives them. Several hours later a blue flicker travels through the root network from the cliffs inward, and the plants reopen.

This is the nearest the country comes to illumination. There are other lights, but none is generous. Tiny lamps move beneath translucent leaves. Rings of cold green appear around certain pools. During electrical storms, branching lines shine inside the trunks of the solitary trees, revealing their internal conduits as if the trees were diagrams of themselves. The flashes make no thunder.

Animals are abundant, yet the landscape looks empty.

Most live within the vegetative mat or migrate through the cliffs. Small six-limbed grazers push tunnels beneath the moss, leaving slow ripples on the surface. Flat, eyeless creatures cling to the undersides of leaves and drink collected condensation without puncturing the plant. Pale burrowers tend heat colonies around the roots, carrying pellets of reactive stone in their throat pouches. Their bodies are insulated by hollow quills so fine that they resemble frost.

Larger animals cross the open plateaus one at a time. They are tall and almost weightless in appearance, with narrow torsos suspended between long, many-jointed legs. Broad pads distribute their mass over the living ground. They stop for hours beside the black pools, lowering branching mouthparts to the water but never visibly drinking. Sometimes their reflections continue to bend after they have raised their heads.

Predators descend from the dark ceiling. They possess no wings in the ordinary sense; instead they spread ribbed membranes that couple to rising currents of charged air. They can remain motionless over a valley for days. When they strike, there is no cry or rush of feathers—only the sudden compression of vegetation and, afterward, a trail of droplets moving uphill across the leaves.

The most numerous creatures are seen only during the brief migrations that follow condensation events. Then the emptiness changes all at once. Fissures pour out streams of black-bodied animals, each small as a seed, traveling in braided columns toward newly filled basins. Threadlike fliers rise from the cliff gardens and drift between plateaus. Silent herds emerge from cloud-filled saddles. Within a few hours the surfaces teem with feeding, mating, pursuit, and dispersal. By the next dark interval, almost everything has withdrawn underground or over the edge, and the plateau again appears uninhabited.

Silence governs the place, but it is not inactivity. Sound is suppressed by the moss, the fog, and the low pressure of the heights. Footfalls vanish. Water enters pools without splashing. Leaves flex without rustling. Even falls from the cliffs seem to be swallowed before they can echo.

What remains are intimate noises: a droplet ticking inside a hollow stem; the faint crease of a membrane unfolding; ice expanding in a rock seam; the nearly inaudible clicking of root valves underfoot. Once or twice in a long span, a vibration passes through the basalt too low to hear. Every creature becomes still while it moves beneath them. The pools dimple from below. The solitary trees lower their curtains of leaves.

Then activity resumes.

The cold is severe but strangely selective. Exposed skin, open water, and bare stone freeze quickly. Within the vegetation, temperatures differ from patch to patch. A traveler could pass in a few steps from brittle frost into humid warmth, then into a zone so cold that vapor crystallizes in midair. These thermal boundaries migrate according to subterranean cycles. Animals follow them. Plants fold away from them. Narrow bands of thaw move across the plateau like invisible rivers, carrying entire ecologies in their wake.

Nothing here wastes matter. Bodies are dismantled within hours, not by visible decay but by coordinated extraction. Moss closes over the dead. Fungal threads remove soft tissue, mineral organisms take the bones, and charged roots draw off the remaining salts. By the next cycle there is no carcass, only a temporary bloom of dark red leaves.

The plateaus obey other rules as well. Water sometimes climbs porous stone toward a colder surface. Certain shadows remain fixed while the clouds move. Distances across open ground change with the electrical state of the root network: a solitary tree that appears an hour away may require three days to reach, while another on the far rim may stand suddenly near after a blue pulse passes beneath the moss. Migrating animals seem to understand these contractions. They wait for the land to become shorter.

At the outermost cliffs, vegetation hangs downward into the cloud sea in cables kilometers long. Whole ecosystems travel along them: grazers descending, predators waiting at knots, colonies opening their luminous cups in the warmer depths. The cables vanish into grayness, tensing periodically as though caught by tides below.

No one on the plateaus can see what anchors them.

In the longest darkness, when the cloud ceiling meets the cloud sea and even the mesas disappear, the living machinery enters its busiest phase. Heat wells open. Hidden fluids circulate. Pollinators move in the cliff fissures. Predators glide between invisible thermals. Root systems exchange charges across the gulf by means of drifting spore clouds.

From any single point, the country appears vacant: one immense tree, one black pool, a low field fading into darkness.

Beneath that emptiness, and beyond it, the entire high world is working.
