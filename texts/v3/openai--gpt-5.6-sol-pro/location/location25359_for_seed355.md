The city occupies a shallow basin where the land has almost forgotten how to rise. Its outer districts dissolve into salt flats, warm marsh, and sheets of black water no deeper than a hand. There is no horizon in the ordinary sense. Ground and sky meet through layers of vapor, each reflecting the other so imperfectly that distance becomes difficult to measure. A tower two streets away may appear beyond the basin; a far embankment may seem close enough to touch.

It is always night here, though not always the same night.

The sky is usually a dense, unlit overcast, low enough to turn the upper stories of buildings indistinct. Sometimes the cloud ceiling has texture—long ribs, overlapping scales, the pleats of an enormous dark curtain—but no wind appears to move it. At other times there is no visible sky at all, only a black vertical continuation above the rooftops. Light comes weakly from standing water, from mineral films on walls, and from pale seams that open without warning between stones. These illuminations are not bright. They reveal edges, wet surfaces, and the undersides of arches while leaving open spaces untouched.

Heat gathers at ground level. It rises from the pavement and from the old masonry in slow breaths, carrying smells of brine, iron, soaked plaster, hot clay, and something faintly medicinal. Rain falls often but does not cool the city. The drops arrive warm, sometimes nearly hot, and strike the streets with a soft granular hiss. Afterward, condensation forms on every surface, including surfaces facing downward. Water beads beneath ledges, under bridges, along the ceilings of abandoned halls. It hangs there in trembling rows until, instead of falling, it draws itself sideways into cracks.

The streets were laid out with urban certainty: broad avenues, radial plazas, drainage channels, retaining walls, stairways, courtyards, arcades, and long blocks divided into narrow chambers. Yet there is no evidence that tools made any of it. The city has no chisel marks, no joints, no nails, no hinges, no glass, no fired brick. Towers and walls are continuous with the bedrock below them. Doorways have the rounded smoothness of cavities left by dissolved minerals. Colonnades resemble the ribs of eroded caverns. The paving is a single dark stratum, folded upward at the curbs as if the whole street had once been soft.

It may be less a city built on the basin than a pattern into which the basin weathered.

The architecture has long since settled into equilibrium with water. Roofs sag exactly enough to collect shallow pools, but never collapse. Channels have worn through plazas in branching paths, polished by centuries of runoff. Building fronts lean over the avenues until their upper edges almost meet, creating deep corridors of shade within shade. In the lowest wards, whole streets lie under warm, still water. Their facades continue below the surface, doorway after doorway descending farther than the basin should permit.

There are no animals. No insects stipple the pools. No fish disturb submerged rooms. No birds pass above the roofs, and no shells, bones, tracks, nests, webs, or droppings suggest that they ever did. The absence is so complete that certain shapes in the city seem to have developed in compensation. Gutters curve like sleeping serpents. Drain mouths resemble open jaws. Rows of stones along the floodwalls have the spacing of vertebrae. None of them move when watched.

Vegetation is scarce and widely separated. In cracks where rainwater remains for days, low mats of black-green tissue spread no wider than a plate. They have no leaves. They tighten into glossy knots during rainfall and unfurl only when the pavement begins to dry. Farther out, at the marsh edge, stand occasional reedlike growths made of translucent tubes. Each patch may be hundreds of paces from the next. Their roots descend into the warm mud, while their hollow stems collect condensation from the air. They never sway together. Even in stillness, one stem at a time bends toward some distant district, as if indicating a route.

The largest vegetation grows in enclosed courtyards: pale, columnar organisms with mineral skins, indistinguishable at first from architectural supports. Water circulates visibly inside them. A slow dark pulse rises through their cores, pauses near the top, and returns downward. They produce neither flower nor fruit. Over decades they add thin layers of stone to themselves, gradually sealing windows and narrowing passages. Some buildings may be groves that have become masonry. Some groves may be buildings that have begun to live.

Rain is the city’s chief activity, but not its only one.

Water migrates continually through the basin. It seeps upward between paving slabs, crosses streets against the slope, enters dry wells, and emerges days later from high walls. Pools vanish without evaporation. Elsewhere, empty rooms fill from the corners inward, the water maintaining vertical faces until the chamber is occupied. Canals reverse direction at irregular intervals. Their surfaces remain level while their currents climb steps and pass through closed stone.

Erosion is everywhere, though little is being destroyed. The place has been weathered into stable forms. Corners have softened; thresholds are hollowed; rain has drawn deep fluting down every exposed facade. But decay has reached a kind of maturity. Material removed from one district appears to accumulate in another. A wall thins in the eastern quarter while a new wall, damp and seamless, thickens in the west. Silt gathered from a flooded avenue hardens overnight into stairs. When those stairs are climbed, they may lead to the same avenue before the silt was disturbed.

This is one of the city’s quieter failures of reality.

Others are more difficult to ignore.

Reflections do not consistently belong to what stands above them. A shallow puddle may show a courtyard roofed in stars, though the actual courtyard is enclosed and the sky has none. A canal may reflect towers absent from the shore. If approached, those reflected towers lengthen downward into the water rather than coming nearer. Sometimes a personless street appears in every pool for several nights: colonnades, a low arch, three pale plants, rain falling upward. There is no known location in the basin that matches it, yet water draining from the pools carries reddish dust from its paving.

Distances change most often after rain. An avenue that normally crosses six blocks may take hours to walk. A narrow service passage may open directly onto the marsh beyond the outer wall, bypassing half the city. These distortions are not violent. The architecture does not twist or shimmer. It remains heavy, wet, and convincing. Only the relation of one thing to another has altered.

Interiors are less dependable than streets. A chamber may have more corners than its exterior permits. Two doors in neighboring walls may open onto the same room from opposite ends. Basements descend into humid spaces where the pressure of unseen water can be felt in the ears, yet the floor remains dry. In some buildings, rain can be heard beneath the ground, falling through immense buried plazas.

Sound rarely carries far. Most of the city is occupied by soft water-noise: dripping, seepage, the faint drawing-back of mud, rain brushing stone, occasional heavy drops striking flooded courtyards. Each sound seems isolated from the others. There is no general murmur, no blended ambience. A drip ten paces away can be heard with perfect clarity, while a cascade at the end of the street may be silent.

At intervals, the basin produces a low resonance. It is not quite a tone. It resembles pressure becoming audible—a deep, brief trembling that passes through foundations and warm water alike. During it, condensation shivers on the walls. The sparse reeds incline toward the city center. Doorways darken. Then the vibration ceases, leaving no echo.

The central district surrounds a depression that might once have been a civic plaza. Wide steps descend from all sides into a circular expanse of water. The water is perfectly black and warmer than the rain. No debris floats on it. The surrounding buildings are low, broad, and almost entirely eroded shut, their entrances reduced to narrow vertical seams.

The pool has no fixed depth. Stone poles lowered into it strike bottom almost immediately, yet submerged stairs can be seen descending for hundreds of levels. Their landings are lined with the facades of another city, inverted beneath the first. At times dim lights move through those lower streets—not carried lights, not living ones, but bands of pallor traveling from window to window as if dawn were passing horizontally through rooms.

There is never any dawn above.

The place has rules, though they are local and prone to migration. Water remembers shape better than stone does. Enclosures become larger when unobserved. Nothing falls in precisely the direction expected twice. A route remains reliable only while rain continues. Names, if given to districts, begin to describe their weather instead: the place called the Dry Arcade floods; the southern reservoir appears in the north; the street named for stillness develops a current.

Above all, emptiness is preserved. However densely the streets and buildings gather, they do not produce the feeling of occupation. Plazas are too broad for their entrances. Windows are spaced too far apart. Whole neighborhoods contain only one standing structure, surrounded by acres of wet paving. The city seems organized around absences—carefully measured spaces left for inhabitants that never existed, or that exist elsewhere in the layout, displaced from their own rooms.

Beyond the last floodwall, the basin opens into marsh and dark mineral flats. Warm water lies just under the crust. Each footstep would bring a brief gloss to the surface, though there are no tracks. The translucent reeds stand far apart, collecting the night. Low ridges of eroded stone wander across the flats like abandoned roads, converging on points where nothing has been built.

From there the city appears intermittent. Towers rise above vapor, vanish, and rise again in different arrangements. Its reflection spreads across the marsh even where no open water lies. Rain drifts between the viewer and the walls, but sometimes falls behind the eye instead, tapping softly on streets that are not there.

The city does not feel dead. Death would imply that life had withdrawn from it. This place is active in another way: circulating, condensing, eroding, rearranging its distances, patiently exchanging stone and water. It has no need of creatures. Its weather performs the work of blood. Its canals think in gradients. Its buildings grow old without becoming ruins.

And in the hottest, darkest part of the night, when the rain pauses and every surface shines, the entire basin can be heard taking one slow breath—not inhaling air, but depth.
