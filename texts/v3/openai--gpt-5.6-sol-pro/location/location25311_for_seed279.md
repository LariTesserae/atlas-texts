At first it seems to be a flooded plain under bad weather: low, cold, and without a horizon clear enough to trust. The light has no visible source. It lies evenly across everything in a dim gray-violet wash, as if the sky were a deep ceiling of wet stone.

But the plain is not flat. It is crowded with newly risen mineral forms—black fins, pale ribs, clusters of angular pillars packed so closely that the distance between them becomes a maze of channels. Some are no taller than a hand; others lean above the mist like broken towers. Their edges remain sharp. Little has had time to round them. Where fractures expose their interiors, the stone is glassy and blue-black, webbed with white salts and translucent seams.

Water is everywhere without gathering into any great body. It occupies cracks, films surfaces, hangs in the air, and moves beneath thin mineral crusts. Pools lie in hollows only a few finger-widths deep, yet their bottoms cannot always be seen. They are dark beyond what their depth should permit. A dropped fragment may take minutes to strike below, although a hand can touch the visible floor.

The rocks weep continuously. Beads emerge from unbroken faces, swell, and descend in slow, heavy paths. In places the droplets do not fall. They crawl sideways toward certain veins, collecting along the stone in bright cords. Under overhangs, condensation rises from pools and fixes itself to the ceiling, forming downward-facing puddles that tremble but do not release. Water here appears to obey not the pull of the ground, but the attractions of particular materials.

Nothing green grows. There is no moss in the wet seams, no reed in the shallows, no stain of lichen on the exposed stone. The absence is made more severe by the moisture, which seems generous enough to support whole marshes. Instead, the pools hold only mineral skins: silver membranes, rust-colored dust, and delicate rafts of crystals that fracture into geometric petals when disturbed. Even decay is missing. Nothing smells of soil. The air tastes of cold iron, rain, and the faint bitterness of struck flint.

The land is loud.

From beneath the plain comes a continuous, many-layered booming. It is not a sequence of impacts but a sustained resonance, like surf trapped far underground. Each mineral fin takes up part of it. The thin black blades whine. Hollow columns give out low organ notes. Dense white nodules buzz against the bones of the jaw. Water amplifies and redistributes the sound: channels pulse, cavities gulp, and the suspended droplets shiver in place.

Yet almost nothing visibly moves.

The noise belongs to processes too slow to watch. The plain is settling by fractions. Pressure travels beneath it over days and seasons, squeezing buried water through newborn faults. A pillar may sing at one pitch for a century, then deepen by a note when its base sinks the width of a grain. Whole regions answer one another across the murk. Standing in the channels, one can hear changes approaching long before they arrive—a bass vibration that may take several weeks to cross the basin.

This makes travel peculiar. Sound is more reliable than sight, but not in the usual way. A high, clean ringing marks stable glass. A trembling chord indicates stone under strain. Silence is dangerous. It means either open water or a cavity so deep that no resonance returns.

The plain extends for many leagues, though its density keeps it from feeling vast. Every route is enclosed by near-touching forms. Beyond one cluster waits another: fields of needle stone, terraces of wet slate, crowded basins where rounded mineral bladders press together like eggs. The higher formations remain visible as silhouettes through the condensation, giving a sense of region and distance without ever revealing the whole.

Scattered among them are structures.

They are small and difficult to distinguish from geology. Some resemble roofless chambers made from slabs stood on edge. Others are rings of fitted stone around dry pits. There are narrow arches too regular to be accidental and long, low walls that divide channels without stopping the water. No mortar binds them. No tool marks remain—indeed, no tools could easily have produced their seamless joints. The stones meet along their natural fractures as though selected and patiently leaned together.

Many of these constructions are younger than the plain around them. Their corners are crisp, their floors unworn. Yet nothing inhabits them, and there are no roads between them. A few chambers contain neat accumulations of a single material: clear pebbles, hollow cones of salt, disks of dark glass. Whether these are offerings, nests, or simply the consequence of the local attractions of matter is impossible to decide.

The rare animals do not clarify it.

One may pass several days without seeing any. Their presence is first betrayed by a temporary change in the sound: a pillar goes quiet, then another. Eventually something pale unfolds from a flooded seam. It has the thinness of a membrane and the shape of a cloak caught underwater, though the water may be scarcely ankle-deep. It moves by fastening its edges to stones and contracting over the course of minutes. Within its translucent body, grains of mineral turn in slow spirals.

These beings do not feed on plants or prey. They settle against resonant rock and remain there, sometimes for years, absorbing vibration. When full, they stiffen. Their soft tissue becomes clear, then milky, then indistinguishable from stone. Several of the apparent walls may be colonies of such creatures after death—or after transformation, if the distinction means anything here.

Farther out, in the deeper hollows, there are heavier animals that announce themselves only by pressure waves. Water rises suddenly in nearby cracks. The stone choir drops half a pitch. A broad shape passes beneath the crust without breaking it, too slowly to follow and too large to see entire. It may take a night to pass under a single cluster of pillars. Afterward, new springs sometimes open along its route.

Rain occurs, but not from clouds. At long intervals the upper atmosphere condenses all at once. Droplets appear throughout the air, already falling, so that the basin fills with rain without any visible beginning overhead. The shower can continue for weeks. Water rattles against the innumerable edges, turning the plain’s constant drone into a deafening metallic roar. Pools join, channels overflow, and the lower chambers vanish beneath black surfaces.

Then comes the dry interval—not truly dry, but quieter. Water withdraws into seams. Salt blooms over everything in rigid, colorless fans. The air cools. Sound travels farther. In that season, distant sections of the basin can be heard speaking through the ground: immense harmonic exchanges, notes passing back and forth over the low country.

The landscape is young, but its youth is not energetic. It has emerged recently and is taking a very long time to decide what shape it will keep. Its sharpness comes from newness; its patience comes from mass. Nothing here hurries except sound.

Underfoot, every surface feels provisional. Thin plates tilt over dark water. Fresh cracks reveal warmer stone below, though the air remains cool enough to numb exposed skin. Now and then a crystalline spire splits without warning—not explosively, but with a drawn-out shriek lasting hours. The halves ease apart. Water immediately finds the opening. By the following year, a new channel has begun.

There is no center to the basin and no summit from which to understand it. Only successive districts of wet mineral density, scattered chambers, veiled distances, and the immense subterranean chord. The place seems empty of life until one learns that emptiness here has structure, that stone can wait, water can choose, and a wall may merely be an animal at the end of its slowest movement.
