At first it appears to be a high, pale emptiness: a plateau lifted beyond the ordinary weather, broad enough to contain several horizons. Its surface is old stone planed into long shelves and shallow bowls. Nothing rises sharply except at the plateau’s rim, where cliffs stand in successive walls, descending through blue distance toward lands that cannot be seen.

The altitude announces itself in the light. Sunlight is not warm here; it is white, hard, and nearly vertical, stripping color from exposed rock. Shadows are cobalt and precise. The air is cool even at midday, too thin to soften the distances. Every fracture in the ground can be seen for miles, and farther features seem close enough to touch until some moving creature gives them scale.

The stone has been weathering for an immense time, but it no longer looks actively ruined. Its forms have settled. Wind and frost have rounded the same ledges, widened the same seams, and emptied the same basins until the land has arrived at a durable shape: low limestone pavements, fields of flat slate, blunt pillars with hollows passing through them. Loose fragments are uncommon. What could fall has fallen; what could be carried away has gone. Even the scree beneath the cliffs has locked together beneath mineral crusts.

Moisture survives in protected places. At dawn, condensation beads on the shaded faces of stones and runs down them in narrow silver lines. Deep fissures hold cold black water, sometimes only an arm’s reach across but descending farther than light. There are chains of shallow pools where the bedrock has sagged into basins. They have no visible inlets or outlets. Rain comes occasionally in fine, slanting sheets, yet the pools neither noticeably rise nor diminish. Their edges are marked by dark bands of moss and orange mineral stain, as though each has kept exactly the same level for centuries.

There are no streams in the usual sense. Water travels inside the plateau, through pores and caverns, emerging only as a persistent dampness beneath overhangs. In some hollows, placing a hand against the stone reveals a cool pulse, too slow to be called vibration.

Vegetation is sparse and lies close to the ground. Silver lichens cover the sheltered sides of slabs. Between layers of shale grow hard cushions of blue-gray leaves, each plant as compact as a clenched hand. A few wiry grasses occupy cracks where dust has collected, all bent in one direction and apparently incapable of straightening. There are no forests, no shrubs, and almost no true soil. What green exists seems less to grow than to endure.

Yet the plateau is crowded with animal life.

Dark-winged creatures fill the open air beyond the cliffs. They resemble birds at a distance, but they do not flap. Thousands hang at fixed heights in the updraft, arranged in broad, patient tiers. Their wings are narrow and translucent at the edges, revealing fine internal spars like the ribs of leaves. Some remain over the same outcrop for days. Others slide sideways without turning, as if moved along invisible tracks.

Smaller animals occupy the stone. Pale, six-legged grazers rasp lichen from the pavements with black mouthplates. Round-bodied burrowers emerge from fissures to drink condensation directly from the rock. Their fur is dense and lies in mineral-colored bands, making whole colonies vanish when they become still. Long-necked scavengers stand around the pools, lowering hooked beaks into water that appears empty and raising them with tiny glassy organisms clinging to their tips.

Life is abundant here, but never restless. Feeding, flight, and migration proceed with such exact repetition that they seem less like events than permanent features of the landscape. The same lines of grazers cross the same slabs. The same winged shadows occupy the same circles. Predators wait at familiar cracks, and prey pass those cracks at familiar intervals. Nothing multiplies beyond its place. Nothing disappears. Birth and death occur, but the plateau’s numbers do not change.

Above all of this is the sound.

It is continuous and nearly unbearable: a vast, low roar with higher tones braided through it. At first it resembles wind. Then it resembles distant cataracts, though there is nowhere near enough surface water to produce it. Near the plateau’s center, the cause becomes partly visible. The bedrock is pierced by thousands of vertical shafts, from finger-wide vents to chasms large enough to swallow a herd. Air rises through them from caverns inside the mountain. Each opening contributes a note—whistle, moan, thunder, metallic shriek—and together they make a single sustained chord.

The strange thing is that the air at the surface barely moves.

Grass blades do not tremble. Condensation hangs undisturbed. Dust rests on exposed shelves. The sound pours upward without a corresponding wind, as though the mountain is not breathing but remembering one breath forever. Within hollows, the roar becomes bodily. Teeth ache. Vision quivers at the edges. Birds—or whatever the cliff-winged creatures are—use the pressure of it to remain aloft.

There are places where sound shapes matter. Thin flakes of stone, loosened along natural seams, vibrate against one another and slowly sharpen their own edges. Some animals take these flakes in their forelimbs. They pry moss from cracks, open the shells of pool creatures, or score shallow marks into softer rock. The behavior resembles primitive tool use, but no objects are kept. A stone is selected, used, and returned to the ground. There are no nests built from gathered material, no huts, fences, cairns, roads, or abandoned implements. Intelligence exists without accumulation. Craft appears briefly in the hand and then dissolves back into geology.

The score marks are perhaps the only signs of intention. They occur in shaded niches and consist of repeated parallel cuts, each set matching the spacing of the mountain’s dominant tones. Whether they record the sound or merely imitate it is impossible to tell. New cuts cannot be distinguished from ancient ones. The rock heals slowly under mineral seepage, preserving every mark at the same apparent age.

Toward the rim, the plateau divides into long promontories overlooking the abyss. Here the brightness becomes almost absolute. Clouds lie far below, a motionless white floor perforated by distant peaks. The sun reflects from them and strikes upward, so that ledges have shadows on neither side. Animals adapted to the glare possess dark inner eyelids or mirrored scales. Human eyes, if brought here, would soon water and close.

Night does come, but it changes less than expected. The stone releases stored radiance as a faint chalky glow. The pools reflect a sky packed with hard stars, while the mountain’s chord continues at precisely the same pitch. Temperatures fall only slightly. The winged multitudes remain suspended beyond the cliffs, now visible as orderly absences among the constellations.

Nothing here suggests catastrophe or anticipation. The plateau is not waiting to thaw, collapse, bloom, or be discovered. Its dampness will not gather into rivers. Its animals will not build. Its weathered stone will not meaningfully diminish. Even the deafening voice beneath it contains no crescendo.

It is an extreme height that has somehow escaped both barrenness and change: a cool, radiant country of ancient surfaces, hidden waters, innumerable creatures, and one unending sound—the world held open, bright and almost empty, while the mountain sustains a note older than weather.
