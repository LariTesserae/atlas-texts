The land lies only a little above the water, and in many places it is difficult to say which is which.

It is an open country of black shelves, pale mud, and long basins polished into the underlying stone. There are no hills in the ordinary sense. Whatever mountains stood here have been worn down past memory. Their last remains are broad-backed ribs of basalt and green-gray mineral, rising no higher than a house before sinking again beneath sheets of rainwater. The horizon is low and unimpeded, interrupted by isolated stacks whose shapes have been simplified by age: a blunt tooth, a leaning slab, a dome split cleanly in two. Nothing has a fresh edge.

Water is everywhere without forming a continuous sea. It occupies cracks, shallow pans, flooded galleries, and miles of interlinked channels no wider than a hand. Rain seeps up as often as it falls. Beneath the thin mineral crust lies a saturated layer that breathes under pressure; when the distant tide rises, clear water wells from holes in the ground and spreads in trembling films across the flats. When the tide withdraws, those films sink back, leaving salt lace and metallic stains.

The stone itself weeps. Dark beads gather beneath overhangs even after weeks without rain, run down grooves polished by innumerable previous drops, and strike the pools with a slow, heavy regularity. The air is cool and close to saturation. Mist does not hang above the ground so much as adhere to it, rounding distances and collecting on skin, shell, cord, and stone.

Nothing green grows here. There is no grass in the cracks, no moss on the shaded faces, no line of reeds to mark the water. Bare mineral continues from foreground to horizon. In places it is smooth as old bone; elsewhere it has weathered into low honeycombs filled with brine. White salts bloom and collapse. Rust-red oxides creep outward in branching forms that resemble roots from a distance, though they are only chemistry. Along certain warm fissures, translucent films spread over the rock—neither plant nor fungus, but colonies of soft-bodied mineral feeders, each film only one cell thick and collectively several hundred meters wide. They catch no light. They live on gradients of iron, sulfur, and dissolved stone.

These films support nearly everything else.

The animals are abundant but rarely large, and almost none keep permanently above water. The shallow pans are crowded with slate-colored swimmers whose jointed fins rasp against the bottom. Thumb-sized shellbacks gather in such numbers that entire pools appear paved with moving coins. Long translucent bodies thread through the flooded cracks, luminous organs pulsing faintly beneath their skin. Pale burrowers push cones of mud upward from below, then pull them down again when vibrations pass across the flats.

On exposed shelves, thousands of six-legged clatterers move between pools. Their bodies are low and plated, their feet ending in hard mineral hooks. They scrape the living films from stone with crescent jaws, leaving clean arcs behind them. When startled, a whole field of them locks flat against the rock and becomes nearly indistinguishable from scattered shale.

Predators follow. Narrow, high-jointed runners cross the open ground in sudden bursts, balancing on four needlelike limbs while a pair of grasping appendages combs the cracks. They have no visible eyes. They sense pressure through the wet crust and freeze whenever the subterranean water goes still. In deeper basins, broad mouths wait beneath layers of silt. At intervals one erupts upward, closes around a cluster of shellbacks, and sinks, leaving only a circular cloud.

The country is never quiet.

Far offshore—or perhaps beneath the flats—the tide enters a labyrinth of ancient cavities. Air is driven ahead of it through miles of narrowing stone. The result is a continuous, overwhelming voice: bass thunder underfoot, organ-like moans from fissures, and abrupt concussions from blowholes. Some vents breathe in rhythms lasting minutes. Others scream without pause as compressed air tears through mineral throats. The flatness of the land gives the sound nowhere to break. It rolls across the basins and returns from the low cloud, doubled and blurred.

Close sounds struggle inside it. Rain hisses on water. Shellbacks click together. Clatterers pass in dry, rushing multitudes. The thin-bodied swimmers make high glassy notes by flexing internal rods, and whole pools sometimes begin chiming in coordination. Yet all of this is swallowed repeatedly by the deep impact of the tide chambers. Conversation, where it occurs, must be conducted by touch or signs.

Scattered through the open country stand small constructions.

They are made from the only durable materials available: slabs, shell plates, twisted gut-cord, and rods chipped from dense black stone. Most are low shelters built into the lee of ancient stacks. Their walls are carefully fitted but roofless, or roofed with layers of broad shell that knock and flex in the wind. Doorways face away from the loudest vents. Drainage grooves surround every floor. Raised sleeping ledges keep occupants above the water that rises through the stone at high tide.

Some structures are not dwellings. Along safe routes, three-stone markers stand above the mist, each stone drilled or naturally pierced. Lengths of shell hung in the openings rattle when pressure changes belowground. Their pitch warns whether a basin is filling, whether a crust has become hollow, and how soon the next great exhalation will come. Near the most productive pools are crescent walls used to guide migrating shellbacks into shallow enclosures. There are scraping benches, cleaving stones, racks of hooked bone, and communal pits lined with impermeable clay.

Metal is almost unknown. A few green-stained fragments, hammered cold from native copper washed out of the vanished highlands, serve as needles, awls, or ceremonial blades. Most tools are chipped stone, fired mud, shell, cartilage, and teeth. Nothing combustible grows here, so there are no ordinary fires. Heat comes from deep vents. Cooking pits are dug where the bedrock is warm; meat and mineral paste are sealed into shell vessels and lowered on cords. Light comes from captured animals, from phosphorescent brine, and from pale ceramic bowls painted inside with luminous secretion.

At night—or during the long dim intervals that pass for night—the settlements resemble scattered constellations laid flat upon the earth. A handful of blue bowls. A line of moving lantern-creatures in netted jars. A warm fissure glowing dull red beneath suspended cookware. Beyond them, darkness and mist reclaim everything.

The inhabitants, if seen at a distance, appear bent and deliberate. Upright movement is dangerous on slick flats, so they travel close to the ground, using short staffs with broad stone feet. They wear layered shell scales over woven sinew. Their faces are protected by polished masks shaped to shed spray, and their hearing organs are packed with waxy clay before journeys. They communicate through hand pressure, cord tugs, flashes of reflected light, and patterns tapped directly into the bedrock.

They do not build high. This is partly practical—the wind and concussion destroy tall structures—but also a rule older than any surviving shelter. Nothing made by hand should rise above the oldest stones. A wall may follow a stack’s shadow. A marker may occupy a natural crack. A roof must bow toward the flats. To impose a new silhouette on the horizon is believed to attract the attention of the deep chambers.

That belief is not entirely superstition. Sound governs much of this place.

Certain vibrations cause burrowers to surface. Others send predators into feeding frenzy. A poorly struck stone can wake an entire basin of shellbacks, whose mass movement may undermine the crust. Rhythmic hammering sometimes produces an answering rhythm from below—not an echo, but a slower pattern arriving through the saturated rock. Old work sites have been abandoned around such places. Their tools remain where they were dropped, whitened by salt, while warning cairns form wide circles around the ground.

Water also obeys rules that are only partly tidal. It follows pressure, mineral charge, and perhaps the movements of creatures living far beneath the accessible channels. A pool can drain upward in a column of droplets. Rain falling over one basin may vanish before touching the surface, condensing instead beneath nearby ledges. Every few days a cold front passes over the flats and the exposed water tightens into a skin—not ice, but a temporary crystal mesh. Animals crawl on its underside. Then the tide-thunder shifts pitch, the mesh breaks into glittering dust, and the channels run again.

Storms do not darken the landscape much because it is already dim. They erase distance. The cloud descends until stacks appear and disappear within a few dozen paces. Rain arrives nearly horizontal, filling the air with silver threads. Blowholes throw columns of brine into the cloud, and the falling water becomes briefly warm. Animals flood the high shelves. Shellbacks pile in living ramparts against the current; runners hunt among them; clatterers wedge themselves into the shelter walls. The inhabitants retreat to pressure-proof chambers and lay their palms against the floor, reading the storm through stone.

Afterward, the whole country is rearranged in minor ways. Channels have migrated. Mud fans cover old tracks. A familiar basin contains unfamiliar animals. One marker has fallen; another, buried for generations, protrudes from newly scoured clay. Yet the greater shapes do not change. The blunt stacks, flattened ribs, and endless shelves have reached an age beyond ordinary erosion. They are not untouched, but reduced to forms the world finds difficult to simplify further.

There is a sense that this landscape comes after something—not necessarily after civilization, or even after life, but after height, heat, and haste. Everything vertical has been lowered. Everything soluble has traveled. Everything fragile has either learned to bend, withdraw, harden, or inhabit the intervals inside stone.

Across the widest flats, the mist occasionally lifts enough to reveal hundreds of shallow waters shining under the gray sky. Each holds a separate ecology, a separate color: iron red, sulfur yellow, ink blue, milk white, absolute black. Between them move sparse figures, animal and tool-user alike, taking long careful routes through an expanse that is crowded with life but empty of shelter.

Then the underground tide enters its deepest chambers.

The ground trembles. Every pool rings. Thousands of plated bodies flatten at once. Shell ornaments on the distant shelters turn in the same direction.

A roar rises through the ancient stone, too large to hear as sound alone, and the whole low country seems, for a moment, to breathe.
