The hollow lies beneath a low, warm plain, though from inside there is no sense of depth—only enclosure. It is not a cavern of great passages or plunging shafts, but a single broad chamber, perhaps the size of a small grove or village common. Its ceiling rises unevenly into darkness. The floor settles toward the center by only a few paces, forming a shallow basin where nothing gathers.

There is almost no water here. The stone is dry even in its cracks, and anything touched leaves a powder on the fingers: pale salt, old volcanic dust, flakes of gypsum, grains of black glass weathered dull at their edges. No drops hang from the roof. No channels shine on the walls. Where liquid once moved—perhaps ages ago—there are only rounded seams and shallow scallops, their surfaces now matte and warm. The chamber has reached a finished condition, not pristine but settled. Loose pieces have already fallen. Fragile edges have already broken. What remains seems unlikely to alter.

The ground is mostly bare mineral. Low shelves of brown basalt emerge from a floor of compacted ash. Here and there, narrow ribs of limestone or salt rise no higher than an ankle, dividing the basin into irregular cells. They resemble the boundaries of dried pools, but there has been no pool within any living memory. Between them lie broad spaces of undisturbed dust.

Life occurs sparsely and without vigor. On a few stones near fissures in the floor, dark crusts spread in thin circular patches. They are colonies of lichen-like growth fed by mineral warmth and the faint chemistry of air passing through the rock. Their colors are difficult to see: soot-green, rust, perhaps a bruised violet where the crust is thickest. Short, pale filaments stand among them like coarse hairs, never more than a finger’s length. They grow too slowly for movement to be noticed. A patch may take generations to cross the width of a hand.

Nothing grazes them. There are no insects beneath the stones, no pale cave animals, no wings overhead. The dust bears no tracks.

The warmth comes from below and is remarkably even. It does not pulse or gather around a single vent. The floor is warm enough for bare skin, the air warm enough that breath makes no mist. There is no true wind. At most, a nearly imperceptible exchange of air passes through hairline fissures, carrying a dry mineral odor: salt, heated clay, and the faint bitter smell of sulfur without smoke.

Darkness is the chamber’s principal substance. A person entering with a small flame would discover the place only in fragments: first a shelf of stone, then an empty reach of powder, then the low mineral borders crossing the basin. The ceiling would remain mostly absent. Light would be absorbed by rough black walls and would travel poorly through the dusty air. When the flame was covered, the chamber would not retain even an afterimage. No crystals glow. No fungus shines. There is no moonlike opening and no distant blue from the surface.

The acoustics are soft. A footstep makes a brief dry crush, then disappears. Words do not ring grandly; the irregular walls break them apart and return only a faint, low remainder. In stillness one may hear the tiny settling of powder under its own weight, or mistake the pressure of blood in the ears for something moving far away. No regular drip measures time.

The stone offers natural materials but shows no sign that anyone has used them. Along one wall, nodules of flint protrude from a chalky seam. Some have split through heat and age, leaving edges sharp enough to cut hide or scrape wood. Flat pieces of basalt could serve as grinding stones. Fibrous mineral crusts can be peeled away in brittle sheets. These possibilities give the place the quality of an untouched workshop: everything needed for the simplest handcraft lies scattered about, yet no hand has selected anything. There are no chipped cores, hearths, cord marks, stacked stones, or soot stains. The chamber has never become a shelter.

Near the center is its only strong feature: a low, broad mound of pale stone, rounded by ancient erosion. It is neither altar nor cairn, though darkness encourages such interpretations. Salt has formed a thin skin over it, crazed into small polygons. Three black growths occupy its shaded side, spaced far apart. Otherwise the mound is bare.

Nothing here announces danger. The floor does not open. The air does not suddenly poison. The warmth does not increase. The landscape’s severity comes instead from its completeness. It has no season, no weather, and almost no cycle. Growth persists at the edge of dormancy. Minerals rest where they fell. Dust keeps the same shallow contours.

It is a small, low world under stone: warm, dry, and old enough to have worn away every unstable shape. If left alone, it may remain almost exactly itself—dark crusts on basalt, pale dust in a basin, sharp stone waiting unused—for longer than anything outside would think to count.
