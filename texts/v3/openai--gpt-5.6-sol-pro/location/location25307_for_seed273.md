The mountains are hollow.

From a distance, this is not obvious. They rise in broad, weather-softened ranks beneath a white sun, their flanks green, their summits lost in warm haze. Valleys divide them. Cliffs flash with water. Forests descend in layered terraces, and flocks wheel over the ravines. The range seems old enough to have acquired its own weather.

Only nearby do the lines resolve: ribs beneath the ridges, stairways in the cliffs, inspection galleries running horizontally for kilometers. What looks like pale stone is poured ceramic and slag concrete. The darker escarpments are cast iron, oxidized to deep brown beneath quilts of moss. Every summit is a roof. Every gorge is a service interval between structures. There is no natural ground anywhere—not even buried far below. The oldest available substrate is another floor.

The range was built in stages, though no one now distinguishes clearly between stages of construction and geological eras. The lowest visible works are massive industrial vaults, each large enough to contain districts. Above them stand newer terraces, factory plateaus, reservoir courts, garden decks, and housing scarps, all weathered into a single established form. Corners have rounded. Seams have widened into crevices. Rain has combed lime and rust down the walls in long mineral curtains. Steel rails have worn smooth beneath generations of wheels.

Nothing is ruined enough to be dead. Nothing is new enough to be clean.

The highest ridges are immense load-bearing arches. Between their buttresses hang cultivated valleys, open to the sky but suspended hundreds or thousands of meters above the levels below. Some are crossed by bridges so large that orchards grow on their upper surfaces while freight moves through their enclosed spines. Others hold whole warm basins: neighborhoods, machine halls, reed beds, aviaries, and bright plazas arranged around shallow collection ponds.

The elevation is everywhere felt. A person may stand in a grove of broad-leafed trees and look down through the branches into a gap that continues for a kilometer. Far below, another forest covers another deck. Beneath that, lamps reveal a lower city through steam. Freight elevators move slowly along exposed towers, their cages diminishing until they resemble insects. Cableways cross the voids in patient diagonals.

Paths often have a wall on one side and an abyss on the other. Railings are thick, warm from the sun, and polished by hands.

The climate is warm without being oppressive. Heat rises from the machinery embedded through the range, but the architecture is designed to shed it. Tall ventilation canyons draw air upward. Their constant breath lifts leaves and carries scents between levels: hot oil, wet bark, flowers, yeast, ozone, animal musk, and the metallic odor of rain touching sun-heated plate.

Water is present, but never plentiful enough to be careless with. It arrives in short, hard showers when clouds snag on the upper towers. Rain rattles across a million roofs, runs into grilles, disappears through gutters, and reappears lower down in controlled cascades. For an hour, every wall streams. Then the sun returns, bright enough to turn the remaining droplets into a skin of silver.

Condensation supplies what rain does not. At dawn, cooling fins along the eastern faces sweat steadily. Droplets gather on vast ribbed panels and descend into cisterns hidden behind the terraces. In shadowed maintenance corridors, pipes bead with moisture. Ferns grow beneath them in dark green rows, and small animals come to drink where imperfect joints tap against the floor.

There are pockets of open water: cooling ponds, rain courts, emergency basins, canal loops around old turbine houses. They are rarely deep. Their bottoms are tiled, riveted, or lined in black sealant, visible through greenish water. Fish gather where pumps release oxygen. Floating gardens crowd the margins. Mosquitoes hatch in neglected sumps and are eaten by swallows that nest beneath the decks.

The vegetation is lush because every surface has had a long time to become hospitable. Soil is manufactured from compost, ash, fiber, crushed masonry, fungal residue, and the fine red dust of corroded metal. It accumulates in gutters and corners. Tree roots exploit expansion joints, drainage channels, and abandoned cable conduits. Some terraces were planted deliberately; others have become forests through centuries of leakage and neglect.

Near inhabited levels, the greenery is disciplined. Citrus trees stand in gridded orchards around ventilation stacks. Vines are trained over mesh screens to cool factory walls. Banana leaves rise between stair towers. Herb gardens occupy the flat tops of ducts. Deep troughs support bamboo that bends in the thermal wind. Pale flowering trees line public ramps, their roots enclosed in segmented boxes that can be opened and repaired.

Farther out, order loosens. Fig roots wrap around columns. Ferns fill old assembly pits. Epiphytes form thick gardens in the braces beneath bridges, nourished by mist and bird droppings. Creepers descend several stories before finding another ledge. On remote faces, vegetation hangs so densely that the underlying structure is visible only as regular shadows.

Yet the forest never becomes wilderness in the ordinary sense. Pull aside the leaf litter and there is a stamped drainage plate. Follow a stream and it enters a numbered culvert. Dig beneath the oldest tree and its roots eventually meet ceramic vaulting, then insulation, then a ceiling over some lower chamber.

The animals have adapted to this completeness of construction. Bright lizards bask on steam pipes. Monkeys travel the cable runs and know which moving gantries are safe to ride. Flocks of green parrots inhabit disused siren horns and repeat fragments of shift bells. Swifts fill the vertical canyons at dusk, turning in tight black spirals between towers. Cats live in the warm transformer galleries, where they hunt rats among humming cabinets. Small deer browse on the wider garden decks, confined less by fences than by their reluctance to cross metal grating over open air.

Larger birds nest on crane booms. Their droppings mark the machinery white, and operators pause work during breeding season. In the cistern courts, long-legged waders pick through mats of algae. Fish sometimes appear in reservoirs that are supposed to be sealed, carried as eggs on feet or transferred unknowingly through the waterworks.

The densest districts lie along the middle elevations, where the air is warm and the distances between decks narrow. Here structures overlap until streets become tunnels of foliage, pipework, balconies, conveyor housings, and pedestrian bridges. Houses are inserted into old industrial frames. Shops occupy former control booths. Schools fill turbine galleries where painted numbers still rise ten meters high on the walls. Markets gather under moving belts that now carry baskets, refuse, mail, and harvested fruit rather than ore.

Everything is close. Laundry brushes leaves. Tree branches knock against windows. Birds nest inside signal boxes. People lean from one level to speak to someone on another. Beneath the floor, gears turn. Behind the wall, water passes. Above the roof, freight travels in slow, enclosed chains.

The machinery is neither sleek nor mysterious. It is heavy, accessible, and repeatedly repaired. Motors sit on concrete plinths stained black around their bolts. Fan housings are patched with mismatched plates. Indicator lamps burn behind thick glass. Elevators engage with an audible sequence of relays, brakes, and counterweights. Across the range, enormous pumps lift water from low reservoirs to high distribution basins. Their work is constant and cyclic, more like breathing than production.

Factories remain active, though many now manufacture the materials needed to maintain the constructed mountains themselves: bearings, tiles, pipe sections, cable, filters, structural resin, glass, fasteners, replacement stair treads. Foundries glow in deep halls. Machine shops open their doors onto gardens. The long roofs above them carry nurseries and ponds, so that industrial heat rises into root beds before escaping into the air.

The landscape is loud, but rarely sudden. Its noise has depth. Fans drone from unseen chambers. Water hammers distantly in pipes. Freight chains clank at regular intervals. Insects whine in the vegetation. Parrots quarrel. Elevators groan up the cliffs. Somewhere below, stamping machines beat with the slow certainty of a second heart.

Because many sounds travel through the structure rather than the air, they are difficult to locate. A vibration felt beneath one’s feet may come from a workshop three valleys away. Thunder can be confused with shifting loads in the upper arches. At night, when the smaller workshops close, the range does not become quiet; the deeper systems simply become audible.

Change happens slowly here. Lifts climb slowly. Reservoirs fill over days. Forests advance by the width of a joint each season. A facade may spend fifty years turning from gray to orange as its reinforcement oxidizes. Whole neighborhoods migrate between elevations over generations, following repairs, heat, water access, or the gradual redirection of freight routes.

Major alterations are discussed in terms of decades. To close a bridge, even briefly, is to reroute people, animals, air, water, and power through dozens of connected levels. Demolition is rare. New work is usually wrapped around old work, and obsolete machines are more often left in place than removed. Their housings become walls. Their flywheels become stages, planters, shrines, or foundations for kiosks.

The range has laws that are partly civic and partly structural. Water must always be allowed a downward path. No rooted tree may be planted without knowing the thickness of the deck beneath it. Every inhabited terrace must have two routes upward and two routes inward. Open flame is forbidden in the ventilation throats. No one may silence a machine without first learning what depends on its vibration.

There is also an older rule, painted near lifts and cisterns in many languages:

**Nothing rests on the world. Everything rests on something else.**

Children are taught to trace the supports beneath their homes: floor to beam, beam to column, column to arch, arch to lower vault. The lesson always ends before a final foundation is reached. Survey records descend through layer after layer of chambers, but the deepest plans are incomplete. Workers in the lowest maintained districts speak of sealed levels below, warm and still powered, where inspection lamps can be seen through grilles that no map includes.

This is the landscape’s one persistent strangeness, though it is treated less as a mystery than as an inconvenience of age. The entire mountain range is clearly made. Its plates bear foundry marks. Its voids are geometrically spaced. Its ridgelines repeat load patterns too regular to be accidental. Yet there is no agreed-upon account of who built the first supporting level, or what, if anything, lies beneath it.

From the uppermost gardens, such questions feel remote. The view is enormous but not infinite: ridge after ridge of constructed highland, green with trees and silvered by ducts, extending through warm atmospheric haze. Sunlight glances from reservoir roofs. Clouds form around exhaust towers. Faraway cranes move with almost imperceptible slowness.

Then rain begins somewhere across the valley. One section of the range turns gray beneath it. The shower advances over orchards and factory roofs, over hanging forests and crowded terraces. Gutters wake in sequence. Birds lift ahead of the water in a widening cloud.

For a while, the whole made world gleams as though newly cast. Then the storm passes, the machinery continues, and the leaves begin their patient work of covering it again.
