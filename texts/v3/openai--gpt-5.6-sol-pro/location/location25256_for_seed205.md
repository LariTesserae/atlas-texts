## The High Green

From a distance, the plateau appears empty.

It rises out of the heat in one long green-black line, too broad for the eye to understand as a mountain. There are no summits, only shallow swells extending beyond the curvature of sight. Its escarpments are terraced and deeply weathered, reddish where the living surface has peeled back, pale where old roots have mineralized into something like limestone. Clouds form along its rim, travel a little way inland, and vanish.

Only from above does the truth become apparent: the plateau is not covered by vegetation. It is vegetation.

The whole high country is a single, ancient colonial growth, or many growths so thoroughly interwoven that the distinction no longer matters. Its upper surface has the scale and apparent solidity of land. Tough photosynthetic plates overlap like continental leaves. Between them run seams of cork, fibrous ridges, hollow veins, and vast areas of low, close-grown tissue as springy as packed soil. The distant humps that seem to be hills are old vascular knots. Valleys are folds formed by centuries of sagging under the colony’s own weight.

There are very few trees in the ordinary sense. Space is open. Horizon follows horizon under a white-hot sky. Here and there a solitary spire rises: a flowering mast, straight and ribbed, sometimes three hundred paces high. Elsewhere there are sparse groves of bladderlike towers with translucent walls, each standing far from its neighbors. Nothing crowds the view, yet no step can be taken off the living world.

The ground gives slightly beneath the foot. In some places it is scaled; in others velvety, bristled, waxy, or laid in long parallel fibers. Its colors change gradually over hundreds of miles—sage green, bruised purple, yellow-gray, almost black. Narrow breathing slits open and close in the heat. Fine root hairs emerge at night and withdraw before noon. If the surface is cut, clear sap beads up, turns amber, and seals the wound with a skin tougher than horn.

Soil exists only in pockets: dark crumbs caught in folds, mixed with droppings, shed wings, pollen husks, powdered bone, and the decayed remains of smaller plants living parasitically upon the greater one. Stone is similarly rare on the surface. Loose rock has long since been swallowed, wrapped, dissolved, or pushed toward the plateau’s edges. What looks like a field of rounded boulders may prove to be a line of old galls, hardened through centuries and colonized by lichens that themselves are broad enough to shelter animals.

The country is high, but its height offers little coolness. Heat gathers above the broad green table and remains there. Days are bright without being dazzling: a normal, pitiless light under skies bleached by altitude. The air smells of hot resin, crushed herbs, animal musk, and the faint sourness of fermentation deep below.

Water comes irregularly.

Rain may fall violently for half an hour, then not return for weeks. It rattles against the plateau in drops large enough to bruise soft tissue. The living surface changes at once. Scales tilt upward. Channels open. Wrinkles deepen into gutters, carrying water toward widely separated cisterns. For a few hours, every hollow shines. Then the plateau drinks.

Some water remains in deep leaf-cups and along the shaded sides of vascular ridges. These pools are warm, tannin-dark, and thick with life. Others lie inside the plateau itself. They can be reached through breathing pits where hot air rises from subterranean chambers, carrying mist and the echo of unseen splashing. In the dry season, condensation forms each dawn beneath overhanging plates and drips steadily for an hour. Whole migrations are timed to those brief falls.

There are rivers, but few flow continuously. The principal channels are empty most of the year: winding grooves lined with pale, waterproof tissue. When distant rain reaches them, they fill all at once. Water travels across the plateau in a glossy advancing wall, crowded with swimming things awakened from sealed cocoons. By the next day the river may have disappeared into pores along its bed, leaving only scattered basins and stranded shells.

The plateau keeps its water hidden because everything wants it.

Fauna are not merely abundant here. They are the weather.

At first glance the open country seems almost unoccupied. Then a shadow passes over the ground, and the sky resolves into layers of wings. Countless needle-bodied fliers stream between flowering masts. Broad sail-backed grazers move along thermal currents without landing for days. Dense columns of gnats rise from damp seams and lean downwind like smoke. High above them circle pale hunters with transparent membranes stretched between six jointed limbs.

Near the surface, small animals pour through the vegetation’s textures. Beetles as large as bowls rasp at wax. Long files of red carriers follow chemical roads invisible to anything else. Soft-bodied siphoners cling around breathing slits until the ground contracts and flicks them away. Burrowers travel inside the upper layers, visible as ripples moving beneath the green skin.

The largest grazers cross the plain singly or in loose groups separated by miles. They are high-legged, narrow animals with mouths built like shears. They do not bite deeply. They harvest the fine night-growth that the plateau produces and sheds each morning. Their feet are broad and cushioned to avoid puncturing the substrate. Smaller creatures live in the warm shelter beneath their bellies, feeding on parasites and drinking condensation from their hides.

Predators appear scarce only because they remain still. Some imitate flowering stalks. Some flatten themselves against the ground and alter their color over the course of an afternoon. One kind grows a mantle of living moss and feeds it with secretions; prey approaches expecting shade. Another lives inside old galls and extends a tongue across the surface like an innocent vine.

The plateau itself also hunts, though perhaps not deliberately. Certain red-veined districts close around prolonged vibration. An animal resting too long may find the ground slowly cupping beneath it, the edges rising. By the time it struggles, hooked hairs have emerged. Days later there is no sign of the animal except a darker patch and a sudden growth of flowers.

Everything makes noise.

At noon, when heat currents are strongest, the plateau becomes almost impossible to hear across. Wings roar overhead like a continuous cataract. The breathing slits whistle. Dry seed membranes snap in the sun. Grazers call across immense distances with low pulses felt through the feet before they reach the ear. Burrowers drum against the inner rind. Predators produce abrupt metallic shrieks to scatter flocks into confusion.

The loudest sound comes at evening.

As the surface cools, millions of small animals emerge from seams where they have sheltered from the day. They begin with isolated clicks. Within minutes the clicks join into a continent-wide vibration, rising and falling in bands. The air trembles. Leaf pools shiver into concentric rings. Loose objects crawl across the ground. Human voices vanish entirely unless spoken mouth-to-ear.

The sound is not chaotic. Long exposure reveals recurring intervals. A pulse begins near the eastern escarpment and travels west, answered by another from deep inside the plateau. Different species occupy separate pitches, but their cycles fit together too precisely to be accidental. Some travelers believe the animals are following signals generated by the great plant. Others believe the plant has grown for ages in response to the animals’ chorus, so that its chambers and ridges now act as an instrument.

During certain hot nights, the entire plateau gives one immense, low note. Flowering masts open soon afterward.

Human habitation is sparse and difficult to see. Settlements cannot become heavy, permanent, or numerous without damaging the surface beneath them. Most consist of a few raised platforms balanced on shed vascular ribs. Their roofs are made from naturally fallen plates stitched with sinew and resin. Ladders are light enough to pull up when the ground begins to feed.

Metal is uncommon. Stone is carried from the escarpment at great cost, and even a good cutting flake may pass through generations. Most tools are made from tooth, chitin, baked gall, hardened fiber, or splinters taken from dead portions of the plateau. Hooks are more useful than blades. Needles, awls, baskets, breathing masks, sounding rods, and water skins are the essential crafts.

Fire is kept in sealed clay cups and used cautiously. Open flame causes the ground to contract for miles. It also draws pale, heat-seeking creatures out of the sky.

The inhabitants build near old scars, where the plateau has already hardened and is less sensitive. Some scars are natural lightning wounds. Others are relics of settlements that grew too large. They form circular badlands of cork and gray tissue, with collapsed platforms preserved inside. Nothing is entirely ruined: roots thread through abandoned walls, animals nest in old baskets, and rain cisterns become breeding pools.

No road can last. Paths close, shift, or become dangerous as the surface changes. Navigation depends on flowering masts, the tone of the evening chorus, prevailing smells, and the direction in which the ground’s fine hairs lean. Travelers test every campsite with a drumstick. A clear, shallow sound means the tissue is thick enough to sleep upon. A hollow resonance may indicate a reservoir, a burrow colony, or a digestive chamber.

There are rules understood even by children:

Never cut a green vein.  
Never sleep within a newly formed circle of flowers.  
Do not drink from a pool without watching it through one full breath of the ground.  
When all the insects fall silent, lie flat.  
When the ground falls silent too, run.

The plateau is old enough to have reached a kind of restless balance. Wind and rain erode it, but growth rounds every edge. Channels migrate yet remain within ancient valleys. Escarpments collapse, exposing roots as thick as city walls; the roots harden in air, become cliffs, and are slowly occupied by hanging ecosystems. Nothing here is fixed, but most change takes place along well-established lines.

Only the flowering masts represent true upheaval. For years they stand closed, scattered spars across the immense plain. Then, after a sequence of rains and resonant nights, they open almost together.

Each unfolds into a pale canopy miles across.

For several days the high country is transformed. The open sky becomes a broken ceiling of petals. Their shade lowers the temperature. Condensation gathers underneath and falls in warm, localized showers. Animals arrive from every direction until ground, air, and inner chambers seem saturated with bodies. The noise exceeds thunder and continues without pause.

Then the canopies tear loose.

They rise on the plateau’s own heated air and drift beyond the escarpments like floating islands, carrying eggs, spores, seeds, nests, pools of captured rain, and entire temporary ecologies. Some cross deserts. Some fall into distant seas. A few remain aloft long enough to become green shadows known in lands that have never seen the plateau itself.

Afterward the High Green looks empty again.

Across its enormous surface stand only the stripped masts, a few scattered shelters, wandering grazers, and the dark marks of rain evaporating beneath the sun. Yet under that apparent emptiness, every layer moves. Water travels in concealed vessels. Animals chew through luminous chambers. Roots grind buried mountains into food. New folds deepen by fractions of an inch. The evening chorus prepares beneath the skin.

And far below, where no tool has reached, something vast contracts once, slowly, and the whole high country lifts with its breath.
