Beneath the surface lies a country that has never seen the sky.

It is not a cave in the ordinary sense. There are no weathered walls, no fallen scree, no mineral columns slowly lengthening drop by drop. The surrounding stone is too young for that: pale, warm, and almost featureless, as if the earth opened a hollow before deciding what kind of rock should enclose it. In places the boundary remains soft enough to take the impression of a leaf. Elsewhere it has hardened into smooth shelves and low, blunt ridges, all without cracks or signs of erosion.

Within this unfinished geology, vegetation has become the finished world.

The ground is not soil but a closely woven mat of roots, runners, flattened stems, and old leaves that never quite decay. It gives slightly under pressure, dense and resilient, warm as an animal’s flank. Great ribbed roots descend into it and vanish, while finer roots climb upward into bridges, terraces, partitions, and tangled groves. There is no clear distinction between forest floor and forest canopy. One plant’s crown becomes another plant’s foundation. Trunks grow horizontally for long distances, supporting whole meadows of smaller growth. Leaves overlap in tiers, making broad green plains suspended above deeper green plains.

The region extends beyond easy sight. Its horizons are made by vegetation: walls of stalks, curved root embankments, and immense leaves standing edge-on like hills. Between them are open reaches the size of valleys, though even these are densely occupied by low plants. Nothing is truly bare. A hand placed anywhere would touch living tissue or some dry, preserved part of it.

The light is astonishingly bright.

It comes from no visible source. The pale stone overhead holds a white-gold radiance throughout its depth, as if the buried surface above were translucent to a sun that never moves. Thick leaves soften the glare into green, amber, and citron layers, but in the open basins the brightness is nearly shadowless. Flowers turn toward it without tracking. Their faces remain fixed in one orientation, although there is no east or west here.

The air is hot and nearly still. Moisture gathers without making the place wet. Clear beads stand permanently along leaf margins. Condensation coats the undersides of broad fronds and fills the hollows where stems divide. Here and there, root systems encircle pockets of water: black-green pools no wider than courtyards, perfectly level and almost perfectly motionless. Narrow channels connect a few of them, but these are not rivers in motion. The water seems to occupy them rather than flow through them.

Rain occurs only in particular chambers. Beneath certain high leaves, droplets release one at a time from the same points and fall along the same paths. Each drop strikes a thick cushion of moss with a soft, contained sound. The moss receives it without splashing or wearing away. The intervals are so regular that the rain feels less like weather than a permanent arrangement.

Nothing here visibly grows, though everything is alive.

Buds remain on the verge of opening. Tendrils hold themselves in mid-reach, curled around empty air or lightly touching the supports they have chosen. New roots, white and delicate at their tips, press into the young stone but do not advance. Seedpods are full without ripening. Fallen petals lie fresh where they landed. The whole buried landscape occupies a stable instant of luxuriance: not dead, not dormant, but held at the exact point where abundance has filled all available space.

This stillness prevents decay. Old leaves persist beneath new ones in deep laminated beds, each retaining its color. The lower layers are dark olive, copper, and purple rather than brown. They compress into firm platforms and banks. In some districts, centuries—or whatever passes for centuries here—seem to be stacked as intact foliage, yet no layer is more deteriorated than another.

Fauna are rare enough to seem accidental.

A pale, long-legged insect may stand in the center of a leaf, motionless except for the slow opening of its breathing pores. Tiny glassy snails inhabit the rims of the condensation pools, each fixed to its own arc of stem. Far away, something sometimes crosses behind the foliage with the shape of a small quadruped, but it makes no footfall and leaves no disturbed leaves behind. There are no swarms, no birdsong, no constant labor of pollinators. Flowers produce pollen without assistance; fruit exists without being eaten. The plants appear to have dispensed with almost every partnership except their partnerships with one another.

The resulting quiet is large rather than empty. It contains the faint strain of fibers under weight, the occasional drop of water, and a subtle dry whisper from pores opening in millions of leaves at once. These sounds do not travel far. Layers of vegetation absorb them, so each grove has its own pocket of silence.

Scattered through this living terrain are structures.

They are small and difficult to distinguish from natural growth. A ring of upright slabs stands in one bright clearing, each slab wrapped in a separate sheath of vine. A low stair descends beside a pool, though it has only five steps and ends beneath the water. Farther on, walls of fitted pale blocks divide a root terrace into rooms with no roofs and no doorways. No tool marks appear on the stone. The blocks may have grown in place, persuaded into rectangles by some vanished habit of roots. There are no hinges, vessels, blades, lamps, or machines.

In one district, narrow shelters sit beneath immense leaves. Their frames are made of bent branches that remain alive and rooted at both ends. Woven mats hang between them, but the fibers have fused and sprouted, turning the walls into vertical gardens. Whether anyone built these shelters is impossible to say. They may be an architectural phase in the life of the plants themselves.

The deepest pools hold the strangest features. Under their clear surfaces are clusters of smooth forms resembling unglazed bowls, masks without faces, or unopened shells. They cannot be reached: the water above them is shallow to the eye but has no measurable bottom. A stem lowered into one of these pools never touches the submerged objects, no matter how near they appear.

Distance behaves more reliably than depth. One may cross the country from grove to grove, following broad rootways over luminous marshes and between columns of bundled trunks. The route bends, but does not shift. Every landmark remains where it was first seen. A torn leaf is always torn in the same shape; a drop is always gathering at its tip. Even footprints, where the root mat accepts them, slowly lift away without growth or motion, returning the surface to its established form.

The place permits disturbance only as a temporary guest. Nothing can be permanently removed, worn down, planted, drained, or built upon. Stone does not erode. Water does not diminish. Plants cannot be cut for long: separated stems remain green, while the severed place closes into precisely its former shape. Fire will not catch despite the heat. Digging reveals more roots, then pale unfinished stone, and eventually the same roots again.

There is no night, no season, and perhaps no time in the biological sense. Yet the landscape does not feel frozen. Heat rests against the skin. Leaves maintain their tension. Moisture shines. Every surface seems full of an imminent act—opening, falling, flowing, hardening—that never arrives and is never abandoned.

It is a world beneath the world, brilliantly lit and almost entirely vegetal, held forever at the beginning of becoming.
