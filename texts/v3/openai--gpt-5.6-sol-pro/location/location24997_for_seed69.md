The highlands begin above the cloud line, though there is no way to see either cloud or line.

Darkness here is not merely the absence of sun. It has depth and weather. It gathers in the valleys, pours over saddles, and thickens beneath the enormous leaves of the upland marsh-forest. Even lightning remains hidden inside the clouds: thunder travels through the plateau for minutes at a time, but no flash accompanies it. Flame burns black. Heated metal can be felt from several paces away, yet gives no glow. The few creatures with eyes use them only to judge pressure and warmth at close range. Nothing in this country has ever cast a shadow.

The land reveals itself by other means.

Underfoot, the plateau is a succession of wet stone terraces, reed basins, steep-sided peatlands, and broad shelves of ancient volcanic rock. Rain has worked on them for so long that there are no raw edges. Cliffs are fluted like worn pillars. Gullies interlock in branching systems, each channel polished to a dark smoothness beneath the moss. The harder ridges remain as rounded backs between watersheds, while softer strata have become deep, warm hollows full of saturated soil. At the brink of the largest escarpments, rivers do not fall cleanly. Wind catches them and combs them sideways into curtains of spray, feeding hanging wetlands miles along the cliff face.

Water is everywhere without quite becoming an ocean. It trembles in root cups, travels beneath the skin of the ground, condenses on cable and chitin, seeps from porous rock, and hangs in the air as a fine, warm suspension. There are lakes on the higher shelves, but most have no exposed surface. They are roofed by interwoven vegetation: floating mats thick enough to carry forests, factories, and migrating herds. Beneath those mats, black water may descend hundreds of meters through drowned fissures. A heavy footstep can send a slow wave through several hectares of apparently solid ground.

The plateau is vast enough to make its own climate. Warm vapor rises continuously from fractures in the bedrock, and colder air descends from heights no native animal attempts to reach. Their meeting produces long belts of rain that walk across the uplands like grazing things. A storm can remain in one basin for a season, revolving around the same ring of mountains, while a neighboring watershed dries to damp stone and fog. The atmosphere is heavy, mineral-tasting, and rich with spores. Breathing through cloth leaves a faint green-black paste on the fibers.

Vegetation grows densely, but it does not resemble a sun-seeking forest. There are no orderly crowns. Stems spread laterally through the dark, following gradients of heat, vibration, water chemistry, and electrical leakage. Great paddle-leaved plants lie nearly flat over the marshes, overlapping like shingles. Rope fungi bridge ravines and tighten when rain increases, hauling their fruiting bodies above flood level. Groves of hollow, jointed trunks cluster around warm vents; their roots descend into the stone and their upper chambers pulse as air pressure changes. During storms these groves become immense organs, sounding bass notes that mingle with thunder.

Some plants feed on industrial current. Their root sheaths creep along buried conductors, not quite breaking the insulation but drawing charge through it. Around old substations, the growth is particularly lush: layered fans, conductive moss, black creepers with leaves as thin as foil. None shines. When charged, they announce themselves by a dry ticking, a smell of rain on metal, and the way nearby hairs lift from the skin.

Sound maps the country more faithfully than sight could.

Water strikes leaves, drums on hollow roots, pours through culverts, and bellows in caverns beneath the peat. Insects produce continuous bands of vibration, some too low to hear as sound but strong enough to loosen silt from a bank. Far-off herds communicate by stamping, and the plateau answers through its buried stone. Predators click against rock, sending precise echoes through the undergrowth. The loudest weather is not always the storm: when pressure falls, millions of marsh bladders open at once to release buoyant spores, producing a vast soft detonation followed by hours of hissing.

Through all of this runs machinery.

The industrial works are scattered rather than urban. One may travel for days across root marsh and water-cut upland before encountering a structure, but each structure belongs to a system of continental extent. A pumping tower stands alone on a basalt promontory, its foundations sunk through fifty meters of living mat. An intake house crouches at the bottom of a gorge, reachable only by a rusted stair that vibrates with unseen falls. Rail lines emerge from tunnels, cross impossible spans, and vanish again beneath forests of wet membrane. Kilometer-long pipelines are mostly buried, disclosed by warm strips of ground and the regular knocking of expansion joints.

No one place contains the whole industry. The plateau itself functions as the factory floor.

Deep turbines harvest the descent of water through the mountain mass. Thermal wells draw heat upward from the ancient volcanic foundation. Compressor stations inhale the dense air and force it into reservoirs below. Mineral separators strain dissolved metals from underground rivers. Automated crawler rigs move slowly between extraction fields, their many feet broad enough to cross peat without sinking. They can take a century to complete a circuit.

The machines do not illuminate their work. They use pressure sensors, sonar, chemical sampling, and whiskerlike arrays that touch the air. Their warning systems are acoustic: bells, horns, coded hammer blows transmitted through rails, pulses below the threshold of human hearing. At close range, the landscape is full of mechanical speech. Valves chatter to distant valves. Pumps alter rhythm when migrating animals approach. A damaged bearing cries in a repeating three-note scrape, sometimes for years before a maintenance engine arrives.

The makers are absent, or else no longer distinguishable from their maintenance system. There are shelters beside some works: narrow concrete rooms with rounded corners, bunks molded into walls, drains in every floor. They are dry only intermittently. Fungal mats have softened their interiors, and small warm-bodied animals nest inside old control cabinets. Labels remain as raised lettering, legible to touch. Directions point toward settlements that have either sunk, migrated, or been dismantled so completely that only differences in soil chemistry mark them.

Elsewhere, structures appear less abandoned. Doors open before storms. Gantries reposition themselves above floodwater. A single elevator may still descend through several kilometers of rock, arriving at platforms where the air smells of lubricant and deep salt. Maintenance drones, blunt and many-legged, emerge during quiet weather to clear roots from vents and replace corroded bolts. They tolerate most creatures. They react violently only to anything that obstructs water.

That seems to be the oldest rule of the works: water must be allowed to move.

Every dam has bypass channels. Every road is perforated with drains. Every foundation divides itself around springs. When silt accumulates beyond a certain measure, gates open without warning and entire basins are rearranged. The resident ecology has adapted to these releases. Filter-feeders cling to spillways before the flood. Seed pods roll toward the lowest sluices. Long-bodied grazers gather on ridges when they feel the preliminary tremors in the pipe network. Predators follow them.

Fauna is abundant, though rarely encountered first as a body. It is breath against the hand, ripples moving through grass, musk caught in humid air, claws on distant metal.

The largest grazers carry colonies of smaller organisms in the folds of their armored hides. They move slowly across the floating forests, cropping fungal fans and crushing channels that later fill with water. Their broad feet sense the depth of peat. Calves remain between the adults, guided by infrasonic calls that make puddles tremble.

In the reed basins live quick, jointed animals that skim the water on six or eight spread limbs. They hunt by disturbing the surface and reading the returning waves. In storms they climb the vegetation and lock together into living nets. Burrowing filter-creatures occupy the saturated ground in such numbers that a whole hillside may contract faintly when touched. Airborne animals are less like birds than folded kites of skin and cartilage. They launch into pressure fronts and remain aloft for weeks, feeding on spores and tiny ballooning larvae. One knows they are overhead by the occasional snap of a wing membrane and the soft rain of waste onto leaves.

Predators take advantage of the machines. Some wait beside hot pipelines where prey gathers. Others imitate the pulse codes of safe crossing signals. A marsh hunter called, in the surviving maintenance lexicon, a false gate can produce the exact rhythm of an opening sluice. Herds climb toward high ground when they hear it. The hunter waits above.

There are stranger partnerships. Small crusted animals live in electrical cabinets and keep contacts clean by eating oxide. A species of pale root grows only inside drainage tunnels, where it coils around pipes and seals minor leaks; maintenance drones carry its seed nodules in dedicated compartments. Certain amphibious scavengers have learned to strike inspection plates to summon machines. When a drone arrives and opens the housing, the scavengers rush in for warmth and insects. The drone removes them patiently, one by one, and begins again.

Because nothing produces visible light, distance is understood as delay. A nearby cliff returns a call at once; a remote escarpment answers after several heartbeats. The greatest voids answer so late that travelers may have forgotten making the sound. In some regions, survey towers emit a low note at regular intervals. The returning echoes overlap, turning the terrain into a slow chord. One can navigate by listening for changes in harmony.

There are places where the chord fails.

These are the deep erosion basins, ancient hollows carved through the plateau’s upper rock into sound-swallowing deposits beneath. Their walls are padded with dense growth and saturated mineral foam. Echoes enter and do not return. Machinery routes around them. Animals approach in silence, drink from their warm peripheral pools, and depart quickly.

At the center of some basins stand isolated stacks of weathered material—neither wholly stone nor wholly manufactured. Their surfaces are ribbed with channels, polished by rain and the touch of generations. Water climbs these stacks rather than descending them, drawn upward through capillary galleries until it spills from the top as cold mist. The mist falls only a little way before vanishing into the air.

Around these structures the vegetation grows in concentric forms, and buried engines keep a rhythm too slow to be heard as individual strokes. Once every several days, perhaps, the ground completes a single pulse. Root systems tighten. Water levels rise a finger’s breadth. Creatures become still. Then the plateau relaxes, and movement resumes.

Whether these stacks regulate the climate, feed the pipes, or merely survive from an older purpose is impossible to tell. The whole highland has been weathered into cooperation. Erosion no longer appears to be destroying the works; it has become one of their processes. Channels abrade turbine stone to the correct shape. Roots hold slopes at their angle of rest. Floods clear obsolete structures. Corrosion releases minerals needed by marsh life. Animals spread sealant spores from station to station. The machines prune the vegetation, and the vegetation cushions the machines against landslides.

Nothing is pristine, and nothing is entirely ruin.

The plateau continues beyond any local map: warm, elevated, rain-laden, and crowded with hidden motion. Rivers pass beneath forests that pass beneath railways that pass beneath migrating animals. Storms circle mountain systems large enough to have names no single species could pronounce. Pumps labor in sealed caverns. Wet leaves drag against cable. Predators rehearse false alarms. Somewhere far below, millions of tons of water begin to change direction.

The warning arrives through the ground first.

Then the horns start, one after another, across the dark.
