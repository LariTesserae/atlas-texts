### The Uninhabited Quarter

The city stands in a shallow depression no wider than a small town, though from a distance it appears larger. Perspective behaves poorly here. Alleys stretch when seen end-on and contract when crossed; towers that seem several streets away can be reached in twenty steps. Nothing rises very high. The tallest structures are perhaps the height of a cathedral nave, their narrow crowns silhouetted against a dim, mineral sky.

There is no water. There has never been water.

The ground is a pale, compacted salt that contains no trace of sea. It formed instead from the cooling breath of the earth: dry vapors crystallizing layer by layer until the basin acquired a floor as smooth as unfired porcelain. Where the surface has split, it reveals black glass beneath. The cracks are new and sharp, with no dust collected in them and no rounding at their edges. Some continue directly through walls and plazas, suggesting that the city and its foundation are the same object.

The buildings are not ruins. They are too fresh for that.

They have doorways, stairs, courtyards, colonnades, blind windows, covered passages, and little squares where five or six streets meet. Yet there are no tool marks, hinges, fastenings, or joined materials. Each structure is a single mineral growth. Walls rise from the salt in pleated sheets; arches divide as crystals divide; staircases unfold one thin tread at a time. In places, entire blocks resemble clusters of quartz forced to imitate civic planning.

The imitation is exact enough to be unsettling. Gutters run beneath the eaves despite the absence of rain. Narrow channels cross the streets, but they have never carried liquid. Balconies face other balconies. Alcoves wait at conversational height. There are empty niches for statues, empty sockets for lamps, empty frames where signs might hang. The city expresses an intimate knowledge of habitation without having known inhabitants.

It continues to build itself.

Growth occurs mostly during the long dusk. A wall may acquire a doorway over the course of an hour, its substance thinning silently until an opening remains. Cornices extend like frost along a window ledge. New stairs push upward from courtyards and stop against blank ceilings. Elsewhere, architecture retracts: towers shorten, arches seal, whole rooms fold flat and become part of the pavement.

None of this makes a sound.

Even fractures are silent. A spire can split from top to base and fall into a hundred bright shards without producing so much as a click. The pieces touch the ground gently, as though sound here were not merely absent but forbidden. Footsteps are swallowed. A thrown stone lands without report. If one speaks, the sensation of speaking remains in the throat and chest, but no voice enters the air.

The air is thin, cool, and faintly metallic. It carries dry reactive gases that stain exposed surfaces in slow waves. During the basin’s brief morning, walls turn violet from the ground upward. By midday they fade to bone-white. At dusk, soot-dark bands descend from the roofs. The process resembles shadows passing over the city, except that the sky remains uniformly dim and the discoloration continues around corners and into sealed rooms.

There are no clouds. Overhead hangs a low gray radiance without any visible sun. Objects cast several weak shadows, each pointing in a slightly different direction. These shadows migrate continuously, even when their objects do not. At certain intersections they accumulate in dense pools, becoming almost tangible. Loose salt skates away from them in delicate fans.

Life persists, though sparingly.

Along the north-facing walls grow stands of wire-thin vegetation: black stems with translucent, angular leaves. They do not root in soil, for there is no soil, nor do they drink. Their buried filaments draw charge from the boundary between salt and glass. At intervals the leaves snap from one orientation to another, following invisible currents beneath the streets. A whole grove may turn at once, perfectly coordinated and perfectly quiet.

The plants grow far apart. One might occupy an entire courtyard alone, a dark branching figure no taller than a person. Others form sparse rows beside avenues, giving the impression that they were planted deliberately. Their seed structures are hollow prisms. When mature, these detach and drift a few inches above the pavement, borne not by wind but by differences in electric potential. They gather beneath arches and in empty rooms until the chemistry changes, then scatter again.

A second form of vegetation appears only on newly grown architecture. It resembles a skin of dull green scales, each scale separated from the next by a finger’s width. The colony slowly maps the geometry of a building. It avoids doors and windows, outlines stairs, and forms concentric patterns around empty niches. When a structure begins to retract, the scales lift away before the stone moves, hovering briefly like a loosened mosaic.

Animals are rarer.

There are pale, many-jointed creatures that cross the plazas at dusk. Their bodies are almost flat, and their legs end in broad pads that never seem to touch the ground. They feed on electrical residue left by the black plants, moving from stem to stem with ritual care. They have no mouths and no visible eyes. A fan of mineral fibers opens along the back when they are feeding, then closes as they depart.

Something else lives inside the walls.

It is never seen whole. Occasionally a sequence of small depressions travels beneath a facade, passing upward across balconies and around corners. The wall bulges slightly, as if accommodating a body within its solid mass. The black plants turn their leaves away as it passes. The flat plaza-creatures become motionless. Afterward, the building often begins to change: a new corridor opens, a stair twists sideways, a room seals itself around a pocket of cold darkness.

Whether the hidden animal directs the architecture or merely feeds on its growth is impossible to tell.

The city has weather, though not weather in the usual sense. Pressure moves through the basin in transparent fronts. As one passes, salt lifts from the ground in thin standing curtains. These curtains advance through streets, divide at intersections, climb facades, and disappear over roofs. They are visible only because they distort the weak light. Nothing stirs in their wake. There is no wind against the skin.

At longer intervals, the black glass under the city warms by a fraction. The entire basin responds. Pavements bow upward. Streets narrow. Buildings crowd closer together without touching. New districts begin as geometric ridges in open squares, rise to knee height, then either mature into houses or subside before completion. The process can transform the city’s plan in a single cycle. A route that led to the central court yesterday may end inside a narrow tower today.

There is no true center. Every district contains a plaza that appears central when entered. Each plaza holds the same feature: a low octagonal pit covered by a delicate lattice of mineral ribs. Looking through the lattice reveals not darkness but another dim sky, far below, crossed by the tops of unfamiliar towers. Sometimes the city beneath is arranged differently. Sometimes sparse black vegetation hangs upward from its streets.

Nothing can be dropped through these openings. Objects placed between the ribs remain suspended, midway between the two cities, until the next pressure front passes. Then they vanish from one side or the other.

The boundaries of the Uninhabited Quarter are abrupt. Streets reach the basin’s edge and stop at vertical salt faces only a few feet high. Beyond lies a nearly empty plain, flat and dark, extending into the haze. No roads continue outward. No debris has been carried away. From outside, the city’s movement is difficult to perceive, but its outline changes from hour to hour: an extra tower, a lowered dome, a gate where no wall stood before.

It may be young. Its corners are unweathered, its surfaces unscarred, its crystalline growth still vigorous. Perhaps it emerged only recently from the cooling basin.

Or perhaps age cannot accumulate here.

There is no erosion, no audible breakage, no sediment laid down by water, no ordinary decay. When a wall becomes old, the city does not preserve it. It draws the wall back into the ground and grows another in its place. The same may be true of the plants and animals. Nothing leaves remains. Nothing becomes a fossil. Every form is either active or absent.

The place therefore seems perpetually unfinished: a settlement in the instant before occupation, rehearsing streets, rooms, and public spaces for inhabitants it has not yet produced.

Its one consistent rule is that emptiness must be maintained. Whenever too many structures gather in one district, some fold away. When the black plants grow into thickets, sections detach and drift beyond the basin. If the pale animals converge in a single plaza, doorways open around them until they disperse into separate corridors.

Even shadows are prevented from crowding.

The city is active everywhere, but busy nowhere. It builds, rearranges, feeds, migrates, stains, germinates, and renews itself in profound quiet. Its spaces remain generous and expectant. Every balcony faces an absence. Every stair leads toward a room that may not exist when reached.

And in the dimness, under a sky without a source, the mineral streets continue preparing for someone.
