### The High Cistern

Near the summit of a young black mountain, behind a seam narrow enough to miss with an outstretched hand, lies a sea no larger than a room.

It is wholly submerged. There is no beach, no breathable roof, not even the silver underside of an air pocket. Water occupies everything: cracks, chambers, porous stone, the hollow stems of the few plants. Yet it is not dark. Daylight enters through the mountain’s thin upper crust, filtered by ice and pale volcanic glass until the whole cistern holds the clear, ordinary brightness of an overcast afternoon.

The light makes its depth difficult to judge. The opposite wall seems close enough to touch, then withdraws behind moving veils of water. Pebbles on the floor appear sharp and near, although they may be many body-lengths below. Distance is unstable here.

The cistern sits at a tremendous elevation, above the cloud line. Storms strike the exposed summit and force water through a thousand hairline channels. These streams converge in the hidden chamber with extraordinary violence. Water enters from every direction: descending in white columns, spurting sideways from fissures, rising from the floor in cold mineral jets. It collides at the center and knots itself into revolving masses. Nothing remains still for more than a few breaths.

The sound is overwhelming.

It is not the muffled hush expected underwater, but a continuous architecture of impact: deep hammering from the mountain’s interior, granular thunder as stones are rolled through unseen passages, the shriek of pressure passing through narrow cracks. Each jet has its own pitch. Together they make a deafening, almost metallic roar. At intervals the entire chamber rings like a struck bowl, and the water trembles so sharply that suspended grains arrange themselves into flat, shining sheets.

The mountain is still being made. Its stone is raw, warm only in its deepest pores, and scarcely weathered. Surfaces have not yet acquired the softened forms of age. They are abrupt, crystalline and unfinished: shelves ending in torn edges, folds of basalt arrested halfway through curling, pale ribs of mineral deposit still soft enough to score with bone.

Here, water does not chiefly wear the rock away. It builds.

The turbulent currents carry volcanic flour, salts and transparent fragments of silica. Where opposing flows meet, these materials precipitate rapidly, assembling thin walls, cups and branching ridges. A ledge may grow overnight, its lip extending grain by grain into the current. Delicate towers rise from the floor like stacks of translucent scales, only to be broken by the next pressure surge and rebuilt elsewhere. Some formations resemble unfinished stairs. Others look like vertebrae, masks or the foundations of structures too small for human occupation.

The floor is mostly empty. The chamber’s violence allows few objects to settle, and those that do are separated by broad reaches of clean stone. A single white cobble may occupy an entire shelf. A tuft of dark water-thread clings to a crack with nothing living around it for several paces. In the central hollow, there is only bright, rotating water.

Vegetation survives in protected seams. It is sparse and low: black-green filaments, soft mineral-coated fans, and clusters of translucent bladders no larger than fingernails. They have no obvious leaves. They feed on light passing through the summit and on minerals carried by the jets. During the strongest surges, the bladders collapse flat against the rock. When pressure eases, they inflate again, all at once, producing a faint clicking beneath the larger roar.

A few pale crusts grow on the newest stone. These are the first colonists, if they can be called plants at all. They follow the edges of fresh mineral deposits and perish when those deposits harden. Thus the living margin is always moving. Old rock is barren; only the mountain’s unfinished parts are fertile.

Animals are rarer still.

Sometimes a long, nearly colorless swimmer appears in the open water. It has four narrow fins and no visible eyes. Rather than fight the currents, it places itself at the intersections between them, where opposing forces hold it motionless. It may remain suspended for hours, vibrating in the noise, before folding its fins and vanishing into a fissure.

Small shell-bearing creatures inhabit the quiet sides of the mineral towers. Their shells are irregular, patched together from grains caught in the water. Each resembles a little piece of unfinished masonry. When disturbed, they release their grip and are swept away; most are never seen again.

There are also signs that something larger visits. Certain ledges bear parallel scrapes. Occasionally a structure is found overturned, though the currents alone should not have moved it. In the deepest interval of the chamber’s ringing, a slow pulse can sometimes be felt beneath the floor—too regular for geology, too heavy for any creature that could pass through the visible cracks.

Human marks, or marks close enough to human to invite the name, are scattered along the walls.

A ladder of wooden pegs climbs a vertical seam, though there is nowhere dry to climb toward. The wood is dark, swollen and bound with braided plant fiber. Some pegs are recent; others have been buried under clear mineral skins. On a narrow shelf stands a low cairn made of seven stones, each pierced through its center. The current whistles through the holes, changing pitch as the cairn slowly accumulates crystal.

Farther in, three shallow bowls have been chipped directly into the basalt. They are positioned where distinct currents cross. Within each bowl, heavier grains spin ceaselessly, polishing one another without visibly wearing the bowl itself. Bone wedges, stone scrapers and a simple copper hook rest in nearby niches. No metal fastenings hold anything together. Fiber, pressure and the growth of the mineral crust do most of the work.

Whoever built these things understood that nothing here could be completed by hand alone. Objects were placed where the water would finish them.

A basket frame set into a jet becomes, after several seasons, a solid vessel lined with glassy deposit. A row of sharpened sticks grows connected by limestone until it forms a grate. Broken tools are not discarded; they are wedged into fertile cracks and allowed to acquire new handles, new edges, sometimes entirely new purposes. Craft in the cistern is partly an act of waiting and partly an act of predicting where the chamber will build next.

The place follows several dependable rules.

Water always enters faster than seems possible, but the cistern never fills, because it is already full.

Sound travels toward fresh stone. If one follows the loudest ringing, one finds a surface still soft enough to carve.

Loose grains move upstream during the mountain’s periodic tremors.

Nothing dead remains in the central current. Bodies, leaves, shed shells and tools are carried into the walls through cracks too narrow to admit them. Much later, their shapes may reappear as mineral reliefs in another part of the chamber.

And at noon, when the sun stands over the summit, shadows briefly point upward.

During this hour the turbulence intensifies. The pale ceiling brightens, every unfinished ridge casts a dark plume toward the light, and the whole hidden sea seems to overturn without changing position. Towers fracture. Shelves extend. Jets trade directions. The wooden ladder bends like grass. Creatures disappear into stone.

Then the upheaval subsides—not into silence, which the cistern never knows, but into its ordinary thunder.

In the newly altered chamber, there may be one more step than before, one fewer wall, a fresh niche holding an ancient-looking tool, and a narrow passage where solid rock had been that morning. The place is small enough to cross in moments, yet it cannot be mapped. It has not existed long enough to possess a final shape.

High above the valleys, sealed inside a summit that is itself still rising, the little sea continues to construct its shores where no air has ever touched them.
