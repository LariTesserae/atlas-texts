### The White Choir

The place is hidden inside a foundation block no larger, from the outside, than a village house. There is no door. The only approach is through a seam between two courses of masonry, a pale vertical slit that narrows until shoulders must turn sideways. Beyond it lies not a chamber so much as an excess of construction: walls folded within walls, floors laid over older floors, ribs crossing vaults, buttresses supporting other buttresses at distances of only a handspan. Every surface has been shaped, fitted, fired, polished, scored, or mortared. There is no exposed earth beneath it and no native stone breaking through. Even the dust is manufactured—the fine residue of ceramic rubbing against ceramic over an age too long to measure.

Everything is low. Passages stoop beneath lintels. Terraces rise by the thickness of a finger, descending again in broad, shallow steps. Domes barely clear the crown of the head. The landscape has no summit and no abyss; its relief consists of minute ridges, grooves, sills, basins that never held liquid, and crowded fields of waist-high fins. It feels less like entering a building than walking across the intricate surface of something enlarged: the inside of a seal, perhaps, or the densely carved underside of a tile.

There is almost no empty space. Columns stand so close that they merge visually into fluted walls. Niches contain smaller niches, each filled with lattices, plugs, blind windows, and tightly nested arches. Where two structures might have left a gap, the gap has been packed with white tesserae. Where a corridor might have widened, ranks of thin partitions divide it again. Movement is possible only along certain inherited threads: polished channels through the abundance, just wide enough for one body.

The materials are chalk-white ceramic, dull silver glass, bone-colored mortar, and a porous substance resembling pumice but bearing repeated mold lines. None of it is truly natural. The oldest pieces have rounded corners and softened inscriptions. Reliefs have worn down until processions of figures resemble rows of pale weather marks. Steps sag in their centers. Pillars carry shallow scallops where something once passed them repeatedly, though nothing passes now.

This erosion is complete rather than ongoing. The Choir has arrived at its final degree of wear. No grain falls. No crack lengthens. Dust lies in bevels and does not settle further. Broken edges possess the calm, sealed smoothness of old teeth.

It is cool, but not cold. The air has the stable temperature of a deep cellar and the dryness of fired clay. Breath vanishes into it without mist. There is no dampness in joints, no stain beneath ledges, no mineral bloom left by seepage. Water seems not merely absent but inadmissible. The architecture provides thousands of bowls, gutters, channels, and cistern-like hollows, yet all are dry and perhaps always have been. Their purpose could not have been hydraulic. They conduct brightness and sound instead.

Light comes from the surfaces themselves.

The pale ceramics shine without heat, not as lamps shine but as a white sky does through thin cloud. Every wall is equally illuminated. No object casts a complete shadow; it only produces a faint gray thickening on the surface behind it. Silver glass inserts multiply the radiance through the packed interior until even the narrowest seams are painfully clear. Tiny scratches stand out. Mortar grains glitter. After a while the eye longs for darkness and finds none—not under stairs, not inside sockets, not even when closed, for the glow reddens through the lids.

There are no mechanisms behind the light. The slabs contain no wires, fuel chambers, moving parts, or concealed furnaces. Brightness is simply one of the fixed properties of the place, like weight or dryness.

So is the sound.

The White Choir is deafening.

A single, immense chord occupies every cavity. It is composed of a low pressure felt in the sternum, a middle register like stone horns held without breath, and a high, almost clean ringing that seems to arise along the teeth. No individual source can be found. Putting an ear to a pillar makes it neither louder nor softer. Blocking the ears removes only the sharpest edge; the lower tones continue through jaw, heel, and ribs.

The sound does not pulse. It does not swell or decay. There is no beat between its notes, no tremor, no rhythm by which time can be counted. The countless fins, bowls, slots, and resonant hollows appear to shape it, but nothing visibly vibrates. A handful of dust cast into the air hangs only for an instant before dropping normally; it does not dance. The Choir is not performing. It is a place in which a performance has become a permanent condition.

Speech is nearly useless. A shouted word becomes a pressure in the mouth and disappears into the standing chord. Footsteps cannot be heard, though their contact is felt. This makes walking strangely private. Two people could remain within arm’s reach and seem acoustically alone.

Life survives only in sparse, deliberate-looking patches. In sheltered mortar lines grow flattened fans of gray-green tissue, each no wider than a fingernail. They resemble lichens, but their surfaces are ridged like miniature leaves and hard as varnish. Their roots enter the manufactured mortar without widening its cracks. They do not spread. No dead patches appear, and no new ones bud. Here and there a threadlike stalk rises from a dry basin and ends in a translucent disk, angled toward no particular light because light comes from everywhere.

These growths have no scent. They produce no visible spores or seeds. They may be alive only in the broadest sense: holding a boundary, maintaining a color, persisting in the exact posture allotted to them.

Nothing eats them. There are no insects in the seams, no mites in the dust, no pale creatures in the deeper recesses. No shed skin, web, shell, feather, dropping, or gnawed edge suggests that animals ever entered. Even the usual small sounds of habitation are absent beneath the overwhelming chord. The place is packed with forms but empty of inhabitants.

Its carvings imply makers without depicting them clearly. On low friezes, crowded silhouettes kneel before lattices, carry ring-shaped objects, or press their faces to walls. Erosion has removed hands and features first. Repeated symbols remain: an empty cup, a closed eye, seven parallel bars, a spiral ending in a square. Yet there are no tools, machines, hinges, or devices anywhere. Doors, where represented, are only shallow outlines. The architecture appears built by intention but not by technology—as though matter had been persuaded into masonry through rules no more mechanical than the way a crystal takes shape.

The chief rule of the Choir is that nothing develops.

The luminous surfaces do not dim. The vegetation neither advances nor retreats. The air does not circulate enough to produce a draft. Temperature is the same against the floor and beneath the vaults. Dust remembers its placement. The permanent chord permits no silence and admits no variation. Even the wear belongs to a completed past, evidence of changes that the place has since exhausted.

A visitor is the only gradient introduced: a warm body, a moving shadow, moisture in breath, thoughts succeeding one another. The landscape does not resist these disturbances, but neither does it retain them. A palm pressed to the wall leaves no heat. A footprint in dust appears briefly and is gone when one looks back—not blown away or filled in, but absent, as though the settled pattern has reasserted itself without transition.

This gives the narrow passages their peculiar pressure. The architecture is already fully occupied, not by people but by its own final arrangement. Every groove has its allotted brightness. Every cavity contains its exact portion of the chord. Every speck of dust rests where the whole requires it to rest.

There is room to enter, but no room to alter anything.

Far inside, if “far” can be used for a place folded so tightly into itself, the passages converge on a low circular court. It is only a few paces across. Hundreds of ceramic ribs lean inward around it, stopping just short of contact and leaving a small white interval at the center. The interval is empty. It is the largest unfilled volume in the entire Choir.

Here the chord seems no louder, yet the body feels more completely contained by it. The luminous ribs crowd the eye. Gray-green disks grow at equal distances around the lowest ring. The floor is worn into a shallow depression, smooth and dry, like the imprint of a vanished bowl.

Nothing happens there.

Nothing has happened there for a very long time.

And still the whole hidden landscape remains brilliantly, thunderously present: a finished world embedded in a block of masonry, holding one note, one temperature, one arrangement of matter, with the inexhaustible force of something that has mistaken permanence for life.
