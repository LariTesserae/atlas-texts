The land is called the Low Works, though there is no one here to call it that.

It lies almost level beneath a hot, ordinary sky: a constructed plain extending beyond every visible horizon. There is no native ground. Dig anywhere—through pale dust, through the seams where rainwater has settled, through the thin crusts of salt—and the tool would meet fitted material. Brick, tile, rammed clay, lime mortar, slabs of fired mud: the whole continent is fabricated. Even its hills are hollow embankments, their slopes laid in courses too broad to recognize from nearby.

Nothing operates. There are no machines, lamps, cables, pumps, or mechanisms hidden under the surface. Whatever built the Low Works did so with elementary materials and an almost geological patience. The scale suggests industry; the evidence suggests hands. A ramp fifty kilometers long rises no more than the height of a person. A retaining wall may run straight for a week’s journey, made from blocks small enough to carry. Basins the size of inland seas are paved one tile at a time.

Because the land is new, it has not yet learned the shapes of erosion. Corners remain sharp. Mortar still bears shallow impressions: fingers, woven mats, smooth boards, the drag of a reed. Embankments possess ideal slopes that no old hill would keep. Rain has begun to make suggestions rather than valleys. Here and there, runoff follows a flaw between courses and forms the first dark thread of a future channel. A few joints have opened under heat. Dust gathers in them. Seeds follow.

Most of the Low Works is hot and empty. The sun warms the pale masonry slowly in the morning and then, by noon, seems to rise from the ground as well as fall from the sky. Distance trembles. Long walls become suspended lines; low platforms appear to float. The air smells of mineral dust, baked clay, and lime. Where the paving is dark—great fields of near-black brick—the heat is difficult to cross. Where it is white, brightness presses upward into the face.

Yet the place is damp in secretive ways.

Its builders shaped nearly every surface with a slight fall. Rain may come only occasionally, but when it does, the entire continent receives it. Water travels along gradients too subtle to see, slipping from broad aprons into gutters, from gutters into descending courts, from courts through stone throats into cisterns open to the sky. None of this is machinery. It is only slope, gravity, porous ceramic, and shade.

A storm can awaken hundreds of kilometers at once. Dry seams begin murmuring. Shallow channels fill and branch, though they were made to look like branches before water ever entered them. Sheets of rain move across the paving, thin enough to reflect the clouds without concealing the joints below. In the lowest plazas, water accumulates into temporary lakes, warm at the margins and tea-colored from dust. Their shores are exact: stairs, curbs, concentric terraces. For several days the plain gleams with separated mirrors.

Then the heat resumes its work.

The temporary lakes contract into pockets held beneath arcades and in deep square wells. Salt appears around them in white rings. Dampness retreats into brickwork, lingering below the sunlit face. At night, the stored heat rises from the slabs while cooler air settles into the basins. Condensation pearls on shaded walls and gathers under projecting courses. Drops fall at long intervals into channels already drying from the previous rain.

These are among the loudest sounds in the Low Works.

A single drop may be heard through an entire covered court. Wind makes a low breath at the mouths of culverts, but seldom enough to become a voice. Hot masonry clicks as it expands. At dusk, whole paved districts cool and contract with minute reports—one snap nearby, another far off, then silence again. During rain the sound is enormous, not because the storm is violent, but because every made surface answers it differently: tile hisses, brick darkens quietly, hollow chambers drum, and broad lime floors receive the water with a soft continuous applause.

There are no animals. No insects gather at the pools. No birds cross the glare. Nothing grazes the sparse growth or carries its seeds deliberately from place to place. The absence is clearest after rain, when so much water lies ready and no tracks appear around it.

Vegetation survives in widely separated enclaves. Tough grass rises from mortar cracks where windblown dust has accumulated. Low gray shrubs occupy the sheltered feet of north-facing walls. Their roots do not descend into soil, because there is no soil; they spread through joints, following moisture between bricks. Some cistern rims support thin collars of moss, black when dry and suddenly green after rain. In a few deep courts, tamarisk-like trees stand alone, each rooted in centuries—or perhaps only decades—of silt caught in a drain.

Without animals, the plants form a slow ecology of water, heat, and chance. Seeds are carried by wind or floated across flooded floors. A storm may connect distant basins for a single afternoon, allowing a ribbon of grass to advance into a new district. Then years of dryness isolate it again. Dead stems collect against curbs and become the beginnings of soil. The Low Works is therefore gaining a natural surface, but only in flecks and seams: a continent being colonized line by line.

Its most common terrain is not the open plaza but the almost-feature: a rise too low to be called a ridge, a depression too broad to look like a basin. The builders worked on a scale that confuses the body. What seems like a flat horizon may be the rim of a single reservoir. A line mistaken for distant land may be one stair, hundreds of kilometers long and scarcely higher than the plain below. From its edge the traveler would see another level spread out, equally vast, paved in a different material and draining toward a different unseen center.

There are districts of red brick, districts of yellow clay, districts of close-set basalt blocks brought from no visible quarry. There are fields of upright ceramic fins whose purpose is simply to cast shade and condense moisture. There are lattices of open courts, each one several kilometers across, separated by walls that reach only to the knee. Elsewhere stand monumental unfinished forms: ramps ending bluntly, foundations enclosing nothing, pillars composed of stacked unfired blocks beginning to slump after their first wet season.

The unfinished portions make the land feel not abandoned but paused. Pallets do not remain; scaffolds do not remain; there are no tools or workshops. Yet fresh clay lies in smooth, prepared beds. Kiln-blackened trenches stretch along the margins of certain plateaus. Some walls have been built to half their intended height, with their upper surfaces carefully wetted and roughened for a next course that never arrived.

Weather has only begun to modify this precision. The first erosion is small enough to watch. Rain rounds the edge of an unfired brick. A thread of runoff steals red sediment from one terrace and lays it across a white floor below. Thermal stress lifts a tile at one corner. The next storm gets beneath it. Dust collects in the opening; moss darkens the dust; a root widens the seam. Nothing here is ancient enough to be picturesque. Decay has not yet become beauty. It is still an active negotiation between design and climate.

The governing rule of the Low Works is that everything visible was given a shape, but almost nothing was given a single purpose. A wall retains earth, casts shade, guides wind, marks a level, and catches dew. A stair is also a dam. A courtyard is also a reservoir. A road becomes a spillway in rain. Even damage is recruited: cracks take water deeper than intact paving could.

Across the vastness, these simple interactions create weather of their own. Heated black districts raise columns of air by afternoon. Cooler reservoir fields draw in weak winds. Moisture from a recently flooded basin may gather into clouds and fall again farther down the gradient. Thus rain can migrate through the Works in stages, basin to basin, each storm partly descended from the water released by the last. The continent breathes without lungs or engines.

Near sunset, the distinctions soften. Red brick, white lime, salt, dust, and shallow water take on the same copper light. The heat shimmer collapses. Long alignments become visible—walls and channels converging toward centers far beyond sight. For a short time the land’s full intention almost seems legible.

Then darkness fills the lower courts first. The upper paving remains warm under the last light, empty and immense. Somewhere below, hidden water moves through a clay passage. A drop falls. A wall answers with a quiet crack as it cools.

Across the made continent, new channels are beginning.
