The city is hidden in a crack near the summit of a mountain.

From the open sky the crack resembles nothing more than a dark line in pale stone, narrow enough to step across. It runs for perhaps forty paces along the highest ridge, then disappears beneath a blade of newly uplifted rock. No road approaches it. No smoke rises. The surrounding summit is bare, cold, and sharp-edged, a place of powdered salt, black volcanic glass, and slabs that have not yet weathered into gentler forms. Even the wind leaves little mark. There is no snow, no frost, no dampness in the shadows. The mountain is utterly dry.

Inside the crack, however, the line opens downward.

It becomes a vertical country.

The walls fall farther than daylight can follow, though they remain only a few arm-spans apart. Ledges, bridges, foundries, dwellings, cable yards, lift cages, and exhaust galleries fill almost every interval. Seen from above, the city resembles densely packed clockwork inserted into a wound in the mountain. Its highest roofs are built beneath overhangs to conceal them from the sky. Its lowest districts are small amber constellations miles below, distorted by rising mineral fumes.

Everything is steep. There is no ground in the ordinary sense. A floor is simply a platform secured to one wall or the other. Streets are ladders, spirals, counterweighted elevators, and sloping rails polished by continuous traffic. Public squares are suspended cages. Warehouses hang in ranks from iron staples driven into the rock. Furnaces occupy natural pockets where the stone can bear their heat, while sleeping chambers are stacked in the cooler layers above them. Doors tend to open upward so that falling debris cannot jam them.

The city has been built from what the fissure provides: basalt ribs, pale salts pressed into ceramic, hammered nickel, braided wire, black glass, sulfur mortar. Timber is unknown. Cloth is made from mineral filament and the shed casings of local animals. Leather, where it exists, is metallic and finely scaled. Pipes are everywhere, but none carry water. They convey dry gases, abrasive powders, lubricating oils, electric charge, and streams of tiny calibrated pellets used as both fuel and currency.

The air is thin and cool. It tastes of stone dust, ozone, hot insulation, and faint sulfur. Breath—or whatever passes for breath here—is carefully managed. Districts are partitioned by flexible baffles, pressure doors, and curtains of hanging plates. There are no fountains, drains, cisterns, gutters, or wells. The very idea of runoff has no place in the architecture. Surfaces are slightly concave not to collect liquid, but to catch useful dust before it falls into the depths.

Despite its machinery, the city is mostly quiet.

The thin atmosphere cannot carry much sound. Great engines labor almost inaudibly behind insulated walls. Fan blades blur without roaring. Foundry hammers descend with only a distant tap, though their impact can be felt through every handrail and tooth. Communication often travels through solids: coded vibrations in bridge cables, knocks transmitted along iron columns, pulses sent through the rock itself. A resident can place a palm against a wall and feel freight descending three districts away.

The quiet is not stillness. Nothing here is still.

The mountain is young and continues to settle around the city. Its two walls creep, shudder, and occasionally spring apart by the width of a finger. At other times they close with enough force to crush bridges and buckle whole terraces. The settlement is therefore constructed as a moving mechanism rather than a fixed arrangement. Buildings ride toothed rails. Stairs fold. Support beams telescope. Entire neighborhoods migrate upward or downward as stresses change.

At any hour, one may see a row of dwellings being winched from one wall to the other while, below, crews cut apart a factory before the narrowing stone can take it. New scaffolds blossom rapidly in fresh cavities. Old ones are dismantled with equal speed. The city is always becoming and unbecoming, but its turbulence is orderly at close range: clamps released in sequence, loads transferred, cables tightened, bridges swung aside like gate arms.

Dust gives the motion away. It pours soundlessly from new fractures, rises in industrial thermals, and drifts through shafts in slow gray veils. Electric fields gather the finer particles into twisting ribbons. These ribbons sometimes cling to railings or figures, outlining them in pale fire. During periods of strong mountain strain, static discharges pass between opposite walls. Blue threads branch across the gap, briefly illuminating thousands of roofs and moving bodies.

Life is abundant, though nothing grows.

There are no leaves, roots, spores, or soft green films. The base of the ecology is a dull electrochemical skin that forms on certain metallic ores. It is scarcely thicker than a tarnish. Fed by gradients of charge, sulfur, and carbon-bearing gas, it spreads over newly exposed rock in geometric stains: bronze hexagons, black fans, violet rings. It does not reach toward light. It has no stems or fruit. It simply alters the surface molecule by molecule until grazers find it.

The smallest grazers are dust-mites with mirrored shells. They move in dense, glinting herds across warm machinery and fresh fracture planes, scraping the reactive film with comb-like mouths. Larger animals follow them: six-legged salt crabs that wedge themselves into corners; pale ribbon-bodied climbers that coil around live cables; slow shield-backed creatures that graze the insides of exhaust ducts. None contains free water. Their tissues are built around oily ionic gels, and their joints are sealed against the desiccating air.

They are everywhere.

They nest beneath steps, cling to lift cages, ride freight baskets, and gather around leaking capacitors. When a district shifts, its animals shift with it in a compact migration. Thousands stream across temporary bridges just ahead of the demolition crews. Small hooked fliers launch themselves into rising gas currents, steer with translucent mineral membranes, and land with faint ticks against the opposite wall.

Predators are fewer but conspicuous. Long, jointed wall-runners patrol beneath balconies, their feet magnetically gripping iron fasteners. Funnel-mouthed ambushers hide in abandoned pipe ends. In the deeper warm galleries, packs of narrow-bodied hunters pursue dust-mite herds by sensing their collective electrical field. Their chases are frantic and nearly silent, a scatter of sparks and trembling cables.

The inhabitants tolerate most of these animals. Some are harvested for filament, oil, shell, or conductive organs. Others are essential to the city’s maintenance. Salt crabs clear reactive buildup from electrical contacts. Ribbon-climbers reveal damaged insulation by gathering around current leaks. Tiny glass-backed scavengers consume grease contaminated with metal dust. There are breeding chambers, veterinary workshops, migration tunnels, and market stalls stacked with labeled cages, but domestication remains incomplete. The fauna behaves less like property than like another mobile district of the city.

Light comes from narrow industrial sources. Furnace mouths glow dull orange behind grates. Inspection lamps cast cones of chalky blue. Signal beads move along cables in red and green sequences. Much of the fissure remains in layered dusk, crowded with silhouettes: gantries behind bridges behind descending elevator frames. The brightest moments come when a fracture exposes raw piezoelectric crystal. For several minutes the stone shines from within, cold and lilac, until the charge drains away.

There is one rule upon which the entire place depends: nothing may be trusted to remain where it is.

Tools are tethered. Furniture is bolted to tracks rather than floors. Every room has at least two exits leading in different directions. Every citizen learns the load paths of their neighborhood and can identify, by touch, which beams are bearing weight. Maps are printed on flexible strips and revised constantly. Addresses include not only district and tier, but the most recent direction of travel.

A second rule is almost as important: nothing useful is allowed to fall.

Nets span the larger voids. Funnels collect dropped fragments and feed them into sorting mills below. Even dust is captured, graded, and returned to the furnaces. Far down the fissure lies the Salvage Dark, where generations of lost objects have accumulated in natural shelves: bolts, broken shells, coins, machine teeth, bones, whole collapsed rooms. Recovery crews descend there in sealed cages, guided by touch-signals transmitted through the cable.

Above all of this, only a slit of sky is visible.

At noon it is a white blade. At night it is black, sometimes crossed by a single star. The summit wind passes over the opening and draws a weak breath through the city, lifting dust, turning the lightest turbines, and setting thousands of suspended scales into motion. Their vibration is too faint to hear in the air, but the walls receive it.

Anyone touching the stone feels the whole hidden city at once: the engines, the elevators, the migrating beasts, the shifting houses, the deep corrective labor of countless mechanisms—and beneath them all, slower and stronger, the mountain closing and opening around its narrow, crowded heart.
