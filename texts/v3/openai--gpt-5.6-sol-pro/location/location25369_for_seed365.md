The town occupies a single high basin, no broader than the interior of a ruined cathedral. Its boundaries are obvious: a ring of newly lifted stone, black-blue and sharp as broken crockery, encloses it on three sides, while the fourth falls away into cloud. Beyond that edge there is no visible lowland—only pale depth, occasional rain moving beneath the town, and the slow backs of distant mountains surfacing like islands.

The basin is young. Its cliffs have not yet softened into slopes. Columns of stone stand at awkward angles, still carrying glassy seams where the mountain split and cooled. Loose shards collect in clean fans beneath them. There is little soil except in sheltered crevices, but wherever soil has begun, vegetation has seized it extravagantly.

Moss pads the walls in saturated greens. Ferns rise shoulder-high from cracks no wider than a finger. Ropes of vine stretch between buildings, not tangled so much as deliberately suspended, heavy with waxy leaves and white, bell-shaped flowers. Small trees grow on the roofs, their roots wrapped around rafters and masonry before descending in braided cords to the ground. Because the air is cool and the sun seldom reaches the basin directly, every leaf seems made to gather dimness. Their surfaces are broad, dark, and faintly silvered, turning the entire town into a machine for catching condensation.

There is no river. Water arrives indirectly.

At night, cold mist pours over the rim and settles against stone, hide, leaf, and tile. Droplets gather along the veins of plants, travel down root-cords, and fall into hundreds of shallow ceramic basins set beneath them. After rain, narrow channels appear in the streets, running urgently for an hour or two before vanishing into cisterns. The central reservoir is scarcely larger than a village pond. It lies beneath a canopy of black reeds and is divided by low stone partitions so that drinking water, washing water, and the water used for cultivating larvae never mingle.

The settlement around it is urban in organization, though not in grandeur. There are streets, courtyards, storehouses, public stairs, drainage laws, neighborhood boundaries, and an elevated market—but everything has been made by hand. Buildings are assembled from stacked volcanic slabs pinned with hardwood pegs. Roofs are steep and woven from reeds, bark, feathers, and sheets of hammered copper so thin they flex in the wind. No house is quite rectangular. The fresh stone resists regularity, and the inhabitants have learned to build into the mountain’s fractures rather than erase them.

The streets are sparse. Structures stand apart from one another, separated by gardens, bare shelves of rock, and deliberate gaps through which animals pass. From above, the town resembles a handful of dark boxes scattered across a green bowl. Its apparent emptiness is misleading.

Nearly every unoccupied surface belongs to fauna.

There are pale, long-legged grazers in the roof gardens, no larger than dogs, stepping between seedlings with the delicacy of insects. Thumb-sized frogs cling to walls in vertical congregations, their translucent bellies showing the slow movement of swallowed moths. Flightless birds with velvet plumage sleep beneath stairs in orderly rows. Beetles occupy the gutters. Silk-worms fill woven frames hung between houses. Tiny shell-bearing creatures move through the moss, leaving lines that shine briefly and then evaporate.

Higher in the vegetation, the abundance becomes difficult to comprehend. Leaves fold and unfold because they are not leaves but resting wings. Seedpods detach and crawl to better soil. What first appears to be lichen on a tree trunk turns its many eyes toward passing shadows. The flowers are crowded with narrow-bodied pollinators whose wings are clear and almost motionless; they drift rather than fly, as though carried by currents no person can feel.

Yet the town is quiet.

The animals have voices, but seldom use them. Predators hunt by vibration, and loudness reveals distance too precisely. The frogs communicate by changing color. Birds court with synchronized movements of their crests. Herd animals stamp patterns too faint for human ears but readily felt through bare feet. Even insects have soft bodies and padded wings. Their overwhelming presence registers as motion at the edge of sight: foliage trembling without wind, a thousand minute repositionings, shadows exchanging places.

Human beings also speak little outdoors. Doors are padded with moss. Potters wrap their mallets in hide. Stone is shaped in a quarry below the basin rim where the mountain’s own creaking conceals the work. The loudest common sound is water dropping into ceramic—isolated notes, irregular and clear. During rain the entire settlement becomes audible at once, roof by roof, channel by channel, and people come outside to listen.

Silence here is not peace. It is camouflage.

The basin’s largest predators are called hollows. They are rarely seen whole. A hollow waits beneath hanging plants or lies flat against a dark wall, matching the color and texture of whatever supports it. Its mouth is a vertical fold; its limbs are thin enough to be mistaken for roots. It hunts sound, unfolding toward a snapped twig or spoken word with terrible speed. Bells are therefore forbidden except at the outer rim, where suspended clay chimes serve as defensive lures. When a hollow enters a street, someone cuts a cord from a safe distance. The bells begin beyond the houses, and the animal follows them toward the cliff.

Nothing is wasted when one falls. Its skin becomes raincloth. Its bones, light and resilient, become roof struts and spear shafts. Its ear-stones—smooth internal nodules that tremble near movement—are mounted beneath floors as alarms.

The townspeople possess few metals and use them sparingly. Copper comes from green veins in the surrounding rock. Iron, found only in fallen stars and traded fragments, is reserved for surgical blades, carving points, and the narrow teeth of saws. Most tools are stone, shell, horn, or fired clay. This has not made the town simple. Its cistern system is intricate. Its gardeners can graft six kinds of condensation vine onto one rootstock. Its builders understand the settling of fresh cliffs and construct walls with sacrificial seams, meant to open harmlessly during tremors. Every house has a painted line showing where it should split.

The mountain is still changing.

At irregular intervals, warmth moves beneath the basin. Frost withdraws from the stones. Threads of steam appear among the ferns, and dormant eggs hatch in numbers. The town then passes into a brief, dense season of pursuit. Soft-winged insects rise from the reservoir; frogs flood the walls; birds descend from the rim; the pale grazers give birth under market stalls. Predators follow. Vines grow visibly from one day to the next, crossing lanes, sealing doors, pulling loose stones into green suspension. For several weeks the inhabitants prune constantly, harvest constantly, and sleep in shifts.

Then the ground cools. The insects vanish. The gardens become motionless again, though never empty.

Because erosion has barely begun, the basin has few gentle places. Gardens are terraced in pockets of crushed rock and animal compost. Stairways end at fresh fissures and are rebuilt elsewhere. Entire rooms sometimes rise several finger-widths in a night. People mark these movements with white clay lines, so older walls carry dense records of displacement: branching diagrams that resemble roots or lightning.

No one agrees whether the mountain is growing or waking.

The oldest civic structure stands at the open edge above the cloud. It is a low platform of fitted slabs with no walls and only a canopy of living branches. Here the town keeps its maps. They are not maps of roads, for roads change too quickly, but maps of relationships: which roof feeds which cistern; which tree roots support which retaining wall; where each colony of silk-worms winters; which predator trails must remain unobstructed; which flowers open before mist and which after.

The maps are braided from colored fiber and altered every morning.

From the platform, the settlement seems intimate enough to hold in two hands: black roofs, green courts, narrow smoke from cook fires, the reservoir reflecting a scrap of gray sky. But the closer one looks, the less contained it becomes. Every wall is habitat. Every gutter is a migration route. Each empty courtyard is crossed by creatures traveling according to calendars older than the town. Under the stones are warm chambers full of eggs. Above the roofs, invisible animals pass through the mist, betrayed only by droplets striking their bodies and outlining them for an instant.

At dusk, the cloud rises through the open side of the basin and fills the streets from the ground upward. Lamps are covered rather than lit brightly. Their glow remains low and amber, illuminating ankles, wet paving stones, and the underside of leaves. People move along rope guides between buildings. Pale grazers stand motionless on roofs. Frogs darken against the walls. Somewhere, too far away to locate, a single clay bell sounds once.

Then it stops.

The town waits, lush and crowded and nearly silent, while something large changes direction in the mist.
