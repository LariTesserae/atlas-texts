The land lies low beneath a permanent roof of weather.

There are no mountains here, only long black swells of recently hardened earth, shallow basins, split ridges, and plains that seem flat until they begin moving underfoot. The ground is young. Its crust has not yet been rounded or taught stillness. Fresh seams open with edges sharp as broken pottery. Shelves of dark stone buckle upward overnight and subside again within a season, leaving pale roots exposed in the cracks. In places, the surface is a dense clay the color of bruised fruit; elsewhere it is porous black rock, warm enough to steam when rain touches it.

The region extends for many days in every direction: a broad, warm depression hemmed not by visible heights but by storm banks and forests too dense to see through. Nothing offers a distant horizon. Space is felt through sound instead. Thunder travels across the basin for minutes. Flocks rise far away like fabric being torn. The calls of unseen animals answer from one wet hollow to another, revealing distances the darkness conceals.

Water is present, but seldom gathered into anything grand. There are no wide lakes and few enduring rivers. Moisture comes in pulses. Warm vapor exhales from fissures, condenses on leaves, and falls in heavy, local showers beneath the canopy even when the sky itself gives no rain. Pools occupy footprints, root bowls, collapsed vents, and the narrow troughs between lava folds. Some last an hour. Others remain for years beneath mats of floating plants, their surfaces hidden except where a drinking animal has opened a black, trembling hole.

Rain arrives abruptly. It may hammer one grove while the next remains dry enough for dust to rise. Water races over the unweathered stone without carving it deeply, spills into cracks, and disappears. Minutes later the ground begins breathing mist. In the warmest hollows, condensation is so constant that every frond has a silver edge and every branch drips with its own private weather.

Above all this hangs darkness.

The sky is not entirely lightless. It is a deep charcoal ceiling scored now and then by silent veins of violet illumination, followed much later by concussions of thunder. A dim greenish haze collects near the ground where spores, insects, and plant vapors catch the weak light. Certain flowers shine around their mouths. Fungi burn in slow blue fans on fallen trunks. The eyes of animals create brief constellations among the stems, then vanish all at once.

But illumination never spreads far. Light belongs to surfaces: a wet shell, the throat of a blossom, the flank of something turning. Nothing is fully revealed. Even at the basin’s brightest hour, the forest remains a collection of fragments.

Vegetation grows with almost indecent force. It does not have the stately architecture of an old forest. There are few immense, ancient trunks. Instead there are millions of young stems competing in the same warm air—reed columns, fleshy saplings, blade grasses, jointed vines, fan-shaped shrubs, and soft trees with bark no tougher than peel. They rise quickly, collapse quickly, and are consumed before decay can make the place quiet.

The plants grow in layers so close they seem poured into one another. Broad leaves overlap at shoulder height. Below them, fernlike fans close around the legs. Above, cords and runners knot between springy crowns, making a second ground where small creatures travel without descending. Some vines tighten when shaken. Some shrubs shed entire branches ahead of a grazing swarm, sacrificing their outer growth to preserve the root mass. Several kinds of grass recoil from heat and crowd toward cool bodies, so that a sleeping animal may wake beneath a woven mantle of pale blades.

The forest is lush but never serene. Growth is one of the basin’s forms of turbulence.

A clearing cannot remain open. Within hours of a treefall, winged seeds descend in sheets. Within days, translucent shoots cover the exposed soil. Within weeks, grazing herds find them and churn the place back into mud. Then burrowing animals raise the mud into cones, fungi lace the cones together, and new thickets take hold. Every patch passes rapidly from bare stone to green abundance to trampled ruin and back again.

The fauna are not simply plentiful; they are the dominant weather of the place.

At ground level, columns of many-legged grazers move through the litter with a continual dry rushing. They are small individually, no longer than a finger, but their migrations can take half a night to pass. Soft-bodied browsers hang from leaves in clusters and drop when alarmed, pattering against the ground like sudden rain. Armored diggers overturn the clay in search of swollen roots. Long-backed animals with six splayed limbs run across the bog crust without breaking it, pursued by low, blind hunters that follow pressure rather than scent.

The trees are crowded with life. Membranous gliders snap between trunks. Hook-footed fruit eaters shriek while hanging upside down in quarrelsome colonies. Pale amphibious things cling to wet bark and inflate resonant throat sacs larger than their heads. Tiny warm-blooded animals nest inside living flowers, pollinating them as they sleep.

Overhead, when there is room to fly, black-winged flocks circle the steam plumes. They descend in spirals to seize the insects swept upward by hot air. Their wings are nearly silent, but their navigation calls are not: each bird emits a hard metallic click, and a flock of ten thousand sounds like stones being shaken in an iron vessel.

Almost nothing here is truly quiet. The basin roars even without storms.

There is the endless percussion of condensation falling from tier to tier. There are mating choruses loud enough to blur the sense of direction. There is the scraping of shells against stone, the pulse of wingbeats, the hiss of opening seedpods, the slap of broad leaves in thermal gusts. Deep beneath these runs a bodily vibration from the earth itself: gas forcing its way through wet fissures, underground chambers collapsing, warm mud rising in slow bubbles.

At intervals, some unknown cue passes through the region. Perhaps it is pressure, perhaps a tremor below hearing. Every calling creature stops together.

For three or four heartbeats the silence is so complete that one can hear droplets striking skin.

Then the entire forest begins again at once, louder than before.

The loudest events are the emergences. After certain rains, the clay splits across whole valleys and winged forms crawl out by the millions. They climb any upright surface, shed their wet skins, and launch into the dark. Predators arrive from everywhere. The air becomes thick with collision, feeding, mating, and falling bodies. Branches bend beneath the weight. Pools turn white with wings. By morning nearly all the emergent creatures are dead, and the vegetation surges on the feast.

No one has built here.

There are no roads, walls, cut timbers, hearths, cairns, or permanent shelters. The land does not preserve intention well. A cleared path closes behind the body that made it. Marks in mud are overwritten by feet, roots, rain, and upheaval. Fallen branches are dragged apart by competing organisms before they can become structures.

Yet there are intimations of craft among the animals. Dark-handed foragers select sharp flakes from fresh stone fractures and use them to split armored fruit. They carry these edges for a few hours, sometimes passing one between members of a group, but abandon them when the stone dulls. Marsh-running creatures probe pools with straight reed stems. A heavy-jawed hunter rolls bitter leaves in clay before pressing them against wounds. Tool use exists here as behavior, not as inheritance carved into the landscape. Nothing accumulates. Nothing becomes a site.

Even fire has little chance to become an artifact. Warm gases sometimes ignite along a fissure, producing low blue flames that run through the wet undergrowth without consuming it. Animals gather near these lines to warm, hunt, or crack hard seeds in the heat. When the gas shifts, the flame sinks back into the earth. No ash circle remains.

The strangest places are the breathing fields. These appear as meadows of low, glossy vegetation, unusually open by the basin’s standards. The ground beneath them rises and falls by the width of a hand, slowly enough to be mistaken for dizziness. Below the thin crust are great bladders of warm vapor threaded with roots. At night, animals gather on the moving surface because biting insects dislike its exhalations. Their combined weight presses fog from vents around the meadow’s edge, making a pale wall that pulses with every communal shift.

Sometimes a bladder ruptures. The field splits with a sound like the breaking of an enormous drum. Mud, roots, startled animals, and luminous spores are thrown into the air. By the next dark cycle, scavengers cover the wound. Within a month it is a pond. Within a year it is forest. Later the ground swells again.

That is the governing rule of the basin: nothing is permitted to finish becoming what it began as.

Pools become thickets before they become lakes. Ridges split before weather can soften them. Forests are buried, then sprout through their own burial. Species erupt into abundance and nearly vanish. The world here is fertile because it is unstable, and unstable because so much life answers every opening at once.

To cross it is not to travel through scenery but through continuous reaction. Leaves fold at one’s warmth. Calls spread outward. Small bodies flee and larger ones turn toward the disturbance. Mud liquefies beneath a misplaced step. Flowers open in the carbon-rich breath behind a passing animal. The landscape notices weight, heat, noise, blood, and stillness.

Especially stillness.

Anything that remains motionless for too long begins to be treated as terrain. Spores settle. Filaments test the skin. Pale mites arrive to graze the spores. Predators arrive for the mites. Vines lean gradually nearer.

In the warm dark, amid the thunder and the ceaseless animal clamor, the land is always beginning—and always beginning over.
