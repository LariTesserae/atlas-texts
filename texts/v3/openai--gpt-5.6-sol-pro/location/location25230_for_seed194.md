### The Hollow City of the Root

It lies beneath a salt field, though “beneath” is misleading. The field is only a skin: a pale mineral crust stretched over an older hollow, and the hollow itself is contained within the buried body of a single plant.

From the surface there is almost nothing to find. No dune grass, no thorn, no lichen. The plain is low and nearly level, worn so long that wind has lost any useful grip on it. Ancient ridges survive only as changes in the grain of the salt. Here and there, black seams describe broad, slow arcs beneath the crust. One seam opens at a narrow fault no wider than a hand.

Cold air breathes from it, dry enough to roughen bone.

The passage descends through layers of petrified rind. There is no light within—not dimness, not starlight carried down a shaft, but complete and settled darkness. Touch becomes the first geography. The walls are fibrous under the fingers, each fiber hard as fired clay and running downward in parallel strands. Farther in, those strands separate, curve, and become columns. The columns branch into beams. Beams divide into ribs, balconies, arches, hanging conduits, lattices, and whole districts of empty rooms.

This is the Root, although it has not touched soil in ages.

It is still alive.

Its life is not maintained by water. There is none in the chambers, and perhaps none anywhere beneath the plain. The plant’s chemistry runs through dense oils, waxes, and slow-moving mineral resins. These circulate in capillaries too fine to see, carrying sulfur, carbon, salts, and traces of metal from the buried rind toward a small, cold knot at the center. A pulse may take thirty years to travel from the outer skin to the innermost bud. Growth is measured not in shoots or seasons but in alterations of pressure: one wall thickening by the width of a fingernail, one sealed chamber opening along an old seam, one avenue bending imperceptibly as its supporting tissue relaxes.

Almost everything inside is plant.

The floors are broad laminations of woodlike tissue, polished not by feet but by age and strain. The streets are gaps deliberately preserved between bundled vascular trunks. Doors are valves in the grain. Stairways are rows of shelf-fungi-shaped excrescences, though no fungi live here; they are organs the Root learned to grow after generations of cutting and binding. Domes resemble the inner surfaces of seed husks. Bridges are braided tendons. Thin partitions of amber pith divide neighborhoods from one another, translucent in substance but useless in the absolute dark.

The city is dense in design and nearly empty in occupation.

A traveler may cross a plaza large enough to contain a market and touch nothing but floor until reaching the opposite wall. Corridors branch with municipal precision into hundreds of chambers, yet most chambers hold only dry air. Towers rise upside down into the hollow, suspended from the ceiling on living cables, but no lamps burn in their windows. The great conduits are hollow. The cisterns were never meant for liquid. They contain curled ribbons of resin, stored warmth, dormant spores, or nothing at all.

There are no animals here. No insects bore the timber. No worms aerate its buried margins. No bats hang beneath the arches, no mites inhabit the dust, and no unseen eyes follow movement through the black. Decay has therefore never learned to hurry. A fallen fiber remains where it fell for ten thousand years. Pollen lies in corners like fine ash, neither eaten nor rotted. The ecology is almost purely botanical: root feeding leafless stem, stem sheltering dormant bud, bud producing chemical grains that drift through the chambers and settle onto receptive scars.

Pollination occurs through architecture.

At immense intervals, chambers contract in sequence. The contraction forces dry air through the streets, lifting clouds of black pollen from floor grooves and carrying them toward deep, cup-shaped organs in another district. Each such breath may last several days. The wind it creates is cold and sharp with resin. When it stops, the air becomes perfectly still again, and the pollen descends for months.

These breaths are one source of the city’s noise, but not the chief one.

The Root is loud.

In darkness, its sound seems too large for the enclosed place. The entire buried organism is under tension from the salt plain above. Fibers twist against one another. Vast wooden membranes flex by fractions of a degree. Resin plugs fracture in the conduits with reports like distant stone hammers. A single structural adjustment can pass through the city as a rolling detonation: first a low groan underfoot, then a sequence of cracks racing along the avenues, then a long, trumpetlike bellow from the hollow towers.

Nothing moves quickly enough to see, yet the noise suggests continual catastrophe.

Some sounds repeat with such regularity that they serve as clocks. From the western galleries comes a deep strike every nine hours, produced by a tendon slipping over a mineral nodule. In the central wards, three ascending booms mark the cooling of the outer crust after the unseen surface day. A thin shriek travels through the upper ducts once every few weeks. The oldest avenues mutter without rhythm, a continuous subsonic strain felt in teeth and joints.

Those who shaped the Root—if they were truly inhabitants rather than an earlier phase of the plant itself—possessed little machinery. Their marks remain everywhere. Stone blades were used to score young tissue. Wedges of horn-hard bark held passages open while they lignified. Cords were twisted from internal fibers and looped around growing branches, training them into arches and grids. Clay was absent, metal rare, fire impossible in the close, resinous atmosphere. Construction was horticulture conducted by touch.

A wall was planted as a line of wounds.  
A doorway was pinched into a sapling chamber and kept from closing.  
A public square required centuries of pruning.

Small tools remain in niches: black glass flakes, awls made from hardened thorns, mallets grown as dense knobs and cut from their stalks. There are no bones, no leather, no signs that the builders ate or slept in an animal manner. Their “houses” are too narrow in some places and impossibly tall in others. Many contain vertical grooves leading from floor to ceiling, as though their occupants clung to walls or were themselves rooted there.

It may be that the old citizens were temporary organs of the Root: upright stalks capable of cutting, binding, and planning, shed when their work was complete. If so, the city was never separate from its population. Its builders became its beams, its doors, its empty air.

The oldest district lies at the lowest point, though the difference in elevation is slight. There the ancient tissue has settled almost flat. Ceilings have descended until streets are long slots between compressed layers, and buildings have softened into horizontal bands. Erosion is internal here. Without water or animals, surfaces are worn by microscopic flexing, by pollen passing over them, by the slow migration of salts through the grain. Inscriptions have become smooth depressions. Sculptures have lost faces, limbs, and finally outlines. Even broken edges have rounded into calm, final curves.

Nothing looks ruined in the ordinary sense. Ruin implies an interruption. This place has continued so long that collapse and maintenance are the same process.

When a chamber closes, its contents are pressed into the wall and become reinforcement. When a tower tears loose, the Root grows a lip around the wound and turns the fallen structure into a new district floor. When an avenue narrows past usefulness, adjacent capillaries enlarge and take over its function. The city does not preserve its plan. It digests it slowly.

Near the center is the only space that feels crowded.

Thousands of leaflike plates surround a pit no deeper than a basin. Each plate is thin, cool, and utterly black. They overlap in spirals, forming a bud large enough to fill a room but small enough to embrace. The plates do not open. They have never opened within any remembered interval. Fine resin rises between them and hardens into glossy beads, each containing a faint chemical warmth.

This is the Root’s future, stored but not begun.

The bud waits for a condition the world above may no longer be able to provide: rain, perhaps, though its present chemistry would be poisoned by water; a certain pressure of vanished gases; the vibration of neighboring roots; a dawn bright enough to penetrate the salt. It may be waiting for no real event at all. Plants can inherit instructions long after the world that gave those instructions has ended.

Around the bud, the loudness diminishes. The central plates absorb the city’s groans and turn them into a low, continuous hum. Touching one, a person would feel the entire buried organism: the distant crust contracting in cold, the resin inching through capillaries, the pollen settling in the abandoned plazas, the old structures folding gently into newer tissue.

The place is hidden not merely by the salt above it, but by its scale of time. One can walk its avenues in hours and still fail to witness a single action. One can sit through a hundred thunderous cracks and see nothing change. The city’s events are distributed across lifetimes, while its sounds arrive all at once.

Eventually the surface fault will close. Fibers are already growing across it, pale and blind, narrowing the only entrance by a hair’s width every few years. The salt will settle over the scar. The cold breath will cease.

Inside, the avenues will remain hollow. The towers will strain and bellow. Black pollen will lift in the next long exhalation and drift through a city with no animals, no lamps, and perhaps no distinction between citizen, building, and soil.

At the center, in perfect darkness, the unopened bud will continue preparing for spring.
