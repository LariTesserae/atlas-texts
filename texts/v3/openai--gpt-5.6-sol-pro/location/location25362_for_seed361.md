High on the worn crown of a tropical plateau, where the mountains have been eroded almost to the memory of mountains, there is a narrow opening in the stone. It resembles a shadow cast by a leaning slab. From three paces away it is easy to miss; from directly above it appears too shallow to contain anything. Yet the opening gives onto a descending cleft, warm and breathing, and the cleft opens in turn into a hidden basin no larger than a village courtyard.

The basin is not a cave. It has a sky.

Its walls are the roots of an ancient ravine, polished down through ages until every ledge and fracture has become smooth, low, and final. The rock is old red gneiss veined with black glass and pale mineral seams. Nothing here is sharp unless it has grown recently. Water has rounded the floor into overlapping bowls, lips, runnels, and shallow channels. The largest pool might cover a dining table; most are no more than wet handprints in the stone.

Rain itself is occasional. The plateau above may remain dry for weeks, its grasses yellowing in warm high-altitude wind, while this little interior stays damp from condensation. Air drawn through cracks in the mountain cools during the night and emerges along the basin walls as beads of water. At dawn, every mineral vein sweats. Droplets gather, travel through grooves too subtle to see, and fall one by one into the bowls below.

By midday the basin is hot. Sunlight enters plainly through its narrow open roof—not mystical light, not perpetual twilight, but ordinary blue-white daylight, moving predictably from one wall to another. For two hours the whole floor is illuminated. The wet rock shines brown, copper, and muted rose. Then the sun passes beyond the rim, leaving brightness in the sky while the basin itself returns to warm shade.

Vegetation grows richly where it grows, but it does not fill the place. There are wide intervals of bare stone. Ferns occupy only the condensation seams, each colony dense as green fur and sharply bounded by dryness. Small trees rise from three deep pockets where sediment has survived: crooked, glossy-leaved things with silver bark and roots that descend through holes in the rock. Their crowns do not meet. Between them lie broad open surfaces stained with algae, old pollen, and the faint white tracks of mineral-bearing water.

Vines are absent from most of the walls. Moss avoids the sunlit center. Orchids grow in isolated knots, extravagantly flowering over emptiness. The landscape has the sparseness of an arrangement made over immense time: one tree, one pool, a shelf of ferns, twenty feet of bare stone, then a single crimson fungus attached to a crack.

The animals make the basin seem far more crowded than it looks.

They are everywhere, though seldom still enough to be counted. Thumb-sized gliders launch from the upper walls and circle the opening in shrieking flocks. Narrow frogs cling beneath ledges, their inflated throats producing notes disproportionate to their bodies—horn blasts, rattles, metallic knocks. Beetles drill in the dead silver bark. Pale crabs no larger than fingernails patrol the wet channels, raising translucent claws against passing mites. Clouds of midges form precise vertical columns in the warm shafts of light, dissolve when a shadow crosses them, then reassemble elsewhere.

The pools are louder than their size should permit. Each contains layers of life: thread-worms, flat swimming larvae, shelled grazers, quick black fry that cannot have reached the basin by any ordinary stream. When condensation begins after sunset, the first falling drops trigger a frenzy. Aquatic bodies strike the surface. Frogs call. Winged predators leave cracks in the walls. Something beneath the algae makes a repeated rasping sound like a saw drawn slowly through wet wood.

Above all this is the constant collective roar of wings. It rises and subsides but never ceases. At certain hours it becomes difficult to speak. The acoustics of the basin compress every sound; a chirp at one end returns as a chorus from the other. The hidden place feels full not because large animals occupy it, but because every available scale has an animal moving through it. A hawk would find little room here. An ant can cross whole nations.

There are larger inhabitants, though they are rarely seen clearly. A long, warm-bodied grazer lives in the wall fissures and emerges at night to strip algae from the open stone. It has six gripping feet and a ribbed back that collects dew. Its passage is marked by clean arcs through the green film. Small orange hunters follow it, feeding on creatures exposed by its tongue. Once or twice each season, a plated animal descends from the plateau through the entrance, drinks from every pool in sequence, lays a chain of soft eggs beneath the central shelf, and climbs out again before morning. For weeks afterward, nearly every predator in the basin alters its habits.

Among the rock bowls stand scattered constructions.

At first they seem to be mineral formations: seven low arches, each the height of a hand, made from a gray substance without grain or seam. One arch protrudes from a pool. Two are embedded sideways in the wall. Another has been nearly enclosed by the slow swelling of a tree root. There is also a thin column under the eastern ledge and, near the basin’s center, a disk flush with the stone.

They are older than the current ecology and newer than the rock. Lichen will not grow on them. Water passing through an arch sometimes emerges warmer, sometimes colder, and occasionally does not emerge until days later. The amount is always conserved, but not necessarily the interval. A drop may enter during a dry afternoon and fall out during last night’s condensation—if “last night” is understood from the basin’s interior rather than from the plateau above.

The animals have accommodated this.

One species of frog deposits its eggs only in delayed water. A beetle times its emergence to the disk’s nearly imperceptible vibration. The black fry in the isolated bowls are connected by the arches, though the connections do not appear to pass through ordinary space. Predators wait at the wrong pool and are often correct. During the brief rains, the structures route excess water into surfaces that look solid, preventing the basin from flooding; in the dry season they return some of it as fog.

No one knows whether this is their intended function. The technology may be irrigation, computation, weather storage, or the remains of an instrument whose active element happens to be water and living metabolism. The constructions show no controls. They answer patterns, not commands. A sequence of footsteps can cause the column to darken. Three species calling at once may make one arch emit a cool blue shadow despite the ordinary daylight. Removing an animal from the basin sometimes changes the geometry of the pools by a few millimeters. Returning it restores them.

The place appears to treat its ecology as part of its machinery.

This is its deepest rule: nothing here is merely resident. The frogs, midges, roots, condensate, echoes, and predators occupy functional positions in a system that predates their present forms. Evolution has grown into the scattered devices just as roots grow into cracks. The devices, in turn, have selected for noise, timing, migration, and abundance. Silence is not natural here. On the rare occasion when the animals all become quiet—usually just before a violent plateau storm—the arches begin to hum, supplying a low substitute vibration until the calls resume.

Storms transform the basin without enlarging it. Rain pours through the roof in silver cords. The dry expanses vanish under a skin of fast water. Ferns flatten; pools overflow; crabs climb the trees. The seven arches brighten only by becoming more reflective, throwing ordinary lightning back and forth with impossible precision. Water rushes into channels carved long before any surviving species evolved, circulates around the disk, and disappears.

An hour later, the basin is merely damp again.

Leaves rise. Wings restart. The pools contain slightly different populations. A flower that was closed is open. Somewhere in the stone, water has been stored outside the sequence of the weather.

From the plateau, none of this can be heard clearly. The opening releases only a faint, continuous buzz, easily mistaken for wind passing over dry grass. The hidden basin remains warm beneath the high open air, a loud and crowded world folded into a handful of ancient stone: deeply eroded, nearly finished in shape, yet furiously active in every living detail.
