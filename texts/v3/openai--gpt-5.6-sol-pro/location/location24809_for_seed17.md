The sea begins above the mountains.

It is not a sea gathered in a basin but a planetary layer of water suspended at altitude: a cold, clear envelope several kilometers deep, wrapped around a world of high plateaus and unfinished stone. Beneath it, the ground rarely descends toward anything that could be called a lowland. Ridges, mesas, volcanic tables, and black escarpments stand at elevations where, on another world, only snow and thin air would remain. Here they are drowned beneath an overhead pressure of pale water.

There is no visible surface from the bottom. Looking upward, one sees brightness rather than sky: an immense, evenly luminous ceiling, silver-white by day and iron-blue during the long night. Light penetrates improbably well. It falls through the water in broad, almost vertical sheets without the green murk of a living ocean. There are no forests of kelp, no meadows, no drifting veils of algae. Nothing roots in the cracks. Nothing softens the new rock.

The land is young and has kept its edges.

Cliffs rise in raw facets, their break lines sharp enough to seem machined. Fields of shattered basalt lie where they fell, every slab still angular, every crevice clean. Volcanic glass forms entire slopes, black at a distance and translucent smoky brown at the margins. Elsewhere the ground is pale anorthosite, blue granite, or immense shelves of metallic sulfides that gleam like old mirrors. Sediment exists only as a sparse flour in hollows. The cold water has little chemical appetite, and its circulation is so perfectly balanced that it carries almost nothing from one place to another.

There are currents, but they do not wander. They occupy fixed routes.

A current may pour forever through a canyon without widening it, its speed and direction held in exact equilibrium by pressure, salinity, and the rotation of the world. At the edge of a plateau, water streams upward in a permanent transparent cataract, climbing rather than falling as denser layers descend invisibly around it. Long ribbons of suspended ice mark the boundaries between these flows. They neither melt nor grow. Needle crystals remain poised in place, pointing along forces older than any surviving record.

The emptiness is difficult to comprehend. Distances that appear to be tens of kilometers are hundreds. A black ridge seen across the plain may take weeks to reach even in a powered vessel. Between landmarks there is mostly clear water and naked rock: no marine snow, no leaves, no clouds of silt. The clarity enlarges everything. Mountains do not fade naturally into haze; they remain visible as dim blue silhouettes long after scale says they should have vanished.

Yet animal life is abundant.

It does not carpet the terrain. It gathers into moving constellations separated by enormous vacant tracts.

The commonest creatures are the bell colonies: chains of clear, muscular chambers, each colony hundreds of meters long but scarcely more substantial than a net. They feed not on plants but on dissolved salts, free electrons, and slow chemical differences between layers of water. Their bodies contain lattices of conductive protein and grains of magnetite. When they cross a current boundary, the colony lights from end to end in a muted white pulse. Seen at a distance, a migrating population resembles a field of stars passing sideways through the sea.

There are heavier animals on the plateaus. Six-legged grazers—though there is nothing recognizable for them to graze—move their ceramic mouthparts across exposed ore. They scrape films of bacteria and consume the mineral beneath as well, leaving thin parallel scores in the stone. The microbes are not photosynthetic and produce no green or brown stain. They exist as transparent skins only a few cells thick, harvesting radiation from the rock and catalytic energy from metal-water boundaries.

Predators are rare in number but enormous in reach. Pale raylike animals wait along escarpments, each held motionless in a permanent upwelling. Their wings may span a kilometer, but they are nearly invisible except for the rows of red pressure organs along their undersides. They do not hunt by pursuit. They alter the conductivity of the water around them and draw bell colonies off their migration lines. A feeding may last a century. The ray never visibly moves.

Life here has adapted not merely to cold but to constancy. Most creatures have no season, no juvenile migration, no urgent metabolism. Some hearts beat once every few hours. Eggs are anchored in mineral cracks and open at exact pressure intervals, not after a measured duration. An individual may spend decades crossing a single valley. Death is generally sudden: a structural failure, an encounter with a predator, an electrical discharge. There is little decay. Empty shells accumulate in small white drifts beneath the fixed currents, still sharp and clean after millennia.

Across this almost pristine world stand scattered industrial structures.

They are too few to make the sea feel inhabited. One may travel over a plateau the size of a country and find only a single tower.

The towers are built of dull nickel alloy, broad at the base and narrowing into stacks of concentric vanes. Their surfaces carry no corrosion and almost no ornament. Cables descend from them into drill holes so deep that the surrounding rock trembles faintly when touched. Other installations crouch in trenches: pump houses, pressure foundries, sealed transformer yards, chains of turbines set directly into the fixed currents. Some are no larger than huts. Others are factories kilometers long, assembled from repeating ribs and windowless chambers.

No roads connect them. There are only sparse guide pylons hammered into high ridges, each holding a steady amber lamp. Many factories appear abandoned, but their machinery continues. Wheels turn. Pistons cycle. Intake shutters open and close with faultless regularity. Mechanical arms sort nodules from empty conveyor belts. In casting halls, furnaces—cold furnaces using pressure and electrical fields rather than heat—compress dissolved metals into flawless ingots. The ingots stack themselves in vaults. No carrier comes to collect them.

The machines may be maintaining the ocean’s equilibrium, or exploiting it, or causing it. The distinction has been lost.

Their combined sound fills the water.

It is not a local noise that grows louder near an engine and diminishes with distance. The entire suspended sea acts as a resonant body. Turbine vibration enters the high plateaus, crosses the stone, and returns through the water. Every pump, every compressor, every oscillating tower contributes to a planetary standing wave.

The result is deafening.

A deep mechanical thunder exists everywhere, even in regions where no structure is visible. It passes through bone more readily than through the ear. Over it lie stable bands of sound: the endless horn-note of the northern current, the rapid iron hammering of distant pressure mills, high turbine shrieks bent into chords by the density layers. Cliffs hum at their characteristic frequencies. Fields of volcanic glass ring continuously, each shard sustaining a thin tone without visibly moving.

Nothing begins. Nothing stops. There is no crescendo and no silence.

Animals use the noise as geography. Bell colonies align themselves with the bass pulse. Mineral grazers communicate by briefly interrupting a local vibration with their bodies. The great rays are tuned to the factories and would perhaps become helpless if the engines ceased. Even the smallest microbes arrange their internal filaments according to the standing waves. What would be intolerable noise to an outsider is, here, the framework within which perception evolved.

Near a working installation, the water is bright with controlled electrical discharge. Blue-white arcs creep over insulated housings, slow and thick as luminous vines, though nothing botanical grows anywhere. Warning lamps burn at ordinary intensities: amber, red, clean white. Their practicality is strangely reassuring. Handrails remain where hands might use them. Number plates mark pressure doors. In certain airless maintenance chambers, tools hang in orderly ranks, each locked to the wall against current and vibration.

The few signs of former operators are ambiguous. A helmet rests in one observation blister, its faceplate turned toward the illuminated water. Three seats stand before dead consoles in a control station buried high on a cliff. A row of narrow shelters contains heating pipes, lockers, and bunks, all too small for the immense local fauna and proportioned approximately for human bodies. No remains have been found. No personal marks survive beyond shallow scratches on one door—possibly writing, possibly stress fractures.

Because the environment does not change, these absences do not age into ruins. Dust never settles. Paint does not peel. A dropped wrench may remain suspended in the same eddy beside the same stair for ten thousand years.

There is weather, though it has no unpredictability. Above each major plateau sits a permanent pressure system. In some regions, a wall of denser water travels around a circular route and passes the same peaks at the same interval, pressing visibility into wavering bands. Elsewhere, needle ice falls upward in a perpetual storm. The needles rise from a shadowed ravine, rotate through a bright layer, and descend miles away, returning through cracks under the plateau. Not one is lost from the cycle.

The temperature is uniformly near freezing. Where industry releases heat, another mechanism extracts precisely the same amount. Warmth cannot accumulate. Touching an active engine housing gives no sensation of heat, only violent vibration. Exhaled gases condense at once into silver globes and drift along established paths. Open water remains liquid because of pressure and salts, not comfort.

At the highest summits, the water thins into layers crowded with bubbles. These peaks come closest to the invisible upper boundary of the sea. Their crowns carry isolated stations shaped like broad, inverted funnels. The funnels collect something descending from above—light, charged particles, perhaps fresh water—and feed it into buried conduits. Around them, shoals of small armored animals orbit with machine-like regularity. Their shells flash in normal daylight: gray, pearl, cobalt, then gray again.

From such a summit, one can look across a drowned continent.

Black mountain chains run to the curve of the world. Fixed currents appear as faint distortions crossing the clear expanse. Here and there, an industrial tower stands with its amber beacon. Farther away, bell colonies pulse like slow lightning. Great animals hang over abyssal valleys. All of it is immersed in the same relentless brightness and the same crushing, harmonious roar.

The place feels newly made and impossibly old at once. Its fractures are fresh, its machines unworn, its ecosystems complete. It possesses no visible history because history requires alteration. Events may occur—animals feed, ingots are cast, travelers cross the plateaus—but the landscape absorbs them without becoming different.

Even catastrophe may already have happened here.

It may simply still be happening, held forever at equilibrium: the flood never finishing its rise, the factories never completing their task, the mountains never losing their first sharp edge, and the whole high ocean ringing like a struck bell that cannot decay.
