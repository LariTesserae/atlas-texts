The plateau is called the Dry Crown, though there is no single summit and nothing here resembles a crown except its height.

It occupies the upper atmosphere of a small, old world: a broad, nearly level tableland raised above the cloudless mineral basins by cliffs so weathered they have lost the appearance of having broken. From a distance the plateau seems cut from dark felt. Closer, it resolves into vegetation—continuous, layered, and immeasurably ancient.

There is almost no water. Frost does not form. Breath does not cloud. The few hydrogen-bearing minerals were stripped from the surface ages ago, and nothing living here expects rain.

The plants use another intimacy.

Their tissues circulate a cold ionic sap held in channels finer than molecules ought to permit. It is neither quite liquid nor quite charge: a procession of coordinated defects moving through crystalline fibers. Cut a stem and nothing spills. Instead, the severed surface pales as its internal geometry rearranges, sealing itself with a faint blue discharge. The local botanists—if “botanist” still means anything in a city grown from the same tissues—say the sap is an absence passed from cell to cell.

Vegetation fills everything, but not lushly. There are no jungles, no green profusion. The Crown is open and austere. Low black mats run for kilometers across the ancient plain, interrupted by widely spaced structures that might be trees, towers, organs, or civic monuments. Each stands alone in a generous territory of dimness.

The ground itself is alive.

It consists of overlapping plates of root-bark, silver-gray and hard as fired ceramic. Their edges have been polished by millions of years of abrasive wind until they fit together like old paving stones. Beneath them lies neither soil nor bedrock but further strata of former growth: compressed vascular forests, obsolete sensory layers, pollen archives, buried transit fibers. At great depth, the plateau’s geology and its botany can no longer be separated. Mountains were consumed and replaced without changing their outward shape.

Here and there the living pavement opens into long grooves. These appear to be erosion channels, but no water cut them. They are migration routes, worn by generations of armored grazers and by the slow retraction of the ground during the planet’s cold season. Some grooves are broad enough to hold neighborhoods.

The city is not concentrated. It extends across the plateau in districts separated by kilometers of low growth and empty air. A residential quarter may consist of six hollow trunks arranged around an acoustic clearing. A market may be a single ribbed canopy beneath which caravans tether their animals to conductive roots. Civic buildings rise as pale, asymmetrical spires, each grown over centuries from a buried knot of programmable cambium. Their windows are not openings but translucent patches where the wall has been instructed to become an eye.

No masonry has been laid here in recorded history. Construction is done by persuasion.

Street-planners send patterned vibrations into the substrate. Over years, the ground thickens along the desired routes, raises windbreaks, extrudes arches, or grows chambers with predetermined resonances. Older infrastructure is vast and almost invisible. Transit signals pass through the roots faster than sound, and goods vanish into sealed buds at one district, later emerging from fruiting capsules many kilometers away. The process does not seem to move objects through intervening space. Attempts to observe the transit from within have yielded only duplicate instruments, lost memories, and once, a room full of ancient insects thought extinct before the city was founded.

The inhabitants therefore treat the system as municipal rather than miraculous. They complain when it is late.

Most towers are thin because the air is thin. They branch only near their tops, spreading vast, sparse crowns into the twilight. Their leaves are black on one side and mirror-bright on the other. During the dim day they hold the black face toward the weak sun, harvesting heat and charged particles. At night they turn in unison, exposing their reflective undersides to the stars. Seen from the lowlands, the entire plateau flashes silver as though a second, horizontal sky has opened above the cliffs.

There are brighter organisms, but most brightness is functional. Orange nodules mark safe paths. Violet bands warn of predatory root systems. Small blue flowers appear around recent deaths, feeding not on decomposition but on the electrical disorganization of dying tissue. Their petals close when approached by anyone carrying a coherent memory.

The Dry Crown is cool throughout the year. The wind is a permanent fact: a hard, dry stream pouring across the plateau and over its edges. It has been blowing so long that exposed forms have settled into final shapes. Towers lean in agreement. Branches grow as narrow blades aligned with prevailing currents. Even moving animals are wedge-shaped.

The wind makes the Crown loud.

Every plant is tense with fiber. Every hollow stem is an instrument. The low mats hiss like distant surf, though no sea exists on the planet. Groves of pipe-trees produce sustained chords deep enough to tremble in the ribs. Seed rattles race across the plain in waves. Ridge-vines crack like rigging, and the tall civic spires answer one another with slow metallic booming.

The city uses this noise as infrastructure.

Announcements are sung into the wind by fan-leaved organs mounted high on the towers. Traffic is directed by rhythm. Public time is kept through a sequence of enormous root pulses that travel beneath the pavement and make cups, tools, claws, and teeth vibrate at the hour. During festivals, whole districts retune themselves. Walls loosen, branches stiffen, and the city becomes an orchestra audible from the basins far below.

Silence is correspondingly alarming. When the wind drops, doors close. Grazing animals kneel and press their jawbones to the ground. Predators stop hunting. The rare windless intervals are called the Listening, because in them a much older sound becomes perceptible beneath the city: a faint, regular knock rising from kilometers below the living strata. No excavation has reached its source. The deepest bore returned to the surface from directly overhead.

Fauna are abundant, though often difficult to distinguish from the plants they browse.

Long-legged shardbacks move in loose herds over the open mats, clipping mineral fronds with rotating mouthparts. Their bodies are lattices rather than solids, allowing the wind to pass through them. Small bell-winged creatures swarm around the high crowns and make no sound of their own; instead they alter the towers’ tones by landing on particular branches. Flocks can change a grove from a minor chord to a warning siren in seconds.

There are burrowing animals, though “burrowing” means slipping between layers of the living pavement. Pale ribbon-beasts travel through vascular seams and surface at night to breathe charged air. Their predators stand motionless above likely exits, roots temporarily grown around their feet. Some hunters wait for years. They resemble dead trees until the ground beneath them flinches.

More conspicuous are the sailers: broad, six-limbed animals that climb the westernmost spires at dusk, unfold translucent membranes, and let the wind carry them across entire districts. They navigate by city-song. When a tower is damaged or mistuned, sailers strike buildings in numbers, so repair crews are followed by circling flocks impatient for the correct note.

Nothing here drinks. Animals obtain charge-rich resin, scrape salts from fruiting bodies, or host gardens inside their hollow organs. Many do not eat in the usual sense. They carry fragments of the plateau’s metabolism with them, exchanging tired tissues for fresh ones at communal grafting stations. A shardback may arrive with six legs and leave with eight, having surrendered a damaged breathing lattice to become nursery material.

Death is not rare, but decay is. Without water, rot is slow and specialized. The dead become increasingly light as scavenger lichens remove useful electrical asymmetries. Eventually only a lace of inert mineral remains. Wind lifts it, tumbles it across the plain, and lodges it against the leeward sides of buildings. Some of the city’s oldest walls have accumulated pale drifts composed entirely of ancestral skeletons.

Beyond the inhabited districts, the plateau becomes flatter and older. The towers thin out. The living pavement is folded into huge, shallow ripples, each one perhaps a day’s walk from crest to crest. These are not dunes. They are growth rings of the entire Crown, laid down during climatic eras so ancient that the present species appear only in the uppermost fraction.

At the plateau’s center stands—or grows—the Hollow Meridian, the oldest known structure. It is a ring several kilometers across, made of upright trunks spaced widely enough that the landscape continues through them. Each trunk is dead, polished, and perfectly smooth. Nothing grows on their surfaces. No animal touches them. Yet at certain hours they cast shadows in directions unrelated to the sun.

The city’s root network converges beneath the ring, and all long-distance transit is said to pass through it. So are civic memories, weather predictions, construction patterns, and the personalities of those entrusted with maintaining the Crown. The Meridian may be technology. It may be the fossilized reproductive organ of a planetary plant. It may be the reason the plateau was raised in the first place.

On the coldest nights, the trunks begin to turn.

They rotate too slowly to see directly, but the gaps between them narrow, opening a perspective onto somewhere that is not the other side of the ring. Witnesses describe additional plateaus beneath unfamiliar stars, each covered in the same black vegetation, each with a sparse city singing into a dry wind. Sometimes the distant city sings back.

By morning the view is gone. The Crown resumes its ordinary activity: shardbacks in the migration grooves, sailers launching from the towers, market buds opening, roads slowly growing toward neighborhoods not yet built. The wind pours over the ancient edge. Leaves pivot. Spires boom. Beneath the living pavement, the deep knock continues at its patient interval.

Nothing is hurried here, but nothing is still.
