At the top of a warm, cloud-buried escarpment is a hollow no larger than a ruined cloister. It is not a crater, though from above it resembles one: an oval room of dark stone, bounded on three sides by low, inward-leaning ridges and on the fourth by an abrupt drop into vapor. The ridges are too new to have names. Their surfaces are smooth in some places and granular in others, as if the rock has not yet decided what kind of rock it will be.

The hollow is dim even at midday. Cloud presses across it in slow, wet folds, snagging on every projection. Light enters without a visible source, diluted to a gray-green radiance that makes distances uncertain. A ledge ten paces away can appear to be a far wall; a passing moth can cast a shadow like a bird.

It rains frequently, but seldom downward.

Moisture condenses first as a shining skin over the ground. Beads rise from this film and hang ankle-high, enlarging until the air can no longer support them. Some fall. Some drift toward the cliff. Others burst soundlessly, leaving brief rings of coolness in the warm atmosphere. The hollow’s one pool is shallow and restless, continually forming along a seam in the floor and continually draining through pores too small to see. Its shoreline migrates by several handspans each hour. There are no channels yet. Water has begun to imagine channels, tracing silver hesitations across the stone, but the land is so young that no course has earned permanence.

The stone itself is being made.

Along the back ridge, glossy black nodules push out of pale mineral paste with the patient pressure of teeth emerging through gums. Sheets of translucent basalt blister, cloud internally, and harden with soft ticks. Pebbles assemble from airborne grit. Cracks open by a finger’s width, exhale mineral-scented heat, and seal again before rain can deepen them. Nothing here has been weathered for long. There are stains but no patina, scratches but no gullies, fallen fragments but almost no soil. Erosion is present only as a proposal.

Because of this, vegetation is sparse. A few copper-colored sedges grow where the moving pool most often pauses. They have no true roots; each plant grips the rock with a disk of braided fibers and can release itself when the ground warms, shuffling a few centimeters toward cooler stone. Under overhangs, colonies of translucent liverwort spread in separate, hand-sized patches. Their cells hold dark droplets that swallow light. After rain, wiry stalks rise from them, release a single puff of blue spores, and collapse within minutes.

There are also seven small trees, if trees is the right word. They stand widely apart, each no taller than a person. Their trunks are porous white columns, warm to the touch, and their crowns consist of a few black membranes held open like broken umbrellas. They do not grow from seeds. During certain storms, one of the membranes detaches, sails to bare stone, and slowly mineralizes into a new trunk. The oldest tree bears a ring of tiny bronze tags around its base, though there is no writing on them.

Plant life is scarce, but animal life is everywhere.

The pool boils with glass-backed skimmers, small six-finned creatures that breathe through bright filaments along their sides. They leap from the water in synchronized sheets whenever the stone beneath them begins to set. Pale crabs roam far from the pool, carrying droplets in bowls formed by their own upturned shells. Flights of needle-winged insects occupy the warmer air near the cracks. Their wings are too narrow for ordinary flight; they hold themselves aloft by vibrating against some local property of space, producing a continuous, metallic chord.

Larger animals pass through the cloud.

Long-legged grazers descend the ridges in loose groups, placing split hooves carefully on the raw geology. They feed not on the sparse plants but on fresh mineral foam, scraping it from the ground with comb-like teeth. Their hides are naked and charcoal gray, veined with faint amber light. Each animal is accompanied by a cloud of tiny red mites that clean its skin and, when danger approaches, rise together into the shape of a much larger animal.

The predators are low, soft-bodied things called folds by those who once worked here. A fold resembles a strip of wet fabric until it moves. It advances by transferring its body from one patch of shadow to another, crossing the illuminated ground without visibly occupying the distance between. When a fold hunts, the needle-wings change pitch. The grazers freeze. The crabs overturn their water bowls. For several seconds the whole hollow seems to listen to itself.

Then everything moves at once.

The loudness of the place is not merely the loudness of life. Beneath the stone runs a deep, irregular concussion, like immense doors being shut far underground. The newly forming ridge gives off sharp reports. Water hisses into hot pores. Insects hold their ceaseless iron note. Grazers knock their teeth against stone. From beyond the cliff comes thunder without lightning: cloud collapsing into the valley and being driven upward again.

Against this noise stand the structures.

There are five, scattered around the hollow with no obvious symmetry. Each is a narrow arch about twice a person’s height, made from a dull material that looks neither manufactured nor natural. Rain does not wet it. Moss does not approach it. Seen directly, an arch appears solid and slightly bent; seen from the corner of the eye, it extends upward through the cloud farther than the mountain itself.

No two arches open onto the same thing.

One frames the nearby ridge exactly as expected, except that the ridge inside the opening is older, furrowed by streams and thick with trees. Through another is visible a black sea under stars. The third shows the hollow empty of life and filled to its rim with clear water. The fourth contains only a pale brightness that makes animals restless. The fifth appears to open onto the viewer’s own back, at a distance of several steps.

These are not gateways in the ordinary sense. Nothing can cross them whole. Water thrown through an arch returns as warm mist. A stone emerges as a handful of soil, or as several stones with tiny differences, or not at all. Insects sometimes fly through and return with unfamiliar patterns on their wings. The long-legged grazers avoid the arches, but the folds sleep beneath them.

At the center of the hollow is a sixth construction, though little of it can be seen: three metal petals embedded flush with the ground around a circular patch of unfinished rock. At intervals, the petals separate by a hair’s breadth. The entire hollow then becomes momentarily silent—not quiet, but deprived of the possibility of sound. During this interval, the central stone rises, softens, and rearranges itself. New mineral veins appear. The pool shifts. A ridge gains a few centimeters. Occasionally a living creature is duplicated, with the copy standing beside the original in bewildered stillness.

Then sound returns as a single blow.

The place seems to be an experiment in making landscapes, but the experiment has outlasted whoever began it. The machines do not maintain a finished environment. They continually revise an unfinished one. Weathering is sampled, tested, and partly undone. Species arrive before their niches exist. Water searches for drainage through rock that changes its permeability. Predators exploit shadows whose positions are calculated by arches looking into other ages.

There are rules, though they are local and temperamental.

Nothing here may remain entirely dry.

No scar in the ground lasts longer than three days unless an animal sleeps in it.

Objects left beneath an arch acquire a second history.

Rainfall increases when the hollow contains fewer than seven trees.

A creature killed in direct contact with the central stone returns as geology: bone-white ribs of quartz, skin translated into a dark mineral film, eyes becoming paired beads of salt.

And every ninth cloud cycle, when the warm vapor thickens until the boundaries disappear, the hollow briefly grows larger inside than outside. Paths lengthen. The five arches recede. Calls echo from slopes that do not otherwise exist. For an hour or so, unseen herds move through the fog in enormous numbers, shaking the raw ground with their passage.

When the cloud lifts, the place is small again. The warm pool trembles near the cliff. Seven white trees stand apart in the dimness. Crabs carry their cups of water over immaculate stone. Needle-wings fill the air with their bright, unbearable chord.

Near the central petals, a new groove has appeared.

Rain gathers in it.

For the first time, the water runs downhill.
