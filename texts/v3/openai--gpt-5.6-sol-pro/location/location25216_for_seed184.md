Below the surface of a warm inland sea lies a district called the Drowned Ward. It occupies a shallow depression in the seabed, no more than the size of a cathedral precinct, though its architecture is so layered that distance becomes difficult to judge. Roofs grow beneath bridges; alleys pass through former rooms; chimneys descend into older chimneys. Everything touches something else. There is scarcely a hand’s breadth of unclaimed water.

The Ward was not flooded. It was built in water, by a culture for whom water was not an obstacle but the ordinary medium of streets, labor, and breath.

A low retaining wall surrounds it, made from blocks of black-green ceramic bolted together with bronze ribs. Beyond this wall the seafloor slopes away into darkness. Inside, the ground is paved, terraced, and cut by narrow trenches. Sediment has softened every join. Steps have become ramps under layers of pale silt; engraved gutters are occupied by rootlets and filter moss. The city is old enough that erosion no longer appears to be destroying it. Instead, erosion has completed its design. Corners have reached stable curves. Loose ornament has already fallen. Towers lean against neighboring towers and are held there by centuries of mineral crust.

The water is warm, heavy, and almost still. It contains so much dissolved material that it has a faint viscosity, as if a small amount of oil or sap has been mixed into the sea. A body moving through it leaves a wake that persists for minutes: a transparent fold slowly filling with suspended pollen, rust dust, and luminous spores.

There is very little open space. Machinery crowds the lower streets. Cylindrical pumps stand in rows between residences, their housings quilted with rivets. Pressure pipes wind through windows and emerge from rooftops, bending around columns wherever the original plans met older structures. Ceramic cables lie in bundled loops beneath balconies. Some buildings are little more than shells constructed around engines. Others have been divided repeatedly until their interiors resemble honeycomb: chambers nested inside chambers, each with a grille, valve, aperture, or narrow gate.

The machines still work, but slowly.

Great flywheels revolve once every several hours. Pistons descend with the patience of geological faults. Valves open by imperceptible degrees, releasing warm clouds of mineral-rich water into the upper lanes. The city’s power comes from the pressure difference between the Ward and a shaft driven far beneath it. Dense, cold water rises through one network while warm water sinks through another, and the exchange turns hidden turbines. Nothing spins rapidly. Speed would cause cavitation, vibration, noise—the three forms of waste most feared by the Ward’s engineers.

The factories are therefore nearly silent. Their production is continuous but measured in seasons. One workshop grows sheets of copper crystal across electrified frames. Another separates salts into colored layers inside tall glass retorts. A foundry at the center of the district makes stone rather than melting metal: dissolved minerals are drawn through patterned meshes, where they accrete grain by grain into replacement beams, gears, tiles, and pressure vessels. A gear may take eleven years to finish. When it is ready, the mesh is peeled away and reused.

Light comes from no visible sun. It seeps downward through the sea as a weak blue-gray stain, insufficient to reach the lowest courts. The city supplements it with induction lamps: long tubes of cloudy glass mounted under eaves and bridges. Their radiance is green and faint, brightening and dimming over periods of several days. In the deepest passages, colonies of cultivated bacteria line the pipes with a dull amber glow. The result is not darkness but a permanent late twilight in which edges remain uncertain and every distant structure seems to be behind smoked glass.

Vegetation occupies all surfaces not kept deliberately bare. Broad, soft fronds grow from rooftop cisterns and drape across the lanes like curtains. Black eelgrass rises between paving stones. Rosettes of translucent leaves crowd around warm exhaust vents, each leaf webbed with red capillaries that transport gases from the machinery into swollen root bladders. Climbing plants—if plants is the right word—thread themselves through cable bundles and drink the weak electrical leakage. Their tips produce small pulses of lavender light whenever a turbine changes phase.

There are gardens, though they are indistinguishable from industrial equipment. Walls of porous brick host edible films. Iron trellises are planted with ribbon kelp that absorbs corrosion products and concentrates them in hard metallic nodules. Workers once harvested those nodules as ore. In abandoned rooms, bulbous filter trees have rooted inside tanks and filled them completely, their pale branches pressed against the glass. The trees have no need for open water. They live in the narrow boundary between mineral and current.

This lushness makes the Ward feel more packed than its architecture alone could accomplish. Fronds brush every lintel. Root mats close unused doorways. Moss grows thickly over nameplates and gauge faces, leaving only circular patches where maintenance hands still wipe them clean. There are no vistas. One sees only the next wall, the next hanging garden, the next pipe disappearing into shadow.

Animal life is rare.

Now and then a long, colorless fish passes over the rooftops. It has no eyes and carries a chain of tiny lamps along its belly, though it never lights them within the Ward. Small shelled grazers live inside the machinery, feeding on scale. They move so gradually that they are often mistaken for decorative bolt heads. In the central square, a single bell-shaped creature is said to have hovered since before the oldest repairs. It contracts once each day, rising a few inches, then settles again among the statues.

The scarcity of animals is not entirely natural. The Ward’s water is laced with low electrical fields, trace metals, and deep rhythmic pressure changes. Most free-swimming creatures avoid it. Those that enter tend to become disoriented by the architecture: currents loop through arcades and ventilation towers, returning a swimmer to the same courtyard from a different direction.

Sound behaves strangely here. Water should carry every impact, every motor, every closing gate, but the district has been built to absorb vibration. Walls contain layers of corklike coral. Pipes are suspended in soft collars. Gears have teeth grown from flexible mineral composites. The city’s systems do not clank or roar. They murmur below hearing.

What can be heard is intimate: the tick of cooling metal; a thread of bubbles passing through a cracked seal; fronds rasping against ceramic; distant water moving through the throat of a pump. Occasionally the entire Ward gives a low, nearly subsonic sigh as its pressure equalizes. Silt lifts from the streets, hangs motionless, and settles again.

There are inhabitants, though they are easy to overlook. They occupy sealed capsules incorporated into the upper buildings, or move through the water in close-fitting pressure skins whose surfaces imitate stone, leaf, and rust. Their movements are economical. No one swims across a street if a handrail can be followed. No door is opened until the water on both sides has been brought to equal temperature and salinity. They communicate through touch, through patterned light, and through brief local disturbances in electrical fields. Speech, if used at all, occurs inside helmets and does not enter the city.

Their laws are practical and ancient:

Nothing may release a bubble larger than a thumb joint.

No machine may turn faster than the growth rate of the mineral that repairs it.

A wall colonized on more than three sides is no longer architecture but habitat and cannot be demolished.

Any current that has followed the same route for one hundred years becomes a public road.

Anything entirely enclosed by roots belongs to the garden.

These rules have shaped the Ward more decisively than any original plan. Streets migrate as plants thicken. Factories surrender rooms to filter groves. New chambers are added to the exteriors of old ones, while inaccessible interiors remain intact, sealed around stagnant water and obsolete devices. The distinction between ruin and active infrastructure has disappeared. A collapsed tower may support a pressure line. A dead pump may be the foundation of a nursery. A residential dome may be occupied solely by a tree whose roots operate the dome’s valves through slow expansion and contraction.

At the center stands the Condenser, a squat complex of stacked chambers surrounded by vertical gardens. It processes the city’s water—not to make it breathable or clean, but to preserve its exact character. The Ward requires a particular balance of salt, metal, warmth, spores, and suspended stone. Too clear, and the light would penetrate too deeply, causing the lower gardens to bloom uncontrollably. Too cold, and the mineral foundries would stop. Too fresh, and old ceramic seals would swell and split.

Thus the city maintains its own small sea within the greater one.

At the retaining wall, the difference can be seen. Outside water is dark, cold, and comparatively empty. Inside it is greenish and dense with drifting life, warmed by industry and webbed with slow currents. The boundary has no roof, yet the two waters resist mixing. Heat, salinity, and electrical charge hold them apart in a wavering vertical veil. Passing through it feels like crossing a membrane.

Time within the Ward is correspondingly thick. Rust advances, but gardens consume it. Buildings settle, but neighboring buildings take their weight. Sediment accumulates until maintenance currents lift exactly as much as the previous cycle deposited. Nothing is static, yet little can be said to change.

Only once in several decades does the district perform its great adjustment. Lamps darken in sequence. Valves throughout the streets close. Every plant folds its leaves. For a few minutes the machinery becomes entirely still, and the Ward achieves a silence so complete that the faint pressure of the ocean above seems to become audible.

Then the deepest gate opens.

Cold water rises from beneath the city in one immense, soundless column. Roof gardens stream upward. Curtains of silt unfasten from the walls. The old bell-shaped animal lifts above the central square. The city’s heat and accumulated wastes pass over the retaining wall and spread into the outer sea as a dim green cloud.

Hours later, the gate closes. Warmth returns. Fronds descend across the alleys, gauges clear, and the hidden wheels begin another rotation.

The Ward resumes its work: manufacturing stone, cultivating metal, maintaining the water that maintains it, becoming—at the slowest possible rate—more completely itself.
