Beneath a dry plain there is a hollow no wider, from the outside, than the root-ball of an old tree.

The tree itself is difficult to identify. Aboveground it may be one of several scattered shrubs, or a patch of coarse grass, or the pale bole that appears only after rain. Digging beneath any of them can open the same place. Its entrance is not a tunnel but a momentary error in the soil: a seam between two roots loosens, daylight shows through where no daylight should be, and the earth parts into warm green depth.

The hollow is called a chamber only because it can be entered. It has no dependable dimensions. At times it feels intimate, an ovoid cavity in which the opposite wall is close enough to touch. At other times the walls draw away until their luminous curves resemble a distant horizon. A thrown seed may cross it in a heartbeat; a walker may take half a day to reach where it lands.

Everything solid is vegetable.

The floor is a single taut membrane of interwoven roots, smooth in some places and elsewhere ridged into fresh, angular pleats. It has only recently unfolded. White root tips still protrude from the creases, soft as tongues, testing the open air. The walls are layered stems pressed flat and fused edge to edge. Their bark is translucent, showing slow traffic beneath: sap ascending in amber threads, sugars descending in cloudy streams, bubbles squeezing through narrow vessels. High overhead—or only an arm’s length above, depending on where one stands—millions of leaves overlap to make the ceiling.

Yet the interior is nearly empty. Plant life does not clutter the chamber; it defines its limits. There are no thickets, fallen branches, or drifts of litter. There is only the vast open volume enclosed within one living body. A few hair roots hang down through it, separated by long reaches of clear air. Here and there a flower opens alone, suspended on a stem too fine to see. Its nearest neighbor may be hundreds of paces away.

The light is as strong and ordinary as late morning. Shadows have clean edges. Colors appear natural: leaf-green, root-white, the muted red of new bark. There is no visible lamp and no opening to the sky. The ceiling leaves seem lit from their upper surfaces by the sun outside, but the light passing through them does not weaken with depth. Sometimes a small white cloud crosses overhead. Its shadow travels over the root floor. No one has found the cloud, nor any space between ceiling and sun in which it could exist.

Warmth gathers in the hollow without becoming oppressive. The air rests against the skin like breath. Condensation forms where cool sap channels approach the surface, producing scattered wetlands no larger than bowls: clear beads lying in leaf-cups, narrow runnels along bark seams, and deep, perfectly round wells held between knotted roots. Water is never abundant. For long intervals the chamber remains merely damp, smelling of green wood and mineral-dark soil. Then rain begins.

It does not fall from above.

Drops appear on every surface at once. Moisture beads on the floor, the walls, dangling roots, even on the undersides of leaves, and then rises through the air in bright, wavering lines. Some drops climb toward the ceiling. Others travel sideways, following invisible branches of gravity. For a few minutes the whole chamber is full of moving water, though scarcely any part of it becomes wet. The drops converge into one or two suspended pools, clear globes turning slowly in open space. These last for an hour, sometimes a day. When they collapse, they vanish into the nearest living surface with a deep, satisfied shudder.

The place is loud.

Its loudness is not continuous noise so much as a succession of immense biological events. Stomata open with the hiss of distant surf. Sap columns knock inside the walls. Root fibers, stretched across newly created space, snap one by one with reports like split timber. Pods burst in the warm air and scatter seeds hard enough to whistle. During the condensation rain, every droplet carries a tiny amplified sound: a pulse of thunder, a fragment of rushing river, the drumming of rain on leaves somewhere aboveground. A single bead striking a pool can fill the chamber with echoes for several minutes.

Beneath these sounds is a slower rhythm. The whole hollow contracts and relaxes. The floor rises by the width of a finger, the ceiling pales, and the far wall draws near. Then the chamber inhales; distances lengthen, roots tighten, and the open air becomes briefly enormous. Anyone standing still during this expansion can feel solitude increase around them as if it were a physical pressure.

Erosion has scarcely begun. Nothing here has been weathered smooth. Bark folds meet at sharp ridges. Fresh root plates jut upward in pale fins. Cracks are clean and unstained, leaking clear resin rather than rot. Where a water runnel has started to cut into the floor, its channel is only a shallow bright incision, delicate as a mark made by a thorn. There is no old sediment. Dead material is reclaimed too quickly to accumulate. A fallen petal yellows, thins, and is drawn back into the floor before the next contraction.

The ecology is almost entirely floral, though “floral” becomes an inadequate word. One organism may be the terrain; or thousands of plants may have grown so intimately together that distinction has ceased. The walls bloom according to events rather than seasons. Long red flowers open when someone speaks. Flat silver blossoms appear after silence. Small black flowers emerge in the wake of footsteps, each exactly where a heel lifted from the floor. They remain until touched, at which point they close around the touching finger—not violently, but with the patient firmness of a hand.

Pollination is largely performed by air and geometry. Pollen drifts until distance folds, placing it abruptly against a stigma that had seemed far away. Roots exchange seeds through their sap. Some flowers fertilize their own earlier selves. A yellow bloom may release pollen today and receive it yesterday, leaving a faint golden haze visible at both moments.

Animals are rare enough to be events.

A colorless moth sometimes appears near the hanging pools. Its wings are broad and almost transparent, with veins resembling leaf venation more than those of an insect. It drinks without landing, piercing the water with a long thorn it carries between its forelegs. The thorn is chosen, trimmed against a sharp bark edge, and discarded when blunted. No nest or cache has ever been found. The chamber absorbs each abandoned tool within minutes.

There are also root-runners: small warm-bodied creatures with six fingers and sealed, leaflike eyes. Usually only one is present. It travels by listening to the tension in the floor, tapping with a flaked seed-shell held in one hand. The shell is its knife, sounding stone, and lever. It uses it to open pods and to pry edible nodules from the walls, but it builds nothing. When the edge dulls, the runner presses the shell into a resin seam, where the plant grows a new cutting edge around it. Tool and landscape complete one another.

The root-runner is careful never to cross its own path. A path crossed becomes a loop, and a loop here is dangerous. Space inside the chamber obeys the forms of growth: branching, grafting, spiraling, budding. Two routes that meet may fuse permanently. A person walking such a route can encounter themself approaching from the other branch, sometimes older, sometimes not yet wounded in ways the first has already healed. The runners avoid this by breaking a flower at every turn. Broken flowers cannot be crossed twice.

Other rules reveal themselves slowly.

Down is whichever surface is feeding. When the floor draws water, it is below. When the ceiling begins to drink, loose objects rise and settle among the leaves. During a grafting event, gravity may divide; half the body is pulled toward one wall, half toward another, until the chamber decides which branch will dominate.

Distance follows attention. A lone flower remains far away while watched directly but approaches in the edge of sight. Sounds do the opposite: the more closely one listens, the farther their sources recede.

Nothing may be counted reliably. The hanging roots seem few, perhaps seven or nine, but each recount produces a different total. Marking them does not help. The marks migrate through the bark, assembling elsewhere into crude diagrams of leaves that do not grow aboveground.

Fire cannot sustain itself. A spark flashes green and immediately becomes a germinating point. Smoke roots into the ceiling. Heat is converted into rapid growth, so an open flame, if somehow brought inside, would produce not burning but a violent burst of stems and wet white shoots. This is the nearest the chamber comes to panic.

Its greatest activity occurs after rain on the surface. Water seeping through the outer soil never enters directly. Instead, the hollow undergoes a period of accelerated invention. New ledges thrust from the walls, sharp and glossy. Flowers open in midair before their stems arrive to support them. Root bridges begin to span the emptiness, then reconsider and retract. The chamber booms with growth. Light flickers through the green ceiling as if wind were moving across a sky.

For several hours the place becomes larger than the root system containing it. The surrounding earth bulges by no measurable amount, yet the interior opens into luminous distances. Solitary blossoms appear like trees on a plain. Suspended pools become blue lakes whose curved shores hang free in the air. The tiny colorless moth crosses these expanses with the solemnity of a migrating bird.

Then the moisture passes. The chamber exhales. Lakes close into droplets, horizons fold into walls, and the great empty volume returns to the scale of a hidden room.

The entrance rarely remains where it was. Those who leave do so by finding a root seam through which warm dust can be smelled. They press a seed-shell or thorn into the seam and wait for the plant to open around the simple tool. Beyond is ordinary soil, close and dark. Sometimes they emerge beneath the same shrub through which they entered. Sometimes the shrub has not yet sprouted. Sometimes the plain above is wet with a rain that, inside, has only just begun to rise.
