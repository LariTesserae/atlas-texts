At an elevation where ordinary mountains would have thinned into scree and cold air, there is a hot plateau drowned in green.

It occupies the inside of an immense caldera, though *inside* is not quite the right relation. From the rim, the basin appears below; from the basin, the rim sometimes hangs beneath the horizon, its cliffs descending upward into a white, ordinary sky. Distance remains dependable only along the streets.

The heat is profound and even. It does not rise from any visible sun or vent. Stone, leaf, water, and air all share the same feverish temperature, as if the entire plateau has rested for ages in the palm of something living. Moisture condenses on every surface. Black walls perspire. Broad leaves hold bright beads along their edges, but the beads neither fall nor evaporate. The air has the mineral thickness of the moment after a tropical storm, while the light is merely daylight: neither dim nor radiant, without dramatic color, shadows soft but present.

A city fills much of the caldera.

No one built it.

Its avenues are clefts in the basalt, straight for miles and intersecting at exact angles. Towers rise where ancient lava columns grew together, their exteriors divided into ledges, balconies, apertures, and deep rectangular chambers. Arcades have weathered from bands of softer stone. Bridges are the exposed roots of enormous plants, fused from cliff to cliff and flattened along their upper surfaces by no passage anyone can remember. There are plazas of close-fitted slabs, stairways cut with regular risers, cistern courts, terraces, covered ways, courtyards nested inside courtyards. Nothing bears a tool mark. The stones meet because they have always met.

It is urban in the way a crystal is geometric: not an imitation of intention, but something that makes intention seem derivative.

The oldest districts cling to the vertical walls. Their streets ascend sheer faces without becoming stairs, and a person looking at them too long may feel the direction of gravity loosen behind the eyes. Doorways open into chambers larger than the towers containing them. Some alleys pass between adjoining buildings and emerge several valleys away, although their wet paving can be seen continuously from entrance to exit. In the central quarter, five parallel boulevards occupy the same strip of ground. Each is visible through the others, crowded with vines and colonnades, and each leads to a different horizon.

The city is lush but not wild. Vegetation observes its plan.

Pale-trunked trees stand in ordered ranks along the avenues, their crowns interlocking into high green vaults. Ferns fill stairwells. Moss upholsters plazas in precise, low fields separated by channels of exposed stone. Rope-thick vines descend from roofs and disappear into drains. In sunken courtyards grow plants with leaves broad enough to roof a house, dark green above and the color of wet clay beneath. Their roots pass through walls without cracking them, emerging on the other side in patterns resembling masonry.

There are no flowers, no fruit, no seeds in flight. There are no insects beneath the leaves, no worms in the steaming soil, no birds in the canopy. Nothing browses, nests, burrows, hunts, or decays. The vegetation is abundant but belongs to no ecology recognizable as such. Leaves show no bites. Fallen wood is absent. The green mass does not seem young or old. It has reached a condition beyond growth, a fixed luxuriance in which every tendril has already found its final support.

Water is everywhere near the surface. Narrow canals accompany the main streets, brimful and dark. Wells stand open in the squares, their water level flush with the pavement. Marshes occupy the outer wards, where towers rise from mats of reeds and warm, ankle-deep pools. The caldera walls weep in thousands of silver threads. At the eastern escarpment, a river pours over a precipice so tall that its bottom cannot be located; halfway down, the falling water becomes a second river crossing horizontally through empty air. Elsewhere, sheets of water hang from balconies like transparent banners.

None of it can be seen to move.

The falls retain the shape of falling water—white fractures, suspended droplets, long glass ropes—but each droplet holds its place. Ripples stand on the canals without spreading. Rain occupies certain streets as a permanent architecture of beads, every drop poised between cloud and paving. One may walk among them. They are warm against the skin, yielding slightly, yet they return at once to their exact positions.

And the water is deafening.

The plateau is filled continuously with the roar of cataracts, flood drains, monsoon rain, subterranean rivers, and surf striking cavern walls. The sound has no beginning, rhythm, echo, or variation. It does not grow louder near the falls or softer inside a sealed chamber. At the edge of the marsh, beneath six feet of stone, on the highest roof, and with one’s ears covered, the same immense volume remains. It is less like hearing water than inhabiting the fact of water.

Speech would be impossible here, if there were anyone with whom to speak. A shout can be felt in the throat but produces no addition to the world. A stone struck against another makes no sound. Branches cannot rustle. Footsteps are swallowed before they occur. The roar occupies every available place in hearing, perfectly uniform and changeless.

Yet the leaves do not tremble.

Nothing does.

The entire city rests in an equilibrium so complete that motion seems a local superstition brought in by the observer. Heat does not create wind. Water does not erode its channels further. Moisture does not rot the roots or soften the soil. The cliffs are weathered, rounded at their exposed corners and deeply stained by mineral runoff, but their weathering has ended. Every gully is mature. Every talus slope has reached its final angle. Mud remains mud without drying or being disturbed.

Even the clouds are established features of the terrain. They lie across the upper wards in long white banks, their edges passing through towers. Entering one does not obscure vision; instead, it reveals another version of the city, inverted and roofless, extending beneath the paving. From certain intersections, both cities can be seen at once: one rising in basalt and vegetation, the other descending through luminous vapor, each casting the other’s shadows.

Toward the center stands the city’s densest structure, a mass that might be a palace, a mountain, or a knot in space. Terraces climb its sides by the thousands. Aqueducts enter it from directions not present on any horizon. Its lower chambers are flooded to their ceilings, but trees grow upright within the water, their leaves dry. Above them are roofless halls where warm rain hangs motionless over geometric gardens. Higher still, whole neighborhoods are packed into open courtyards: towers, streets, marshes, and cliffs reproduced at diminishing scales, though not as models. Looking down into one reveals full atmospheric distance and the same normal white sky.

At the summit is a broad square covered by a few inches of clear water. The square has no monument. It needs none. From there, the extent of the plateau becomes visible: green districts, black terraces, mirror-marshes, bridges, vertical rivers, and the distant rim repeating in successive rings. Each ring appears both farther away and more detailed than the last. Beyond them are other calderas containing the same city—not copies, but continuations, as though the landscape has no exterior and scale is only another street one can take.

There are signs everywhere of accommodation and none of habitation. Recesses shaped like rooms. Stone basins at hand height. Long communal halls. Roof gardens. Doorways. Seats grown from walls. Paths broad enough for crowds. The city’s proportions imply bodies, routines, neighborhoods, gatherings. But no remains lie in the soil, no images occur on the walls, and no creature has ever polished a threshold by passing over it.

Perhaps the settlement is waiting. Yet waiting suggests time, and time has little authority here.

The place does not advance toward ruin or recovery. It is not abandoned, because abandonment would be an event. It is not alive, because life would require change. It is not dead, because death has left no mark. The city, the forest, the heat, and the suspended flood persist in one indivisible state.

Only a visitor moves, and even that becomes uncertain. A wet footprint appears on the dark stone before the foot comes down. Leaves make room before shoulders touch them. The canals reflect the visitor standing elsewhere—at the far end of the avenue, on a high bridge, behind the eyes of the reflection itself. After enough walking, one notices that the roaring has not merely drowned out thought. It has taken thought’s shape, becoming indistinguishable from the inner voice.

Then the streets seem less empty.

Not occupied—never that—but complete, as if absence here is a population: dense, organized, and permanent.
