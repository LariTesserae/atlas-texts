### The Cold Lung

High inside the mountain-factory, behind three bolted inspection doors and a crawlspace too narrow to turn around in, there is a chamber called the Cold Lung.

It is not large. A person could cross it in twelve careful steps, though there is nowhere to stand without placing a boot on water, machinery, or living bodies. The ceiling bows low enough to touch. Beyond it rise kilometers of fabricated mass: load-bearing honeycomb, turbine galleries, ice-cased conduits, and the black outer armor of the summit. No natural mountain lies underneath. The entire elevation was assembled—plate by plate, rib by rib—until it became high enough to make its own weather.

The Cold Lung occupies one forgotten pocket near the upper skin.

Everything in it has been made. Its cliffs are cast ceramic. Its gravel is a spill of unfinished ball bearings, clinker beads, snapped rivet heads, and gray nodules extruded to resemble stone. Thin ledges project from the walls, each stamped underneath with part numbers. Even the cracks are deliberate: branching seams molded into panels before firing, as if the builders intended erosion to begin here one day but never returned to activate it.

Thus the chamber resembles a landscape at the first instant of its geological life. There are slopes, but no slope has yet relaxed. There are channels, but they remain sharp-edged, pale, and newly opened. Artificial boulders sit where cranes placed them, their undersides clean, their corners unworn. Nothing has accumulated enough history to become soil.

Water is everywhere.

It beads on bolt heads, hangs beneath cables, and gathers in the embossed letters of warning plates. The walls weep through thousands of microscopic pores. Condensation threads descend the ceramic faces in perfectly parallel lines, feeding shallow basins machined into the floor. Each basin is scarcely deeper than a hand, yet appears black and bottomless beneath the dim blue service lamps.

The water is close to freezing but never freezes. A buried heating mesh holds it a fraction of a degree above solidity, while the surrounding structure remains furred with white rime. As drops fall, they enter the pools without ripples. Surface-tension grids beneath the water absorb the disturbance. Every pool remains smooth as smoked glass, even during the chamber’s constant indoor rain.

There are almost no plants. Here and there, a colorless filament grows along an insulated seam. It has no leaves and produces no visible spore. A few patches of dark green cling to warm transformer housings, cropped so closely that they look painted on. Whatever vegetation once attempted to colonize the floor has long since been eaten.

The animals are another matter.

They occupy nearly every available surface.

Small, white, many-jointed creatures pack the ledges shoulder to shoulder. They have the compact bodies of alpine birds, the translucent shells of crustaceans, and narrow heads ending in tuning-fork beaks. Their six feet grip ceramic, wire, wet steel, and one another. Hundreds hang upside down from the ceiling lattice. Thousands stand in the runnels. More live inside unused screw sockets, drain mouths, relay boxes, and the hollow imitation stones.

Their numbers make the chamber seem smaller than it is. Any gap left between two creatures is immediately filled by a third. The walls appear feathered until one looks closely and sees overlapping plates, folded limbs, wet membranes, and bright black eyes. When they breathe, their bodies do not rise and fall individually. Whole colonies expand together, as though each dense cluster were a single lung divided into innumerable animals.

Nothing hunts them. Nothing visibly dies.

They feed on condensation and on a fine metallic frost produced by the chamber’s machinery. With delicate forked beaks, they scrape zinc bloom from guardrails and drink from the weeping seams. The smallest sift iron dust from the pools. Larger individuals press their mouths to warm cables and draw off stray electrical charge. In return, their hooked feet polish contacts, their shells conduct static into grounding plates, and their packed bodies insulate the pipework.

Whether they were introduced as maintenance organisms or appeared after the factory sealed itself is impossible to determine. The stamped diagrams on the walls show no fauna. Yet certain brackets fit their bodies exactly. Certain niches are warmed to the temperature of their eggs. Certain pipes terminate in rows of tiny drinking nozzles.

The Cold Lung may be an accidental habitat.

It may also be a machine whose components happen to be alive.

The sound is continuous and immense.

Pumps below the floor drive water through narrow throats, producing a bass pressure more felt than heard. Ventilation blades rotate somewhere behind the walls with a fixed metallic roar. Over this, every creature emits a single tone. No two tones are identical. Together they form a shrieking, layered chord so dense that distinctions disappear: whistle, alarm, grinding bearing, avalanche, choir.

The chamber is too small to contain such noise. It seems to pass through the body without diminishing, buzzing in the teeth and making vision tremble. Loose objects would rattle, if there were any loose objects. The pools would shiver, if they were permitted. Speech becomes a movement of the mouth with no audible consequence.

Yet the noise never varies.

No animal calls louder or falls silent. No pump accelerates. No bearing worsens. The chord has continued at precisely the same pressure and pitch for as long as the service instruments have records. A listener returning after a year—or a century—would enter at the same apparent instant of sound.

This constancy governs everything in the chamber.

Drops hang from the same lips of metal. Those that fall are replaced at exactly the rate required to preserve their shape. Water enters each basin as water leaves through hidden capillaries. The white creatures drink but the pools do not lower. They feed but do not grow. Eggs remain faintly visible through the membranes of adults, neither laid nor absorbed. Individuals move—feet lifting, beaks scraping, gill-fans opening—but the colony as a whole never alters its outline.

Even the artificial erosion is held at its beginning. Water passes forever through the sharp channels without rounding them. Metallic grit slides down the molded slopes, while concealed magnetic belts return each grain through the walls and place it again at the top. Ceramic dust is removed and redeposited grain for grain. The landscape is being worn away continuously and is never more worn.

This gives the chamber its peculiar sense of rawness. It does not feel ancient despite its uncounted operation. It feels newly built at every moment: wet sealant, fresh fractures, unweathered scree, a world waiting for its first afternoon. Time occurs locally—in falling drops, scraping mouths, turning rotors—but cannot accumulate.

Near the center stands the chamber’s only tall feature, a waist-high column assembled from black cooling fins. Water runs down it in a thin sheath. The animals will not touch it. Around its base is a narrow ring of empty floor, the sole unoccupied space in the Cold Lung.

The column bears no controls, only a small glass window clouded from within. Behind the glass, a red indicator remains halfway between lit and dark. It has never pulsed. Its label, pressed into the metal in several obsolete industrial scripts, can be translated approximately as:

**TERRAIN FORMATION — READY**

No one knows what would happen if the indicator changed.

Perhaps the ceramic cliffs would begin to erode, the channels deepen, and the grit become soil. Perhaps the pale colonies would hatch their waiting young and fill the final ring of empty floor. Perhaps vegetation would take hold, fed by centuries of suspended nutrients. Perhaps the entire constructed summit would cease maintaining its impossible equilibrium and enter ordinary time.

But the indicator does not change.

The mountain-factory keeps the pocket cold. The walls keep weeping. The creatures keep sounding their unbearable, exact chord. In the dark, under kilometers of manufactured altitude, the little landscape remains poised before its own beginning—crowded, drenched, and deafening, forever one instant away from becoming a place with a past.
