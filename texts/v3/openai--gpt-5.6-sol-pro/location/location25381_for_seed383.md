The city lies beneath a surface no one speaks of as a destination.

It occupies an immense, shallow-bowled cavern, broad enough that its opposite districts fade into white atmospheric glare. The roof hangs low by the standards of underground worlds—sometimes thirty human heights above the streets, sometimes only three—and has been worn nearly smooth. There are no stalactites. Anything delicate enough to become a stalactite broke away ages ago. The remaining stone has the blunt, rubbed contours of bone carried for centuries in moving water.

Yet the cavern is bright.

Light comes from broad mineral seams in the roof: chalk-white bands that kindle when compressed, fade, and kindle elsewhere. Stress travels continuously through the buried strata. As the rock bends by imperceptible degrees, illumination passes across the ceiling in vast, silent weather fronts. A district may stand under hard, colorless brilliance for an hour, every crack visible, while another lies in pearl-gray shade. There is no flame-colored sun and almost no true darkness. Shadows are thin, multiple, and unreliable.

The settlement below is very old, though not because any one building has endured. Its age is in the plan.

Streets follow channels cut before memory, sweeping in broad curves around hummocks of impermeable stone. Steps descend where ancient floods lowered the floor. Courtyards occupy hollows polished by extinct torrents. Houses, kilns, cisterns, market walls, animal pens, shrines, ramps, and public platforms have been rebuilt so many times that they no longer belong to distinct generations. Every structure is temporary; the city itself seems permanent.

Most construction is dry-laid stone. The blocks are pale, flat, and heavily rounded, pried from old sediment beds with antler wedges, stone mauls, and simple copper levers. Walls are kept low and massively thick. Roofs are made from overlapping slabs supported by arches of stacked rubble. Timber is unknown. Rope is braided from animal sinew or from a tough, translucent membrane harvested from the cavern’s larger creatures. Doors are hide, shell, or hanging curtains of small stone disks that make almost no noise when touched.

The city is urban but sparse. It spreads widely rather than rising. Long intervals of empty pavement separate inhabited clusters. There are whole neighborhoods consisting only of foundations, waist-high partitions, and communal cisterns shining like black coins. A lone workshop may stand in the middle of a plaza large enough to swallow a village. The emptiness is deliberate. Everyone knows where the floor is likely to buckle, where ceiling scales periodically fall, where sudden water crosses, and where migrating animals refuse to be obstructed.

Nothing green grows here. Nothing branches toward light.

The ground is mineral from horizon to horizon: cream limestone, iron-gray mud, blue-black shelves of flint, old salt crust, and patches of translucent calcite that resemble frozen puddles. Even in wet places there is no moss. The nearest things to vegetation are colonies of soft mineral-feeding films that spread beneath stones in glossy sheets. They are colorless and nearly invisible until disturbed, when they contract into strings like clear mucus and leave etched lacework behind. Whether they are fungi, animals, or something older than that distinction is a matter for city philosophers.

Water is intermittent and intensely managed. Most streets are dry, furred with fine pale dust. Moisture gathers first as condensation on the luminous roof and on the cooler faces of pillars. It beads silently, swells, and falls at irregular intervals. Beneath favored drip points, people have placed bowls, funnels, stepped basins, and entire forests of tilted stone plates. These catchments are among the few vertical features in the city. From a distance they resemble leafless orchards constructed by someone who had only heard trees described.

Rain occurs underground.

It begins not with clouds but with a shift in pressure and a sudden whitening of the ceiling. Condensation appears everywhere at once. For a few minutes, sometimes for most of a day, droplets descend through the bright cavern. The rain is fine and cold. It scarcely sounds on the dust, though it trembles visibly in cisterns and turns streets dark by degrees. Children and small animals move through it with mouths open. Adults uncover storage jars, scrape clean the gutters, and watch the old flood marks.

The violence comes later and often without warning.

Far beyond the inhabited bowl, buried rivers change course through rubble-filled passages. Water may enter the city as a thin gleaming sheet across a plaza, or erupt upward through a cracked paving slab. A dry canal can become a racing white channel in less time than it takes to cross a street. Then, just as abruptly, the current sinks through another fissure and is gone, leaving fish, silt, fractured walls, and new hollows in the floor.

This is a turbulent place, but not a loud one.

Its upheavals have a strange restraint. The deep rock shifts without thunder. Pressure cracks run through walls with sounds like fingernails brushing cloth. Buildings settle, incline, and unzip along their joints. A whole terrace may descend half a pace overnight, so gently that sleeping people wake only because their bowls have slid to one side. Elsewhere, stones rise. Pavements arch into ridges. Wells seal themselves. New wells open in bedrooms.

The inhabitants rebuild continuously. Construction is less a profession than a civic metabolism.

At any hour, groups are lifting blocks, opening drains, dismantling unsafe roofs, carrying baskets of silt, moving household shrines out of leaning chambers, or marking fresh fissures with lines of white powder. Yet their work produces little clamor. Stone is dragged on hide sledges. Mallets are wrapped in leather. Instructions are given in hand signs whenever possible. The culture values quiet partly because sudden sound can panic the fauna, and partly because listening is a form of survival. A faint ticking beneath a floor may announce water. A dry whisper overhead may mean the roof is shedding a slab. Silence is not emptiness here. It is attention made communal.

Animals are everywhere.

Small pallid runners inhabit cracks between paving stones, emerging in crowds after rain to feed on mineral films and drowned burrowers. They have six or eight limbs depending on the district, though the citizens classify them by the shape of their mouths rather than their number of legs. Their shells are thin as paper and nearly transparent. In bright periods, their circulating fluids cast moving thread-shadows on the ground.

Long-bodied grazers roam the open plazas in loose herds. They feed by pressing rasping mouthplates against salt crusts and microbe-coated stone. Their hides are matte white, loose around the joints, and cool to the touch. Most have no visible eyes. Instead, delicate pits along their flanks register pressure, heat, and the minute tremors of approaching feet. They move around buildings with uncanny grace, dividing and rejoining like water passing an obstacle.

The city does not keep these creatures out. It has been designed around their paths. Doorways are narrow where animals must be discouraged and broad where migration is permitted. Some streets belong to people only during the dry hours. Others are abandoned whenever the ceiling light moves westward and the feeding herds follow it. Low bridges allow citizens to pass above lanes reserved for the slow armored beasts that travel between brine pockets. Their broad shells have been polished by generations of hands as they pass.

In the cisterns live eyeless fish, translucent eels, filter-feeding disks, and soft creatures like knots in clear cloth. After an underground flood, the contents of distant ecosystems appear briefly in public basins. People hurry to sort them: edible things into jars, dangerous things into lidded pits, sacred or unknown things into the deep municipal wells. Many vanish when the water does. Others adapt and become part of a district’s life for centuries.

Flying animals exist, though “flying” is generous. They are little more than pale membranes stretched around hollow bodies, drifting on tiny differences in air pressure. They gather beneath the luminous seams and descend when the light fades. Thousands may occupy the ceiling above a market without making more than the sound of slow breathing. Their shed skins are used for lamp covers, water bags, windows, and ceremonial veils.

Because there is no plant life, almost everything useful comes from stone, clay, water, salt, bone, shell, hide, and the bodies of simpler organisms. Food chains begin in the mineral films and in warm cracks where unseen colonies live on chemicals exhaled from deeper rock. The people cultivate these colonies on scored slabs in damp chambers. The slabs are stacked like books, washed with brine, then scraped. The resulting paste is sour, colorless, and sustaining. Herd animals are milked. Cistern creatures are trapped in woven sinew nets. Eggs collected from mudbanks are preserved in salt.

Fire is rare. Fuel is animal fat, precious and smoky. Cooking is more often done with heated stones drawn from warm fissures. Potters carry raw vessels to geothermal chambers and wall them in until the clay hardens. Metal exists mostly as soft native copper hammered cold into hooks, awls, narrow knives, and thin facing plates. A copper blade is useful, but a properly chosen flint edge is sharper. Complex machinery has never become necessary or perhaps never become possible: the ground will not hold exact alignment long enough.

The deepest civic law is that no structure may resist the cavern too stubbornly.

Walls must yield at prepared seams. Roofs must collapse inward rather than into streets. Aqueducts are assembled in short, separable lengths. Large halls are forbidden unless their columns stand independently. No foundation may be pinned to both sides of an active fault. A building that survives by forcing pressure elsewhere is considered immoral.

Ownership follows the same logic. A family owns the stones of a house, but not necessarily the place where the house stands. If a district tilts, floods, brightens unbearably, or becomes part of a migration path, the stones are numbered, dismantled, and carried to safer ground. Addresses are therefore descriptions rather than coordinates: *behind the third dry basin; under the seam that brightens twice; uphill from the shell road this season.*

At the city’s center lies the oldest open space, a nearly flat expanse called the Worn Forum. Nothing is built there. The pavement is a single bed of dark rock polished by vanished water until it reflects the ceiling like tarnished glass. Shallow grooves cross it in hundreds of directions. Some are channels, some fault lines, some the worn tracks of extinct animals, and some are said to be streets from an earlier arrangement of the city.

During bright phases the Forum is almost painful to look upon. People cross it singly or in quiet lines, their reflections broken by ripples of stone. Pale animals sleep in the grooves. Condensation forms beneath their bodies and remains afterward in silhouettes.

Beyond the settled districts, the cavern continues through ruins that may not be ruins. Low parallel walls vanish into glare. Circular pits occur at regular intervals, all empty. In some places, thousands of hand-sized stones stand balanced on edge, undisturbed despite the shifting ground. No one agrees whether these are abandoned constructions, natural patterns produced by erosion, or the work of animals whose behavior has since changed.

There are older levels beneath even these.

When streets collapse, they sometimes reveal buried streets, complete with drains and thresholds, lying at angles to the present city. The stonework below is always more eroded, its corners softened beyond usefulness. Occasionally there are rooms with no entrances, filled with clear water. Occasionally there are pens containing the bones of creatures unlike any now alive. Once, according to civic record, a sinking courtyard opened onto a vast lower avenue illuminated from beneath. It remained visible for nine days. Then the opening closed, not by collapse but by the slow convergence of its walls.

The city’s people do not think of the cavern as dead stone. Nor do they claim it is alive. Such words are considered too simple.

The world compresses, releases, sweats, shines, rearranges its hollows, and brings forth animals from passages no one has mapped. It destroys without anger and provides without generosity. Its oldest surfaces have been eroded almost to final forms, yet those forms never stop moving. Everything has been settled for ages; nothing is stable for a day.

From a high terrace, the whole landscape appears calm: pale roofs scattered across an immense floor, silver catchments under the glowing vault, isolated crowds moving along wide roads, herds threading through empty neighborhoods. No smoke rises. No trees interrupt the view. No bells mark the hour.

Then the ceiling light begins to travel.

A distant quarter brightens until every wall shines. Animals rise together and turn their blank heads. Covers come off the cisterns. Workers put their palms to the pavement. Somewhere, too far away to hear, a street lifts like a slow wave, and the city quietly begins to move itself again.
