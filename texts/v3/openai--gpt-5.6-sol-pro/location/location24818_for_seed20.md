At the summit of a young mountain, where the air should be thin and cold, there is an ocean with no shore.

It occupies a space no larger than a cathedral precinct: a steep-sided basin of black volcanic glass, several hundred paces across, opening directly onto the sky. Yet the water does not merely fill the basin. It rises above it in a trembling, dark mass—a vaulted body of sea held around the summit as if by an invisible vessel. From a distance it resembles a storm cloud snagged on the peak. Up close, its boundary is a vertical skin of rain. Water races downward along the outside while the sea within remains suspended, replenished by its own falling.

To enter, one passes through this wall.

There is an instant of battering silver, then warm submersion.

Inside, the summit is dim even at noon. Light arrives bruised and green-brown, scattered through layers of silt and innumerable bubbles. The sun sometimes appears overhead, but never in its proper shape. It may be a pale ring, a vertical wound, or several trembling disks at different depths. Shadows lean in conflicting directions. Distances cannot be trusted. The opposite wall may look close enough to touch, then recede for minutes as one swims toward it.

The mountain beneath is newly made. Its stone has not been rounded into patience. Ridges of basalt rise like broken blades from the drowned floor, glossy along their edges and rust-red in their cracks. Some are still warm. Curtains of bubbles stream from vents between them, and the surrounding water shivers with heat. Mineral crusts—white, saffron, blue-black—spread over the rock in delicate, fresh layers. A hand laid against them would erase years of growth.

There is little sediment. Nothing has had time to settle.

The sea is in constant upheaval. Currents plunge from nowhere, strike the basin floor, divide around the spires, and climb again. Whole columns of water turn over with the slow force of collapsing buildings. At intervals, the ocean’s upper vault buckles inward. Pressure passes through the place as a visible distortion, bending every ridge and crevice without moving them. Then the summit seems to exhale, and a swarm of bubbles tears upward into the dark.

The noise is absolute.

It is not the muffled quiet of ordinary depth. The stone conducts the mountain’s cracking directly into the water. Vents bellow. Cavities boom as currents compress the air trapped inside them. The enclosing rain-wall pounds without pause, a circular cataract heard from within. Beneath these sounds is a deeper one: a continuous subsonic concussion, felt in the teeth and ribs, as though the entire sea were striking a distant door.

Close to the basalt, sharper noises emerge. Mineral plates snap as they cool. Gravel lifts, rattles violently in place, then drops. Now and then a fissure opens with a report like thunder and extrudes a glowing seam before the water smothers it. The glow lasts only seconds, but in those seconds the surrounding rock appears crowded with silhouettes that are not visible afterward.

Life is sparse and tenacious. Pale threads cling to the warm cracks, neither quite algae nor fungus. They have no leaves. They grow in loose, waist-high veils where minerals seep from the stone, each filament tipped with a faint amber bead. The beads brighten when the pressure changes, so that an approaching current sends a wave of dim fire through the growth before the water itself arrives.

Elsewhere stand isolated fans of translucent tissue, widely separated, each no larger than an open hand. They fold shut at vibration and may remain closed for days. Nothing grazes them. Nothing pollinates them. They appear less like a population than repeated instances of the same organism, placed apart.

Animals are rare enough to be events.

A blind, ribbon-bodied swimmer sometimes winds among the vents. It has no evident mouth and is always seen moving uphill, even when “uphill” changes. Tiny shelled things inhabit the undersides of overhangs, but their shells contain only warm water when broken. Once or twice in a season, a large shadow circles beyond the outer rain-wall. It never enters. Whether it is a creature, a cloud, or the mountain’s own shape cast against the sea is impossible to determine.

Scattered across the drowned summit are signs of construction, though there is no sign that any tool was ever used.

Three low archways stand between basalt ridges. They are made of fitted black stones, each stone sharp and unweathered, yet the joints wander through them like natural veins. The arches lead nowhere: one opens onto bare rock, another into a crevice too narrow for a body, and the third frames a patch of water in which bubbles sink.

A flight of six steps descends from one ledge and stops above a chasm. Their surfaces bear shallow depressions resembling footprints, but the marks face in several directions at once. Near the basin’s center is a ring of upright slabs enclosing nothing. Currents avoid this ring. Silt crossing its threshold rises, separates into grains, and returns to the place from which it came.

These structures may not have been built. The mountain may be remembering architecture before there were builders.

That is one of the summit sea’s laws: forms often arrive before their causes.

A crack may open several moments before the stone breaks. A cloud of debris may gather itself into a ridge, remain there briefly, and then scatter so that the ridge can erupt from below. Sounds precede events inconsistently. The boom of a collapsing overhang may be heard at dawn, though the collapse itself occurs near dusk. At other times the water goes momentarily quiet—an unbearable, pressurized silence—and only afterward does one realize that something violent has already happened.

The water also refuses a stable direction. Buoyancy usually draws bodies toward the vaulted surface, but near the center of the basin “up” rotates slowly around the stone ring. Bubbles travel sideways. Loose fragments fall in arcs. A swimmer who follows the sun may arrive beneath the floor and see the summit inverted overhead: black pinnacles hanging down into a second, dimmer sea.

That lower sea exists only from certain angles. Entering it is possible but returning is uncertain.

The outer boundary is no safer. From within, the wall of rain looks like a gray, luminous curtain enclosing the whole site. Crossing it should release one into the mountain air. Most of the time it does. But during the summit’s convulsions, the curtain opens elsewhere. One may emerge from the opposite side, from an hour earlier, or several hundred feet above the peak with the rain falling upward around the body.

Nothing here remains for long except the boundaries.

New basalt teeth rise from the floor and crack apart within days. Mineral gardens bloom, bleach, and slough away. An archway that stood in the eastern cleft may be found the next morning upside down on the western wall, its stones still fitted. Warm currents redraw the habitable places continuously. The few living filaments tear free in storms and drift through the water like illuminated hair, only to fasten themselves to stone that did not exist before.

At night the summit sea becomes almost black. The world outside disappears behind the torrent. The amber-tipped growths pulse in scattered colonies, far apart, emphasizing the emptiness between them. Heat glimmers faintly in fresh fissures. Every sound grows larger without sight to contain it.

Then, in the deepest hours, the whole enclosed ocean sometimes rises.

The water lifts away from the basin floor, exposing the jagged summit beneath it while remaining suspended as a single vaulted mass. For a few breaths there is air between mountain and sea. The arches stand dripping in warm darkness. Blind swimmers writhe overhead, trapped in the underside of the water. The deafening roar ceases.

In that brief silence, the bare rock can be heard making a small, deliberate sound—stone placed carefully upon stone.

Then the ocean falls back down.
