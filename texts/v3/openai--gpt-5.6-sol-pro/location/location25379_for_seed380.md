The city lies in a shallow chamber beneath the old ground, not deeply buried but decisively below the reach of ordinary weather. Its ceiling is a sagging shelf of limestone, pierced here and there by root holes, drainage shafts, and circular wells through which a gray suggestion of daylight descends. Rain reaches it slowly. Hours after a storm has passed above, water begins to bead along the ceiling, gather in seams, and fall in heavy, widely spaced drops.

The whole settlement could be crossed in twenty minutes. It occupies the hollow left by an ancient underground river: a long room with rounded ends, scarcely broader than a railway yard. Buildings stand along the former banks while the river’s diminished remnant moves through the middle in a stone-lined channel. There are perhaps forty structures, though it is difficult to count them. Workshops lean into retaining walls. Warehouses have been fitted into natural alcoves. Narrow brick houses rise two or three stories and stop just short of the ceiling, their chimneys entering drilled sockets overhead rather than opening into sky.

Everything looks permanent without looking intact.

The brickwork has softened at the corners. Iron stairs have been polished by condensation and generations of careful feet. Copper pipes run in deliberate bundles across facades, green-black and sweating. Their brackets are furred with mineral deposits, each leak having built a pale cone beneath itself over decades. Nothing here is ruined in the dramatic sense. Erosion has reached an agreement with construction. Walls lose a grain at a time; channels deepen by a fingernail’s width over years; worn steps are periodically turned over and set back into place with their unspent faces upward.

The principal street is a strip of dark flagstone beside the channel. It is almost empty. Every thirty or forty paces stands some singular piece of infrastructure: a pressure cabinet, a cast-iron capstan, a rain gauge enclosed in wire, a ceramic switchboard with six immense handles. There is no clutter between them. The city’s machinery has been arranged with the spaciousness of monuments.

Electric light comes from low amber lamps bolted to walls and from bluish tubes submerged in the watercourse. Neither is bright. The lamps illuminate droplets more readily than surfaces. A passerby first sees a vertical constellation of falling water, then the archway behind it. At the chamber’s farther end, where several shafts admit the color of the upper world, everything appears silvered and remote.

Most of the city’s industry is concerned with the slow movement and separation of water.

At the upper end of the channel stands the Condensary, a compact factory of black columns, ceramic vanes, and broad turning drums. It catches vapor from warmer tunnels and combs it into liquid. Banks of cold metal leaves collect mist until droplets grow heavy enough to descend into labeled troughs. Water from limestone, water from brick, water from root wells, water exhaled by animals, and water condensed from the factories are kept apart until their mineral and biological characters are understood. Only then are they blended.

The machines move almost imperceptibly. Great flywheels complete a rotation in several minutes. Pistons rise with the patience of locks filling. Leather belts advance so slowly that pale cave moths rest on them without being carried far. The machinery is industrial in mass but not in tempo. It was built for continuous operation over centuries, and speed is regarded as a form of damage.

There are no sirens. Gear teeth are made from resin-soaked wood, horn, and compressed fungal paper; bearings float in water or oil. The result is a city of engines that rarely sounds louder than breathing. Close to a wall, one may detect a low hydraulic pressure traveling through the pipes. On the central bridge there is the periodic tick of droplets striking brass. Farther off, a valve sighs once and is silent for an hour.

The loudest common sound is animal movement.

Fauna are everywhere, although the place initially seems deserted. Pale eels inhabit inspection trenches and knot themselves around warm conduits. Thumb-sized translucent crabs browse mineral films from the channel walls. Long-legged water beetles gather under lamps, each carrying a dim reflection on its shell. Small black frogs sit inside disconnected gauges, their throats inflating soundlessly. In the high brickwork, colonies of velvet-winged bats cling in orderly rows, shifting one place to the left whenever a lamp is serviced.

There are also tunnel foxes: slight, gray animals with broad feet and whiskers so long they brush both sides of a drain before the fox enters it. They do not hunt much. They feed chiefly on blind fruiting bodies, eggs from the eel troughs, and a sweet grease that accumulates in the Condensary’s filters. They walk the streets without hurry and sleep on the warm housings of electrical transformers.

The strangest animals are the bell carriers.

They resemble narrow-backed salamanders, though adults may be as long as a person’s forearm. A small mineral cup grows from the spine of each one, formed layer by layer from salts in the water. As the animal moves, the cup fills. When it becomes too heavy, the salamander climbs to one of the city’s grates and tilts itself until the water pours through. The empty cup makes a faint porcelain note against its rim. Hundreds perform this work, redistributing condensation from low floors into the drainage system. The citizens claim they were once bred for the purpose. The salamanders, however, have been found in unopened strata behind the oldest walls.

Vegetation is scarce and localized. Nothing forms a continuous green surface. In certain cracks, stalks of glassy horsetail rise in groups of three or four. Flat moss grows only around bolts made before a particular date. A few root tips descend from trees above, pale and muscular, ending in cages so they do not enter the machinery. Near the central bridge stands the city’s only garden: nine stone basins containing black water, each with a single white reed. The reeds flower at intervals measured in decades. Their pollen is collected by hand and used to mark archival paper.

The inhabitants, when visible, dress for damp rather than cold. The chamber is cool enough for breath to appear near the Condensary, but it never freezes. Coats are waxed, boots soft-soled, tools wrapped so they will not ring when set down. Houses are sealed around small dry rooms suspended inside their wet masonry shells. Curtains of felt and oiled silk keep condensation from sleeping areas. Cooking is done communally, because open heat disturbs the city’s carefully managed gradients.

A peculiar civic rule governs every surface: no place may be made completely dry.

Dryness causes brick dust, static discharge, coughing, and agitation among the animals. More importantly, the oldest mechanisms rely on conductive films of moisture. Every workshop maintains a shallow dish of water. Each home has a wetted threshold. Vacant buildings are visited monthly by attendants who sponge the floors and inspect the resident frogs. To abandon a structure is permitted; to let it desiccate is considered dangerous.

There is another rule: nothing that drips regularly may be moved.

A drip is surveyed, numbered, and allowed to establish its own small geography. Over time it makes a cup in stone, then a runnel, then perhaps a route into the central channel. Streets bend around old drips. Rooms are divided by them. One warehouse has a round opening through all four floors because a ceiling drop has fallen through the same vertical line for at least six hundred years. Goods are stacked in a ring around it. At certain hours the drop passes through the entire building and strikes the basement pool with a sound like a distant clock.

This is how time is kept here: by recurrence rather than motion.

Residents speak of the Long Wetting, the interval during which the western wall darkens after rain above. They speak of the Turning of Wheel Seven, which takes most of a day, and the Return of the Silver Gnats, which occurs every eleven years. Some changes are too slow to be observed directly. The channel migrates toward the eastern bank by a few millimeters each generation. The ceiling lowers through mineral growth even as the floor is worn down. Buildings are modified in anticipation of contact with stalactites that will not reach them for another century.

At the lowest end of the settlement, the channel disappears beneath a rustless iron gate. Beyond it lies a sump that no map agrees upon. Animals enter freely, but objects lowered on lines return subtly altered. Iron comes back warm. Glass returns flexible for several minutes. Once, a measuring rod was lowered three meters and retrieved with seven meters of wet markings on it.

The city has built a modest station around this inconsistency. It consists of a tiled room, two winches, a desk, and a row of numbered hooks. Technicians visit once a week. They lower sealed instruments and wait. No one expects the sump to explain itself quickly.

There is little wind. Smells remain where they are made: wet lime by the retaining walls, machine oil near the drums, cold metal at the railings, animal musk beneath bridges, and the faint mushroom sweetness of inhabited houses. A person can navigate by these stationary atmospheres. The city is less a collection of streets than a sequence of moist, dimly lit climates held within one stone room.

Seen from the highest gallery, it does not resemble a metropolis. It resembles a careful arrangement of isolated lights around black water: a pocket of industry, habitation, and animal life fitted into a cavity barely large enough to contain it. Yet its pipes continue far into the surrounding rock. Some rise toward cisterns in the unseen world above. Others vanish through walls without known destinations. Pressure gauges occasionally respond to distant operations for which no local record exists.

The settlement may be small only in the portion that can be entered.

For now, it persists in equilibrium. Water arrives. Animals redistribute it. Machines separate it. Stone records it. The inhabitants repair what wears, but do not restore what has already found a stable shape. Under the quiet lamps, the city is always being dissolved and rebuilt, neither action proceeding fast enough to appear as change.
