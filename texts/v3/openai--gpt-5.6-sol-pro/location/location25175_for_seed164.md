The plateau is so high that distance loses its usual haze. Under the pale, ordinary light of a cold sun, the horizon remains sharp for hundreds of kilometers: black ridges, white vapor, green-black wetlands, and the thin geometries of an industrial city repeated until they resemble geological strata.

It is not a flat plateau. It is a continent caught in the first instant of its formation.

Long cracks divide the high country into enormous slabs. Their edges are angular and unweathered, their exposed faces dark as split iron. Needle ridges stand without scree at their feet. Escarpments descend in clean steps, as if the stone had been broken that morning, though no fracture has advanced within recorded time. There are no rounded hills, no old valleys, no beaches of pulverized rock. Even where water passes over stone, the stone retains its sharp grain. The landscape is young, but not becoming older.

Water is everywhere without ever gathering into abundance. It hangs in the air, beads on cables, wells up through seams in the pavement, and moves in shallow silver sheets across the black rock. The marshes are broad but rarely deep. Their surfaces lie between islands of raw basalt and industrial ceramic, trembling beneath constant condensation. Runnels descend from weeping cliffs and enter culverts whose mouths are tall enough to admit buildings. They emerge many kilometers away with exactly the same volume, temperature, and rhythm.

Nothing floods. Nothing dries.

Clouds form continuously against the higher ridges, but they do not travel in the familiar sense. Their shapes boil and fold while their boundaries remain fixed over the same slopes. Rain falls beneath them in vertical gray territories. One can step from dry air into rainfall, and then out again, as if crossing an invisible wall. Every drop strikes where some previous drop has struck. The wetlands neither rise nor recede. Icicles beneath the viaducts keep the same length year after year, clear at their tips, forever on the verge of dripping.

The cold is persistent rather than severe. Exposed water grows a skin of ice at night, but the ice never thickens enough to close the marsh channels. Snow remains only on shaded steel and the eastern faces of the highest stone blades. Steam rises from vents, cooling into a low ceiling of mist before it can disperse. Metal is painfully cold to the touch. Breath clouds the air—if there is anyone present to breathe.

Vegetation covers more of the plateau than first appears. There are no forests, because height and cold forbid trees, but almost every wet surface supports some form of growth. Moss rises in dense cushions around pipe joints. Glossy mats of liverwort occupy drainage terraces. Tough, dark grasses fill the fissures between paving slabs, each blade edged in white frost. In the marshes stand forests of hollow reeds, pale green and nearly transparent, some taller than a person. Their stems do not sway. When the air moves, it moves through them, producing low organ tones.

Farther from the city, plant life becomes stranger. Black fronds spread over the warm sides of conduits, absorbing waste heat. Sheets of red algae climb vertical rock where mineral water seeps. On the open plateau, immense circular colonies of silver lichen lie kilometers apart, each one perfectly intact, with no younger growth at its edge and no dead tissue at its center. They might be organisms or diagrams. Their branching patterns repeat the utility maps of the city.

There are no animals.

No birds cross the sky. No insects touch the standing water. The marshes contain no fish, no larvae, no small movements under the surface. Flowers, where they occur, open into empty air and remain open. They produce no scent that seems intended for anything. Seed heads are present, but no seed is ever seen to fall. There are no tracks except those pressed into service roads by machines.

The absence is most noticeable because the place is so loud.

A bass vibration passes through the entire plateau. It can be felt in the teeth before it is heard. Above it, separate industrial voices emerge: turbine roar, transformer hum, the hammering of remote pumps, pressure valves releasing in immense sighs. Somewhere beyond every ridge, machinery seems to be starting up. It never finishes starting.

The marsh reeds add their fixed chords. Water rings in metal channels. Suspended power lines sing in the cold, though there is seldom enough wind to move them. At regular intervals, the city emits a deep concussion—not an explosion, but the closing of something very large. The sound crosses the plateau for minutes. It returns from distant escarpments unchanged, as if the echoes have been built into the air.

The city occupies a chain of terraces along the largest fracture. From far away it looks dense: towers, stacks, bridges, elevated rails, tank fields, cooling structures, floodgates, antenna forests. Up close, its emptiness becomes apparent. Buildings stand kilometers apart, connected by disproportionately broad roads and pipelines mounted on pillars. A single plaza may be wide enough to develop its own fog. Stairways climb exposed cliff faces for thousands of steps and reach doors no larger than ordinary doors.

Construction follows no single period. Low blocks of poured black aggregate support older-looking brick furnaces, which in turn carry bright electrical substations and silver pressure vessels. Entire neighborhoods are housed inside the open frameworks of factories. Apartment towers stand among distillation columns, their windows lit in repeating vertical bands. Municipal gardens occupy the roofs of turbine halls. Reservoirs double as public squares: shallow water over engraved stone, crossed by raised walkways with railings warm from buried current.

It is undeniably urban, but the city has been built for an urban population that is not visible.

Streetlights come on under clouds and go off in clear daylight. Trams move along elevated tracks, arriving at platforms precisely as warning tones sound. Their doors open. Their interiors are heated. Condensation clouds the glass. Then the doors close, and the empty carriages continue across bridges into the mist.

Factories operate in the same manner. Conveyor belts carry slabs of dark mineral from one sealed structure to another. Gantries pivot. Cranes lower hooks into shafts and raise them again without cargo. Presses descend with enough force to shake water from nearby moss. Electrical arcs flare blue behind frosted windows. Yet there are no stockpiles, no smoke plumes traveling downwind, no evidence of a finished product. Material entering the works cannot be distinguished from material leaving it.

The machinery may not be producing objects. It may be maintaining conditions.

Pumps keep the water at its exact shallow depth. Furnaces sustain the warm seepage on which the black fronds grow. Compressors hold the clouds against the mountains. The rail system distributes heat, vibration, or timing. The great closures heard across the plateau may be valves regulating pressure within the continental fracture itself.

Beneath the city, the rock is hollow.

Access shafts descend through fresh basalt into chambers so large that their ceilings produce weather. Most are nearly empty. Bridges cross them without visible destinations. Pillars vanish downward into cloud. In some chambers, rain falls upward from black pools and condenses on the roof, feeding the weeping cliffs above. In others, suspended lights reveal only mist and the far outlines of pipes, each broad enough to contain a district.

The city is therefore not merely built on the landscape. It passes through it, braces it, ventilates it, and perhaps defines its edges. What appears on maps as a mountain range may be the upper flange of a buried structure. What appears to be raw stone sometimes rings when struck, sending a note through the ground that is answered days away.

Still, almost nothing occupies this vastness. Between installations lie hundreds of kilometers of wet upland where there is only fractured rock, low vegetation, fixed rain, and the continuous industrial thunder. A traveler can stand on one ridge and see three cities, two marsh systems, and a chain of power pylons disappearing over the curve of the world, yet encounter no moving thing nearby. Presence is distributed thinly. Each tower, reed bed, engine hall, and shaft mouth seems solitary despite belonging to a planetary system.

There is no decay. Paint does not peel beyond its present degree of peeling. Rust stains spread down walls but never reach the ground. Broken windows remain broken, their shards held in frames or scattered across floors without being displaced. Moss covers abandoned machinery but does not consume it. Water runs over stair edges without smoothing them. The industrial city has the appearance of having operated for millennia and of having been completed less than an hour ago.

Even sound does not mark time. The hammering does not accelerate or falter. Echoes return on schedule. The reeds hold the same notes. Thunder remains inside the stationary clouds, rolling continuously without a first strike or a final silence.

There are clocks in the stations, but their hands do not move. They disagree with one another, each showing a different hour, though all their second hands shiver as if current is passing through them. Digital signs display arrival times that are neither approaching nor delayed. In one district every window reflects morning light; in another, under the same sky, the glass holds the color of late afternoon.

Nothing in the place is dead in the ordinary sense. The vegetation is green, water circulates, lights burn, engines labor, and heat passes through pipes. Yet nothing completes a life, a journey, or a task. There is activity without event, maintenance without deterioration, ecology without animals, and weather without change.

At the highest point of the plateau stands no monument, only a ring of cooling towers around a natural-looking lake. The lake is perfectly clear and impossibly deep. Its rim is crowded with frost grasses and low white flowers. Pipes descend into it, vanishing in the blue-black below. Clouds pour steadily from the towers, join the fixed weather, and return as rain across the marshes.

From there, the whole high continent can be felt operating.

The ground hums. Towers flash in distant sequences. Water shines in fractures. Empty trains cross empty bridges. Green growth clings to the wet margins of steel and newborn stone. Beyond the last visible ridge, the same immense system continues—sparse, cold, deafening, and held forever at the moment just after its construction, before erosion, before habitation, before history has decided to begin.
