The high sea begins far below the ordinary ocean.

It rests on the shoulders of a drowned mountain system: a plateau raised nearly to the surface, though its edges fall away for kilometers into black water. Above it, the sea is cool, clear, and green-lit. Daylight reaches down without becoming darkness so much as becoming color—white at the wave ceiling, then pale jade, then the deep, even blue of old glass. There is no visible land. The nearest shore, if one exists, lies beyond the curvature of the world.

Vegetation fills the plateau from stone to surface, but not as a crowded forest. It is a plant world arranged around immense intervals.

Single trunks rise from the drowned heights, each one kilometers from the next. They are not trees in the terrestrial sense. Their lower bodies are braided cables of rootwood, broad enough to enclose valleys, gripping the eroded basalt through pores dissolved over geological ages. The cables join into pale columns, ribbed and flexible, which ascend through the water until they spread into horizontal crowns just beneath the sunlit surface.

Each crown covers an area large enough to make its own weather.

From below, these crowns look like continents suspended upside down: dark green undersides, capes of leaf, bays of open water, long peninsulas of frond extending into blue emptiness. Their upper surfaces lie against the waves and drink both air and sea. Rain gathers above them. Mist hangs over them. On calm days—though every day is equally calm—the leaves show through the surface as green fields trembling beneath a sheet of glass.

Yet the crowns never grow outward, never shed, never decay. Their extent is final.

The entire ecosystem has reached an exact equilibrium. No branch is advancing into light. No root is thickening in rock. No leaf is newly unfurling, and none is dying. Individual cells perform their work, water passes through tissues, sugars circulate, minerals are exchanged, but every transfer is countered perfectly elsewhere. It is not stasis like ice. It is motion without change: an engine running forever at one fixed speed.

That engine is loud.

The great trunks are hollowed by their own anatomy into chambers, ducts, pressure reservoirs, and serial pumps. Cold water is drawn through root-fans in the plateau’s fissures and forced upward in regular columns. Warm surface water descends through separate veins. Mineral brines pass through membranes of bark. Gas bladders compress and release. Stiff cilia turn in cylindrical galleries, millions together, resembling turbines grown from cellulose and shell.

There are no factories here, no pipes laid by hands, no tools, no bolts, no worked surfaces. Nevertheless, the landscape has the character of heavy industry.

The vegetation fractionates salts. It concentrates iron and manganese into dark nodules held among its roots. It strips carbon from the water and presses it into black structural fibers. Along the trunks, banks of translucent organs pulse with a steady internal glow, electrolyzing seawater and routing the gases into buoyancy chambers. Huge leaf vanes exploit the pressure of the tides, although the tides themselves seem never to alter. Root plates exchange heat with volcanic stone that has been cool for ages yet continues to provide the same faint thermal difference.

Nothing was invented. Nothing was constructed. Production is simply what life does here.

The sound never swells or fades. It is one continuous planetary chord: the low cavitation thunder of sap rising through kilometer-long vessels; the metallic ticking of mineralized valves; the smooth rotary hum of ciliary mills; the rushing of water through millions of leaf pores. Near a trunk, the noise enters the bones. Far out in the open blue between crowns, it becomes a uniform pressure, as if the sea itself were voicing a single note.

There are sharper sounds within it. Shoals of thin, bright fish hold station around the intake vents, and their fins make a dry whisper. Crustaceans graze films from the root cables, clicking continuously at an unvarying average rate. Long-bodied swimmers call across the open intervals in tones like foghorns heard through mountains. The calls overlap so regularly that they cease to seem like messages. They are part of the machinery of the place.

Fauna is abundant, though the vastness makes it look sparse.

A trunk may support millions of animals, while the kilometers between trunks hold almost nothing visible. Life gathers at interfaces: beneath leaf shelves, inside curtains of hairlike roots, around the cold upwellings, and along the borders where crown-shadow meets clear water. The open sea remains immense and bare, populated mainly by drifting transparent forms whose organs are scattered like small colored instruments inside them.

Some animals have become nearly sessile in the unchanging conditions. Broad rays hang beneath the crowns without beating their fins, mouths open to the constant stream. Spiral grazers travel around the trunks so slowly that several generations might be mistaken for one individual making a single circuit. Pale whales, narrow as needles at this scale, occupy fixed routes between the vegetation-columns. They swim continuously, but every pod is always found in the same reach of water, passing the same suspended forests at the same intervals.

Predation occurs without altering abundance. Birth occurs without increasing population. Death occurs without diminishing it. Whatever is eaten is restored; whatever is born replaces an exact loss. No species gains advantage. No mutation spreads. The ecology does not tend toward balance—it has already arrived there, and time cannot push it away.

Even erosion has become a settled condition. The plateau is anciently weathered, rounded into long ridges and basins under the roots. Water moves through its gullies, but removes no further grain. Cliffs at the outer edge trail mineral-green beards into the abyss. Their profiles are smooth, final, and repeated over hundreds of kilometers. Loose sediment lies in rippled fields whose ripples do not migrate. Fine particles hang above them without settling or rising, each caught in the exact counterforce of current and buoyancy.

At the rim, the world opens.

The rooted plateau ends in a descending wall veiled by vertical gardens. Far above, the plant crowns continue to spread beneath the bright surface; far below, the mountain face passes through blue into violet and then apparent black. Great roots descend the cliff like frozen waterfalls, seeking nothing, already attached wherever attachment is possible. Between them are caves washed into arches and amphitheaters. No cave enlarges. No arch collapses.

Beyond the rim, the trunks seem impossibly tall. Their crowns merge with surface glare, their bases cling to pinnacles in the dark, and their middle reaches stand in luminous open water. Fish circle them in horizontal belts according to pressure, temperature, and color. Each belt is as stable as a ring in stone.

The coolness is pervasive but not severe. It is the temperature of deep shade, wet mineral, and metal touched before dawn. There are no warm currents or polar intrusions, no seasons, no sudden thermoclines. The water maintains one narrow range from horizon to horizon, modified only enough around the living columns to drive their perpetual metabolism. Skin would numb slowly here, then remain at the threshold of numbness forever.

At the surface, weather exists but does not develop. A gray-white ceiling of clouds stands above the crown fields. Rain falls in fixed territories, each downpour a silver wall extending from cloud to sea. Elsewhere, circular openings reveal ordinary daylight. The rain does not travel. The clearings do not close. Waves cross the exposed water in identical ranks, break softly over the buoyant leaves, and are absorbed by mats of flexible tissue. From below, every raindrop is a brief bright wound in the surface, repeated so constantly that the pattern appears motionless.

Navigation would be possible but unnerving. There are landmarks everywhere—the pillar with red lichenous plates, the split crown, the root ridge shaped like a sleeping animal—yet none offers evidence of before or after. A traveler could leave a trail only in memory. Bent fronds return instantly to their established positions. Disturbed silt restores its old suspension. A wake closes without dispersing outward. Even bubbles, once released, rise along predetermined paths as if they have risen there forever.

The world seems to permit movement, but not consequence.

Nothing can be built because no alteration persists. Stone cannot be displaced from its bed. Fiber cannot remain cut. A shelter woven from leaves would unweave itself—not violently, but with the irresistible gentleness of the landscape returning to its sole allowed configuration. Fire is impossible, not merely because of the water, but because combustion would create a new distribution of matter. Waste cannot accumulate. Extraction cannot deplete. Every attempted mark is edited away by equilibrium.

And yet the place is not hostile. It has no storms, no famine, no bloom, no collapse. Its predators do not multiply beyond their prey. Its engines do not fail. The rain does not intensify. The cliffs do not give way. It offers endless food, endless clear water, endless sound, and no history.

In the broad spaces between the plant-columns, the normal brightness of day descends through kilometers of sea. The suspended crowns cast shadows like nations. Animals move through those shadows in fixed migrations. Beneath them, the drowned mountains hold their weathered forms. Inside them, the vegetal machinery turns without wear.

Seen all at once, the high plateau is less like a forest submerged by a flood than a single green apparatus occupying an ocean: vast, diffuse, rooted in mountains, breathing through the surface of the planet.

It has been running for longer than anything remembers.

It has never started. It will never stop.
