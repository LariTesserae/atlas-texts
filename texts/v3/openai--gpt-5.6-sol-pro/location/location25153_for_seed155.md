The city occupies the summit of an ancient plateau, though “summit” suggests a peak sharper than anything remaining here. Time has worn the mountains down to a broad, high table of shallow basins and long green inclines. The horizon is open in every direction. Low ridges overlap like the backs of buried animals, and beyond them stand pale remnants of higher ranges, so eroded that clouds pass over their summits without seeming to notice.

There is no exposed stone.

Everything underfoot is vegetal. The apparent bedrock is a compressed mass of roots, lignin, dormant seed tissue, and black leaf-felt extending downward farther than anyone has drilled. Roads cross it in straight gray-green bands, but these too are alive: tough braided rhizomes laid under tension, their surfaces polished by carts and foot traffic. In dry weather they harden to something like horn. After rain they swell, closing cracks and gently lifting misplaced objects. A road left unused does not crumble. It curls.

Vegetation is total without looking lush. The plateau is too cool, too high, and too old for exuberant growth. Much of its living surface has settled into final, patient forms: close moss with the toughness of hide; silver lichens that are actually the exposed skins of immense underground leaves; low mats of red-black clover fused edge to edge. Trees are rare and widely separated. Each one stands alone in an enormous allotment of sky, its crown flattened by centuries of wind. Their roots may meet beneath the city, but aboveground there is room between trunks for districts, factories, reservoirs, and empty squares.

The city itself is grown rather than built, though its inhabitants speak of construction and maintain guilds of engineers. Towers are hollow stems trained upright around temporary scaffolds. Once mature, they lignify into dark, ribbed cylinders several stories high, with windows opened by selective pruning. Their interiors smell faintly of apples, tannin, and wet rope. Floors are shelves induced to grow across the central cavity. Staircases spiral naturally because the towers themselves twist toward the sun.

Long residential blocks are made from pleached hedges whose trunks have fused into continuous walls. Roofs consist of broad overlapping leaves, thick as leather and permanently angled to send rain toward cistern flowers. Doors are muscular valves. They must be stroked along the grain to open; striking them makes them clamp shut. In poorer quarters, windows occasionally heal over during winter.

There are factories among these structures, and they are as loud and organized as any city of iron. Yet iron is almost unknown. Machinery is made of seasoned heartwood, seed-shell ceramic, resin bearings, toothed cones, tendonlike vines, and flywheels grown from circular burls. Great fermentation halls consume pruned biomass and exhale heat through branching chimneys. Presses squeeze oils from pale nuts the size of barrels. Sap is distilled into solvents, lamp fuel, lacquer, medicine, and explosive gel. Loomworks tease fibers from the plateau’s underlayer and weave cables strong enough to suspend bridges across the old erosion gullies.

Power comes partly from wind and partly from the city’s slow internal tensions. The towers sway. Their movement draws braided root-cables through reciprocating engines below the streets. On windy days the whole settlement manufactures energy simply by bending. The transfer houses thunder with wooden pistons. Camshafts knock. Membrane pumps boom like drums. Resin whistles shriek when pressure rises. Seed-hoppers rattle in long descending bursts.

The noise carries astonishingly far in the thin, cool air. From outside the city it sounds like a storm trapped underground: pounding mills, groaning trunks, the pulsed exhalation of bellows, and the continuous dry applause of leaves. Sound travels through the root substrate as well. Place an ear against the road and one can hear factories several districts away, distorted into vast, slow impacts. Workers navigate partly by these vibrations. Each ward has its own mechanical rhythm.

There are few animals. Small translucent mites live in warm joints of machinery, and now and then a dark, long-winged bird crosses the plateau without landing. Worms are treated as important civic visitors. Pollination is accomplished mostly by wind and by municipal devices: wheeled brushes, pollen cannons, and crews carrying soft fans between distant flowering towers. The absence of abundant animal life gives the city an oddly unobserved quality. Fruit ripens without birdsong. Refuse does not attract scavengers. At night there are no insect choruses—only valves ticking shut as the temperature falls.

Water is present, but never generously. Rain comes in brief diagonal veils. It darkens the roads, beads on waxy roofs, and vanishes into thousands of leaf-cups. Condensation forms every morning inside the taller buildings, where warm factory breath meets the cold outer walls. Gutters—pale, open-veined stems—carry this moisture to reservoirs grown in sheltered hollows. These ponds are small, black, and perfectly still, each surrounded by pumping reeds. Beyond them the plateau may remain dry for weeks.

The inhabitants ration water less for drinking than for growth. A building can survive a drought by going dormant, but it becomes difficult to inhabit: doors stiffen, stairs shrink from their sockets, and rooms contract by a hand’s breadth. During wet periods the reverse occurs. Streets become springy. Walls soften. Entire neighborhoods make faint creaking adjustments through the night. Surveyors walk the avenues at dawn, driving marked thorns into the ground to measure how far the city has expanded.

Despite its industry, the settlement is sparse. Structures cluster around processing works, then fall away into broad planted emptiness. A person may leave a crowded turbine hall, cross a threshold of woven bark, and find themselves in a plaza half a kilometer wide with nothing in it but one wind-shaped tree and a line of condensation basins. Rail lines—parallel runners of polished root—extend across vacant green slopes toward isolated mills. Elevated conduits stride over the land on branching supports, carrying sap, steam, pollen, and electrical charge from district to district.

The openness is deliberate. Crowding plants causes them to exchange dreams.

This is known, measured, and regulated.

All vegetation on the plateau is connected at some depth, but distance usually blurs the exchange into harmless background sensation: impressions of light, pressure, mineral taste, approaching frost. When too many cultivated structures grow close together, those impressions become coherent. Buildings begin to share an idea of what they are.

At first the effects are minor. A factory grows bedroom alcoves. A row of houses develops loading apertures and turns its doors toward the freight lines. Then geometry becomes unreliable. Corridors lengthen according to how much fatigue is carried through them. Windows open onto the weather remembered by the wood rather than the weather outside. A staircase may descend into the root-state of the city, where all districts exist adjacent as points of pressure.

Older quarters are particularly unstable. Their walls have accumulated centuries of grafts, repairs, uses, and human expectations. They no longer distinguish consistently between blueprint, memory, and present form. One municipal archive can be entered from three separate eras depending on which door is used. Its clerks tie colored ribbons around their wrists so they can identify the version of the day to which they belong.

No one calls this magic. The engineers describe it as architectural convergence.

The city’s strictest laws concern pruning, proximity, and metaphor. It is illegal to describe a public structure as “hungry” within reach of its leaves. Bridges must never be praised for patience. Hospitals are prevented from rooting into cemeteries. Every new tower is planted with a false name and given its official designation only after its foundations have learned the difference between themselves and the ground.

At the oldest edge of the plateau, erosion has nearly completed its work. Valleys that once must have been deep are now shallow channels filled with tough green tissue. Cliffs have become long ramps. Even the distinction between slope and plain seems exhausted. Here the city thins into abandoned infrastructure: silent pumping stations, collapsed wind organs, and rows of empty towers whose crowns still turn to follow the sun.

These ruins are not dead. Nothing here dies cleanly enough for that. They have simply withdrawn from urban time.

On clear afternoons, under an ordinary blue-white sky, their shadows point in directions that do not agree with the sun. Some shadows lead downhill into places higher than the plateau. Others move with the factory rhythm, shortening at every distant hammer stroke. Workers avoid stepping across them, not because doing so is always dangerous, but because repetition teaches the shadow one’s shape.

The weather remains brisk and changeable. Wind sweeps the summit most days, carrying resin vapor and fine yellow pollen over the open districts. Brief rain rattles against the leaf roofs loudly enough to halt conversation. Fog sometimes forms in the erosion basins, collecting around the lower streets while the towers stand in clear daylight above it. Lightning is rare, but when it comes, the city receives it deliberately. Copper-colored vines lift from the tallest crowns, flower into branching conductors, and direct the charge into deep storage bulbs. For several hours afterward, every plant on the plateau glows faintly along its veins.

Then the engines accelerate.

Root-trains slide along living rails. Presses beat faster. The great wind-grown flywheels spin under their canopies. Across the sparse, ancient green, isolated industrial districts answer one another with whistles and concussive bursts of steam. The plateau trembles from rim to rim—not violently, but with the unmistakable sense of a vast organism performing work.

From a distance the city can be mistaken for empty land. Its towers resemble trees, its roofs merge with the ground, and its avenues are only subtle differences in green. Only the sound betrays it: a broad mechanical roar rising from a landscape that appears to contain nothing but low plants, open sky, and a few widely spaced trunks.

Closer in, another impression takes hold. The city does not seem to have been placed upon the plateau, nor even grown from it. Rather, the plateau has spent an age imagining a city, and this—roads, mills, dwellings, laws, errors in space—is the form that imagination has finally taken.
