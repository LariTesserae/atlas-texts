### The Throat in the Summit

The landscape is hidden inside a crack no wider than a hand.

It opens near the crown of a newly risen mountain, where the outer air is thin enough to sting the lungs and the horizon falls away in every direction. The summit itself is raw: black glass, sulfur-yellow seams, blades of basalt still sharp from cooling. Nothing roots there. Nothing has had time to soften it. Rain strikes the exposed stone, flashes briefly to steam, and runs into fractures.

One fracture drinks more than it should.

Within, the crack widens into a steep, enclosed hollow—a pocket landscape suspended inside the mountain. It is perhaps the size of a small room, though measurements fail almost immediately. Its walls lean away at impossible angles, and the bottom seems both an arm’s length below and far beneath the mountain’s roots. A pebble dropped into it can be heard touching the floor almost at once, yet may be seen falling for several minutes.

The rock is hot enough that bare skin cannot rest against it. Its surfaces shine with condensation.

Water is everywhere, but almost nowhere gathers into a stable pool. It sweats from the black walls in clear beads. It threads through hairline faults, hangs beneath overhangs, and crawls uphill in trembling skins. Droplets swell, hesitate, then detach in several directions at once. Some fall. Some rise toward the narrow strip of sky. Others pass sideways into the stone and emerge from another wall with their shape preserved.

After rain, the hollow becomes saturated. Steam fills it without becoming opaque, a hot clarity in which every surface seems newly washed. The atmosphere condenses on itself. Strings of droplets form in empty air, marking invisible planes like dew on glass that is not there. When these planes intersect, the water makes bright corners.

There are no plants. No moss softens the ledges, no algae color the pools, no roots probe the cracks. The mineral surfaces remain naked: obsidian black, basalt gray, raw iron red, salt white, sulfur in bruised yellows and greens. In a few places, translucent crusts spread across the rock, but these are not leaves or lichen. They are colonies of simple mineral-bodied organisms—or minerals practicing the motions of life. They grow only during moments when no one is looking directly at them.

Nearly every other space is occupied by animals.

They are so numerous that at first they resemble texture. The walls appear furred, scaled, or stippled until portions of the texture detach and move. Thousands of pale heat-mites crowd the condensation lines, drinking one bead until their bodies become transparent. Flat black crawlers stack beneath stone lips in layers, each fitted so precisely over the next that the colony looks like a deck of wet cards. Needle-legged hunters stand across the tiniest rivulets, their feet dimpling the water but never piercing it. Soft-bodied knot animals squeeze from pores smaller than their own eyes.

Most are no larger than grains of sand. Their abundance gives them the gravity of herds.

The floor, where it can be called a floor, is a shifting congregation of shell-backs. Their shells are chipped directly from the surrounding glass—not carried armor, but living growth with obsidian edges. They graze on salts, steam-borne particles, one another’s discarded skins, and the brief chemical blooms around hot vents. Predators pass among them as narrow gaps. A lane of sudden emptiness moves through the multitude, and only afterward does the hunter become visible: a translucent ribbon with a mouth on whichever end is nearer prey.

Nothing cries out. Wings, where they exist, beat without buzzing. Shells touch without clicking. Even predation is almost ceremonial in its quietness. The loudest ordinary sound is water: a close ticking of droplets against stone, the faint hiss where they cross hot seams, and an occasional thick plop from a pool that cannot be found.

The silence does not come from stillness. The hollow is ceaselessly active. It comes from the softness and smallness of its inhabitants, and perhaps from a defect in sound itself. Noise travels poorly here. A scrape made on one wall may emerge as a vibration in the opposite wall, felt through the fingers but not heard. A shout from the entrance dwindles to a dry breath. Meanwhile, the footfall of a mite can sometimes be heard from impossibly far below: one precise tap, enormous and solitary.

The animals live by the cycles of rain, heat, and condensation. When clouds wrap the summit, fresh water enters and the hollow expands. Passages appear between droplets. Entire migrations move through the curved interior of a single bead: swimmers, hunters, egg-bearers, scavengers. To them the droplet may be a lake several days across. When sunlight returns and the summit steams, these waters contract. Shorelines race inward. Populations crowd onto salt ridges or seal themselves inside bubbles in the stone.

Some do not survive evaporation. Their bodies remain as tiny clear husks, heaped in drifts along the hottest cracks. Others climb out of the shrinking water and become different animals upon reaching air.

This is one of the hollow’s rules: a creature is partly determined by the surface that contains it. In water it may have fins and a single silver eye. On rock it unfolds jointed legs. Crossing a hanging plane of condensation, it becomes a flattened silhouette moving within that plane, visible from either side but possessing no thickness. If two planes intersect while it is crossing, the creature divides. Both descendants continue with equal certainty that they are the original.

The larger inhabitants are rare only by comparison. Blind, six-limbed things the length of a finger lurk in deeper folds. They have soft gray hides and mouths lined with dark volcanic glass. Several carry flakes of obsidian gripped between paired forelimbs. They chip the flakes by striking them against fresh edges, producing minute scrapers and hooked points.

They build nothing.

There are no nests, walls, traps, or monuments. Tools are used, abandoned, and soon claimed by the hot mineral crust. One animal may split a shell with a sharp flake, leave it where it falls, and never return. Another may pick up the same flake generations later, though only minutes have passed outside the crack. Their craft does not accumulate into architecture. Knowledge survives as gesture: the angle of a wrist, the choice of a fault line, the instinct to cool a cutting edge in a rising drop.

At the hollow’s narrowest point, a vein of water emerges from the mountain and divides around a blade of glass. On one side it runs downward. On the other it runs upward. Animals gather densely along both streams, drinking head to head while oriented in opposite gravities. Predators cross between them and briefly possess two undersides. Shed skins drift toward both ends of the chamber.

Above, ordinary daylight enters through the summit crack. It is not eerie light, nor colored by any supernatural source. When the sky is blue, the opening is blue. Clouds pass white or gray. Sunlight lies plainly on wet stone. That normality makes the deeper distortions more severe. Under honest noon light, shadows detach by a fraction and continue movements their owners have not yet made. Reflections in droplets show the hollow as it was before the mountain erupted. Occasionally they show it old: the glass dulled, the crack widened, every animal gone.

The mountain is geologically young. Its interior still shifts and settles. Fresh fractures open with no more sound than thread breaking. Heat wells upward. Gas blisters form beneath mineral films and burst into cold vapor. Rainwater reaching buried hot rock returns as pulses of steam, and each pulse rearranges the ecosystem. Colonies scatter. Eggs hatch all at once. Transparent hunters inflate like sails. The hanging water-planes tilt, merge, and vanish.

Yet the place does not erode as quickly as it should. Water rounds nothing. Edges remain keen. Channels cut themselves, then seal again, restoring the original sharpness of the stone. The hollow seems to resist becoming old. Perhaps each rainfall creates it again from the first moment of fracture, along with every creature inside it. Perhaps the dense animal life is not inhabiting the crack but is one of the forms the crack takes while wet.

From outside, there is almost nothing to see: a dark seam near a summit, breathing a little steam after rain.

Inside that seam, multitudes drink from airborne lakes. They hunt across opposing gravities, split at invisible corners, and quietly work flakes of newborn glass. The mountain falls away beneath them for kilometers. Above them is a strip of ordinary sky. Around them, hot water moves through stone that has scarcely finished becoming stone, and the small enclosed world repeatedly forgets which direction, size, and moment it belongs to.
