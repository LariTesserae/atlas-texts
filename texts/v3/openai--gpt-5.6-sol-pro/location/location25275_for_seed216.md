The high country is not land in the usual sense. It is a construction so immense that weather gathers inside it.

From a distance—though distance is unreliable here—it resembles a dark green plateau suspended above the lower clouds. Its edges descend in curtains of roots, braided ramps, and weeping walls. There is no mountain beneath. The entire elevation has been made: terrace upon terrace of living timber, baskets the size of valleys, vaults woven from trunks, and causeways stitched together with vines. Every surface carries the marks of hands or of the simple tools that extended them: wedge cuts, peg holes, lashings, graft scars, clay-smeared seams. Yet the oldest workmanship has weathered beyond any clear distinction between building and growth. Branches have swallowed their bindings. Stakes have become forests. Foundations bloom.

There is no bare mineral ground. What seems at first to be soil is a deep, springing felt of roots, husks, moss, leaf mold, and decayed cordage. Beneath that lies another floor, woven from living boughs; beneath that, often, nothing.

The emptiness is the first thing travelers misunderstand. The place is luxuriant, almost completely occupied by vegetation, but its volume is mostly void. A few colossal structures enclose distances that would elsewhere hold provinces. Forests climb their outer ribs while inside them open chambers miles across: dim green hollows crossed by one thin bridge, one hanging orchard, a strand of mist. The greatest halls contain their own clouds. Rain can fall in one end while the other remains warm and dry. Flocks pass through apertures in the walls and take half a day to reach the opposite side.

Some chambers have no visible bottom. Leaves shed into them without ever being heard to land.

The architecture was built with stone-age patience rather than advanced machinery. There are no engines hidden in the roots, no metal skeletons, no humming systems. The plateau holds together through joinery, tension, growth, and the accumulated correction of generations. Vast beams were encouraged from saplings over centuries. Bark was peeled in narrow bands to bend a trunk without killing it. Forks were grafted into load-bearing arches. Ropes were braided from wet fiber and tightened as they dried. Clay was carried upward in gourds and pressed into joints by thumb. Where a span sagged, gardeners planted a supporting grove beneath it. Where a wall split, they laced the wound shut and fed it until the seam sprouted.

No one could build the whole country now. Perhaps no one ever could. Each community understands only its local craft: how to maintain a stair of fungal wood, how to persuade a roof-tree to thicken, how to drain a flooded nest court. The total design exceeds not only any individual mind but any historical memory.

It may no longer have a total design.

The upper surfaces are warm despite their height. A slow, dark atmosphere wraps them, holding heat and moisture close. Sunlight arrives weakened by permanent layers of cloud and by canopies stacked one above another. Noon is a muted bronze-green, bright enough to distinguish the gloss on leaves but seldom bright enough for a sharp shadow. In enclosed groves, luminous molds and pale flowers provide a soft counterfeit dusk. Their light does not spread far. It rests on the things that emit it, as though illumination here has become viscous.

Water is everywhere without becoming an ocean. It beads along fibers, gathers in the cups of leaves, and runs down the interior walls of hollow trunks. Warm rain passes frequently over the plateau, brief and fine, striking the canopy with a hush like sand poured onto cloth. Afterward every railing drips. Bridges darken. Lichens swell and open their pores. The huge woven floors bend by imperceptible degrees under the added weight.

Water travels through the construction in ways that seem intentional until one follows them too closely. Gutters carved into branches feed cistern-fruits with hardened skins. Root pipes carry seepage along shallow gradients. Chainlike vines guide droplets across open air. Yet some streams climb. A thread of water may creep up the underside of an arch, divide at a junction, and vanish into flowering knots high overhead. In certain halls, condensation forms as floating spheres, each containing an inverted reflection of a different part of the landscape. Birds drink from them in flight. If touched by a human hand, they sometimes fall upward.

The plateau’s margins are wettest. There the woven country frays into a marsh-edge without a marsh: suspended reed beds, shallow pools held in leaf-lined baskets, mats of sedge stretched over cloud. Frogs live among them, calling so softly that their throats can be seen inflating before any sound is heard. Slender fish move through flooded root channels. During heavy rain they cross from pool to pool along temporary streams that run over the walkways. When the water recedes, some remain in the damp moss, breathing through the red filaments along their sides.

Vegetation fills every role that elsewhere would be divided between plant, landscape, building, and tool. There are groves that are also streets. Flowers serve as cups, lamps, traps, bells, and calendars. Thick-skinned gourds form storerooms while still attached to their vines. Great leaves are pegged into overlapping roofs; when they die, their replacements grow beneath them. Rope orchards produce long fibrous tendrils already twisted around one another. Certain trees bear smooth, dense nodules used as hammerheads. Others produce glass-hard thorns that are split into blades.

Even fire has been partly replaced by plants. Heat-fermenting beds warm sleeping chambers. Resin pods give a low smokeless glow when pierced, though their light is reddish and brief. Cooking is done in black flowers whose bowls become hot during pollination. A meal placed within must be removed before the flower closes.

There are animals everywhere, but the country remains quiet.

Most creatures have adapted to a world in which a loud mistake can travel through living beams. Hoofed browsers step on divided pads that distribute their weight across the woven floor. Arboreal grazers have long balancing tails and fur the color of wet lichen. Small six-legged things move beneath broad leaves in disciplined lines, trimming molds and carrying spores in translucent sacs. Moths as wide as sails drift through the great chambers without wingbeats, steering on differences in temperature. Pale cats hunt them from hanging platforms, leaping into the void and returning along elastic vines.

Birds are abundant but seldom sing. Many communicate by changing the color of their throats or by opening patterned fans beneath their wings. Others tap coded rhythms directly into branches, so quietly that the message is felt through a resting palm rather than heard. Predators listen for these vibrations. Villages therefore stand on isolation knots: complex tangles of soft wood and corky roots that prevent footsteps from passing into the wider structure.

The largest fauna inhabit the voids. They are slow, air-buoyant animals shaped like hollow rays, browsing on dangling moss and the pollen haze below flowering bridges. Colonies of tiny insects live within their translucent bodies. At night the insects glow, outlining the animals as moving constellations. They seem close enough to touch even when they are miles away. Scale behaves badly in the interior halls; a creature can remain the same apparent size for hours as it approaches, then pass overhead small enough to settle in two hands.

This is not an optical illusion. The spaces do not preserve size consistently.

A narrow door may open into a grove larger than the tower containing it. Two paths that run parallel can experience different seasons. A traveler looking through a lattice sometimes sees the back of their own head far below, walking on a bridge they have not yet reached. Seeds dropped into particular shafts emerge months later from flowers in the past. The inhabitants mark such places with braided loops—not as warnings, exactly, but as statements that ordinary promises do not hold there.

The oldest woven districts have settled into stable distortions. Their impossibilities are weathered and dependable. A staircase called the Long Descent has led upward for at least six hundred years. Rain in the Court of Knots always falls toward the nearest living heart. One orchard grows its fruit around memories: anyone eating there recalls a childhood belonging to one of the gardeners who planted it. These effects are treated as local conditions, like slope or wind. Children learn them alongside the names of edible leaves.

Less stable regions are abandoned. Their bridges reattach themselves overnight. Their interiors exchange places. Sometimes an entire inhabited grove can be seen hanging upside down in an unreachable chamber, its people going calmly about their work under a rain that rises from the canopy. Whether it is a reflection, a future, or another grove entirely is not known. Signals sent across the gap are answered, but never with the same sequence that was given.

Weather passes slowly through this enormous made world. Warm fronts climb from the cloud ocean below, entering through torn walls and ventilation mouths. Mist condenses on the lower gardens. Then the moisture rises through rootwork and animal breath until clouds form under the upper roofs. For days the air grows heavy and leaves turn their undersides outward. At last rain begins in many chambers at once. Water drums faintly on stretched leaf membranes, runs down miles of carved channels, and sets the whole plateau under a minute additional strain.

The strain is answered by growth. Tension vines contract. Root bundles swell. Old joints creak once, very deeply, and become still. Maintenance crews go out with bone knives, wooden wedges, coils of fiber, and baskets of grafting paste. They work barefoot, feeling structural changes through their soles. A vibration too low to hear can tell them where a valley-sized truss is failing.

Erosion here does not reduce the world so much as mature it. Rain rounds cut edges. Moss softens exposed joins. Walkways sag into graceful curves and are reinforced where they settle. Dead towers collapse into the void, but their trailing vines catch on lower levels and become new suspended gardens. Rot opens chambers. Beetles widen them. People fit doors into the openings, and what was damage becomes habitation.

Nothing remains as originally made, yet almost nothing is allowed to become merely wild. The distinction would be meaningless anyway. Gardeners prune storms by managing the leaves that seed them. Animals reshape bridges by nesting. The floors digest the dead. Villages migrate along growing branches, abandoning rooms before those rooms close into solid wood. A wall may be cultivated for five generations, inhabited for three, then harvested as a raft and lowered through the clouds.

At the highest known point stands a sparse crown of arches exposed to the dim sky. The wind there is stronger, but even there sound seems absorbed by the immensity. A few black leaves turn slowly on stalks taller than towers. Between them lie circular basins of warm rainwater, each perfectly still despite the wind. Their surfaces do not reflect the sky above. They show green structures continuing upward—more terraces, more woven continents, faint with distance, suspended in a darkness where the sun ought to be.

Sometimes debris falls from those reflected heights: unfamiliar petals, lengths of ancient cord, tools made from the bones of animals that do not live anywhere below.

The people of the crown leave offerings beside the basins, usually a seed, a cup of water, and a loop of hand-braided fiber. They do not worship what lies beyond. They say only that every structure requires something to hang from.

No one has found what this world hangs from.
