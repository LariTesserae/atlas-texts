### The Drowned Interval

It is not a sea in the ordinary sense, though it is entirely under water.

The place occupies a narrow interval beneath the seafloor: a pocket caught between two layers of newly formed black stone. From outside, there is almost nothing to indicate it. A fissure no wider than a hand exhales cold mineral water into the greater ocean. Pale filaments grow around the opening, combed flat by the faint current. Beyond that throat, the rock parts unexpectedly, enclosing a submerged chamber dense enough to feel less like a cave than the interior of some saturated organ.

There is no open ground. Every surface is occupied.

Young basalt presses in from above and below, still sharp at its fractures, its edges not yet rounded by centuries of water. Needles of volcanic glass jut from the walls. Thin mineral shelves overlap like scales. In some places the stone appears to have split only moments ago: the exposed faces are clean, dark, and faintly iridescent, with none of the silt or pale crust found elsewhere. Yet nothing here moves quickly. The fractures widen by the width of a cell over decades. Small sounds, when they come, arrive years apart.

Water fills the chamber without seam or horizon. It is cool, almost motionless, heavy with dissolved salts and metallic traces. There is no true current, only a slow exchange between warmer water bleeding upward through the floor and colder water sinking from the concealed entrance. These gradients are so weak that they would be imperceptible in a larger place. Here they govern everything.

The vegetation—or what most resembles vegetation—is lush but lightless.

Forests of soft black fronds rise from the lower rock. They do not photosynthesize. Their tissues are fed by colonies buried in their roots, organisms that draw sustenance from iron, sulfur, and the minute electrical differences between adjacent mineral veins. Each frond is finely divided, more plume than leaf, and absorbs what little chemical wealth passes through the water. They grow packed together until their crowns interlace, filling cracks, hollows, and the spaces beneath overhangs.

Among them stand thicker forms: translucent columns with green-gray interiors, ribbed and hollow, bending only when the chamber gives one of its rare subterranean sighs. Their color is not visible at first. It emerges slowly to an eye that has ceased expecting light. A bruised olive here. A vein of deep red there. Pearly films on the stone. White nodules clustered like buds.

Some of the growths produce their own dimness—not illumination exactly, but local failures of darkness. Around certain leaf tips, blackness thins into a faint blue haze. When disturbed, mats of threadlike organisms release slow rings that remain suspended in the water for hours. These rings reveal nearby surfaces, but incorrectly: a wall may appear farther away than it is, or curved in the opposite direction. A fracture may cast a shadow toward the unseen source of light.

The chamber has no stable shape.

Measured from the entrance, it should be only a few paces across. Its enclosing rock confirms this. A hand placed against one wall can feel vibrations transmitted through the wall opposite. Yet a swimmer passing between the dense fronds may travel for an hour without reaching the far side. Turning back does not shorten the return.

Distance accumulates in the water.

Every narrow gap between leaves contains a little more depth than its dimensions permit. The spaces are not portals and do not lead elsewhere; rather, the chamber seems unable to decide whether water is substance or extent. Where the water is richest in minerals, distances thicken. A finger pushed through a cloud of pale spores may vanish to the wrist before touching an object visibly a finger’s breadth away. Sound behaves similarly. A click of stone can be heard at once, then again several minutes later, then once more from inside the listener’s bones.

For this reason, the chamber is mostly quiet. Noise has too many places to go.

The commonest sound is a soft granular settling as mineral dust descends onto leaves. Occasionally a bubble forms in the warm seep beneath the basalt. It swells for months, silver-black, held under a shelf of stone. When it finally pulls free, it does not rise. It travels sideways through the crowded vegetation, nudging fronds aside with a succession of delicate taps, and disappears into a fold of water that is deeper than the ceiling allows.

Animals are rare. The vegetation has largely ceased to need them.

A few blind, colorless swimmers inhabit the outer reaches. They are small and ribbon-thin, with fins like wet silk and no visible mouths. They drift along the temperature boundaries, feeding perhaps on bacterial dust, perhaps on nothing. Each has a faint line down its body that reflects shapes not present in the chamber: open waves, a bright sky, sometimes a procession of dark vertical forms resembling trees. Whether these are inherited memories, views from another place, or merely patterns made by the observer’s mind is impossible to tell.

Deeper in, there are shells fixed to the stone. They are always found empty. Their spiral mouths face inward, toward the densest part of the chamber. If one is touched, all the others close, though there is no flesh inside them and no visible connection between them. Afterward the black fronds lean in the same direction for several days.

Near the center—if the place can be said to have one—stand scattered structures.

They resemble little walls and roofless rooms, built from flat flakes of basalt fitted edge to edge. None rises higher than a knee. Some enclose a single tuft of pale vegetation; others surround bare water. There are thresholds too small for any known inhabitant, stairs of three or four sharp steps descending into solid rock, and low arches through which only the narrowest swimmer could pass.

No tool marks appear on the stones. No binding substance holds them together. The structures may be natural arrangements produced as mineral shelves crack and settle. Yet their repetition is difficult to dismiss. Each tiny room contains the same feature: a smooth depression in the floor, filled with water darker than the water around it.

The depressions are not holes. Their surfaces can be touched. They yield like the skin of fruit and give off a coolness that penetrates even the surrounding cold. Looking into one reveals the chamber overhead—but not as it is. The stone is older, its sharpness worn away. The vegetation has receded into sparse threads. Fine sediment covers everything, and the miniature walls lie collapsed.

Thus the place contains images of its own future.

Or it may be the future that contains the chamber, holding this young, crowded interval as a dark inclusion.

Nothing visits often enough to decide.

The principal cycle is one of growth and burial. The mineral-fed forests extend by imperceptible degrees, packing themselves into every available crevice. Their oldest lower branches harden, turn brittle, and become coated in stone. New basaltic films seep over them. The living chamber is slowly building the rock that will entomb it. At the same time, hairline fractures open above, admitting fresh cold water and exposing new surfaces. The enclosure closes and renews itself in the same motion.

There are seasons, though no sun marks them. Every several decades the chemistry of the seeping water changes. Iron blooms through the fronds, staining them dark crimson. Then sulfur comes, and soft ivory veils spread from wall to wall. During the rarest season, perhaps once in several centuries, every organism in the chamber becomes transparent. For a few days there seems to be nothing inside but black stone, suspended bubbles, and the countless crowded outlines of things that cannot otherwise be seen.

Those outlines are much larger than the chamber.

They remain perfectly still.

When opacity returns, the forests are unchanged, the little rooms continue to stand, and the empty shells keep their inward vigil. No creature flees. The water settles back into its deep, folded quiet.

At the hidden entrance, pale filaments sway against the open sea. They make the only obvious boundary between the ordinary ocean and the drowned interval beyond. Even there the distinction is uncertain. Sometimes the filaments bend inward, as though the greater ocean were draining into the pocket. Sometimes they stream outward, while no water visibly leaves.

And sometimes, for a moment, they stand upright in the darkness, growing toward a light that has never existed.
