The place occupies a shallow saddle near the crown of a warm, weather-rounded mountain. It is not much larger than a grove, though its edges are difficult to judge. Low rims of stone rise on three sides; on the fourth, the ground narrows into a pale lip beyond which there is only sky and a distant, humid brightness. Clouds pass below as often as above.

The saddle has been shaped by water over an immense interval. Nothing is sharp. The exposed rock—dark greenstone veined with cream and rust—has been worn into basins, channels, blunt shelves, and smooth-backed ridges. Some hollows are no wider than a hand; others are broad enough to lie down in. They hold clear rainwater warmed by the stone. Where the rock overhangs, droplets gather continuously and descend one at a time, even during dry weather. The mountain seems to be condensing the air through its skin.

There is no stream in the ordinary sense. Water is everywhere near the surface, but it moves reluctantly. It darkens seams, fills shallow cups, travels beneath mats of roots, and reappears several paces away as a trembling bead. After rain, the channels join briefly into a silver net. Within an hour the net breaks apart again, leaving isolated pools and black, glistening runnels.

Vegetation grows richly wherever a little sediment has collected, yet these fertile places are scattered widely. The result is both lush and sparse: intense green islands separated by expanses of wet, bare stone.

Each island has its own character. One may be a dense cushion of copper-tipped moss, another a stand of waist-high fronds with blue undersides, another a single broad plant whose leaves lie flat over the ground like overlapping wet hands. Fine grasses emerge from cracks in disciplined lines, following mineral veins. In deeper pockets grow pale, soft-stemmed shrubs, seldom more than shoulder height. Their bark peels in translucent ribbons. Their leaves are few but enormous, dark above and silver beneath, turning slowly as moisture gathers on one surface and evaporates from the other.

There are no animals. No flies hover over the pools, no worms turn the black soil, no birds cross the exposed sky. Even the water contains no visible swimmers. Flowers, where they occur, have no need to advertise themselves: they are small, greenish, and nearly scentless. Pollen is released in damp clumps that creep along leaf edges through capillary action. Seeds travel by rain. Some are shaped like flat chips of stone and slide down the polished channels; others swell when soaked and push themselves, by degrees, into cracks.

Decay is equally quiet. With no mouths to eat and few organisms resembling ordinary fungi, fallen leaves do not vanish quickly. They soften into thin brown skins, layer upon layer, until the pressure and warmth beneath living roots turn them into a dark, fibrous earth. A leaf may remain recognizable for years. The saddle keeps a slow archive of its own growth.

The strangeness becomes clearer with attention. Many plants meet the stone as if working it.

Certain root tips harden around grains of quartz and carry them forward. Driven by daily swelling and nightly contraction, they score repeated lines into softer rock. Other roots enter existing fissures and wedge them open over decades. The resulting flakes are remarkably regular: thin, sharp-edged, palm-sized. No hand has struck them, and nothing here possesses hands, yet they resemble the earliest tools. They lie where they have fallen, quickly filmed with moisture, among strands of root fiber strong enough to twist into cord.

Elsewhere, reedlike stalks split naturally into equal lengths when dried. Broad leaves cure into waterproof sheets. Hollow stems retain embers for hours after lightning, though storms rarely ignite anything in this wet place. The landscape appears full of primitive technologies without a maker: edges, fibers, vessels, pigments, binding saps. None has been gathered or assembled. There are no paths, shelters, hearths, carvings, or discarded objects. The saddle is untouched, but it is not materially innocent. It seems to have arrived, by blind growth, at the threshold of craft.

Light is ordinary in strength but softened by vapor. At midday the pools show a hard white sky while the plants cast blurred shadows. When a cloud climbs over the rim, visibility contracts to a few steps. Droplets form on every surface, outlining roots and leaf veins, and the open saddle becomes a small room without walls. When the cloud passes, the distance returns suddenly. The cliff lip shines, and beyond it the lower world appears green and indistinct through layers of warm haze.

The air smells of wet mineral, bruised fern, and something faintly metallic. Heat rises from the rock after noon, but the moisture prevents the place from becoming harsh. Skin would remain damp here. Breath would feel thick. At night the warmth stored in the mountain seeps upward, so that mist continues to form even beneath clear stars.

Sound is minimal. There are no calls, wingbeats, or rustlings of hidden life. Wind usually passes overhead. Close to the ground one hears only droplets: an isolated tap into a basin, the nearly inaudible thread of water under moss, the soft release of a leaf peeling away from stone. Occasionally a saturated pocket of soil settles by a fraction, producing a low sigh. During rain the whole saddle whispers, but never roars; its channels are too shallow, its distances too short.

Change occurs through accumulation rather than event. Pools deepen by the width of a grain. Rims lower. Root-cut grooves become channels; channels become seams of soil; seams host new green islands. A shrub may spend a century leaning across open rock before its farthest branch touches another patch of earth and takes root there. The scattered vegetation is slowly joining itself, though erosion opens empty spaces at nearly the same rate. The landscape has maintained this balance for so long that neither stone nor plant seems to be winning.

Its boundaries also drift. In clear weather the saddle is visibly finite: rim, lip, cliff, sky. In mist, the polished channels appear to continue beyond the known edges. Water sometimes arrives along a groove that should slope outward. Seeds placed downhill are found days later in higher basins. Nothing moves quickly enough to witness. The only evidence is the quiet rearrangement of small things.

Here, direction seems to belong less to gravity than to wetness. Water goes where the mountain remembers water going. Roots follow old channels even when those channels rise. Loose flakes turn edgewise overnight, aligning themselves with cream-colored veins in the bedrock.

The place offers no threat in motion, no stalking life, no sudden weather beyond warm rain. Its unease comes from patience. Everything has had time to settle into an arrangement that is stable but not entirely natural—or natural according to rules too old and local to be recognized elsewhere.

At the center lies the deepest basin, oval and perfectly still. It is surrounded by ample bare stone, as though the vegetation avoids it. The water is only knee-deep, clear to the bottom, and warmed from beneath. On the basin floor rest hundreds of thin greenstone flakes, all pointing toward a narrow crack.

Droplets fall into the pool, but rings do not spread outward. They contract toward the point of impact and disappear.

That is the loudest thing the saddle does.
