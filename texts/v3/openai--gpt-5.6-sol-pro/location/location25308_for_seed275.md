### The Underleaf

The city lies below the world’s mineral surface, though “below” is not quite the same as buried. Above it is a ceiling of interlocked roots, compressed stems, and dormant seedstone several kilometers thick. Below it is older growth: dark structural tissue descending beyond the surveyed depths. There is no exposed soil. There is no bedrock in the ordinary sense. Everything that bears weight was once alive, remains alive, or is slowly preparing to become alive again.

The open spaces are vast.

They occur where the subterranean organism has withdrawn its flesh from its own interior, leaving long chambers between load-bearing ribs. Some chambers are narrow as streets; others open into cool, level expanses large enough to contain districts, factories, grazing grounds, and horizons of pale columns. Because the supporting growth is widely spaced, the place feels sparse despite being made entirely of vegetation. A single trunk may be eighty meters across, yet stand half a kilometer from the next. Between them stretches empty floor: hard, matte, and faintly resilient, like cork pressed under enormous weight.

The city is built into these intervals.

Its towers are cultivated rather than raised. Architects score geometric wounds into the young columns, insert brass forms, and allow the plant to heal around them. When the forms are removed, rooms remain: clean-sided chambers with fibrous walls, ventilation pores, and veins that can be tapped for resin or electrical current. Older buildings have softened at the corners as the tissue slowly closes its wounds. Doorways narrow over decades. Windows develop calluses. Staircases sometimes bud.

The youngest districts look raw. Their streets are only shallow grooves in the floor, marked by guide-lights and rows of black germination pegs. Foundations twitch beneath translucent membranes. Walls rise as pale folds, still wet-looking though no water is present. The Underleaf has no rain, rivers, groundwater, or humidity in any familiar sense. Its biology is conducted through oils, liquefied waxes, volatile ethers, and slow-moving ionic sap. Cut tissue does not bleed; it beads with amber solvent that smokes gently in the cool air.

This sap is the city’s chief resource and greatest hazard.

Refineries stand far apart in the open chambers, each enclosed by rings of dead, fire-resistant bark. They distill plant oils into fuel, lubricants, plastics, medicines, and nutrient fog. Their machinery is heavy and deliberate: flywheels with rootwood spokes, ceramic pistons, copper ducts, and black pressure vessels sunk into living floors. Electrical stations clamp induction collars around the larger vascular trunks. The trunks carry faint pulses from distant metabolic organs, and the collars harvest a fraction of each pulse, making the surrounding lamps brighten and dim in rhythms too slow to notice at first.

There is ordinary light here, or something close enough that generations have ceased to distinguish it. Broad luminous fronds grow from the ceiling in sparse clusters, holding themselves flat like white leaves pressed against glass. They cast a clear, cool illumination with gentle shadows and recognizable colors. At scheduled intervals, the fronds darken from their tips inward. District lamps then take over, glowing warm gold along roads and loading platforms. The alternation is called day and night, although it follows the metabolism of the ceiling rather than any sun.

The temperature remains cool. Breath hangs briefly near the deeper transit cuts. Metal railings are cold to the touch, and workers wear layered coats of woven bast fiber. Heat is expensive because it accelerates evaporation and can wake dormant growth. Factories recover almost all waste warmth, routing it into broodhouses, fermentation chambers, and the slow germination vaults beneath the municipal gardens.

The word *garden* means something peculiar here. There are no planted beds distinct from the landscape. Instead, gardeners persuade portions of the city’s fabric to express different forms. A park may be a broad patch of soft green cilia suitable for sitting, interrupted by sparse towers of fruiting fungus-like flowers. An orchard is a ceiling persuaded to lower hundreds of pendant bladders, each filling with edible oil and starch. A civic square might bloom once a year, covering its own paving in cool silver petals until sweepers arrive to harvest them for insulation.

Unmanaged vegetation is almost unknown. Even emptiness is plant emptiness: a pause in growth, a deliberately unexpressed possibility.

Fauna moves everywhere, but little of it is loud. Pale, six-legged browsers cross the open floors in loose files, trimming films of fresh tissue with flat mineral teeth. They resemble deer only from a distance. Their bodies are low and jointed; their ears are fans of vibration-sensitive cartilage folded against the skull. Tiny courier moths travel between luminous fronds, carrying pollen packets and coded tags attached by factory clerks. Glass-backed burrowers live within the walls, visible as moving shadows under thin bark. They are tolerated because they open ventilation channels.

Predators hunt by pressure rather than sound. Long-bodied hushcats lie along the upper ribs, matching the color and texture of dead wood. When herds pass below, the predators loosen their grip and fall without a cry. Even the prey do not call out. Their alarm is a chemical flash: a sharp scent like cut citrus and hot copper, spreading through the district seconds before they scatter.

The quiet is not absence. It is restraint.

Machines are mounted on pads of dense pith. Wheels run on soft resin rims. Public signals are conveyed by light, scent, and pulses through the floor. A person can stand in a busy freight quarter and hear only the tick of cooling pipes, the faint brush of insect wings, and, at long intervals, a deep wooden concussion traveling through the city’s supports.

Those concussions announce growth.

The landscape is still forming. It has not yet acquired the scars that would make it old terrain. There are few gullies, broken cliffs, or fields of debris. Surfaces are fresh, rounded, and undecided. Yet forces resembling erosion have begun to appear. Herds wear shallow paths into the living floor. Industrial vapors bleach the walls around refineries. The evaporation of volatile sap leaves delicate saltlike crusts that crack underfoot. In transit tunnels, repeated vibration separates fibers into long, feathery ravines.

The city studies these marks with fascination. Erosion is new here—perhaps because the entire cavern system is new. Some believe the Underleaf is a recently formed organ, still expanding into the deep. Others claim the enclosing organism has become ill and is hollowing itself out from within.

Evidence supports both.

At the regional margins, survey trains run along elevated root rails into unfinished country. Beyond the last inhabited chambers lie spaces whose floors have not hardened. Pale sheets slowly peel apart, opening fresh voids over months or years. New columns descend from the ceiling but sometimes stop halfway, blind and trembling. Elsewhere the floor rises in ridges, meeting descending growth and sealing off entire valleys. Towns near these active fronts are designed to migrate. Their workshops stand on sledges; their dwellings grow detachable root-hooks; their street plans include routes of retreat.

There is no wind, but there is weather.

Pressure changes move through the caverns as the great organism breathes solvents from one region to another. During an amber front, the air becomes fragrant and optically thick, and distant structures appear honey-colored. Static gathers on fur and cloth. During a dry pulse, volatile compounds are withdrawn so quickly that bark contracts with a soft, citywide whisper. Lamps flare blue. Small animals retreat into cracks, while harvesting crews hurry out to collect the mineral blooms left behind.

Open flames are forbidden everywhere. Water is also forbidden, though most inhabitants know it only as a dangerous laboratory reagent. Even a small amount can rupture local tissues, disrupt solvent membranes, and provoke explosive bacterial growth. Old warning signs depict it as a blue droplet enclosed in a red circle. Children are taught that water is the element of rot.

The city’s deepest rule is simpler: never cut a vein you cannot see end.

Every wall may be part of a larger circulation. Every plaza may cover a sleeping bud. A new rail tunnel once severed a minor conduit and caused a district twenty kilometers away to fruit uncontrollably, burying streets under hard black seeds. Since then, engineers map not only structures but intentions—where the growth appears to be going, what shape it may be attempting, and whether a building is truly a building or merely scaffolding for an organ not yet born.

From the high tramways, the Underleaf appears both metropolitan and unfinished. Pools of white light hang in the distance. Factory stacks rise between colossal stems, releasing carefully cooled vapor into filtration canopies. Herds move as pale commas over dark floors. Freight engines slide silently along resin rails. Entire neighborhoods glow from within living walls, while beyond them stretches chamber after chamber of unoccupied vegetal plain.

Nothing here is natural in the sense of being untouched. Nothing is artificial in the sense of being separate from life.

The city is an industry, an ecosystem, and perhaps a temporary pattern inside a plant too large to recognize it. It hums without noise, grows without water, and spreads through the cool underground not by conquering empty terrain, but by persuading the living dark to make room.
