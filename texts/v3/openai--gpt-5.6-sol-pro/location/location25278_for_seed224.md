The place is called, where it has been noticed at all, the **High Recess**.

It lies near the summit of a black mountain, inside a seam no wider than two fingers. From the outside the seam resembles an ordinary fracture in volcanic glass: a narrow darkness lined with pale dust. Instruments lowered into it report contradictory depths. A probe may descend three centimeters and transmit from twelve kilometers above its point of entry. Another may touch bottom immediately, though its cable continues unwinding into the crack for several hours.

There is no practical route in. Entry is less a matter of fitting through the seam than of approaching it at the correct angle to gravity.

Within, the Recess opens into a shallow basin perhaps the size of a room. This estimate changes with position. Seen from its rim, the opposite wall is close enough to touch; walking toward it requires hundreds of measured steps. Looking back reduces the journey to a single stride.

The basin is high in every available sense. It occupies the mountain’s uppermost rock, but it also sits above the surrounding world in pressure, in gravitational potential, and—according to the oldest functioning instruments—in probability. Loose objects tend to fall out of neighboring possibilities and come to rest here. They are seldom recognizable.

The ground is composed of dark, fine-grained material with the dry looseness of ash. It contains no water, not even as trapped crystal hydrate. Under magnification each grain appears to be a chipped-off corner: tiny angular fragments whose geometry suggests they once belonged to objects with more dimensions than the basin permits. Most are dull gray. A few hold a weak violet sheen, not reflected light but the afterimage of light that passed nearby long ago.

Nothing grows. Nothing grazes, burrows, molts, spores, sleeps, or decays. There are no preserved traces of such things. The place is not dead in the sense of having suffered death. It seems to have been designed—or to have formed—without ever admitting biology as a relevant category.

The cold is deep and steady. It does not bite so much as remove. Heat drains from exposed surfaces without any obvious medium carrying it away. Metal becomes brittle; glass becomes strangely flexible. The basin’s thin atmosphere, where it exists, is mostly argon, neon, and heavier unidentified gases that settle into layers a few centimeters thick. These gases do not make wind. They creep along the ground in transparent sheets, pouring around stones with the patient behavior of liquid while remaining too rarefied to feel.

Above the basin is not a sky but a dark concavity. It may be the inner surface of the mountain fracture, though no light reaches it and no ranging beam returns consistently. At intervals, dim points appear overhead. They look like stars until they move in straight lines, stop, and divide into smaller versions of themselves. Their light is insufficient to cast ordinary shadows. Instead, objects acquire faint duplicates displaced to one side, as if each thing’s shadow were an alternate location it had declined to occupy.

The terrain has been weathered for an immense time. Ridges are rounded, hollows filled, sharp edges reduced to patient curves. Yet there has never been rain or flowing water. Erosion here is performed by minute failures of position. Every few years, a grain of the surface becomes slightly uncertain about where it belongs. It flickers across several adjacent points, abrades them all, and settles. Over geological spans this produces smooth channels, scalloped slopes, and shallow bowls resembling the work of vanished streams. They are not drainage courses. They are routes along which location itself has gradually worn thin.

Across the basin stand seven structures, though counting them twice rarely gives the same result.

They are sparse, low, and widely separated: black uprights, each no taller than a seated person, sunk into the ash without foundation seams. Their surfaces are composed of something between ceramic and absence. Dust does not cling to them. Light approaching one pylon bends slightly aside, leaving a narrow dimness behind it that can persist for minutes after the source is removed.

No pylon has an opening or visible mechanism. Near the base of each is a shallow pattern of parallel grooves. The grooves are too fine to be cut marks and too regular to be weathering. They rearrange when unobserved, but slowly. A complete change may require centuries. At present, several of the pylons display patterns that correspond—imperfectly—to the basin’s contours. One seems to map the positions of the others. Another describes a basin with nine pylons. The seventh bears only a single groove running around it without meeting itself.

Between the structures lie occasional artifacts: a ring thin as foil and too large to lift despite having almost no mass; a transparent block containing a darkness that rotates once every forty-three years; three identical rods, each provably older than the other two. These objects do not appear damaged. Their makers, if they had makers, expected erosion of matter and seem to have engineered instead against erosion of causality.

There are signs of one larger construction beneath the ground. In places the ash has settled into long rectangular depressions. When the surface is scanned, the buried form appears as a lattice extending downward, though “downward” changes from one scan to the next. It may be a machine, a foundation, or an anchor holding the Recess against the mountain. It emits no heat. Once in several decades, all seven pylons lean toward it by less than a hair’s breadth and remain leaning for a year.

This is the basin’s most dramatic event.

Sound is nearly absent. The thin gases cannot carry much, and the terrain absorbs vibration as though storing it. A footstep produces a soft granular compression but no echo. Struck stone gives a click that seems to occur far away. Very rarely, one of the pylons releases a single low tone. The tone is not loud, but every loose grain in the basin turns one face toward its source. Afterward there is a silence with texture—a sense that something has finished listening.

Time does pass here, but not evenly. Mechanical clocks lose agreement while continuing to function perfectly. Radioactive samples decay at different rates depending on which pylon they face. Movement toward the center takes slightly longer than movement away from it, although travelers return with less elapsed time on their bodies than on their instruments. No one has remained long enough to discover whether these discrepancies accumulate or cancel.

The Recess has several reliable rules:

- Nothing wet can enter. Water freezes before the seam, then separates into hydrogen, oxygen, and a residue that cannot be identified. The gases may pass inward; the residue remains outside as white dust.
- No flame will sustain itself, even with supplied fuel and oxidizer. Combustion begins, hesitates, and reverts to its unburned state.
- A straight line drawn in the ash eventually closes into a loop, though it never visibly curves.
- Objects left unattended become more symmetrical.
- Any attempt to remove a pylon results in the removal of the tool instead. The missing tools have not been found elsewhere.
- The basin cannot contain an exact copy of anything already present. Replicated objects acquire small differences: an extra edge, reversed chirality, a memory of manufacture that predates the original.
- Living tissue does not die immediately, but its processes lose coordination. For this reason entry, where possible, is made only by remote devices. Even microbes become chemically inactive at the threshold. None have adapted.

There is therefore no ecology in the biological sense. Yet the place has relationships resembling one. The ash migrates toward the artifacts. The artifacts redistribute the weak light. The pylons regulate some deeper asymmetry. The gas layers thicken around geometric irregularities and slowly polish them away. Every component alters the conditions under which the others can remain real.

Over long periods the entire basin seems to breathe—not in volume, but in accessibility. For centuries the mountain seam may be merely a crack. Then, gradually, its interior acquires depth. Signals begin to pass through. The violet grains brighten. The seven pylons become countable again.

At the crest of this cycle, the central ash flattens into a flawless plane. For several minutes it reflects a landscape that is not the mountain outside: a vast cold expanse filled with similar recesses, each embedded in a different world, all positioned at impossible heights. Thousands of black pylons stand across that greater terrain, so widely spaced that their arrangement can only be perceived from a distance no observer could survive.

Then the surface roughens. The reflection closes. Depth drains from the seam.

The High Recess returns to its hidden scale: a pinch of gray dust in a fracture near the summit, cold beyond weather, silent beyond abandonment, maintaining a machine whose purpose may not be to do anything at all, but simply to ensure that this particular pocket of impossibility continues to have happened.
