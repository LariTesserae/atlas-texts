The place occupies a shallow basin near the crown of an old mountain. The surrounding peaks have long since been worn down. They are not sharp enough to deserve the name peaks anymore, only broad gray backs rising above the cloud layer, their stone reduced to low ribs and whale-smooth pavements. Nothing here looks recently broken. Every surface has passed through fracture into rounding, through rounding into polish, and through polish into a final dull grain that seems incapable of further aging.

In the middle of this nearly level height stands a small city.

It was never built with tools.

From a distance it resembles a cluster of roofless towers, courtyards, narrow streets, and covered passages, all contained within a ring scarcely wider than a hamlet. Up close, the walls prove to be the calcified remains of an ancient colonial organism. Each building is a bundle of vertical chambers grown around a shared hollow. Doorways are places where membranes once opened; stair-like shelves mark seasons of arrested growth. The city’s pale material lies somewhere between limestone, antler, and dead wood. It has grain, pores, and branching seams. In places it is thin enough for daylight to glow through.

The builders were not individuals. No one laid out the streets. The colony grew according to pressures passing through the basin: prevailing wind, frost, the weight of snow, the position of summer light. Yet the result is unmistakably urban. Alleys converge upon plazas. Arched galleries join one hollow tower to another. Channels run beneath the paving. Rows of small chambers face sheltered courts. There are districts distinguished by wall thickness and aperture, and a central structure larger than the rest, a many-lobed cathedral whose inner vault is open to the sky.

Whatever inhabited the colony is gone. There are no furnishings, inscriptions, hearths, machines, or marks of hands. The architecture is the artifact of a life process, and the absence inside it is profound.

The basin itself is mostly empty. Its open ground is a pavement of ancient dark rock, interrupted by the city, a few boulders, and low islands of vegetation. Distances are not great, but because so little occupies them, each object stands apart with unusual force. A solitary stone twenty paces from the walls can seem exiled. A patch of red moss becomes a district of its own. The open spaces between presences feel larger than their measurements.

The air is cold in every season. Even beneath a clear midday sky, warmth does not accumulate. The high ground sheds it too quickly. Frost survives in the shadows of the western walls, and thin ice skins the deeper channels after sunset. Snow is infrequent but persistent when it comes, collecting in the hollow city and remaining there in blue-white folds long after the outer basin has cleared.

There is little standing water. Moisture arrives indirectly.

Clouds pass low over the mountain and drag their undersides across the towers. The porous walls drink mist. Condensation gathers inside thousands of hair-fine tubes, beads along the edges of old chambers, and descends through the architecture in slow, hidden threads. On clear mornings, droplets appear from apparently dry stone. They fall into basins no larger than bowls and into long troughs grown beneath the streets. For several hours the city trickles everywhere.

After rain, the change is dramatic. Water enters the upper mouths of the towers and rushes through the hollow walls. The buried channels fill at once. Courtyards become shallow pools; alleys carry fast silver streams; water erupts from low arches and runs across the empty pavement before vanishing into cracks at the basin’s edge. By the next day most of it is gone. Only dark stains, hanging drops, and pockets of black water remain.

Vegetation is concentrated around these brief wet routes. The open basin supports little beyond crustose lichens and wiry silver grass, but within the city the growth is unexpectedly rich. Moss upholsters north-facing chambers in deep green. Liverworts spread like small translucent scales along the drains. Matted alpine herbs occupy the courtyards, their leaves dark and thick, pressed close to the ground. In protected galleries, pale ferns unfurl during the wet weeks and fold almost completely during dry cold.

Some towers contain vertical gardens rooted nowhere visible. Filaments descend from cracks high above, braided into curtains by the wind. They are neither true roots nor stems, but the searching bodies of a communal plant that stores water in knots along its length. After mist, these knots swell and become clear as berries. By afternoon they contract, releasing droplets to the moss below.

The city is loud.

Wind crosses the smooth mountain without resistance and enters the old colony through hundreds of apertures. Each chamber answers differently. Narrow pores whistle at pitches too high to locate. Larger shafts produce low, organ-like notes that can be felt through the soles of the feet. Covered streets amplify the rush into continuous surf. The central structure gives out a deep, irregular booming whenever gusts strike its eastern face.

The sounds are not stable. Frost closes some openings and tunes others. Moss muffles a passage for half the year. Rain floods the lower chambers and forces the wind upward, changing the whole city’s voice within minutes. During a storm the towers seem to call across their plazas: groans below, shrieks along the roofs, sudden percussive cracks as trapped water freezes in the walls. When thaw follows, the ice loosens with a fusillade of sharp reports.

Even in calm weather there is dripping, the creak of saturated vegetal curtains, and the faint granular hiss of old mineral surfaces shedding particles too small to see. Silence occurs only beneath deep snow. Then the city’s many mouths are sealed, and the absence of its voice feels unnatural.

Animals are rare. A few black mountain birds visit after rain to drink from the courtyards. They do not nest in the towers, though the cavities would seem ideal. They remain watchful, never entering farther than daylight reaches, and depart before the wind strengthens.

Tiny pale grazers live among the mosses. They have no obvious eyes and move only when droplets strike nearby, converging upon the impact as if water itself were a summons. In dry weather they withdraw into the pores of the walls and may not emerge for months. Their chief predator is a flat, slow creature like a six-legged leaf, seen so seldom that finding one is treated by the birds as an emergency: they gather above it, crying, but refuse to descend.

The strangest life is inside the stone.

On certain cold mornings, especially after several days of mist, the walls pulse. The motion is slight—an expansion, a pause, a release—and can be mistaken for changes in light. Water rises within some of the capillaries instead of falling. It climbs toward the upper chambers, carrying a faint milkiness with it. The city has been dead for an age, yet portions of its old circulation continue whenever temperature and moisture align.

This does not restore the vanished organism. Nothing grows larger. No new tower appears. The process seems instead to preserve the final form. Dissolved mineral is moved from exposed lower walls to weathered upper rims. Cracks are slowly lined from within. Fragile arches thicken by fractions across centuries. Erosion continues everywhere in the basin, but the city redistributes its own dissolution, surrendering one surface to maintain another.

Thus its age has not made it ruinous in the usual sense. It has settled into an equilibrium older than memory. Fallen material is carried underground, dissolved, raised, and deposited again. The city is always wearing away and always returning itself to approximately the same shape.

Its streets do not lead beyond it. At every outer passage the grown walls taper down and merge with the basin floor, as though the settlement had emerged from the mountain and never imagined another place. Around it extends the smooth, cold emptiness of the summit. Beyond the basin’s low rim, the land falls into cloud.

In ordinary daylight the scene is clear and unsentimental: gray stone, white walls, green interiors, blue shade. There is no perpetual twilight to soften it, no glow except the sun passing through thin mineral membranes. The strangeness remains fully visible.

At noon, clouds may tear away and expose every courtyard at once. Water flashes in the channels. Mosses open. The hanging filaments tighten in the dry air. Birds wheel once over the central vault. Wind enters from the east, and the whole little city begins to sound—not as one voice, but as hundreds of empty rooms responding to weather.

By evening, frost advances from the shadows. Drains stiffen. Notes vanish one by one as apertures close with ice. The last sound is usually the deepest shaft beneath the central court, breathing at long intervals into the dark.
