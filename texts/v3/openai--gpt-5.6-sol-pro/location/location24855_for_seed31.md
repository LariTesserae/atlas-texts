The city occupies a high, broad saddle between two unfinished mountain ridges. From a distance it is difficult to distinguish settlement from geology. Its walls are made of the same dark stone that breaks through the soil in angular shelves, and its streets follow the shallow joints of the plateau as if the builders had widened existing cracks rather than imposed a plan.

The land is young. Cliffs retain sharp crowns and clean facets; boulders lie where they first cooled or split, their corners only lightly softened by moss. There are few deep gullies. Water has not had long enough—or enough violence—to carve them. Instead it remains near the surface, spreading horizontally through porous stone and emerging everywhere in restrained forms: a clear seam beneath a wall, beads on the underside of a lintel, a dark gloss along a stair. Low places hold shallow marshes no deeper than an ankle, crowded with sedges and pale floating leaves. The water is warm from its passage through the mountain, though not hot enough to steam.

Nothing rushes. Channels cut beside the streets carry water at such a slight grade that their surfaces appear motionless. Only the faintest distortion around a stone shows that they flow. Basins are always full to the same mineral line. Overflow slips through notches and descends from terrace to terrace in transparent sheets too thin to make more than a soft, continuous whisper.

The plateau extends well beyond the city. Open ground lies between clusters of construction: wet meadows, exposed black ledges, planted groves, and large courts in which only rain-dark paving remains. Farther out, the saddle widens into a sequence of green shelves. Beyond them stand the two ridges, steep but not immense, their flanks striped by springs. On clear days their fresh scarps are visible in detail—rust-red fractures, charcoal columns, and isolated fans of pale rubble that have not yet settled into soil.

The air is warm for such height. It has the mild, enveloping weight of a valley after rain, but the horizon remains open and the clouds do not press low. Moisture gathers on stone throughout the day. Walls facing the upland breeze are silvered with condensation; sheltered walls remain nearly black. Sunlight is ordinary and diffuse, bright enough to reveal color without bleaching it. There is no dramatic mist, no perpetual storm. The atmosphere simply carries as much water as it can without obscuring the world.

Vegetation grows richly where water and soil coincide, but those places are separated by broad expanses of stone. Ferns fill the joints of retaining walls. Mosses form deep green pads around cistern mouths. In the meadows, reedlike plants rise in orderly bands according to the depth of the water, giving way uphill to low shrubs with thick, glossy leaves. Small trees have been planted around reservoirs and along the edges of public courts, but they stand far apart, each with an open circle of wet ground beneath it. Their roots spread shallowly over the bedrock.

There are no animals. No insects cross the water. No birds stand on the parapets. The marshes hold neither fish nor frogs, and the damp soil contains no tracks, burrows, shells, castings, or gnawed stems. Flowers are uncommon. Most plants spread by runners, fragments, spores, or cones shaken open by changes in humidity. Fallen leaves decay slowly under films of fungus and soft bacterial stain. The ecology is complete but narrow: water, mineral, plant, mold, and light, with none of the quick disturbances that animal life would bring.

This absence gives the city its quiet more than its emptiness does.

The settlement is large enough to have districts. A long central way crosses the saddle, paved with fitted slabs and edged by covered drains. On either side, roofless courts open into workshops, storehouses, kitchens, sleeping compounds, and communal halls. The buildings rarely rise above two levels. Their foundations are stone; upper walls are packed earth strengthened with woven reed; roofs are made from timber poles, mats, clay, and broad overlapping slabs where the supports can bear them.

Construction is careful but technologically simple. Stone has been split along natural planes with hammerstones, wooden wedges, fire, and water. Some door fittings and cutting edges are made of soft hammered copper, green with damp, but most joints depend on pegs, fiber lashings, clay sockets, and gravity. Aqueducts are not raised on monumental arches. They are shallow contour channels, patiently traced around the slopes. Where they cross a cleft, they pass through hollowed logs or stone troughs balanced on stacked piers.

The city’s greatest structures are its reservoirs. They occupy natural depressions lined with clay and flat slabs. Broad steps descend into them, though the water level does not vary enough to expose more than the highest few. Some are open to the sky. Others lie under low columned roofs, cool chambers where condensation falls from beams into the same water from which it rose. The columns are massive, not from grandeur but because the builders had no means to span wide spaces lightly.

Every district has a wash court, a milling floor, and a garden terrace. Yet the buildings are dispersed, divided by wet ground and raw rock. There is no dense core of narrow alleys. Even at the center, one can look through gateways and across empty courts to the distant ridges. The city feels planned for a population larger than the landscape ever held—or perhaps for people who valued distance, water access, and firebreaks more than proximity.

No inhabitants remain. Their departure does not appear violent. There are no burned doors, toppled barricades, or scattered weapons. Stone bowls sit in wall niches. Grinding slabs remain beside bins lined with dry grass. In one workshop, blocks are arranged in order of size beside unfinished paving. The last marks are practical ones: a repaired gutter, a stack of reeds under cover, a copper blade sharpened down to a narrow crescent.

Nor has the place become a ruin in the usual sense. With no animals to undermine walls, no tree roots thick enough to prise apart foundations, and little seasonal variation, deterioration proceeds almost imperceptibly. Earthen plaster has rounded at exposed corners. Roof mats have darkened. A few unsupported beams have sagged into gentle arcs, but most structures stand as they were left.

The climate has no pronounced seasons. Water enters the plateau from frequent light rain and from condensation along the ridges; it leaves through slow springs on the lower slopes. The two quantities remain nearly equal. Reservoirs neither flood nor empty. Marsh boundaries stay fixed. Plants grow and shed matter at the same modest rate that dampness returns it to soil. Even the temperature changes little between day and night because the saturated stone holds warmth.

From the highest western terrace, the whole system can be understood at once. Dark roofs and green courts lie among open shelves of rock. Thin channels catch the light and vanish. Reservoirs reflect portions of sky. Beyond the last wall, cultivated terraces pass gradually into natural marsh and then into naked upland stone. Nothing marks a firm boundary between city and watershed. The city is not placed beside the water; it is an articulation of the water’s path.

Its few sounds never gather into noise. There is the near-silent movement of seepage through a drain, an occasional drop from a lintel, the faint settling of saturated earth against stone. Reeds do not chatter without wind. Leaves do not stir unless the upland air passes over them, and even then they produce only a low brushing sound that fades across the open courts.

Most of the time, the city seems to be listening to its own water.

It has been listening for a very long time, and nothing in the landscape suggests that tomorrow will differ from today.
