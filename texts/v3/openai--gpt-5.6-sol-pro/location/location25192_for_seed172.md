The place is called the Upper Throat, though it is neither a summit nor a passage.

It occupies a cavity within the crown of an industrial tower, nearly three kilometers above the ground that supports it. From outside, the cavity is invisible: a sealed interval between the tower’s outer armor and the immense stationary machinery at its center. There are no windows. There is no view of the country below, only the knowledge of altitude expressed through structural pressure, the thinness of the manufactured air, and the deep cold that enters through thirty meters of alloy as if the metal were no barrier at all.

The Upper Throat is small enough to cross in twelve minutes. It feels larger because almost nothing stands close to anything else.

Its floor is a plane of pale ceramic panels, each one hexagonal and perfectly flat. The joints between them are black and hair-thin. In several places the panels have been thrust upward into crisp ridges, not by geology but by design: artificial escarpments, waist-high fault lines, and fields of sharp white splinters arranged to imitate recent tectonic upheaval. None has weathered. Their edges remain keen enough to cut fabric. There is no dust to soften them, no rain to stain them, no foot traffic except that of rare maintenance machines.

Everything here was fabricated. The cliffs are cast refractory. The scree is numbered. Each loose-looking shard is magnetically pinned in place.

Above, a low vault follows the curvature of the tower’s shell. It is ribbed with conduits, insulated pipes, cable trunks, and square ventilation ducts. Frost does not form: the chamber contains almost no moisture. Cold instead reveals itself through stiffness. Sealants turn glassy. Metal handrails pull heat from the skin almost instantly. Breath, where breath is permitted, leaves only the faintest powder of crystals before the reclamation vents take it.

The light is ordinary to the point of uncanniness. Rows of broad-spectrum lamps produce the clean, balanced illumination of a temperate afternoon. There are no dramatic shadows, no red emergency glow, no flicker. Colors appear exactly as they should. White is white. Rust is rust. The sparse plants are a tired green.

They grow from narrow troughs sunk into the ceramic floor at irregular intervals. “Grow” is not quite the right word. They persist.

The plants were engineered for an anhydrous substrate: low fans of waxy blades, black-veined cushions, and branching mineral stems whose tissues carry ammonia salts and liquid hydrocarbons in place of water. Their roots enter porous blocks of carbon foam connected to sealed nutrient cartridges. Each colony is isolated from the next by twenty or thirty meters of bare construction. There is no moss between panels, no opportunistic seedling, no mold hiding in the insulation. Nothing spreads. Nothing dies. Sensors hold every colony at the same arrested stage, replacing cells molecule by molecule so that no visible growth occurs.

A blade bent under a gloved hand will slowly return to precisely its former angle.

There are no animals. No insects pollinate the small, dull flowers. No mites live in the troughs. No bacteria digest fallen tissue; there is no fallen tissue to digest. Pollination, where it is still performed, is done by a narrow brass nozzle that descends once every cycle, although the flowers no longer produce viable seed. The ritual continues because it was included in the original operating schedule.

The sound is overwhelming.

Behind the inner wall, engines turn at constant load. They may be turbines, compressors, gyroscopic stabilizers, or some larger apparatus for which those words are only analogies. Their combined voice fills the chamber without rhythm or variation: a total industrial roar, dense enough to seem solid. It passes through boots and bones. It shivers along the ceramic plains and makes the artificial scree hum against its hidden magnets. Speech is useless. Even shouted words vanish at the lips. Loose straps stream and tremble, although the air itself appears still.

The noise never rises or falls.

Standing there for long enough, one begins to hear structures inside it: an enormous low note under the feet, a metallic band in the teeth, a white cataract in the skull. These seem like changes, but instruments show none. The waveform recorded today is identical to the waveform recorded at commissioning. Every vibration repeats with exact fidelity. If two samples are overlaid, decades apart, they cancel into silence.

That is the principal rule of the Upper Throat: variation is corrected.

Temperature is held at forty degrees below freezing. Lamps maintain one unwavering spectral profile. Atmospheric pressure does not breathe with the tower. The engines do not accelerate. Ceramic fractures do not lengthen. The engineered plants neither colonize nor retreat. A chip knocked from a ridge is found back in position after the next maintenance interval, its broken edge matching so perfectly that the act seems to have been reversed rather than repaired.

There are machines in the landscape, but they are sparse and far apart. A pump housing rises from the floor like a black boulder. A ladder ascends to a hatch that cannot be opened. Three upright radiators stand in a row, their fins silver and immaculate. Near the center is a shallow basin with sloping sides, made in the form of a dry reservoir. It has never contained water. At its bottom lies a circle of blue glass beneath which indicator lights spell out a process status in an obsolete industrial code.

The message is always the same:

**FORMATION IN PROGRESS.**

This may explain the landscape’s sharpness. The place is not unfinished in the usual sense; it is maintained at the instant of becoming. The ridges have just risen. The ceramic crust has just cracked. The mineral plants have just been introduced. The great concealed engines have just reached operational speed. That “just” has lasted for centuries.

At the far end of the chamber, the floor narrows between two artificial cliffs. Here the roar becomes physically difficult to endure. The cliffs are dark nickel alloy, faceted like freshly broken stone and cold enough to numb a gloved hand. Between them stands a simple yellow door.

Beyond it is another yellow door, only a few centimeters away.

The thin space between the two contains nothing but normal light and the same deafening sound. It is too narrow for a person to enter. Frostless, spotless, purposeless, it may be the smallest room in the tower. A plaque identifies it as an exterior observation platform.

There is no exterior.

Or, if there is, the tower has constructed this chamber to stand in for it: a pocket wilderness made entirely of panels, motors, synthetic cliffs, and plants that cannot change. High above an unseen world, the Upper Throat preserves not nature but the condition of newness—a manufactured dawn without warmth, weather, succession, or end.

Its engines continue at their single impossible pitch.

Its afternoon lamps remain on.

Nothing arrives. Nothing erodes. Nothing learns to live there.
