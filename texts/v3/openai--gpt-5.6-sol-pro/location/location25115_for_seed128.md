High in the body of a constructed mountain, behind several kilometers of inhabited machinery, there is a place known only as the **warm cistern**.

It is not a reservoir in the ordinary sense. It is a narrow chamber folded around one of the mountain’s atmospheric conduits, scarcely larger than a courtyard, though its geometry makes it difficult to judge where the walls end. Every surface is manufactured: dark ceramic ribs, porous metal, old sealant, translucent drainage membranes, and load-bearing composites laid down in overlapping generations. There is no soil and no bedrock. Even the grit underfoot is the worn-off substance of the chamber itself.

The place has been functioning for so long that construction and geology have become almost indistinguishable.

Its walls are rounded by established flows. Channels have deepened where condensed water has followed the same routes for centuries. Bolt heads stand like pebbles in the current. Honeycombed panels have lost their original edges, and maintenance seams have become miniature ravines lined with black mineral films. Yet nothing appears ruined. Erosion was anticipated in the design. Beneath every sacrificial layer another waits with slightly different grain and hardness, steering water into new courses as the old ones wear away. The chamber changes continuously but remains, in a broad sense, the same shape.

It is warm here—body-warm in the lower hollows, fever-warm beside the conduit. Moisture gathers instantly on anything cooler than the air. Water beads along the ceiling, swells, and drops in heavy intervals. It runs down weeping ribs and hangs beneath grilles in clear trembling sacks before being shaken loose. Shallow pools occupy the floor’s depressions, none deeper than an ankle, connected by capillary gutters too narrow to see. After a pressure cycle, mist fills the whole pocket and visibility shrinks to a few arm lengths.

The light is dim and indirect. Thin amber markings glow inside the wall materials, mostly obscured by scale and condensation. Here and there, inspection filaments emit a dull blue pulse, revealing crowded outlines: pipes within pipes, meshes behind meshes, tiny bodies clinging to every protected surface. There are no open skies, but a ventilation slit occasionally clears, and through it appears a blade of hot daylight and, impossibly far below, a pale cloud deck. The cistern is high enough that the exterior air would be cold and thin. Its warmth belongs entirely to the mountain.

The sound is overwhelming.

The main conduit produces a continuous roar too deep to hear cleanly. It enters through the ribs and the pools, through teeth and bones. Over it come sharper noises: the hammering of condensate gates, the hiss of pressure equalization, the metallic rain of droplets striking membranes, and the rapid clatter of thousands of small animals withdrawing whenever the walls flex. At intervals the entire chamber resonates. Water rises in concentric wrinkles. Loose shells dance across the floor. Speech, if attempted, becomes visible effort without meaning.

Nothing grows tall. There is no room, light, or stable substrate for it. Vegetation survives as sparse, stubborn stains: rust-red photosynthetic films near the daylight slit, pale threads rooted in the warmth of cable seams, and small waxy fronds that unfold only when the inspection lamps pass through their spectrum. These plants are widely separated, but each supports an extraordinary concentration of animal life.

The most numerous are thumb-sized shellbacks, low creatures with translucent armor and many fine gripping legs. They graze the ceramic walls clean in moving patches. Their shells are not grown from calcium but from particles shaved off the chamber’s sacrificial lining, sorted and welded together biologically. Old individuals therefore match the wall on which they live: black on thermal tile, silver near the conduit, faintly luminous around the inspection strips.

Between them move thread-eels, no thicker than wire. They swim through the shallow gutters and travel over damp surfaces inside sleeves of their own mucus. When a pressure pulse approaches, they knot themselves around mesh intersections. Clouds of minute fan-winged animals gather wherever warm vapor enters cooler air. They do not truly fly; they steer through the conduit’s standing pressure waves, feeding on suspended microbes and condensed organics.

Predators are abundant but small. Flat, six-limbed hunters wait beneath the lips of drains, sensing disturbances through the metal rather than through the water. Long-legged hammer crabs strike shellbacks loose during resonance events. In the ceiling cavities live soft, blind animals that lower adhesive threads into the mist. Their feeding lines quiver in the machinery’s vibration, so prey cannot distinguish them from the background trembling of the chamber.

The ecosystem is crowded without being lush. Most of its living mass is animal, sustained indirectly by heat gradients, chemical seepage, microbial films, and microscopic nutrients captured from the processed atmosphere. Nothing here depends entirely on sunlight. The mountain’s waste is the cistern’s weather, soil, season, and food.

Its day lasts eleven minutes.

During the first phase, warm exhaust enters the central conduit and the walls begin to sweat. Grazers spread out. Fan-wings rise into the thickening air. In the second phase, cooling membranes engage and rain falls hard enough to flatten the sparse fronds. Gutters fill; thread-eels disperse; predators leave their drains. Then the pressure gates close. The roar deepens beyond hearing, the chamber shakes, and much of the fauna locks itself to the nearest surface. Finally the pumps draw the pools down through innumerable pores. For half a minute the cistern becomes merely humid rather than wet. This is when eggs are laid in exposed crevices, when shells are exchanged, and when the pale seam-plants open.

Then condensation begins again.

The chamber repairs itself in equally quiet cycles. Cracks do not close visibly. Instead, the material surrounding them alters its porosity, persuading water to carry dissolved structural compounds into the damage. Meshes tighten. Surfaces soften, flow by fractions of a millimeter, and reharden. The repair system does not distinguish completely between architecture and inhabitants. Empty shells are sometimes incorporated into walls. Animal tunnels may be preserved if they improve drainage. A thriving colony can become, over generations, an official component of the cistern’s hydraulic design.

This is the governing rule of the place: **anything persistent becomes structural**.

Paths made by water become channels. Channels occupied by animals become habitat conduits. Habitat conduits alter airflow, and the mountain adapts around them. There is no untouched nature here, but neither is there a clear boundary between machine and ecology. The chamber was made in totality, and then made again by use, abrasion, metabolism, maintenance, and time.

Despite its density, the warm cistern feels hidden and intimate. One can stand at its center and nearly touch both opposing walls. Its great height is known only through pressure, the occasional blade of sky, and the long delay before discarded water reaches lower systems. It is a pocket in something vastly larger—a damp space behind a panel, a warm crease in the architecture of the heights.

The roar makes it seem enormous.

The dripping makes it seem inhabited.

And beneath both, transmitted through the wet floor, is the slow, regular change in tone of the constructed mountain adjusting its balance against the wind.
