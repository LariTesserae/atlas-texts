Beneath the salt plain there is no cavern in the ordinary sense. There is only an absence being made.

The hollow is young. Its ceilings have not yet decided to become ceilings, and its floor rises in warm, slow convulsions as roots knot beneath it. For a distance of several valleys, the buried world is packed with vegetation: not vegetation growing from soil, but vegetation occupying the place of soil, stone, timber, and weather. Trunks lie against trunks until they fuse. Bulbs the size of houses press together in the deeper chambers. Fibrous mats descend farther than anyone has cut, tightening underfoot when stepped upon. What looks at first like a cliff is the exposed rind of a single colonial plant, layered in cork, green muscle, amber vessels, and old scars.

There is almost no water here. The little that enters from the upper world is seized molecule by molecule before it can gather into dampness. Nothing drips. Nothing glistens with dew. The air tastes of hot resin, chalk, pollen, animal musk, and the bitter salts left after perfect thirst.

Life below runs on other fluids. Clear oils move through capillary veins. Milky latex carries sugars and defensive crystals. Some plants circulate liquid wax kept mobile by the warmth; others use dense ionic saps that would burn the tongue and tarnish simple metal. When a stem is cut, it does not bleed wetly. It extrudes ropes of translucent gum that twitch, foam, and seal the wound in seconds. Fallen leaves do not rot into mud. They dry from within and become curled flakes of lacquer, crisp enough to ring when trodden on.

The light is surprisingly ordinary.

It comes from broad, pale organs stretched across the upper hollows: ceiling-leaves thin as skin, nourished by roots that penetrate upward toward the sun-blasted plain. They pipe daylight down through bundles of glassy cells. The result is neither dim cave-glow nor eerie phosphorescence, but a warm, diffuse afternoon extending where no afternoon should exist. Shadows are soft beneath the great leaves. Colors remain clear—yellow pollen, red bark, blue-black shell, white bone. When the ceiling-leaves twist, whole districts brighten or darken together, as if enormous clouds were passing directly overhead.

This movement is rarely gentle.

The entire buried forest is under construction. Tendrils test the open spaces, recoil from teeth, and return armored. Root-columns swell until they split galleries in two. Seed-pods fire themselves across chasms with concussive cracks. A chamber that is open in the morning may be packed by evening with foaming pith. Elsewhere, feeding swarms can excavate a new avenue through fifty paces of living wood before the fresh-cut surfaces have finished sealing.

Thus erosion has only just begun, and it is mostly biological. There are no old river channels, no rounded stones, no patient slopes. Surfaces are raw: ripped fiber, tooth-scored cork, newly pulverized salt, crumbling combs of plant-mineral. The ravines have ragged edges. Their talus still writhes. Dust has not had time to settle before roots stitch through it and lift it into some new shape.

Across the largest hollow, the land resembles a basin crowded with motion. Low forests of jointed stalks stand upon the backs of buried trunks. Vine bridges sag between root towers. Open flats appear where grazing animals have planed the growth down to a tough red membrane; these flats may be half a mile wide but never remain empty. Shoots push through them in visible ripples. Herds follow the shoots. Predators follow the herds. Parasites follow everything.

The fauna are not merely abundant. They are the dominant weather.

Clouds of winged creatures pour through the galleries, thick enough to change the color of the light. They range from powdery gnats to leathery gliders whose wings strike the air like rugs being beaten. Long-bodied crawlers travel in braided processions across the walls and ceiling. Six-legged browsers crowd the flats shoulder to shoulder, shearing new growth with jaws like stone shears. Pale animals without eyes swing beneath the vine bridges, orienting themselves by rattling hollow scales. Burrowing things move under the floor in such numbers that the ground resembles simmering dough.

Almost every surface carries passengers. Mites graze fungi from the shells of beetles. Small feathered runners hunt the mites. Transparent worms drink oil from the runners’ quills. Seeds cling to the worms and open only after being carried through the hot respiratory folds of a larger animal. No creature is solitary for long. To stop moving is to be climbed, tasted, planted upon, or incorporated.

The sound is immense.

There is no distance at which the place becomes quiet. Wings produce a continuous granular roar. Browsers grind stalks by the acre. Root fibers snap under subterranean pressure with reports like breaking spars. Resonant flowers call to pollinators in notes too low to hear cleanly but strong enough to shake dust from bark. Above them rises the clatter of shells, the shriek of mating swarms, the hammering of seed-pods, the barking cough of resin vents, and the pulse of millions of feet passing over hollow plant chambers.

Speech is possible only at arm’s length, mouth to ear. The scattered inhabitants use gesture, drum-patterns, colored paddles, and cords pulled between stations. They sleep in chambers lined with layers of dead husk, not for softness but to reduce the noise to something survivable.

Their structures are few and temporary. A house here is usually a cage of split stalks lashed around a defensible fork in the roots. Platforms hang above the migration paths. Bone ladders descend into feeding cuts. Small shrines are pressed into hardened wounds in the larger trunks, where the plant’s amber scar tissue seals around carved teeth, shed shells, salt tablets, and crude copper hooks.

Nothing built is permitted to touch the living substrate carelessly. A misplaced peg may provoke a wall to swell shut. Smoke can attract creatures from miles of galleries. Open flame is nearly impossible: the air is warm and rich with volatile pollen, and fires run explosively through dry leaf-lacquer. People use hot stones, friction drills, shell blades, bone wedges, and rare soft-metal tools wrapped at the handles against corrosive sap. Their finest craft is not cutting but persuasion—scoring a growth line so that a plant forms a stair, pinching buds until they grow into bowls, or placing a larval colony where a doorway will be needed months later.

Even these interventions are provisional. There are no ruins in the usual sense. Abandoned structures are swallowed. A ladder becomes a trellis, then a rib within a trunk. A hut may be lifted thirty feet as its supporting root grows, or carried sideways over years until it opens onto empty air. The oldest human marks survive as patterns in bark: square spirals, rows of scar-knots, unnatural symmetry glimpsed beneath fresher growth.

Warmth rises from below, not as volcanic fire but as the heat of metabolism on a continental scale. The deeper mats ferment without decay, compressing dry organic matter into wax, gas, and black vegetal stone. Some lower chambers are almost unbearably hot. Their plants are pale and thick, their animals slow, armored, and glossy with protective oil. Gas bladders inflate in the floor and rupture without warning, overturning groves or lifting whole islands of root-mat toward the ceiling. In their wake, new hollows gape—raw, lightless at first, rapidly colonized.

This is how the world expands. Not by water wearing stone away, but by hunger opening room inside life.

The plants grow because animals wound them. The animals multiply because the wounds release food. New space invites more growth; more growth brings more feeding; feeding enlarges the space. Cavern, forest, city, and body are phases of the same turbulent process. There is no stable ground beneath it, only layers that are moving at different speeds.

At times the great basin seems almost coherent: pale daylight overhead, red grazing fields below, root towers marking the distance like mountain ranges. Then a migration arrives. The floor darkens under a flood of shells. Vines contract. Pollen erupts in golden walls. Predators drop from the ceiling. A root ridge splits open, exposing incandescent yellow tissue, and ten thousand creatures turn toward it at once.

Within minutes, the landscape has become an event rather than a place.

By the time the noise subsides—if it ever truly does—the ridge is gone. A trench lies where it stood, raw-sided and steaming with resin vapor. Bridges are already growing across it. Winged seeds circle in the normal, impossible daylight. Beneath the fresh surfaces, something enormous begins to chew.
