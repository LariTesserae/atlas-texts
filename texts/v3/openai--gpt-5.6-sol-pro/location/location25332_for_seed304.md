The town occupies a shallow basin scarcely wider than a grove. From the low ridge around it, one can see nearly every roof, every lane, every cistern—and yet the place does not look small. It looks compressed. Houses, retaining walls, stairs, kilns, gutters, footbridges, drying racks, shrine niches, and water courts have been fitted together until there is no unused ground. Even the gaps have functions. A slot between two walls becomes a drain; a ledge becomes a sleeping shelf; a crack holds a lamp cup or a twist of cord.

The basin itself is new. Its pale clay has not yet softened into old contours. It broke upward from the marsh in a series of low, sharp-backed folds, leaving slabs tilted at awkward angles and seams that still seep cloudy water. The outer ridge is only a few people high, but its crest is raw and narrow, armored in splinters of gray shale. After rain, small pieces shear away and slide into the ditches below. The inhabitants gather these fragments before they can dissolve, sorting them for roof scales, cutting edges, and drainage baffles.

Beyond the ridge lies flat, cold wetland: dark water, reedless mud, and sheets of ankle-deep mist. Vegetation is scant. A few wire-stemmed rushes grow where runoff loses its mineral bitterness. Lichen makes faint green islands on the oldest walls. In protected courtyards, people cultivate low mats of an edible blackwort, each patch no larger than a cloak and bordered with fired-clay tiles. There are no trees in sight. Wood is rare enough that a straight branch is inherited.

Most of the town is made from what the basin provides. Walls are rammed clay layered with shale, shells, broken pottery, and braided marsh fiber. Their surfaces are smooth from constant handling and wet enough to shine. Roofs are steep little vaults of overlapping ceramic plates. Some are red-brown, some smoke-black, others the bluish color of drowned skin. No roof is broad. Each building leans against the next, sharing walls and warmth, so that the settlement resembles a single complicated vessel divided into chambers.

The lanes are too narrow for carts. Many are little more than shoulder-wide fissures, paved with knuckled stones and crossed by channels of moving water. At intersections, the paving sinks into round settling bowls. Silt gathers there after every storm and is scooped out with shell ladles. The cleaner clay goes to the potters. The coarse grit goes back to the ridge. Nothing washed into town is permitted to leave without being examined.

Water is everywhere but seldom still. It beads on lintels, trickles through wall seams, hangs from the undersides of bridges, and runs in threads along grooves cut into every threshold. The air condenses on cooler stone even when the sky is clear. At dawn, the whole town weeps. Thousands of droplets gather on ceramic eaves and fall into jars arranged beneath them.

The people distinguish these waters carefully. Ridge water is sharp and leaves a white crust. Roof water is mild. Deep seep-water is warm, faintly luminous, and not drunk except during illness. Street water belongs to the kilns and clay pits. Each kind has its own channels, marked not by writing but by patterns of thumbprints pressed into the walls: one row, two rows, spirals, broken spirals.

There are no pipes in the buried sense. All conduits remain visible. A hidden watercourse is considered dangerous because water changes character when no one witnesses it. If a drain must pass through a wall, an eye-shaped opening is left beside it so that daylight can touch the flow. In the oldest quarter—old here meaning perhaps three generations—the houses contain hundreds of these small eyes. On bright afternoons, they project trembling coins of reflected light across the interiors.

The town is cool throughout the year. Rain comes frequently, usually as fine diagonal needles that seem too light to account for the saturation underfoot. Storms are not violent, but they are persistent. A rainfall may continue for six or seven days, withdraw for an hour, then resume from a different direction. During these periods the clay ridge swells. Doors jam. Stairs change inclination. Hairline cracks open with the neatness of cuts.

Because the land is still settling, no structure is trusted to remain where it was built. Houses are shaped to yield. Corners are rounded, roof plates loosely laced, and floors laid in separate pads that can tilt without tearing the walls apart. Every household owns wedges of stone and fired clay. When a room begins to lean, people drive wedges beneath sleeping platforms, hearth slabs, storage jars, and finally beneath the wall itself. The sound of this work—tap, pause, tap—is one of the town’s commonest noises.

Even so, the settlement is mostly quiet. Mud absorbs footsteps. Wet walls muffle voices. There are no wagons, sawmills, or hammered metal workshops. Tools are stone, bone, tooth, shell, fired clay, and occasional pieces of soft copper traded from somewhere beyond the marsh. Work is performed by pressure, abrasion, leverage, and long patience. Potters turn their vessels on low hand-spun plates. Builders cut shale with harder shale. Kiln workers feed flames through narrow vents using dried marsh peat, fungus cakes, and pockets of gas captured from the wetland in bladder skins.

The kilns are the closest thing the town has to towers. They rise from a central yard in a dense cluster of bulbous chimneys, none much taller than a house. Their surfaces are repaired so often that they have become lumpy and organic, like crowded nests or mineral growths. Warmth from the firing chambers draws moisture from the surrounding ground. Steam drifts through the yard, smelling of clay, salt, peat smoke, and something faintly medicinal.

Kiln firing is timed to the basin’s movements. Before the ridge slips, seep-water grows warmer. Before a new crack opens, beads of condensation align along walls in straight vertical chains. The kiln tenders watch these signs. They say the ground dislikes emptiness: if clay is taken from a pit, something must be placed there before nightfall—a broken jar, a basket of rubble, a sleeping animal, even a person keeping vigil. Otherwise the hollow attracts pressure, and by morning a wall elsewhere may have folded into it.

Whether this is superstition or a property of the basin is difficult to determine. Empty spaces do not last long here. Abandoned rooms slowly fill with pale, wet mineral foam exuded from their foundations. It hardens into brittle shelves, first along the floor, then upward in overlapping layers. In a month, a deserted chamber can become solid except for narrow breathing gaps. Residents quarry this growth and use it as insulation. If cut too soon, it makes a soft ticking sound and bends toward the nearest water.

Fauna are uncommon. Small white midges appear after rainfall but vanish by noon. Blind, translucent eels sometimes emerge from warm seep-holes, no longer than a finger. They are returned to the water because their arrival often precedes subsidence. A few long-legged marsh birds visit the ridge, standing motionless among the shale shards. They rarely descend into town. There are no domestic herds; space and fodder would not allow them. Households keep clay bowls of mud-snails for food and dye, and occasionally a quiet, heavy-bodied amphibian called a wall toad takes up residence in a drain. Such an animal is considered part of the structure. Removing one is believed to cause leaks.

Human life fills the settlement more completely than vegetation or wildlife. Yet the town seldom feels crowded in the ordinary way. People have learned to move as water does: yielding, dividing, taking different levels. One person walks the lane while another passes across a bridge overhead and a third crawls through a maintenance channel below. Roofs serve as workshops when dry. Cistern rims become market stalls. At night, communal sleeping chambers unfold from walls in tiers, cloth slings suspended above warm clay benches.

There is no open square. The nearest equivalent is the Rain Court, a space only twelve paces across and almost entirely occupied by jars. Some are no larger than cups; others are shoulder-high, buried to their necks. Narrow paths wind between them. During rain, each vessel speaks with a different pitch. The court becomes a restrained music of plinks, hollow knocks, soft drumming, and occasional resonant booms. Listeners can tell from the changing notes which cisterns are full and which roofs have shifted.

The town’s boundaries are exact. On the inner slope of the ridge, construction is continuous. On the outer slope, almost nothing stands. The ground there is too young and slick, shedding layers into the marsh. Steps have been cut down to water level, but they must be recut after every wet season. At their foot are low docks made not of timber but of bundled reeds coated in waxy clay. Flat-bottomed skin boats are dragged onto them for journeys across the marsh.

From outside, especially in mist, the town appears less built than extruded: a dense gray-brown knot pushing out of the basin, glistening with runoff. Smoke trails cling to the roofs rather than rising. Figures move along the upper paths, briefly silhouetted, then vanish into slots and stairwells. The ridge hides the lower buildings, so the chimneys seem to grow directly from the earth.

The active life of the place is measured in small adjustments. Water levels rise and fall by finger widths. Walls sweat. Kilns breathe. Clay pits are emptied and filled. Roof plates migrate from one house to another. Channels clog, are opened, and clog again. The basin lifts imperceptibly at its center, forcing the settlement outward against the ridge, while the ridge itself slumps inward. Every structure is caught between these motions.

The inhabitants do not speak of completing the town. Completion would imply stillness, and still things here are either drowned or filled in. Instead they say the town is being kept open.

At evening, ordinary daylight fades quickly behind the low rim. Oil lamps appear in wall niches, their flames reflected in wet plaster. The drains continue to murmur. Somewhere a stone wedge is tapped beneath a settling floor. Somewhere a kiln gives a muted cough as marsh gas catches. Then the condensation begins again, forming first on the highest ceramic edges and spreading downward until every surface is jeweled.

By midnight, the whole compressed settlement seems to hang inside a single drop of water—crowded, cool, and fragile, held together not by permanence but by ceaseless attention.
