The place is called the Low Galleries, though no one can say what they are low beneath.

They occupy a shallow underworld: not a deep cavern, but a broad, crowded chamber lying just below an unseen surface. In places the roof is only the height of three people standing on one another’s shoulders; elsewhere it rises into warm, vaulted bays large enough to hold a shrine, a market, or a flock of roosting animals. The whole region could be crossed in an hour if there were a straight path. There is not.

Everything is made.

The floor is brick, though centuries of feet and water have rounded it into slopes, basins, lips, and smooth channels. The walls are assembled from fitted slabs of fired clay, pale mortar, blackened timber, wicker packed with plaster, and fragments of older walls reused without concern for their original purpose. Pillars crowd the passages. Some are square and massive, some no thicker than a wrist, bundled in clusters beneath sagging beams. Stairs climb into ceilings. Doorframes stand inside other doorframes. Narrow bridges cross gutters only a pace wide. Arches have been bricked up, pierced again, worn through, and patched with tiles bearing the fingerprints of long-dead makers.

There is no exposed stone beneath any of it. When a floor collapses, another floor is found below. When a wall flakes open, the inner fill contains tiny painted tesserae, broken pottery, cord, charcoal, and pieces of yet another wall. Even the mud is manufactured: powdered brick, ash, meal husks, animal dung, and the soft remains of plaster. The inhabitants have a saying that the world is thick with previous rooms.

The Galleries are dense almost to discomfort. A person rarely sees farther than twenty paces. Columns, pens, ladders, partitions, drying racks, hanging baskets, cages, roof braces, cistern rims, and stacks of salvaged tile interrupt every view. To walk is to turn the shoulders. To carry anything long requires shouting ahead and negotiating corners. Yet the density has a settled character. Nothing feels newly crowded. Each obstruction has acquired its own path around it; each low beam is polished where generations have ducked beneath.

The place is warm. Heat lingers in the brickwork and rises from lower conduits in slow breaths smelling of mineral dust, wet clay, beasts, and cooked grain. The air does not stagnate, however. Hundreds of simple shafts pass upward through the roof. Some draw air; others release it. Their operation depends on sun, wind, and temperature in the unknown world above, and so the Galleries inhale and exhale according to a weather no one can see.

Daylight comes through these shafts.

It is ordinary daylight by the time it reaches the chambers—white in the morning, yellow toward evening—but it has been gathered and redirected through long plastered throats. Inside each shaft are angled plates of polished shell, pale ceramic, mica, or beaten metal. The plates are simple objects, individually positioned by hand, yet arranged in such numbers that whole courtyards glow. Light falls in solid-looking columns full of dust and gnats. Children place bowls in it to warm water. Lizards flatten themselves on the brightest walls. At noon, certain passages are as clear as an outdoor lane; a few steps away, behind the pillars, there remain deep blue pockets where lamps must be carried.

Water also comes from above, but irregularly.

Most days the Galleries are merely damp. Beads gather on glazed ceilings and creep along incised grooves. Condensation runs down pillars into thumb-sized cups set into their bases. Cloths are hung in ventilation throats to catch mist. Every ledge seems to support a jar, shell, basin, or folded leaf positioned under a dependable drip.

Then, without warning, rain speaks in the shafts.

At first there is a scattered ticking high in the roof. Within minutes the whole chamber rings. Water pours down some conduits in clear ropes and blows from others as spray. Gutters wake all at once, overflowing from one channel into the next. Small sluice gates are opened with wooden mallets. Mats are rolled aside. Animals are lifted onto shelves. People hurry through the passages carrying stoppered jars and shouting the names of cisterns.

The rain seldom lasts long. It may flood a sunken room knee-deep and leave the neighboring hall nearly dry. Afterwards, water lies in hundreds of pockets: stair wells, tile-lined hollows, abandoned hearths, blocked drains, old mortars, and the concave backs of overturned roof pieces. For several days the Galleries smell washed and cool. Then the warmth returns, the puddles shrink, and white mineral margins appear around them.

Erosion here is ancient but not violent. Water has had time to teach the architecture its preferred shapes. Steps dip in their centers. Drains have become little ravines. The corners of bricks have dissolved until walls resemble rows of blunt teeth. Painted faces on tiles have lost their noses and eyes. Rope grooves cut deep into well rims. No surface is raw unless someone has just broken it, and even fresh breaks quickly acquire soot, moisture, handprints, and the pale bloom of salts.

Repair is constant, but repair does not restore. It thickens.

The people use stone-headed mallets, bronze awls where bronze can be found, wooden wedges, cord, baskets, plumb lines, and clay levels filled with water. They can produce excellent brick and astonishing plaster, but little else in quantity. A dangerous arch is not dismantled by machinery; it is supported with more arches. A failing floor is overlaid with reeds, potsherds, and new tiles. A blocked passage becomes a wall. A dry cistern becomes a sleeping room. In this way the Galleries change without expanding, growing inward through subdivision and accumulation.

Vegetation is sparse and closely watched. There is not enough light or soil for a continuous green world. Instead, plants occur as deliberate islands.

Moss pads the rims of the oldest cisterns. Pale ferns grow where spray from a shaft strikes a wall. Gourds crawl on trellises beneath the strongest daylight, their roots sunk into deep ceramic jars. There are narrow beds of onions, bitter herbs, beans, and a tough grass used for mats. Fungi are cultivated in dark, humid cupboards lined with rotting wicker. After a rain, small translucent mushrooms rise from cracks in the plaster and vanish within a day.

The plants never hide the construction. Roots enter mortar joints rather than earth. Vines climb carved grids. Moss spreads across tile and stops sharply at copper-rich glaze. Even decay follows boundaries laid down by hands.

Animals are everywhere.

Small tawny goats live on platforms and ledges, descending by ramps too narrow for people to use comfortably. Long-legged fowl patrol the gutters, snapping up larvae after rain. Rats inhabit the warm inner walls, and sleek hunting cats move after them through gaps between rooms. Geckos click from the ceilings. Beetles crowd beneath water jars. Pale crabs, no larger than coins, emerge from drains during wet periods and graze on the green film along the channels.

There are also shaft-swifts: dark birds that nest in the daylight conduits and descend in spirals at dawn. Their wings fill the vaulted chambers with a rushing like cloth shaken beside the ear. They feed on the immense population of flies and gnats that rises after every rain. At evening they return upward, vanishing one by one into points of gold.

Nothing in the Galleries is quiet. Hard surfaces catch and multiply every sound. Water knocks in pipes. Wooden wheels creak above wells. Goats complain from their shelves. Potters slap clay onto tables. Fowl scream when startled. Vendors call through archways. Mallets strike wedges in irregular volleys. Somewhere, almost always, a wall is being shored, a tile cut, a gate lifted, grain pounded, or a blocked drain hammered open.

Because the passages bend and fork, sounds arrive without their causes. One may hear a crowd where there is only a cistern resonating with voices from three rooms away. A single dropped bowl can seem to break throughout the district. The inhabitants navigate partly by these distortions. They know the hollow boom of the eastern reservoir, the hiss of the warm vents, the sharp double echo beneath the goat bridge. During heavy rain, when all other sound is overwhelmed, ropes are stretched along the main routes so people can find their way by touch.

The ecology of the place follows the rhythm of moisture.

Dry weeks draw animals and people toward the remaining cisterns. Predators become bold. Insects retreat into damp masonry. Plants are covered with woven shades to slow their thirst. When rain comes, the whole system expands suddenly: eggs hatch in gutters, fungi appear, crabs climb the drains, birds feast, goats are moved away from contaminated pools, and every available vessel fills. The abundance is real but brief, dependent on countless little reservoirs rather than any river or lake.

There are rules concerning water, older and stricter than rules concerning ownership. The first flow from a shaft belongs to the drains, because it carries roof dust and bird filth. The second belongs to animals. The third may be gathered for people. No one may seal a drip that also feeds a lower chamber. No cistern may be emptied below the depth of one hand unless the warm winds have ceased. Anyone opening an old wall must listen first for water behind it.

There is one further rule: never dig merely to discover what is underneath.

This is not superstition, exactly. Underneath is always another construction, but not always an empty one. Sealed rooms may hold foul air, old water under pressure, nests, collapsing fill, or passages still used by people who approach the same wall from another side. Sometimes a chisel driven through plaster returns warm and wet. Sometimes light shines through the hole. Once in a great while, something on the far side taps back.

The unusual thing about the Low Galleries is not that they are artificial. To those born there, making is simply the condition of matter. Brick softens into mud; plaster flowers into salt; wood becomes fungus and powder; pots break and are ground into new mortar. Nothing returns to nature, because there is no visible nature to return to. It returns instead to construction.

The oldest inhabitants say the Galleries are not ruins. Ruin implies an earlier whole form. This place has never been whole. It has always been repaired, partitioned, flooded, dried, inhabited, gnawed, plastered over, and opened again.

Toward evening the shafts turn amber. The warm walls release the day’s stored heat, and condensation begins to gather in the cooler vaults. Swifts climb toward the last light while bats or something like bats wake in the closed galleries below. Cooking smoke joins the dust overhead. The cisterns reflect narrow pieces of ceiling. Voices rise, tools continue striking, animals answer one another across partitions, and hidden water moves through the immense thickness of made things.

For a few moments every worn tile shines at its edges.

Then lamps are lit, one room at a time, and the underworld goes on building itself.
