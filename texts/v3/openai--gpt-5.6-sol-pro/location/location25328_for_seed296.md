### The High Drowned Steps

The country stands higher than weather.

From the plains below, it resembles a dark shelf laid across the upper atmosphere: a long, nearly level mass whose edges rise through cloud and whose interior cannot be seen. It is not a young mountain range. There are no bright teeth of stone, no clean summits. Time has worried them away. What remains is an immense elevated ruin of geology—a plateau of old black rock, flattened ridges, and broad valleys worn so deeply that they have lost the appearance of wounds. Their walls are rounded, polished, and furred with moss.

The height is extreme, but the land itself seldom feels steep. One arrives expecting peaks and finds horizons. Roads and neighborhoods continue across shallow inclines while, a few streets away, the ground drops several kilometers through cloud.

Everything is cool. Not frozen, except in shaded cracks and the oldest cisterns, but pervasively chilled. Iron sweats. Window glass clouds from the outside. Breath shows at dawn and often remains visible all day beneath roofs and bridges.

There is little open water. Rain comes intermittently in narrow, disciplined storms, passing along established routes as if following invisible canals in the air. Most moisture arrives as condensation. Cloud presses against the plateau’s rim, beads upon stone, cable, leaf, and corrugated metal, then gathers in gutters. Every surface has been shaped, intentionally or through centuries of accidental adaptation, to conduct droplets somewhere useful.

The cities occupy the plateau’s eroded drainage basins. From a distance they appear almost geological: dense gray-green masses filling old valleys, their towers no taller than the weathered pillars around them. Construction rises in layers. Streets have roofs; roofs have gardens; gardens support pipeways and tramlines; tramlines carry another generation of narrow dwellings on their pylons. Beneath all of it lie earlier streets, still intact in places, descending through the accumulated city like buried riverbeds.

Stone is the principal material, though little of it is freshly cut. Buildings are assembled from blocks that have already served many purposes. A lintel may bear the smooth groove of an ancient water channel. Factory floors are paved with grave slabs, millstones, or sections of dismantled aqueduct. Dark metal braces everything. Copper appears rarely, preserved as green seams around valves and electrical cabinets.

No structure is permitted a sharp outer corner. The old storms remove them quickly, and the city codes merely acknowledge this. Walls round into roofs; stairs sag gently at their centers; railings are polished to the shape of repeated hands. Even machinery looks eroded. Flywheels turn inside housings worn as smooth as river pebbles.

Industry is everywhere, though it does not roar. The thin air and scarcity of fuel favored slow engines: pressure accumulators, gravity works, counterweighted lifts, low-speed turbines fed by collected runoff. Factories operate by patience. For hours, reservoirs fill from a million dripping roofs. Then gates open, pistons descend, looms move, ore presses close, elevators climb the cliffside. A district may awaken for seven minutes and fall silent again.

This gives the plateau its peculiar rhythm. It is active without seeming busy. Motion occurs in dispersed pulses: shutters opening to mist, belts creeping forward, condensation hammers tapping pipes, freight platforms rising soundlessly out of shafts. Far across a basin, one may see an entire hillside of machinery begin to move while hearing almost nothing.

Sound does not travel normally here. The old rock absorbs low vibrations, while mist scatters higher ones. A bell can be seen swinging across the valley but heard only as a soft pressure in the teeth. Footsteps remain close to the walker. Conversation fades within a few meters. As a result, the city communicates visually: semaphore vanes on rooftops, colored lamps beneath eaves, cloth panels unfurled from bridges. At night, dim amber signals pass through the fog like thoughts moving slowly between districts.

There are few animals. The height, cold, and long erosion have filtered life severely. Small pale moths gather inside turbine houses, feeding on mineral films around warm bearings. Long-legged reservoir crabs live in rooftop cisterns and seal themselves into the masonry during dry years. Along the outer cliffs, black gliding creatures emerge at dusk—neither birds nor bats in any familiar sense, but thin-bodied animals that unfold membranes between six jointed limbs. They seldom flap. They descend into the clouds and may not return for days.

People rarely see large wild creatures, though tracks sometimes appear in the moss gardens: three parallel impressions, each the length of a forearm, crossing walls as readily as floors. Whatever makes them leaves no droppings, hair, or disturbed foliage.

Vegetation is abundant wherever moisture pauses. It does not form forests so much as layers. Moss carpets brickwork, tree ferns occupy abandoned courts, and dark-leaved vines grow along steam pipes. Dwarf cedars root in the seams between buildings, their trunks twisted flat by ancient winds. On sheltered terraces, broad edible leaves overlap like roof tiles. Their undersides are silver and funnel condensation toward the roots.

Most plants have become part of the city’s water system. Gardens are arranged above kitchens and workshops, filtering runoff through soil before it enters household tanks. Moss is cultivated on retaining walls because it catches mist. Certain chimneys are wrapped in flowering creepers whose opening and closing indicate pressure changes underground. A blue-black lichen grows only where metal fatigue has begun; inspectors value it more than instruments.

Beyond the dense basins lie the old surfaces of the plateau. These are open, dim regions of worn stone and waist-high vegetation, crossed by maintenance tracks and the raised lines of water conduits. Nothing stands much higher than a person. The land has been reduced almost to its final form: low ridges, shallow bowls, pavements of exposed bedrock engraved with drainage patterns older than the city.

Yet it is not static. Frost opens hairline fractures each night. Mist roots enter them. Water freezes, expands, and carries away grains too small to see. The whole plateau is still descending, molecule by molecule.

The inhabitants measure time by this loss. Official elevations are recertified every generation. Doorways built centuries ago now open a finger’s width below the adjoining street. Bridges are lowered rather than raised. The oldest civic office is the Ministry of Subsidence, whose clerks maintain maps showing not where the land is, but how long each part is expected to remain.

At the plateau’s margins, erosion becomes visible and immense. Entire neighborhoods end at rounded cliffs veiled in cloud. Drainage water spills over them in threads, often blown upward before it can fall. Houses nearest the edge wear collecting nets to catch their own waterfalls when the wind returns them. Far below, the lost water becomes rain over countries whose people may never know the cities above exist.

There are rules here, learned more from survival than law.

Nothing heavy is kept in one place for long. Reservoirs migrate between roofs. Warehouses rotate their stock. Public squares are paved with hollow blocks so their burden can be redistributed through underground chambers.

No water is considered clean until it has passed through living roots.

No machine may operate continuously. Continuous motion is believed to attract structural failure, though engineers argue whether this is superstition or accumulated wisdom.

When fog enters a building against the direction of the wind, all lower doors are opened. Sometimes the fog continues downward into the buried city. Sometimes something else comes upward with it.

The deepest levels are not abandoned, exactly. Their streets still receive power at irregular intervals. Pumps start without instruction. Signal lamps answer patterns no longer used above. Workers descending for repairs report gardens growing in sealed stations, watered by condensation on ceilings that should be dry. They find warm rails and recently greased mechanisms. The official explanation is residual automation.

No one offers an official explanation for the elevators that arrive already occupied by wet footprints.

Most days remain uneventful. Mist gathers. Leaves drip. Pressure builds behind a hundred thousand valves. The old engines wake one district at a time, and the city rearranges its weight upon the ancient stone.

By evening, clouds often settle into the valleys until only upper bridges and rounded towers remain visible. The plateau seems to become an archipelago, each neighborhood an island in a pale, depthless sea. Lamps glow beneath the vapor. Signal vanes turn. Somewhere, too distant to hear, a factory releases its stored water and an entire row of machines begins its brief, deliberate labor.

Then the reservoirs empty, the mechanisms slow, and quiet returns—not the quiet of stillness, but of innumerable processes too gradual, too dispersed, or too old to make a sound.
