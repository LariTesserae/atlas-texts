The country stands at an altitude where ordinary mountains would already have ended.

It is not a summit but an entire high province: a broad, broken shelf lifted above a deeper darkness, its margins falling away in precipices too tall to read as cliffs. From the interior, the edge resembles the end of the world. Beyond it there is no visible lowland, only a dim, heated atmosphere in which distant ridges appear briefly as black teeth and disappear again.

There is almost no water here. None flows, condenses, or hides beneath the ground. The rocks contain no dampness; freshly split stone is as dry within as old bone. What passes through the valleys is a heavy mineral vapor, warm enough to keep certain salts soft. It settles in hollows as a colorless, knee-deep haze, though it is not mist. Where it touches exposed surfaces, it leaves a metallic bloom like tarnish on a mirror.

The plateau itself is newly arriving.

Long seams open without warning across the plain, glowing faintly at their depths—not red, but a deep violet that the eye struggles to hold. Ridges push upward along these seams. Some rise slowly over many days, carrying slabs and fields on their backs; others thrust up in a single silent motion, producing sheer walls several hundred meters high. The stone is so fresh that it has no scree at its base, no gullies, no rounded corners. Peaks emerge already sharp, dark, and almost polished. Valleys are not carved here. They are the spaces left between things that have risen.

Because there has been little time for weathering, every formation retains the violence of its making. Plains end in clean fractures. Needle ranges lean at improbable angles but have not yet learned to fall. Huge blisters of volcanic glass stand intact, their skins unclouded by dust. In places the ground has been stretched thin enough to become translucent. A dull radiance moves beneath it, revealing pressure fronts passing through the crust like muscles beneath hide.

The warmth comes from below. The air has no sun in it.

Overhead is a nearly lightless vault, perhaps a sky and perhaps only a higher layer of the world. No stars are visible. The little illumination that exists comes from the land: sulfurous lines in cracks, pale electrical veils over certain pinnacles, and slow pulses inside towers of smoky crystal. Nothing casts a dependable shadow. A traveler might see their outline fall north, then gather beneath their feet, then lengthen straight upward across a cliff as though gravity had turned sideways.

Despite the constant movement, the plateau is mostly quiet. Upthrusts often happen without a corresponding crash. Stone separates with a soft internal click. Pressure travels for kilometers before releasing as a single deep knock, more felt in the ribs than heard. At long intervals, a glass escarpment splits and gives off a descending musical tone that continues after the fracture has stopped. The tone may last for hours, thinning until it is indistinguishable from thought.

Wind is rare at ground level. Higher up, invisible currents race between the peaks. They pull streamers of hot dust into the darkness and twist them into immense, brief shapes: columns, arches, hollow wheels. Because the grains carry opposing charges, these structures can hold together for minutes. They travel across the plateau without touching it, collapsing only when they strike a newly risen ridge. Then the dust flashes and vanishes into the stone.

The open spaces are enormous but not empty in a simple way. Objects are scattered with deliberate-looking intervals: one black spire in a basin several kilometers wide; three low walls on a ledge; a ring of upright slabs around nothing; a staircase descending six steps into solid ground. They resemble constructions, though no hand made them and no tool marks appear on their surfaces. They are products of crystallization, pressure, and an obscure tendency in the forming crust to repeat certain enclosed shapes.

Some are unmistakably roomlike. Hexagonal chambers grow inside mineral domes, joined by narrow passages that meet at clean angles. Door-shaped voids open in cliffs. Terraces assemble themselves around the flanks of young mountains. Once formed, these structures may persist for a season or be folded back underground the following night. There are no ruins here, properly speaking, because ruin requires age. These are abandoned before they are complete.

Nothing animal crosses the plains. There are no tracks, droppings, nests, bones, calls, or sudden movements at the edge of sight. No winged thing occupies the updrafts. No burrower loosens the ground. The silence has never been taught to anticipate a body.

Life, if it is life, exists only as thin black crusts along the warmest fractures. They do not grow toward light. They spread toward stress, arranging microscopic cells—or cell-like mineral compartments—along faults before the faults open. For a few hours they form branching maps on the rock. When the seam ruptures, the crust is consumed, leaving a white filament embedded in the new cliff face.

Elsewhere, translucent sacs inflate from deposits of waxy salt. They have no roots and perform no visible exchange with the air. They appear after electrical storms beneath the ground, remain taut for several days, and then collapse into flat, iridescent skins. Whether these are organisms, chemical weather, or merely another phase of the plateau’s construction is impossible to decide. They neither compete nor reproduce in any familiar sense. There is too little of them to soften the mineral severity of the place.

The dominant event is formation.

Mountain chains begin as tremors moving beneath a level field. Long black lines appear, each finer than a hair. The ground between them tilts. Over the next few hours, immense plates slide upward past one another, carrying their unweathered surfaces into the hot dark. Basins buckle into amphitheaters. Crystal forests—lifeless, widely spaced pillars—grow from exposed seams and shatter as soon as they reach a certain height. Their fragments sometimes float briefly in the charged air before sinking point-first into the plain.

At the plateau’s outer margin, whole districts are continually lost. The crust folds downward into the unseen depth, taking ridges and chambers with it. Yet there is little debris. Matter seems to be reclaimed rather than broken. A descending mountain narrows, darkens, and passes through the lower haze until only its highest needle remains. That needle may hang alone for a time, unsupported, before dropping without sound.

Distance is difficult to judge. The darkness removes the usual layers of perspective, while the heat keeps the air almost perfectly clear between curtains of mineral vapor. A formation that appears small may require a day to reach. A pale arch on the horizon may be close enough to touch—or hundreds of kilometers away and tall enough to span a valley. The land offers scale only when it moves. Then a crack races toward the observer, and what seemed like a minor ledge lifts into a continental wall.

There are local rules, though they are not stable everywhere.

Stone newly exposed to the air remains soft for several minutes and can be marked by pressure alone. Afterward it hardens beyond fracture, becoming smooth and nearly indestructible until the next deep pulse passes through it. Loose objects tend to align along invisible fields, pointing toward whichever mountain is about to rise. Sound travels best downward. Heat gathers on high ground. Cavities are often warmer than open fissures, even when the fissures reveal the glow below.

Most strangely, enclosed spaces seem to form before the mass around them. In the birth of a dome, the hollow chamber appears first as a region from which dust is expelled. Mineral walls then condense around that absence. Thus the plateau does not carve shelters out of rock; it builds rock around shelters that briefly precede it.

At certain times, all motion stops.

The vapor settles. The underground radiance dims. Floating fragments drop to the plain. For several minutes the entire province becomes absolutely still, its sharp new mountains standing in the warm dark with the starkness of an unfinished model. In those pauses, the scattered chambers and stairways appear less accidental. Their openings face one another across impossible distances. Terraces line up from ridge to ridge. Empty rings answer other empty rings beyond the horizon.

Then a pressure wave arrives from below.

The ground swells. Violet fractures ignite across the plateau. Towers rise, rooms close, and long walls extrude from the plains before bending under their own birth. The temporary alignments are erased and replaced by others. There is no builder, no witness, and perhaps no intended final form—only a world constructing itself faster than time can weather it, rehearsing landscapes in darkness and withdrawing them before they can become old.
