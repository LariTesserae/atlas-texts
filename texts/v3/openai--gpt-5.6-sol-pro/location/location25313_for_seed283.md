At first the lowland seems almost empty.

It is night, and the heat has not diminished. The air lies close to the ground, heavy enough to feel handled, carrying the smells of warm mud, crushed leaves, animal skin, and water that has stood too long among roots. There is no horizon in the ordinary sense. Trees, reeds, creepers, and broad-leaved undergrowth occupy every distance. They do not form a wall so much as a succession of crowded rooms, each opening into another darkness.

The land is nearly level, but not simple. Old floodwater has shaped it into shallow shelves and basins: long, raised backs of compacted silt; narrow channels sunk no deeper than a person’s knee; abandoned meanders filled with black leaf mold; low pans where rain persists for weeks beneath a skin of duckweed. In the dry intervals, the pans contract into separate pools. Their margins become polished by hooves, paws, bellies, and dragging tails. Elsewhere the ground is merely damp, sweating under a crust of fallen vegetation.

Nothing here looks newly broken. Banks have slumped into their settled angles. Roots exposed by ancient floods have darkened and hardened until they resemble the soil around them. Channels shift, but by finger-widths and over years. Even fallen trunks do not seem like interruptions. They sink gradually, collect moss and seedlings, and become long ridges in the floodplain. The landscape has had time to incorporate every collapse.

There are no roads, huts, fences, cut stumps, or pieces of worked material. Yet the place gives an impression of industry.

The impression comes from its density and organization. Leaves are fed into mandibles, stomachs, fungal combs, and fermenting mud. Termite galleries run beneath the surface in branching systems; their mounds rise among buttress roots, ventilated by temperature differences between damp earth and hot air. Ant columns travel the trunks in both directions, some carrying pale fragments of flower or insect, others returning unburdened. Dung is dismantled before dawn. Carcasses lose their shape under beetles, larvae, crabs, and the slow leverage of roots. Nothing is idle for long, though almost everything works in silence.

The machinery is entirely alive.

Above, the canopy is thick enough to suppress the sky. Only a few dim patches show between leaves, not bright enough to cast shadows. Moisture condenses on foliage after midnight and gathers at pointed tips. Drops fall at irregular intervals, striking lower leaves with isolated taps. Sometimes a whole branch releases its accumulated water in a brief patter that resembles rain, though the clouds have given none. Actual rain comes occasionally and hard, filling hollows in an hour, then passing. For days afterward, the country holds that water in pockets rather than rivers.

Vegetation is lush without being uniformly green or soft. The larger trees carry dusty, heat-stressed crowns, while everything below them is dark and wet. Vines wrap the trunks in thick cables. Palms crowd the wetter depressions. Thorny shrubs take the slightly raised ground, where water drains away. Reeds stand in old channels so densely that their dead stems remain upright among the living, forming dry lattices through which small animals move without being seen. Fungi push from the underside of logs. Epiphytes gather dampness in the forks overhead, each holding a thimble or basin of water and its own collection of larvae.

The abundance of animals reveals itself gradually.

Two low points of reflected light appear beneath a fallen tree, close, vanish, and reappear farther away. A branch lowers under a weight that never shows its body. Fish disturb a pool too shallow to seem capable of containing them. Frogs cling motionless to stems, their throats pulsing but producing no call. Bats pass through openings in the canopy with the softness of thrown cloth. Snakes lie along branches whose curvature they imitate. Rodents use the exposed roots as elevated paths. Herd animals stand packed in the reed beds, shoulder against flank, preserving stillness because every splash advertises location.

There are birds everywhere, but most are asleep. They occupy branches in rows and clusters: small birds hidden in foliage, heavier waders folded beside shrinking pools, fruit-eaters hunched under the canopy. Occasionally one shifts, causing several others to adjust in sequence. Wings open and close. Leaves shake, then settle.

The silence is not the silence of absence. It is restraint.

Insects make a thin electrical ticking from high in the crowns, never swelling into a continuous roar. A nightjar gives one low note and waits. Mud releases bubbles with soft, viscous pops. Somewhere beyond sight, a large animal exhales through its nostrils. There may be a sudden rush in the reeds, a single splash, and then ten minutes in which the lowland appears to be listening to itself.

Predation has disciplined the place. So has heat. Movement is expensive, and sound travels unpredictably over water and under leaves. The animals are overwhelming in number but sparing in declaration. Every trunk has occupants, every pool has layers of life, every hollow is being used as refuge, nursery, trap, or feeding ground. The open scale of the floodplain is concealed by this occupancy. It extends for many miles, yet no portion feels vacant enough to grant a clear view.

Toward the deepest part of the night, a faint ground mist forms over the remaining water. It does not cool the air. It collects smells and carries them laterally along the old channels. Animals downstream raise their heads as information arrives: blood, fruit, musk, disturbed silt, a flowering tree, the mineral odor of rain falling somewhere beyond the visible basin.

Change here comes by accumulation. A pool silts up. A colony shifts its tunnels. A fallen tree redirects the next flood. Seeds sprout in the trapped debris, and decades later their roots make a new island of slightly higher ground. Herd paths deepen into drainage lines; drainage lines become wet-season creeks; creeks fill again beneath reeds. The system neither rests nor hurries.

Before dawn, condensation reaches its maximum. The whole forest begins to drip. For a while it sounds as if a light rain is passing through, although the sky remains dry. Animals drink from leaves, from bark, from one another’s tracks. The packed darkness loosens by degrees, but does not disappear. Even in day, the ground will remain dim, hot, and fully occupied—the floor of an immense living works whose engines are digestion, growth, decay, and patient water.
