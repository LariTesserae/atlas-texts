The high country begins without a slope.

One moment there is only the pale distance; the next, the ground has risen beneath it into a broad mineral shelf, warm under a steady cream-colored sun. No foothills lead here. No ravines descend from it. The plateau seems less uplifted than newly inserted into the world: a vast, level component not yet joined to the surrounding geology.

Its surface is made of interlocking plates of salt-glass, pumice, and dull metallic stone. The plates have sharp, unworn seams. Nothing has rounded their edges. There are no channels, no alluvial fans, no stains left by vanished streams. Even dust is scarce. Where a plate has broken, its interior is raw and vivid—black crystal, yellow sulfide, copper-green mineral flesh—but the fracture goes no farther. Erosion has barely begun and appears in no hurry.

There is no water here, not even hidden deeply. The warm air contains almost no vapor. The ground’s chemistry has never accommodated rain. Instead, life uses a family of heavy oils and liquid salts that circulate only within sealed tissues. A cut stem does not bleed; its exposed cells cauterize at once, preserving every drop of solvent inside.

From this dry foundation rises what, at first, looks like a lush forest.

The growth is widely spaced. Miles may lie between one living colony and the next, and the empty intervals are so clean that each thicket seems alone in its own world. Yet each colony is profuse: layered canopies, pleated fronds, towers crowded with vanes, translucent membranes folded over one another in extravagant abundance. Lushness exists here as concentration rather than coverage.

The tallest organisms stand forty or fifty meters high. Their trunks are hollow ceramic cylinders, pale as fired clay and ringed with black conductive bands. They do not branch gradually. At regular heights, whole crowns erupt from them: radial wheels of foil-thin leaves, each leaf stiffened by a lattice of silicate veins. Under the sun these crowns turn, almost too slowly to notice, keeping their dark faces at a fixed angle to the light.

Nothing grows by improvisation. The landscape’s vegetation repeats exact modules.

At the bases of the towers, low mats spread in hexagonal patches. They resemble velvet from afar, but up close they are forests of hair-fine nickel filaments tipped with clear bulbs. Between them stand thick coiled forms like ferns cast in bronze. There are red bladders full of hot ionic fluid, fans dusted with catalytic powder, and clusters of brittle cups whose interiors flash with electrical discharge when shadow passes over them.

This is an industrial ecology without builders.

Every colony is a refinery, a power station, and a reproductive body. Sunlight drives charges through the upper vanes. Deep roots penetrate microscopic seams in the plateau, separating useful metals from the rock and lifting them by capillary forces that do not depend on water. Mineral feedstock passes upward through warm solvent channels. In one organ, carbon compounds are assembled. In another, ceramics are fired at low temperature by dense webs of catalytic fibers. Waste products emerge as tidy ingots and glazed beads arranged around the colony’s perimeter.

There are no pipes, walls, machines, or artifacts separate from life. Nothing has been constructed because nothing here distinguishes construction from growth. A tower does not house an engine; it is an engine. The black belts around its trunk are living electrodes. Its leaf-wheels are generators. Its buried root crown sorts atoms with the steady selectiveness of a factory that has operated for geological ages.

And yet the plateau itself looks newborn.

Shallow ridges rise from the flats in smooth, unweathered folds. They resemble mountains in the first stage of deciding to become mountains. Cones of porous stone stand intact around vents that have never erupted. Sheets of crystal have buckled upward but not broken. There is a sense of arrested formation, as if the region reached the opening instant of tectonic activity and found that instant sufficient.

Nothing develops further.

The sun hangs at the same moderate height. The temperature remains warm but never increases. Shadows keep their direction. Chemical differences that should produce wind, weather, decay, or growth have settled into a meticulous balance. The leaf-crowns turn only because turning is part of that balance; after each revolution, every vane occupies precisely the position it held before. Internal fluids circulate, charges cross membranes, minerals pass from ground to stem and back again, but the total arrangement never changes.

No colony expands. None dies. No seed travels into the bare miles.

The absence of animals is complete. There are no insects inside the folded leaves, no burrowers among the warm roots, no pale thing adapted to feeding on the oily tissues. No tracks cross the salt-glass. The plants have no flowers designed to attract, no fruit, no thorns, no scents that promise or warn. Reproduction, if it can be called that, occurs as an internal rehearsal: small structures form within sealed chambers, mature, are dismantled, and returned to circulation. Potential offspring are manufactured endlessly and never released.

This contributes to the emptiness. The colonies are lush, but they are not busy. No creature moves between their layers. Nothing rustles unexpectedly.

The plateau is nearly silent. If one stands close to a tower, there is a faint electrical ticking from its crown and, at long intervals, a soft ceramic note from the trunk as charge redistributes through the conductive bands. The ground sometimes gives off a dry, glassy murmur—not a quake, but the settling of stresses that never quite settle. Within the mats, ionic fluids make a sound like oil passing through an extremely narrow valve.

From a distance, even these noises disappear.

The light is ordinary enough to make the place stranger. There are no theatrical colors in the sky, no impossible stars visible at noon, no glowing mist. Objects cast clear shadows. Distances are legible. The mineral plain is beige, gray, rust, and white; the living towers carry subdued greens, tarnished reds, and the blue-black of heated steel. Everything can be seen plainly, and nothing explains itself.

Beyond the central shelf, the plateau breaks into a region of broad terraces. Some hold solitary groves. Others are entirely vacant, extending for days with only one black seam across them. Farther still stand rows of embryonic peaks, each separated from the next by immense, level spaces. They do not form a range so much as an inventory: mountain, gap, mountain, gap, each feature distinct and unfinished.

There are no roads through any of it. No ruins. No discarded tools. No evidence that a traveler ever chose one direction over another.

At the highest point is a grove larger than the rest, several miles across, with hundreds of ceramic trunks rising through tiers of metallic foliage. Its underground systems are linked, and the whole colony maintains a single slow electrical cycle. At one edge, charge enters the roots. Hours later it reaches the outer crowns, crosses the canopy as a dim violet shimmer, and returns through the opposite side. The shimmer gives almost no light under the sun, but it can be felt as a pressure in the teeth.

The grove produces nothing that leaves it. All its mechanisms serve only to preserve its mechanisms.

Standing before it, one might think of an abandoned industrial complex reclaimed by vegetation. Closer inspection reverses the thought. There was never a complex, never abandonment, never reclamation. The forest itself discovered furnaces, electrolysis, modular assembly, and closed-loop production without first discovering hunger, locomotion, conflict, or rain.

It has achieved perfect industry in a world where there is nothing to consume its products.

Across the warm, high plateau, the leaf-wheels maintain their angles. The hidden fluids circulate. Raw mountains wait inside smooth folds of stone. Vast spaces remain vacant between lush, self-contained engines of life.

The place is not dead. Nor is it waiting to live.

It is operating.
