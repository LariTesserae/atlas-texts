The land is high enough that weather forms beneath it.

Clouds collect in the valleys far below, a white floor visible through long breaches in the plateau’s rim. From those openings, one might mistake the whole country for a raft of dark stone floating over a luminous sea. Yet the upland itself is almost level. Its mountains have been worn down beyond mountains, beyond hills, into broad undulations perceptible only after days of walking. Ancient rivers reduced the summits; glaciers planed what the rivers left; wind polished the glacial scars. Erosion has not ceased so much as completed its argument. The exposed rock has the low, exhausted shape of something that can no longer fall.

Only distance reveals the elevation. The air is thin and persistently cold. Breath—if there were lungs here to breathe—would crystallize on cloth. Frost lies in the shadows at noon. The sun is neither weak nor strange: it rises, crosses a pale blue sky, and sets with ordinary authority. Its light is clear enough to show every crack in the stone and every separate blade of vegetation, while doing little to warm them.

There are no animals. No insects, no birds passing overhead, no hidden burrowers turning the soil. Nothing grazes, hunts, pollinates, mates, or calls. The land has no footprints except those preserved in old paving and no eyes except knots in timber and the round, black mouths of cisterns.

It is nevertheless green.

Vegetation occupies the plateau in scattered, extravagant territories. There may be fifty kilometers of bare gray pavement, then a basin overflowing with knee-high fern analogues, black reeds, moss cushions, and low trees whose limbs spread flat against the prevailing wind. Without animals to prune or disperse them, the plants have developed other arrangements. Seed capsules fire during sudden pressure drops, throwing their contents across streets and ravines. Vines creep toward the faint vibration of running water. Groves migrate by means of runners: the rear trunks die and collapse into soil while new stems rise at the front, so that over centuries an entire wood crosses a district like a green tide too slow to witness.

Some plants use frost rather than resist it. Their translucent leaves fill with water at dusk, freeze into rigid vanes overnight, and turn on their stems with the dawn. The motion pumps sap downward into insulated roots. At sunrise, whole fields gradually reorient with almost no sound. Light passes through their leaves in long green bands and trembles over the ground.

Moisture comes intermittently. Rain is uncommon but extensive, arriving in shallow silver fronts that can be watched for days as they move across the plateau. More often, water condenses directly from low cloud and gathers on the cold surfaces of the world: on leaves, on standing stones, on roof tiles, on the inner walls of empty rooms. By morning each edge is beaded. Drops accumulate, descend through carved channels, and disappear into cisterns beneath the cities.

The rivers are temporary and immensely old. Most of the time their beds are dry trenches lined with polished black pebbles. When distant rain falls, water enters them without warning. It moves in low, clear sheets, sometimes only a finger’s depth across channels several kilometers wide. For a few hours the river reflects the sky; then it separates into pools under ledges and in the hollows of worn bridges. These pockets may persist for seasons beneath lids of ice. Their bottoms are crowded with green filaments, bladder moss, and pale rooted fans. No creature stirs among them.

Across this vast, nearly empty tableland stand cities.

They do not gather densely around rivers or harbors. There are no harbors. Instead, the settlements are distributed at regular but enormous intervals, each one occupying a slight depression where condensation is dependable. From afar a city appears as a thin discoloration along the horizon: rows of stone uprights, terraces, flat roofs, windbreak walls, and towers no taller than old trees. The streets are broad because space was never scarce. Blocks of dwellings are separated by gardens, reservoirs, frost fields, and stretches of untouched bedrock. A traveler could enter the outer streets and walk half a day before reaching another structure.

The architecture is urban without being monumental. Buildings are made from fitted slabs, rammed mineral earth, woven roots, timber hardened in smoke, and sheets of cloudy ice renewed each winter. There is little metal. Where metal appears, it is soft copper beaten into gutters, needles, clamps, mirrors, or thin resonant plates that no longer ring. Doors turn on stone pivots. Roofs are held down with braided cables of fiber and hairlike fungus. Water is lifted by counterweighted poles, frost-driven pistons, porous ceramic towers, and capillary ropes that draw moisture upward grain by grain.

Everything suggests a civilization of patient hands, but there are no bodies.

The streets are not ruined. That distinction becomes clear slowly. Gutters remain open. Cracked walls are reset. Seed beds are separated by type. Cistern lids are drawn shut before dust storms. At intervals, frost levers tilt broad stone panels to follow the sun. Vines threaded through these mechanisms tighten when wet and contract when cold. Root bundles exert pressure against pegs. Melting ice releases weights. A whole city conducts its maintenance through seasonal change.

It is impossible to tell whether these mechanisms were made by vanished beings or whether the city itself has always been botanical. In sheltered courtyards grow pillar-trees whose branching roots pass through loom frames and pottery wheels. Their annual swelling advances ratchets by one tooth. Some produce hollow gourds already shaped around stone molds. Others deposit wax into channels, making waterproof seals as naturally as laying down bark. There are workshops with flint blades set along the edges of slowly flexing branches. In spring, those branches cut reeds. In autumn, dry tendons draw bundles beneath roofs. Tools are everywhere, but no tool moves quickly enough to reveal its worker.

The silence is immense.

Wind is usually too thin to roar. It slides over the flattened stone with a faint granular hiss. Rain makes no patter because the moss and porous roofs receive it. Water runs beneath streets in muffled conduits. Plants bend without leaves rubbing: many have velvet surfaces or maintain deliberate gaps between their crowns. Even falling masonry descends oddly, often with no audible impact.

This is partly because sound does not behave reliably here.

A struck stone may remain silent for days and then release the blow as a single knock on a clear afternoon. Footsteps—if there were feet—would sometimes be heard kilometers ahead of where they occurred. Certain plazas swallow all vibration. Within them, a dropped object can be seen to bounce, but the event reaches no ear and disturbs no dust. Elsewhere, one can hear frost cracking under a moon that has not yet risen.

The instability is not confined to sound.

Distance has begun to loosen.

A road observed from a tower runs straight for hundreds of kilometers, intersecting six cities and vanishing over the rim. On the ground, the same road may return to its starting gate after an hour. Alleys narrow into seams no hand could enter, yet beyond the seam are visible courtyards with full-grown trees moving in daylight. Some buildings have exterior walls smaller than their rooms. Reservoir stairs descend farther than the thickness of the plateau, passing landings where windows open onto the cloud layer below, then continuing downward into dry, sunlit basins that should lie on the opposite side of the world.

The inhabitants—if the word can apply to rooted, distributed things—have accommodated these failures with simple marks. White stones mean a street currently leads where it appears to lead. Red ochre warns that a doorway changes destination after rain. Three vertical cuts in a lintel signify that the room beyond is older than the building around it and should not be entered during frost. Since the marks themselves sometimes migrate, they are renewed by slow mechanisms of dripping pigment and growing lichen.

Time is uneven but not chaotic. It settles in pockets.

In one abandoned-looking quarter, condensation hangs motionless beneath eaves, each drop elongated but never falling. Nearby, a garden completes its cycle every few hours: shoots rise, unfold, blacken, collapse, and are covered by the next generation. Beyond a low wall lies a square where nothing decays. Woven baskets remain fresh, cords stay taut, and a channel of meltwater preserves the same curling wave year after year. Roads crossing these zones are interrupted by gates designed not to keep anything out, but to make travelers pause long enough to enter the local pace.

The cities map these temporal climates as carefully as they map water. Slow districts store food and fiber. Fast districts cultivate soil. There are avenues planted with trees whose growth rings are used as clocks, though several trees contain rings corresponding to years that have not occurred elsewhere. At the center of each settlement is an open counting court, paved with movable stones. Frost shifts the stones. Roots shift them back. The resulting patterns may be calendars, civic records, weather predictions, or a conversation conducted over geological time.

Activity is everywhere, but almost none of it resembles haste.

Cloud banks build below the plateau and spill upward through notches in the rim. Moss blooms across the paving, then retracts into cracks as the air dries. Rivers arrive and vanish. Forest edges advance by the width of a hand. Stone faces shed a layer of dust so thin it resembles smoke. Cisterns fill. Roofs flex. Frozen leaves rotate toward morning. Workshops complete one cut, one turn, one knot. Whole districts open and close with the seasons as root-grown gates swell in their sockets.

At the continent’s center, the ground rises into what is called a mountain only because everything around it is flatter. Its summit is a broad plain of naked rock. Here the erosion is so complete that the surface has become reflective: not polished, exactly, but stripped of texture at every visible scale. It mirrors the sky imperfectly. Clouds appear in it before they arrive overhead. Stars sometimes appear at noon, not as prophecies but as reflections of some other night.

The oldest city lies on that summit.

Its walls have worn down to waist-high lines. Its towers are circular arrangements of foundation stones. Nothing stands except a few doorframes, though doors still open between them. Through one frame is a cold garden beneath green cloud. Through another, an avenue in the same city as it may once have been, crowded not with animals or people but with upright branching forms carrying baskets of stone. They move only when not directly observed. A third doorway contains darkness and the smell of wet soil. Roots emerge from its threshold, cross the summit, and descend in all directions toward the distant cities.

The possibility arises that the entire plateau is one organism—or one construction—and that the scattered urban centers are not settlements but organs set far apart in a body vast enough to have weather. Rivers are brief circulations. Roads are nerves that sometimes fold back on themselves. Reservoirs are memory. Forests are thoughts moving from one district to another over centuries.

But the landscape resists a final interpretation.

It may instead be a world at the end of erosion, where distinctions have become tired: city and forest, mechanism and organism, distance and duration, maintenance and growth. Nothing here is young except each new leaf. Nothing is dead in the ordinary sense, because animal death never entered the ecology. Wood rots only by chemistry and patient fungi. Plants compete for light, freeze, fracture, and feed their descendants, but there is no fear in the process and no mouth awaiting them.

At evening, the ordinary sun lowers through an extraordinary geometry. Shadows extend eastward, then bend north along roads, avoiding certain plazas. Condensation brightens every roof. The sparse cities become constellations of reflected light across the darkening plateau, each separated from the next by hundreds of kilometers of stone, green basins, and temporary rivers.

One by one, the frost mechanisms engage.

A gate settles into place without a thud. A ceramic valve opens. Water begins to descend beneath a street. Far away—or tomorrow—a single stone knock is released into the cold.

Then the whole vast country continues its work in silence.
