### The Condenser Range

The mountains are manufactured.

From a distance they resemble a pale alpine range under hard morning light: ridges, hanging valleys, steep faces, and broad saddles extending toward a horizon crowded with peaks. Up close, there is no stone. Every slope is assembled from overlapping plates of ceramic, riveted steel, milky glass, and blocks of some dense white composite that rings faintly when struck. The cliffs are load-bearing walls. The scree is made of snapped fasteners, flakes of enamel, and fist-sized pieces of insulation shed from structures farther upslope.

Nothing here has a natural substrate. Beneath the lowest visible terrace are more terraces, ducts, trusses, maintenance chambers, and buried machine halls. Excavation never reaches earth. It only exposes older construction.

The range stands high in a cool, brilliant atmosphere. Its upper ridges pass through thin clouds, but the sky above them is usually an intense, almost metallic blue. Sunlight multiplies across millions of reflective surfaces. Even in shadow, light arrives by reflection from distant fins, silvered towers, and glazed slopes. There are few truly dark places outside the deep machinery. A person crossing the open platforms carries several shadows at once.

The terrain is densely built yet regionally open. Valleys several kilometers long lie between ridges crowded with pipes, gantries, pylons, pressure vessels, switchyards, and narrow habitation stacks. Bridges cross above bridges. Staircases climb the exteriors of sealed factories and continue beyond them into empty air, where perspective makes them appear to join distant slopes. Vertical condenser fields rise like forests of closely spaced blades, but beyond each field is another vista: a white ravine, a suspended reservoir, a plateau covered in articulated mirrors.

The whole range was erected quickly and has not yet learned the softened shapes of age. Corners remain sharp. Fresh weld seams shine blue-black beneath their slag. Ceramic panels break in clean, bright planes. Where a retaining wall has failed, its collapse looks less like erosion than disassembly: orderly fans of bolts and brackets, lengths of cable still bundled together, slabs lying flat as pages.

Weather has begun its work, but only just. Rust appears as narrow orange tears beneath joints. Frost has roughened the black rubber seals. Fine gray filings collect in gutters and the lee of pylons. The landscape has gullies, but many are only channels stamped into its surfaces, designed in advance for water that seldom comes.

Moisture arrives intermittently. Clouds snag on the upper structures and comb themselves apart across antenna masts. Condensation beads along cold pipes, gathers under railings, and travels patiently down chains into cisterns. Rain is brief and precise. It darkens the ceramic, wakes the smell of iron, and fills thousands of shallow pockets before vanishing into drains.

For a few hours after rainfall, the range is full of miniature waters. Puddles lie in bolt wells. Transparent tubes pulse with descending droplets. The floors of disused turbine halls become mirror pools only a finger deep. On certain ledges, flexible membranes have sagged into basins large enough to support reed beds. Far below, a narrow river moves through the central valley—not over a riverbed, but inside an open-topped aqueduct of green copper. It disappears repeatedly into pumping stations and emerges kilometers later at a slightly higher elevation than before.

Water here does not always obey descent. It follows ownership.

Every drop seems bound to the system that first caught it. Water condensed on a north-facing pipe will crawl along that pipe against gravity rather than fall onto an unrelated surface. Rain striking an old roof may collect beneath the roof instead of above it. Streams can cross on a flat plate without mixing, each maintaining its course as if separated by invisible channels. In the central reservoir, waves sometimes stand vertically, bright sheets trembling several meters above the waterline.

The machines responsible for the range are industrial in scale but nearly silent. Great fans revolve inside circular housings as wide as city blocks, turning so slowly that their movement is noticed only by watching the shadows of their blades. Pistons emerge from cliff faces over the course of days. Freight platforms climb inclined rails at walking pace, carrying sealed ingots from one unidentified plant to another. Electrical substations glow with blue corona, yet produce only a faint dryness on the tongue.

There are no roaring furnaces and no pounding presses. Sound is absorbed by the mountain’s porous cladding, by soft insulation packed behind every wall, and perhaps by something more fundamental. A dropped tool may strike the floor without making a noise until several minutes later, when its clang arrives from a different valley. Echoes do not return toward their source. They migrate downhill in flocks, fading among the condenser fins.

The dominant sounds are small: condensed water ticking into trays, wing cases clicking against handrails, the papery rasp of lichen against painted metal. At long intervals, a pressure door opens somewhere under the range. The resulting exhalation is felt in the chest rather than heard.

Vegetation is sparse and opportunistic. Rust moss grows in orange-black mats where iron-rich moisture seeps from joints. Thin blue grasses root in fabric insulation exposed by fractures. Their blades lie flat during daylight and rise at night to catch condensation. Pale reeds occupy the membrane ponds, though their stems are segmented like cable conduit and may not be plants in the ordinary sense.

There are no woods. The closest equivalent is found on the eastern slope, where thousands of slender exhaust stacks have been colonized by a climbing organism with translucent leaves. It spans gaps using filaments of hardened sap, linking stacks into a loose canopy. In bright weather the leaves focus sunlight into drifting points on the ground. Small fires sometimes begin beneath them, but burn only the accumulated dust.

Animal life is unexpectedly abundant. The constructed terrain offers innumerable ledges, cavities, warm housings, wet sumps, and protected cable runs. Colonies of white cliff swifts nest in ventilation slots, entering and leaving in disciplined spirals. Six-legged rail hares graze on sealant and mineral films. They are narrow enough to pass between grating bars and possess magnetic pads that let them run across walls and ceilings without changing pace.

Reservoir moths, broad-winged and almost transparent, spend their larval lives in coolant pools. When they emerge, they gather on sunlit towers in such numbers that entire facades seem to breathe. Small silver fish inhabit the aqueduct, including sections in which the water flows vertically. Their bodies remain aligned with the channel rather than with gravity.

The largest animals are the gantry walkers. They resemble long-legged ungulates assembled from dark cartilage and cream-colored shell. Herds move across the high service decks, placing their feet only on painted safety lines. They never step outside the markings, even when a line ends at a collapsed bridge. There they wait, sometimes for years, until maintenance drones repaint a route. No one knows whether the drones serve the animals or whether the animals have mistaken an industrial instruction for a law of nature.

That confusion is common here because instructions have physical authority.

Warning stripes repel certain birds. Arrows painted on floors generate a subtle directional pull. A door marked **KEEP CLOSED** becomes almost impossible to open, even when its hinges have been removed. Numbered components tend to rearrange themselves into sequence during periods of fog. Where labels have peeled away, local reality loosens. Rooms become deeper than their exterior dimensions. Pipes connect to themselves. A ladder may gain one rung each time it is climbed.

At the highest saddle stands a district of unfinished towers. Their frames are packed close together, a dense lattice extending across the pass, but they contain no floors. Inside them, altitude behaves inconsistently. One can ascend three flights and emerge lower than before, or cross a horizontal bridge into air thin enough to frost the lungs. Clouds collect in cubical masses between the beams. Birds perch on their vertical sides.

This is believed to be the youngest part of the range. The metal remains unmarred, the bolts bright, the painted codes legible. Yet some towers contain fossils: perfect impressions of gears, gloves, feathers, and unknown hands embedded in structural glass. They are not remnants of an older landscape. They seem to be impressions of future objects, pressed backward through the material.

Change proceeds slowly. The range does not bustle despite its density. Herds take weeks to cross a plateau. Reservoirs fill one bead at a time. A turbine rotates through a full turn each season. Maintenance machines repair fractures with patient, almost geological attention, laying down layers of weld as thin as mineral deposits.

Over decades, pipes bend toward moisture. Towers lean into prevailing light. Entire factories migrate by the incremental extension of one foundation and retraction of another. The central valley is narrowing, not through tectonic pressure but because the opposing walls add one new layer of cladding every year.

At noon the brightness becomes immense. The range loses its shadows and appears less like a collection of objects than a single articulated surface, folded and refolded beneath the sun. Animals shelter in vents. Condensation retreats into hidden seams. The machinery pauses at nearly, but never exactly, the same moment.

In that suspended quiet, the made mountains seem newly arrived—sharp, crowded, and unfinished, still deciding what kind of world they have constructed around themselves.
