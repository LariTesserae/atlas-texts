The cave is too broad to feel enclosed.

Its ceiling hangs in darkness beyond the useful reach of sight, supported here and there by blunt pillars of limestone. Between them lies an underground expanse several valleys wide: terraces of pale rock, fields of shattered slabs, long shelves descending toward a buried river. There are no walls in the ordinary sense, only distances that eventually become black. A person standing at the center can turn in a full circle and see no clear boundary—only low ridges, scattered columns, and dim mineral horizons.

The stone is young in its exposure. Much of it has only recently been uncovered by the retreat of deeper water, and its shapes retain an unfinished sharpness. Fresh cracks run white through gray limestone. Fallen plates lie with corners intact. Channels cut into the floor have steep lips rather than rounded banks. Even the stalactites are sparse and short, no more than glassy teeth along fractures in the ceiling. The cave has not had enough wet centuries to soften itself.

Yet it no longer appears to be changing.

The underground river has settled into a narrow course at the lowest level. It is dark, cool, and almost without turbulence, slipping beneath shelves and emerging again several kilometers away. Shallow basins remain across the higher ground where the old riverbed dips. Some hold clear water all year; others are merely damp bowls ringed with condensation. Rain from the surface reaches the chamber intermittently through fissures, announcing itself as widely separated drops. Each falls from a great height and strikes the stone with an enlarged, bell-like sound.

There is moisture everywhere, but little abundance. The rock feels cold beneath the hand. A film of water clouds smooth surfaces, and mist sometimes gathers over the river, though there are no lush banks and no curtains of moss. Most of the floor is bare mineral: chalk-gray limestone, black seams of flint, rusty stains around iron deposits, and isolated patches of brown silt left in hollows.

Vegetation survives only where the surface intrudes.

Far apart across the cavern roof are a few collapse shafts, narrow enough that daylight arrives weak and green. Beneath each opening grows a small, sharply bounded island of life. Ferns spread close to the ground. Liverworts lacquer wet stones. Pale grasses lean toward the shaft, and the roots of trees descend from above like ropes, entering the cave without ever revealing the trunks that sustain them. Beyond these pools of dim light, green dwindles quickly. Moss becomes a thin dusting, then vanishes.

Fungi take its place, though even they are scattered. Small white fans emerge from buried roots. Pin-sized caps rise from guano and sink again into it. There are no forests of mushrooms, only temporary colonies feeding on whatever has fallen or been carried below.

The animals are the true substance of the place.

The ceiling is dark not solely because it is high, but because much of it is covered in bats. They hang in dense, living sheets along the warmer vaults: hundreds of thousands in one chamber, millions across the whole buried region. Their bodies turn the stone brown-black. At rest they rustle continuously, claws shifting, wings folding and unfolding, pups calling to mothers. When disturbed, the colony does not erupt all at once. Movement begins as a tremor on one patch of ceiling and travels outward until the darkness itself seems to peel loose.

Their evening departure fills the largest shafts for hours. Streams of bats spiral upward so thickly that the faint surface light flickers through their wings. Some fly out to hunt over marshes and river woods above. Others remain underground, crossing from roost to roost in the dark.

The cave’s apparent emptiness is therefore deceptive. Every bare reach lies beneath a traffic of wings.

On the ground, cave crickets gather in the cracks by the thousands. Their long legs make them seem larger than they are. Beetles move through guano beds, along with mites, springtails, pale larvae, and small brown moths whose scales have dulled over generations. Blind isopods inhabit the dampest hollows. In the river are translucent shrimp, flatworms, and thin white fish with reduced eyes. At the edges of the light shafts, frogs shelter in crevices and feed on insects drawn below. Snakes occasionally descend after them and remain for seasons at a time.

Predators are sparse in number but seldom absent. Owls wait on ledges near openings. Cave salamanders cling to wet walls. Foxes sometimes enter by sloping passages, following the smell of fallen bats, though they rarely venture into the deepest chambers. Their tracks cross the silt and disappear on stone.

The noise never stops. It is not the noise of weather or machinery but of accumulated life: the fine, constant abrasion of claws; shrieks beyond human hearing mingled with those below it; cricket wings; frog calls; splashes from the river; the sudden leathery rush of a low-flying bat. In the principal roosts, conversation requires shouting. Even in the open reaches, sound travels so far that distant colonies become a continuous surf. Echoes overlap until no single source can be located.

Silence exists only in small pockets behind thick ridges of rock, and even there one can usually hear water ticking somewhere inside the limestone.

The air remains cool and nearly uniform. There is no true day or night in the interior, no season except a slight change in the number of bats entering and leaving. Temperature, humidity, and river level hold within narrow bounds. The pools do not visibly rise or shrink. The same condensation gathers on the same ledges. Guano accumulates, is consumed, and becomes soil at roughly the rate new droppings fall. Dead animals are dismantled rapidly by insects and fungi. Nothing lasts long enough to alter the overall balance.

This constancy preserves the cave’s fresh appearance. Although its exposed floor is geologically young, erosion has nearly paused. Water no longer floods the terraces. Wind does not enter strongly enough to carry grit. Sharp fragments remain where they fell, their edges blunted only by the occasional passage of feet.

There are no structures. No stairs descend from the shafts, no walls divide the terraces, and no bridges cross the river. The vast floor contains no permanent camp, shrine, road, fence, or marked boundary. It offers little fuel and no dependable crop, and the density of animals makes long occupation difficult.

People do enter, but rarely.

Those who know the upper country approach with resinous torches, twisted fiber cord, hide water bags, and chipped stone blades. Their tools are simple because little else is needed—and because anything more elaborate is difficult to carry through the crawlways. They come to collect shed feathers near owl ledges, mineral pigment from the iron-stained walls, or a limited quantity of bat guano for surface gardens. Some hunt the pale river fish with bone-tipped spears. Most remain near the entrances.

They leave almost nothing behind. A torch is burned to ash and dispersed. A broken flint chip is indistinguishable from the natural seams underfoot. Tracks vanish where stone replaces mud. The cave does not become theirs merely because they can enter it.

Its governing fact is equilibrium. There are countless bodies and almost no landmarks of possession. Life is overwhelming, but spread through such an enormous volume that the landscape still looks open. Moisture is always present, but never enough to green the stone. The cavern is newly revealed, yet appears fixed outside time. Every night—or what passes for night below—the bats depart, feed, return, and settle into the same black vaults. The river goes on beneath them, carrying almost nothing, making almost no progress.

From a distant terrace, the place resembles a cool, dim plain under a starless sky. The scattered pools reflect no stars. The pale ridges do not shift. Only the living ceiling moves, and even that movement is part of an order so old and repetitive that it feels less like change than weather held permanently in one position.
