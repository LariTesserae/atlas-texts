## The Highwater Terraces

The city stands on the summit of a mountain that has not finished becoming a mountain.

Far below, beyond the reach of any rope or weighted sounding line, the mountain descends into a warm, black ocean. Above, it rises in broad terraces toward a surface no one living has seen. The uppermost towers are still submerged by several hundred body-lengths. There is no shore. There are only degrees of depth: the heavier dark below, the dim green middle, and overhead a remote paling that may be daylight or merely a different color of water.

The summit is high only in relation to the abyss around it. From its outer walls one can look across leagues of open water and see neighboring ridges appearing and disappearing in blue murk. These ridges resemble islands drowned before they were born. Their crests support gardens, shrines, abandoned watch platforms, and forests of tethered weed. Between them lie valleys of water rather than air, traversed by suspension bridges whose decks hang vertically, sideways, or upside down according to the currents they were built to catch.

The water is warm, almost skin-warm, and very still.

It does not behave entirely like ordinary seawater. It has layers. Some are clear and saline; others are faintly clouded by mineral dust, pollen, or drifting colonies of minute plants. A person swimming upward may pass through an invisible boundary and suddenly become lighter, colder, or drowsy. Loose objects settle at different heights according to the wood, stone, shell, air, or oil enclosed within them. Thus the city possesses streets at many orientations. Heavy avenues follow the mountain. Buoyant lanes pass between towers. Certain public squares are simply volumes of water marked by ropes and carved floats, with no floor at all.

The mountain’s rock is raw. It has not endured enough time to soften.

Its cliffs are sharp-backed and dark, with folds like cloth arrested while being poured. Columns of black glass emerge from pale mineral crusts. Great convex blisters bulge from the slopes, hollow inside and warm to the touch. Cracks are clean, ledges angular, scree uncommon. There are few beaches of gravel and almost no sand. Where sediment gathers, it lies in thin, undisturbed blankets that preserve every finger mark, dropped seed, and trailing cord.

The sea has not yet learned how to wear the mountain down.

Currents here are weak. Storms on the unseen surface arrive only as distant changes in pressure, a tightening in the ears and a slow bending of the tallest plants. Consequently the place retains an embryonic quality. New rock rises faster than old rock erodes. Over years, a terrace may lift by the width of a hand. Doorways warp. Foundations split. Staircases lose alignment with the neighboring building and must be extended by ladders of bonewood. The city is continually repaired, but never hurriedly. A mason may begin compensating for a geological tilt knowing that her grandchildren will set the final wedge.

The lowest quarters are built directly into the young basalt. Rooms have been chipped from warm bubbles in the stone. Passageways exploit cooling cracks, widened with hammerstones and wedges of hardened shell. Higher districts consist of dense frameworks of timber, root, rope, and pale blocks cut from mineral deposits around the vents.

There is no mortar in the usual sense. Walls are held together by compression, dovetail joints, braided fiber, and living roots trained through drilled holes. Buildings are allowed to flex. A rigid house would soon fracture or be pried apart by its own buoyancy.

The roofs are not roofs against rain. They are broad vanes that control lift and catch falling debris from above. Some are planted thickly. Others carry bladders of trapped gas, giving upper stories partial support. Towers taper downward rather than upward, their widest chambers floating above narrow stone anchors. From a distance the city resembles a forest suspended from the summit: dark foundations below, pale trunks in the middle, spreading inhabited crowns overhead.

Its density is deceptive. There are doors, courtyards, storehouses, workshops, gardens, cisterns of freshened water, and narrow processional ways everywhere, but they do not lie on one plane. A traveler may pass beneath a market, through the roots of a public orchard, and along the outer wall of a sleeping chamber without recognizing any of them. Addresses are given by depth, warmth, and anchor: *three spans above the Split Vent; coldward of the red garden; beneath the fifth bell.*

Light comes reluctantly.

By day, a diluted gray-green radiance descends from far overhead. It is strongest around the mountain’s crown, though never bright enough to cast a clear shadow. The deeper terraces are illuminated by bowls of cultivated glow-slime, translucent seedpods, and the dull red fissures of the mountain. Polished shell serves as mirror and lens. In wealthy houses, sheets of clear mica are angled through shafts to guide a little upper light into interior rooms.

At night, if night is truly what causes the dimming, the city nearly disappears. Only its gardens remain visible: threads of blue along handrails, amber knots beneath balconies, red-veined leaves turning slowly toward warmth. The effect is not that of lamps shining in darkness, but of a drowned constellation maintaining its shape.

Vegetation occupies every available surface.

Forests of ribbon-leaf grow from the outer cliffs, their copper and olive blades many times the length of a person. They move so slowly in the weak currents that their bending can be mistaken for growth. Inside the city, fruiting vines are stretched along rope lattices. Their fruits are soft globes full of breathable gas and sour pulp. Small ones are eaten. Large ones are tied under cargo sledges or incorporated into buildings.

There are branching plants with chalky stems, velvet mosses that feed on mineral warmth, and flat black leaves adapted to gather the faintest light. Root curtains filter silt from the water and define private rooms better than walls do. Gardeners cultivate different depths as farmers elsewhere cultivate different soils. A crop that thrives twenty spans higher may sicken if lowered across a heavy-water layer.

Most plants grow with immense patience. New leaves unroll over weeks. Flowers remain open for a season. Seed clouds drift through streets in pale, silent migrations and settle upon carved lintels, hair, tools, and sleeping mats. Sweeping is less useful than pruning. Anything left unattended becomes green, then rooted, then structurally important.

Animals are rare.

One may go several days without seeing anything larger than a thumbnail grazer moving across a leaf. There are white thread-worms near the hot cracks, glass-shelled mites in the gardens, and blind, slow fish that appear singly and are treated as omens. The largest known creatures are long-bodied filter feeders called veils. They pass over the city perhaps twice in a lifetime, dimming whole districts beneath their translucent wings. No one hunts them.

Their scarcity gives every animal encounter unusual weight. A small silver fish entering a house may cause work to stop while the household watches it. Certain civic disputes have been postponed because a spiral crab chose to rest on the speaking stone. People believe animals retain memories from the ocean’s deeper and older parts, places where the mountain has not yet reached.

The citizens themselves move carefully and seldom swim without purpose. Their bodies are adapted to the pressure and warmth, though they still need the cultivated breathing gardens that enrich the water of enclosed quarters. They wear woven belts rather than full garments, with stones, bladders, knives, and tools attached in deliberate balance. A poorly arranged tool belt can turn its owner head-down in a public lane.

Metal is nearly unknown. What little arrives is found as greenish nodules in cracks or recovered from ancient objects that descend from above. It is too precious for ordinary blades. Most work is done with chipped volcanic glass, toothed shell, stone adzes, bone needles, and cords made from split leaf fiber.

Their technology is simple but not crude. They understand leverage, pressure, buoyancy, heat, and the behavior of layered water with great precision. Cargo is moved by adding or piercing gas-fruit. Stone is quarried by driving dry wooden pegs into cracks and allowing them to swell. Food is cooked in sealed pots lowered toward thermal vents. Ceramics are fired inside rare gas pockets in the mountain, where flame can live briefly. Messages are carried along taut cords by patterns of plucks, though the range is limited and the meaning easily muddled by growing plants.

The city is mostly quiet because sound travels too well.

Speech is close and restrained. Workshops wrap hammerstones in fiber. Public gatherings use hand signs, color panels, and touch conveyed along ropes. A dropped tool can ring through the foundations into districts far below, awakening sleepers and startling gardeners. Loud noise is considered both rude and dangerous: vibrations can release mineral crusts, confuse the buoyancy herds of seedpods, or draw attention from the abyss.

What sound there is becomes part of the architecture.

Ropes hum faintly. Leaves brush stone. Gas clicks within ripening fruit. Somewhere, almost always, a mason taps once, waits for the mountain’s answer, then taps again. Thermal cracks murmur in low pulses, felt in the ribs more than heard. At long intervals the entire summit gives a deep internal groan. Dust rises from the streets. Root-bound buildings lean together. People stop where they are and place a hand against the nearest wall.

The mountain is growing.

This is the first rule of the place: nothing fixed stays aligned.

The second is that descent is easier than return. The water becomes denser below the city, and past a certain depth buoyant objects cease to rise. Weighted explorers have vanished into those layers without sinking quickly, simply hanging farther and farther away until dimness erased them.

The third is that empty spaces do not remain empty. A cleared courtyard fills with seedlings. An abandoned room becomes a filtration grove. An unused shaft gathers warm water and develops its own pale ecology. Ruins are not dead here; they are reassigned by growth.

The fourth is that the water overhead is not trusted. Occasionally objects fall from it: wood unlike any local tree, pumice, dead leaves, feathers, worked stones, once an entire roof of red tile. Such things descend slowly, turning for days. The citizens gather on the upper terraces to watch them arrive. They debate whether another world exists beyond the water, or whether the ocean dreams objects and lets them fall.

At the city’s crown stands an unfinished platform of black stone. It is the highest construction, though each generation must raise it as the mountain lifts beneath the lower districts. No one knows its original purpose. Some call it a harbor for the surface. Others say it is the foundation of a temple that can only be completed when the summit enters air.

For now it supports a garden.

Broad leaves lie across its steps. Gas-fruit tug at their tethers. Fine roots probe new fissures in the rock. Above them, the warm, dim water extends without visible boundary, filled with suspended grains that glimmer and vanish.

Everything waits, but nothing is inert. The mountain rises by increments too small to witness. The city bends around it. Forests climb the walls. Streets drift a little from their markers. Generations labor toward a shore that may not exist.

And through it all, the young stone remains sharp beneath the hand: unworn, warm, and still arriving.
