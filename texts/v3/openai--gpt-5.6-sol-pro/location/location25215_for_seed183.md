The country begins above the weather.

It occupies a high, broad shelf between ranks of pale mountains, though “between” is unreliable here. Some peaks stand beyond the shelf in the ordinary manner; others appear inside it, visible through fractures in the ground as if the stone were a window overlooking another range. The elevation is extreme enough that distance has lost its haze. Every edge seems close and sharply cut, even when it lies many days away.

Light pours over everything. There is no obvious sun. The entire upper sky shines with a cold, uniform brilliance, white near the zenith and faintly blue along the horizon. Shadows collect beneath ledges but do not consistently point in one direction. At times a standing stone will cast three: one dark, one translucent, and one that shows the object as it looked before erosion wore it down.

The shelf is crowded with stone.

Needles of chalk-colored rock rise nearly shoulder to shoulder. Between them sit basalt fins, shelves of gray salt, heaps of rounded black cobbles, and pillars composed of thousands of thin mineral laminations. Many formations are perforated. Their holes align at certain hours, making long corridors through the landscape that exist only when viewed from exactly the right place. The density is almost architectural, yet no pattern remains stable long enough to become a city. There are plazas that narrow into cracks, passages that open onto precipices, and amphitheaters no wider from outside than a clenched hand.

Water is everywhere, but rarely in abundance.

It beads on stone beneath the bright sky. It seeps upward through seams, gathers under overhangs, and runs in silver threads along vertical faces. The air is continually condensing. Droplets form around empty points, hanging without support until they become heavy enough to fall sideways. Shallow pools occupy the hollows between mineral towers. Their surfaces are perfectly level in different directions.

Some pools reflect the sky. Others reflect adjacent pools. A few show the underside of the shelf: immense roots of stone hanging into a bright vacancy, each dripping into an unseen depth.

Rain passes through several times a day. It does not descend from clouds; no clouds are visible. First comes a cool pressure in the ears, then a rapid clicking as droplets appear against every exposed surface at once. For several minutes the whole country shines as though varnished. Water rushes through established channels, rattling pebbles, filling basins, and overflowing in clean curtains from the high rims. Then the rain ends simultaneously across the visible region. The channels continue to speak for hours.

There is no plant life. No moss softens the wet stone, no lichen stains it, no grass holds the sediment. Nothing flies in the lucid air, crawls under slabs, or disturbs the pools. The landscape has no tracks except those made by falling rock, flowing water, and the few makers who once crossed it.

Yet the place does not feel empty. Mineral forms occupy every scale. The rims of the pools are furred with hair-thin crystals. Wet caves contain translucent bladders of silica that swell as they take in water and collapse with a sigh when the air dries. Veins of salt inch outward from fractures over years, branching into shapes that resemble frost, coral, writing, or nerves. They are not alive, although they sometimes withdraw from touch.

Across exposed flats lie dense fields of small upright objects: flakes of slate balanced on edge, each facing a different direction. Wind should topple them, but wind is almost absent. Instead they turn slowly during rainfall, scraping their bases in circles. Their combined movement produces a continuous harsh whisper, like countless knives being sharpened far away.

Other sounds are larger.

Waterfalls thunder from cliffs whose summits cannot be seen, though the falling water vanishes before reaching the ground. Cavities in the mountains breathe at irregular intervals. The exhalations are deep enough to be felt through the feet. Stone columns ring when the temperature changes, one after another, until whole valleys resound like struck metal. Sometimes an avalanche can be heard for an hour before the slope begins to move. Sometimes it continues long after the falling rock has settled.

Echoes do not merely repeat sound. They alter it.

A handclap returns as hammering. A shouted word comes back in several unfamiliar voices. The crack of a splitting slab may echo as the roar of the ancient river that carved the gorge around it. In narrow places, footsteps are answered by footsteps at another pace. There are no animals or hidden travelers. If followed, the answering steps lead only to a wall, usually one wet with condensation.

Erosion here is old, practiced, and nearly balanced. The mountains have not been newly broken, nor have they softened into hills. Water has worked every surface into a settled complexity: scallops, flutes, runnels, basins nested inside basins. The pale limestone is worn as smooth as handled bone. Dark ridges of harder material remain where softer beds have vanished. Loose fragments migrate slowly through the channels and collect in crescent-shaped shoals, only to be sorted again by the next rain.

But erosion does not always reduce.

In certain ravines, water carries absences instead of sediment. Wherever it flows, old cracks close and missing corners return. Pebbles grow angular, then attach themselves to cliffs in positions from which they may have fallen centuries before. Anyone placing a chipped tool in such a stream might find it restored to an unworked stone—or restored beyond that, into a piece of the mountain from which it was quarried. These upward-cutting waters are known by their silence. They make no sound even when plunging over a drop.

Scattered through the shelf are small constructions of fitted stone. Most are low shelters pressed beneath overhangs, built from flat slabs without mortar. Their entrances are narrow, perhaps to resist the pressure-waves that cross the plateau. Inside, raised sleeping ledges remain dry above shallow drainage trenches. There are chipped bowls, stone mallets, bone-shaped tools made of mineral, coils of braided wire hammered from native copper, and lenses polished from clear quartz. No remains identify the builders.

Cairns mark the safer routes, though “safe” has changed meaning since they were raised. Each cairn consists of seven stones: six ordinary pieces and one that cannot be viewed directly. Look away and the seventh is obvious at the edge of sight. Look toward it and the stack contains only six.

Near the center of the shelf stands the largest work: a ring of squat pillars surrounding a circular reservoir. The stones are heavily weathered and carved with grooves that channel rain inward. Handprints have been pecked into their faces, thousands of them, all with four fingers and all varying wildly in size. Copper cups hang from short stone pegs. They have turned green despite the absence of any vegetation; from a distance, this mineral stain is the only color that could be mistaken for life.

The reservoir never fills. Water entering it becomes depth without volume. A dropped pebble can be heard striking walls at descending intervals—first near, then far below, then overhead, then behind the listener’s eyes. At noon-brightness, when the sky reaches its most intense white, the basin casts an upright reflection: a wavering column of dark water rising into the air. It may reach several hundred meters before breaking into rain.

Travel is governed by practical rules.

Do not cross a pool in which your reflection arrives late.

Do not shelter beneath stone that feels warm; the air is cool, and warmth means the rock is remembering burial.

When the slate fields turn all at once toward the same point, lie flat. A direction is about to pass over the landscape.

Never count mountain peaks aloud. The total becomes binding.

If thunder comes from below, keep one hand against an eroded surface until it ends. Old stone knows which side is outside.

These rules are scratched into shelter walls in simple pictographs: hands, bowls, falling lines, closed eyes, split mountains. Some warnings have been recut many times. Others have weathered almost beyond recognition, leaving shallow marks that fill with water and briefly become legible after rain.

Toward the shelf’s outer edge, the terrain opens. The thickets of stone thin into long terraces descending toward a radiant gulf. Here the wet rock reflects so much light that the ground seems made of white fire. Streams race along the terraces and leap into the void, but their waterfalls bend outward, then upward, joining the bright sky as vertical rivers.

Beyond them stand distant ranges, densely folded and dazzlingly clear. One appears inverted. Another has a horizon running through its middle. The farthest mountains repeat at smaller scales, range behind range, until they resemble the serrations along a crystal’s edge.

The temperature falls toward evening, though the sky does not darken. Films of water glaze the stones. Frost forms beneath the liquid rather than above it, making pools crack from their depths. The fractures spread with explosive reports. Thin plates of ice rise vertically and drift through the passages, chiming against the mineral pillars before dissolving into dry light.

There is never night. Instead, once in each cycle, brightness loses its distance. The sky comes down among the rocks. Far objects fade while nearby droplets blaze like small moons. Sound swells: ringing stone, running water, grinding slate, the subterranean breath, all layered until silence itself seems impossible.

Then the brightness retreats. The shelf reappears piece by piece, wet and sharpened, every ancient channel running.

Nothing wakes because nothing sleeps. Nothing grows, hunts, nests, or decays. Yet the high country is ceaselessly occupied by change: water assembling and disassembling, frost lifting from pools, stone returning to cliffs, echoes searching for causes, distances folding shut and opening elsewhere.

It is a lifeless place only in the narrowest sense. The world here has taken over the work usually done by living things.
