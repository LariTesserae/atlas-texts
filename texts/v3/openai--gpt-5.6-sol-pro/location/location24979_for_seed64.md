### The Green Throat

The entrance is easy to mistake for a shadow.

It lies close beneath the summit of a black, wind-planed massif, where the atmosphere is thin enough that distance loses its color and the surrounding peaks appear cut from dull metal. The opening is no broader than a doorway. It descends almost vertically, though not as a clean shaft: its walls fold inward and apart in successive chambers, ledges, blind niches, and narrow chimneys. A person can stand at the bottom of any one chamber and touch both walls, yet the whole hidden cleft drops through nearly a kilometer of mountain.

It is an extreme landscape compressed to the dimensions of a room.

Outside, there is gravel, salt, and cold radiance. Inside, everything is green.

Not the green of wet leaves. There is no water here—not in pools, vapor, frost, sap, or chemically bound reserves. The stone itself has been stripped of hydrated minerals. Anything carrying moisture across the threshold acquires a pale efflorescence; its water is separated from it, broken apart by the cleft’s mineral surfaces, and taken into reactions too slow to see. Hydrogen migrates downward through the conductive rock. Oxygen joins the crusts along the walls. Nothing remains that could be called damp.

Yet the Throat is lush.

Its growths occupy every available plane. They layer over one another in scales, fans, blades, ribs, and heavy curtains. Some resemble ferns hammered from green copper. Some rise as tight black-green columns, each faceted like a bundle of needles. Others make broad translucent leaves no thicker than mica, overlapping by the thousands until the walls seem feathered. In the deepest pockets, pale filaments pack together so densely that they have the appearance of fur, although they neither bend nor stir.

These are not plants in the ordinary watery sense. Their tissues are lattices of carbon, silicon, sulfur, and metallic salts. Instead of sap, charge passes through them. Instead of swelling cells, they extend themselves by crystal deposition, atom after atom along electrically favored edges. Their roots are conductive webs pressed into seams of pyrite and graphite. The dim atmospheric glow above, the mountain’s static potential, and faint currents from buried machinery are sufficient to sustain them.

They do not breathe. They do not transpire. Nothing opens, closes, unfolds, or turns toward the light.

The Throat’s forest has reached its final arrangement.

Every blade fits among its neighbors. Every conductive root has found a seam. Every shadow holds a species suited precisely to that intensity of darkness. There is no bare soil. There is hardly even exposed stone. Where a ledge might offer a handhold, it is crowded with brittle rosettes, mineral mosses, and nested cups whose rims are sharp enough to score glass. Where one growth failed centuries ago, another did not replace it; rather, its dead lattice became the substrate of the next layer, and that layer has long since become indistinguishable from the living structure above it.

The effect is one of tremendous abundance without fertility. Nothing sprouts. Nothing decays. The ecology is complete and motionless.

The temperature remains cool throughout, just above the point at which the green lattices lose conductivity. There are no warmer vents, no colder drafts, no daily cycle. The mountain’s mass holds the cleft at one unwavering condition. A hand placed against the wall finds the same chill at the entrance, in the middle galleries, and far below. Even the old machines give off no perceptible heat.

They are scattered through the vegetation.

At first they appear to be unusually geometric outcrops: a riveted corner, a ceramic cylinder, a length of black conduit disappearing under green plates. Deeper down, their industrial origin becomes clearer. There are transformer housings fitted into alcoves. Narrow-gauge rails cross ledges and end at sheer drops. Bundles of insulated cable descend through the chambers like artificial roots. A row of switch cabinets stands along one wall, doors shut, indicator windows glowing a weak amber.

There is no single factory floor. The mountain seems to have been used as a vertical machine, with equipment installed wherever the geology permitted. A compressor occupies one chamber almost completely, leaving only a shoulder-wide passage between its casing and the crowded growth on the opposite wall. Farther down, six iron drums are bolted to a sloping shelf. Their pipes enter the rock. Below them hangs a gantry with no accessible platform, its hook fixed over an empty shaft.

No components move. Flywheels remain in precisely the same orientation. Belts are taut but stationary. Valves cannot be turned. Dust does not settle because there is no loose dust left to settle; every particle has long ago attached itself to a charged surface or become incorporated into the green mineral tissue.

Nevertheless, the Throat is loud.

Its sound is immediate and enormous: a continuous industrial chord made of transformer hum, low compression, metallic ringing, and a high electric whine felt in the teeth. It does not increase near the machines or diminish away from them. Blocking the ears has little effect. The sound seems to exist equally at every point in the cleft, as if volume were a property of the space rather than a wave passing through it.

Nothing vibrates visibly. Loose threads remain still. The blade-growths do not tremble. The surfaces of the old indicator lamps show no flicker.

The prevailing explanation—if explanation is possible—is that the sound is a standing condition established when the machinery was first activated. In this place, a sufficiently exact resonance does not need to repeat itself through time. It occupies the stone all at once. The mountain holds one vast mechanical instant: pressure at every crest and trough, tension at every node, the entire noise permanently present without progressing from one moment to another.

Walking through it does not reveal a rhythm. There are no beats, fluctuations, or accidental knocks. The roar heard on entering is identical to the roar at the bottom. Recordings made years apart are not merely similar; their waveforms are indistinguishable down to the smallest measurable irregularity.

The cleft has weathered, but its weathering also belongs to this settled state. Its walls are rounded where ancient corrosive gases once moved through them. Channels spiral downward, polished smooth by a vanished flow that contained no water—perhaps liquid sulfur, perhaps an industrial solvent no longer present. Bolt heads have softened into hemispheres. Rail edges are worn into shallow gleaming curves. The green blades have accumulated layers of mineral varnish, giving the oldest groves a depth of color approaching black.

All this erosion is finished. No new grain falls. No edge becomes duller.

There is no fauna. No insects hide between the plates, no mites graze the mineral moss, no pale scavengers inhabit the conduits. The packed growth offers countless apparent shelters, but each is empty. There are no webs, droppings, eggs, husks, tooth marks, tunnels, or calls. Even microbial life, in the conventional sense, is absent. The green organisms have no parasites because their chemistry permits no soft invasion, and no pollinators because they have no flowers and no need to reproduce now that every useful surface is occupied.

Their relations are architectural rather than predatory. One kind conducts charge downward. Another spreads it laterally. Black cups collect trace carbon from the dry atmosphere and pass it into neighboring root-mats. Thin silver fronds shed excess electrons into machine casings. Tall emerald fins maintain the field that powers the cups. None could have filled the cleft alone, but together they form a closed solid-state ecology, less like a forest than like a circuit that has grown its own insulation.

It is difficult to decide whether the machinery supports the growth or the growth supports the machinery.

In some chambers, green roots have entered broken junction boxes and fused with the terminals in clean, deliberate bundles. Elsewhere, cables terminate inside trunks whose concentric rings are made of copper salts. One old control panel bears labels beneath a skin of translucent tissue. Most are unreadable, but three remain visible:

**LOAD**  
**FIELD**  
**HOLD**

All three meters point to their marked central values.

The lower end of the Throat is not a cavern but a final pocket barely large enough for one person. Its walls are entirely hidden beneath dark overlapping leaves. The railway, the cables, and several mineral root systems converge there upon a small cylindrical engine set upright in the stone. It has no door and no controls. Around its base is a band of ancient abrasion, as though the cylinder once rotated at great speed; now it is perfectly still.

Above it, suspended in the packed foliage, hangs a single white lamp.

The lamp is not bright enough to illuminate the pocket fully. It reveals only edges: green glass, old iron, salt-white seams, the curved top of the engine. The shadows between these things remain absolute. Yet the lamp never dims, and nothing grows across it. The nearest leaves stop a finger’s width from the bulb, forming a precise empty sphere in the otherwise saturated chamber.

This is the only unoccupied space in the entire cleft.

The noise there is no louder, but it becomes more comprehensible. The industrial chord separates into parts without changing: one tone belonging to the stone, one to the metal, one to the green conductive forest, and beneath them a depthless note that seems to belong to altitude itself—the pressure of the peak above, the immense drop below, the whole mountain held between two electrical conditions.

Nothing is waiting to happen.

The engine will not start, because in some sense it has never ceased. The forest will not spread, because it has arrived everywhere it can exist. The old rails will not rust further. The lamp will not burn out. The dry air will not circulate. Even the sound does not travel.

The Green Throat is not dead, abandoned, or dormant.

It is finished.
