The valley is built from rim to rim.

There is no exposed ground, though at first it appears otherwise. What looks like a dark hillside is a succession of steep roofs, retaining walls, planted terraces, settling tanks, rail beds, and old factory decks laid one above another until construction has acquired the mass and contour of geology. The highest ridges are faced in black brick and poured concrete. Their slopes descend through neighborhoods of workshops, reservoirs, greenhouses, turbine halls, and narrow service courts, finally meeting a braided channel at the valley floor. Even the channel is made: stone-lined, gated, divided by weirs, crossed and recrossed by pipes.

The place has existed long enough for its materials to become weather rather than architecture. Concrete is rounded at the corners. Steps have shallow centers from generations of boots. Iron rails are scaled orange and black, and runoff has drawn pale fans of mineral stain beneath every joint. Roof tiles carry moss several fingers deep. Walls bulge slightly under the pressure of saturated planting beds, then are held in place by bolts, bands, and buttresses added in different eras. Repair has not restored any original design. It has become the design.

The elevation keeps the air cool throughout most of the year. Clouds entering from the western pass descend until they touch the upper works. In wet weather, which is frequent, the topmost towers disappear and condensation gathers on every cable. Rain does not fall hard so much as arrive everywhere at once: beading on handrails, darkening brick, collecting beneath leaves, weeping from seams in old retaining walls. The air smells of wet stone, leaf mold, machine oil, and faintly of hot metal.

Water is always near the surface. It moves under grates, behind walls, through culverts and header pipes. It lies in narrow rooftop ponds planted with reeds, in cooling basins green with duckweed, and in the deep cisterns beneath public squares. Every few minutes some unseen valve releases, and a silver sheet pours down a stepped spillway before vanishing under another district. Nothing drains by a simple route. Water is caught, lifted, filtered, warmed, cooled, used, and caught again.

Vegetation occupies every available interval, but it is not wilderness. It was planted, trained, harvested, replanted, or allowed to spread within structures designed to receive it. Alder and willow grow from long concrete troughs along the canals. Ferns crowd shaded stairs. Ivy covers warehouse walls but stops at copper barriers. Orchards stand on factory roofs, their trees low and wind-pruned, arranged in grids between skylights and ventilator stacks. Rows of cabbage, sorrel, and dark-leaved chard fill terraces that might elsewhere be parking decks. Grapevines run on overhead pipe racks. Moss is cultivated on north-facing panels to hold rain and moderate temperature.

The result is lush without being soft. Leaves press against mesh, glass, brick, cable, and one another. Root systems fill engineered beds to capacity. In the oldest quarters, small trees have been pollarded so many times that their trunks resemble knotted hands gripping the masonry. Nothing has much room. Every wall carries conduits; every conduit supports lichen, vine, or condensation. Every ledge has a use, and where it has lost that use, another has taken its place.

From a high platform the valley seems almost motionless. Smoke is scarce, and the dim light flattens distance into tiers of blue-gray and green. Yet the landscape is in constant reconfiguration.

Freight platforms descend on cable inclines, carrying timber, compost, steel coils, crates of machine parts, and bales of cut vegetation. Gates open and close in the waterworks. Gantry cranes travel slowly along rooflines, lifting modular rooms from one block to another. Retaining crews dismantle a failing terrace while gardeners strip the soil from it; several levels below, the same soil is already being pumped as slurry into a new bed. A row of greenhouses is folded shut before a storm, each roof panel turning in sequence. Bridges retract to let a maintenance barge pass through the central canal. Entire facades hinge outward for repair.

The turbulence is logistical rather than spectacular. Things are always being diverted, replaced, shored, harvested, drained, raised, or lowered. A district may lose its footpaths for an afternoon because a water main has overfilled. A market is transferred overnight into an empty turbine hall. A slope that held workshops in spring becomes a series of reservoirs by autumn. Landslips do occur, but here a landslip means three saturated roof gardens sagging onto the tramway below, followed by days of pumps, jacks, scaffold, and argument.

The valley’s governing principle is that nothing may be idle if it can perform more than one task. A retaining wall is also a heat store. A chimney is also a rain collector and stair tower. Tram shelters support seedling racks. Cooling water feeds washhouses before entering reed beds. The broad roofs of assembly plants are pasture for small flocks during the dry weeks, though animals are uncommon and carefully managed. Space is too constrained, and vegetation too valuable, for much uncontrolled life.

Birds are rare. Occasionally a crow crosses high above the valley, conspicuous against the low cloud. Small bats live in the warm ducts near the lower mills, and pale moths gather around maintenance lamps, but there is little chorus at dawn. Fish exist in a few sealed water circuits and in the deep canal below the final weir. Most are never seen. Their presence is inferred from inspection records and brief ripples under the grilles.

Despite the machinery, the valley is mostly quiet. Its industry was built for proximity: electric drives, enclosed gears, low-speed turbines, belt systems hidden behind heavy walls. Moss, rain, foliage, and layered masonry absorb what sound remains. The largest motions occur with a restrained hydraulic sigh. Cable cars pass with a soft ticking at the joints. Pumps produce a pressure felt through the soles before it is heard. Far below, a train may enter a tunnel, leaving only a diminishing tremor in the handrails.

Human sounds carry farther than engines. A dropped spanner can ring across three terraces. Voices under an arcade become briefly public. At shift changes, doors open throughout a whole slope and release a thin accumulation of footsteps, greetings, bicycle bells, cart wheels, and crockery from canteens. Then the doors close again, and the underlying hush returns: dripping water, moving leaves, the scrape of a brush against stone.

There are no broad streets. Movement follows ramps, stairs, lift cages, towpaths, covered galleries, and inclined railways. Routes change often. A passage marked on a sign may now end at a construction hoist or a temporary pond. Residents navigate less by address than by systems: above the third warm-water pipe, below the west orchard, two bridges past the dye works. Color bands painted on walls identify water quality, voltage, load limits, and evacuation directions. The oldest bands have faded into the masonry and are crossed by newer codes.

Light comes mainly from the sky, but the sky is usually a luminous gray ceiling. The valley walls and the density of construction keep the lower quarters in permanent afternoon. Windows are tall and narrow where buildings face open air, broad and ribbed where they borrow light from roof courts. Lamps are practical and subdued: amber over doors, white in workshops, green along safe drainage routes. Wet surfaces multiply these small lights without brightening the place. Reflections stretch in gutters and black paving, trembling whenever water passes below.

At night, the high works appear first: lines of maintenance lamps suspended in cloud, moving crane beacons, red points on towers. The valley floor remains almost entirely dark except where sluice gates shine under inspection lights. From above, the inhabited slopes do not resemble a city so much as an enormous control panel overgrown with wet leaves.

There is no clear boundary between town, factory, farm, and watershed. Each is inside the others. Families live above stamping rooms and below mushroom galleries. Schools occupy former warehouses whose basements still contain pumps. Public baths discharge heat into seed houses. The principal square is the roof of a filtration plant; one can feel its slow backwash cycle through the paving stones.

Nor is there a clear boundary between old and new. A polished electric motor turns a shaft installed a century earlier. Fresh composite braces grip a brick arch stained by ten thousand rains. Computer-controlled shutters regulate a greenhouse whose drainage channels were cut by hand. The valley does not replace its past. It loads more weight onto it, tests it, reinforces it, and continues.

Beyond the nearer districts, construction climbs into cloud and reappears on the opposite slopes. Reservoir walls show through stands of alder. Long glazed roofs catch a little silver light. Incline lines stitch the elevations together. In the far distance, where the valley bends, whole neighborhoods are reduced to dark, densely textured planes, restless with small movements that cannot be distinguished individually.

The place feels settled and unstable at once. Its forms are deeply weathered, balanced by long practice, yet their balance depends on continuous action. Pumps must run. Channels must remain clear. Roots must be cut away from expansion joints. Roof beds must be drained before they grow too heavy. Every apparent permanence is an agreement renewed by maintenance.

After rain, mist rises from the warmer lower works and moves uphill against the draining water. It passes through trees, scaffolds, suspended walkways, and pipe forests, erasing one level after another. For a while only nearby surfaces remain: a wall shining with runoff, fern fronds trembling under drops, a handrail cold with condensation. Somewhere behind them a gate changes position. Water takes a new route. A platform begins to descend.

The valley rearranges itself quietly in the dark.
