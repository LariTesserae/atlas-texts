The land is almost flat, but not simple.

From a distance it appears to be a warm, gray-green plain stretching beneath a broad sky. There are no trees to establish scale, no ridges or structures to interrupt the horizon. Only scattered clumps of rush, low fans of salt-tolerant grass, and occasional dark shrubs rise above the stone. Water lies everywhere without gathering into a single lake: in shallow sheets, narrow runnels, rain-filled hollows, and long, reflective seams between slabs of rock.

This is an old limestone surface, worn nearly to its final shape. Once it may have stood high, cut by rivers and weather, but immense spans of rain and warmth have lowered it grain by grain. Its remaining relief is subtle. Broad shelves slope by barely perceptible degrees. Their edges have been rounded until they resemble the backs of sleeping animals. Shallow basins occupy ancient weaknesses in the stone, joined by channels no wider than a root. In places the limestone has dissolved into a close texture of cups, grooves, and blunt ribs. None are sharp. Even the smallest hollows seem deeply aged.

Rain has just passed, though rain never feels entirely absent here. The air retains it. Moisture beads on pale rock and collects under overhangs only a hand’s breadth high. Warm vapor rises from surfaces already reheated by the sun. Far away, the plain loses definition in a silver haze where water, stone, and sky take on the same brightness.

The ground is divided into provinces according to differences of only a few inches.

On the slightly higher shelves, the rock is exposed: chalk-gray where dry, almost black where wet. Thin films of algae stain the drainage lines olive and rust. Small cushions of wiry grass occupy pockets of sediment, each clump isolated by several lengths of bare stone. Their roots hold dark soil made from windblown dust, insect remains, decayed leaves, and the slow residue of floods. Low shrubs appear only where a crack has widened enough to shelter them. They grow crooked and open, conserving leaves, their lower branches polished by seasonal water.

Below these shelves lie wide, shallow pans. Some are clear enough to reveal pale bottoms patterned with snail tracks. Others are clouded brown by fine mineral mud. A few have become marshes, though even there the vegetation remains sparse: stands of sedge separated by open water, floating leaves spread far apart, small reed islands rather than continuous beds. The emptiness between plants is not barren. It is occupied by light, warm water, submerged algae, larvae, tiny crustaceans, and the shadows of passing wings.

The lowest country is a chain of sluggish channels. They have no obvious banks and no urgent direction. Water enters them during rain and seeps through them afterward, moving toward a distant coastal basin or disappearing underground into the porous stone. Their currents are so slow that pollen remains in streaks upon the surface. Mats of foam collect around grass stems and may stay there for days. Only during the heaviest storms do the channels become recognizably riverlike, spreading across the plain in broad, shallow pulses before retreating again.

Nothing here changes quickly except the weather and the smallest lives.

The stone alters over eras. The marsh shifts by the width of a grass clump in a season. Mud accumulates in one hollow and is washed into the next. A pool may deepen by the gradual dissolving of its floor, not enough to notice within a lifetime. The plain accepts rainfall, holds it near the surface, and releases it reluctantly. Even under clear skies, water continues to appear: sweating from shaded rock, seeping from seams, condensing before dawn, trembling in the footprints of animals.

The warmth is steady rather than fierce. By midday the exposed shelves radiate heat, but the wet ground moderates it. Air rising from the pools smells of limestone, mud, algae, and the faint sulfurous breath of saturated soil. Clouds form easily. They are tall and white at first, then grayer toward evening, often giving another brief rain that passes across the plain in a visible curtain.

Vegetation never closes over the land. Flooding prevents roots from holding some places; thin soils prevent them in others. Between these pressures, plants survive in scattered arrangements. Pale grasses bend over black water. Small flowering herbs appear after rain, briefly dotting pockets of mud with yellow and violet. Creeping plants spread along cracks but seldom cross open stone. Lichens occupy the driest rises in powdery patches, so old and slow-growing that their boundaries seem part of the geology.

Animal life is far richer than the plant cover suggests.

Frogs inhabit nearly every basin. Some remain concealed in mud until rain softens the surface; then the plain fills with their calls. The sound carries without trees to obstruct it. Deep croaks rise from the larger pans, while smaller frogs pulse and click from shallow margins. Together they produce a continuous, uneven roar, as if the water itself were vibrating.

Insects add a higher layer. Cicadas rasp from the isolated shrubs during the warmest hours. Mosquitoes and midges gather above pools in wavering columns. Dragonflies patrol the channels, their wings flashing briefly in ordinary sunlight. Water beetles break the surfaces of still basins; unseen larvae stir the sediment below.

Birds are widely scattered but numerous. Long-legged waders move with deliberate steps through the open water, pausing to strike at fish or tadpoles. Small shorebirds travel in loose bands over the wet limestone, their calls sharp and repetitive. Broad-winged hunters circle in the warm rising air. At certain hours, flocks arrive from beyond the horizon and settle across the plain so gradually that the land seems to darken in patches.

There are reptiles on the warmer shelves: small lizards lying flat against the stone, snakes moving between marsh pools, and turtles half-submerged in the deeper channels. Fish persist wherever hollows remain connected long enough. When a basin shrinks, they crowd into its center beneath circling birds. When the rains return, they disperse again through passages so shallow that their backs sometimes break the surface.

The landscape is therefore loud despite its apparent stillness. Frog choruses, cicadas, wingbeats, bird alarms, and the soft agitation of feeding flocks travel for great distances. Wind pushes ripples across acres of shallow water with a papery hiss. Rain striking exposed limestone makes a dry clatter at first, then a softer drumming as every depression fills. Thunder can roll across the plain long after the storm itself has passed.

Yet beneath all this sound is patience. The animals respond quickly, but the place does not. Its fundamental movements are seepage, settling, dissolution, condensation, and the nearly horizontal passage of floods. There are no sudden cliffs to collapse, no young river cutting a new gorge, no forest advancing visibly from year to year. The land has already been reduced to a durable compromise between stone and water.

At sunset, its slight differences become more legible. The highest shelves remain pale. Wet channels turn copper, then violet. Each pool holds a separate fragment of sky, and the scattered grasses cast long shadows disproportionate to their size. Birds continue calling after their bodies become indistinct. Frogs take over as the air cools.

Night does not empty the plain. It redistributes it. Warm rock releases its stored heat. Moisture gathers on leaves and bare stone. Bats pass low above the pools, feeding where insects are thickest. Turtles move through channels with almost no sound. Predators follow the wet margins. The chorus rises and falls in broad waves, but the water itself remains nearly motionless.

By dawn, a low mist lies across the basins, not dense enough to conceal the horizon, only enough to soften it. The oldest rock rises through the vapor in broad, rounded backs. Dew joins seepage; seepage joins standing water; standing water waits for the next rain. Across the open plain, life is plentiful but never crowded, each presence separated by expanses of wet stone and shallow brightness.

There are no paths except those made repeatedly by hooves, claws, and flowing water. No boundary distinguishes marsh from pavement, land from pool, or present rain from water that has been traveling through the limestone for years. The governing rule is simply that everything descends toward flatness, and everything living gathers in the minute differences that remain.
