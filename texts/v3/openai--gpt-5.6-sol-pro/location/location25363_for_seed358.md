The land lies inside the land.

From the rim, it appears as a broad, sunken district: an excavation several valleys wide, its floor descending in immense shallow steps below the surrounding plain. Roads arrive at the edge and stop. Drainage ditches lean toward it. Villages keep back from the broken perimeter, their roofs barely visible through the warm haze. The basin is not old enough to have acquired the grace of a gorge. Its walls have no settled curves, no mature gullies or polished channels. They are raw cuts of clay, shale, chalk, and black mineral seams, stacked in damp bands as if the earth had only just been opened and had not yet decided how to weather.

Rain has passed recently, though in this climate it is difficult to say when rain truly ends. Water remains in every horizontal place. It lies on the haul roads in long pewter sheets, fills the impressions of vanished machines, and beads on cables and exposed roots. It seeps from the cut faces without drama, darkening them from within. In some places the walls perspire; in others they release steady threads that fall only a few handspans before joining another wet ledge. The air itself seems condensed, warm enough that nothing dries, dim enough that every surface holds its sheen.

There is no single lake. The floor is too crowded for that.

Instead there are hundreds of pools pressed among spoil ridges, collapsed benches, gravel fans, pipework, tilted slabs, and heaps of angular stone. Some pools are no wider than a room. Others run for kilometers behind low embankments of mining waste. Their levels differ by inches, and water passes between them through culverts, fractures, buried drains, and accidental channels still too new to have names. After each rain, the basin briefly becomes one body of water. Then the partitions reassert themselves.

The water is brown near the walls, green-black in the deeper sumps, and silver wherever the low sky reaches it. Thin films of iron gather in sheltered corners, iridescent but ordinary, breaking around the legs of wading birds. Pale mineral scum marks old waterlines. Here and there, methane lifts a chain of bubbles from drowned soil. Nothing in the basin is supernatural; it is only geology, weather, and industry crowded so tightly together that they seem to be performing one another’s work.

Vegetation is sparse. There are no forests and few true meadows. The exposed ground is too fresh, too unstable, too frequently drowned. Plants survive in narrow privileges: reed clumps at the quietest margins, mats of rushes on silted shelves, wiry grasses along abandoned service tracks. Coltsfoot and willowherb occupy the soft spoil beneath retaining walls. A handful of young willows stand with their lower bark stained by floodwater, all leaning in the same direction from some past collapse. On the acidic heaps even these disappear, leaving only orange lichens and a dusty crust of moss.

The lack of plants does not make the place empty. It makes every animal conspicuous.

Frogs occupy the warm shallows in such numbers that the water appears to tremble. Midges form dense columns above stagnant pockets. Dragonflies patrol the drainage cuts. Sand martins have perforated the soft clay faces with thousands of nest holes, each one a dark thumbprint in the ocher wall. Gulls and terns wheel above the larger ponds, while herons stand among broken pipes as if the pipes were reeds. Lapwings nest on gravel pads. Foxes use the dry conveyor galleries, and feral dogs travel the old roads in loose, wary groups. At dusk, bats pour from machinery sheds and cracks in the high wall. Fish have entered through flooded culverts and multiplied wherever the water remains deep through the hot months.

The basin is deafening.

The loudness does not come from speed. Almost nothing visible moves quickly. The sound is cumulative, packed layer upon layer until there is no quiet left between its sources. Pumps labor in distant sheds with a low rotational thunder. Water drops through steel grilles and hollow shafts. Overflow races through concrete channels, striking corrugated pipe with a continuous metallic roar. Frogs pulse from every flooded terrace. Birds scream beneath the overcast. Insects generate a hot electrical whine. Loose sheet metal knocks slowly in the warm wind. From the far side of the basin comes the intermittent clatter of a conveyor still in service, each impact arriving a moment after the last, too regular to notice and too large to ignore.

Conversation is possible only at close range. Workers use hand signals near the pump stations. Warning horns are pitched low enough to carry through all the higher noise. Sound travels unpredictably below the rim: a diesel engine several kilometers away may seem to idle just behind a spoil bank, while a nearby waterfall vanishes into the general pressure of the air.

The industrial structures are scattered, but each has command over a large territory. A pumping house stands on pilings above the central sump, its walls streaked with rust and mineral deposits. Farther north, an elevated conveyor crosses the flooded benches in a severe straight line, though several of its spans are gone. Concrete switching huts occupy islands of compacted gravel. There are survey posts, half-buried tires, electrical cabinets, stairways descending into water, and lamps mounted on poles much taller than the remaining buildings.

Some equipment still works. Most does not. It can be difficult to tell which is which until a motor starts.

At the eastern floor, a dragline excavator remains in operation. It is so large that distance reduces it to architecture: a dark boom, a suspended bucket, a housing with lit windows. Its cycle is patient. The bucket descends behind a ridge. Minutes pass. Cables tighten. Mud and broken shale rise, cross the dim air, and fall onto a spoil heap with a concussion that sends birds outward in rings. Then the machine pauses again. In a day it changes almost nothing that a person can see. In a year it moves a hill.

This is the governing pace of the basin. Pumps lower the water by fractions while rain restores it. Slopes creep. Clay swells and relaxes. New channels begin as damp discolorations, then become grooves, then rills. They have not yet reached the intricacy of streams. Erosion is only learning the terrain. Small fans of sediment accumulate at the foot of every wall, soft-edged and uncolonized, erased by the next rise in water. A boulder may spend months tipping forward before finally settling a meter lower. Entire terraces are understood to be moving, though their movement can be proven only by survey markers and buckled rails.

The light seldom reaches the bottom directly. The depth of the excavation, the vapor, and the broad roof of cloud reduce the day to a diffuse gray-brown glow. Water gives back what little brightness there is, so the floor is lit from below in broken patches. Under bridges and conveyor galleries, even noon resembles evening. Sodium lamps burn at the active stations, coloring the mist amber. Blue-white service lights reveal fences, wet ladders, and clouds of insects, but illuminate nothing beyond them.

Warmth remains trapped in the cut. The rim may have wind, but the lower benches hold a humid, mineral heat. Metal railings are warm to the touch. The pools smell of wet clay, diesel, algae, and iron. Near fresh excavations there is the darker odor of earth that has not met the air before. Near the sumps, hydrogen sulfide sometimes gathers in hollows, and painted signs warn against entering low chambers alone.

The place has strict practical rules. Never trust a dry-looking surface; crusted silt may conceal water deep enough to swallow a vehicle. Never walk beneath a raw face after rain. Do not cross an inactive conveyor simply because it is inactive today. Keep above the stain line when the western clouds thicken. If frogs fall silent in one pool, leave its bank. If the warning sirens sound three long notes, climb, no matter what the sky looks like.

Every route is temporary. Roads descend in broad switchbacks, but slumps sever them, and water claims the lowest sections. Footpaths thread through rubble only until a pipe is opened or a bank gives way. Maps are printed with dates because the basin cannot be represented without time. Even so, its transformations are slow enough to evade attention. A person returning after a week sees the same walls and pools. Returning after ten years, they find that the water has crossed a road, a spoil heap has flattened, birds have hollowed a cliff, and a pumping station once on shore now stands offshore on a narrow concrete island.

Beyond the immediate floor, the excavation continues in dim terraces, water behind water, machinery reduced to small black strokes. The far wall is often invisible. Its presence is inferred from the return of sound and from the faint lines of service lamps that appear through mist after dark.

It is an unfinished landscape, but not a vacant one. Every hollow is occupied by water, every ledge by birds, every submerged pipe by fish, every dry cavity by nesting animals or machinery or warm air waiting for rain. Space is abundant in extent and absent in detail. The basin is open enough to contain weather, yet packed so completely that even echoes must find narrow passages through it.
