At first it seems to be a forested mountain basin at night.

The air is warm despite the elevation, warm enough that every breath carries the taste of leaf mold and animal breath. Cloud presses low over the heights. It does not often rain heavily here; instead, moisture arrives in pauses and residues. Mist combs through the upper ridges. Condensation beads on hairs, feathers, waxy leaves, and the polished inner walls of nests. Water gathers by degrees, becoming droplets, then threads, then brief runnels that vanish again into the dark construction underfoot.

There is no exposed ground.

The basin, the ridges enclosing it, the terraces descending into it, even what appear from a distance to be cliffs and boulder fields, have all been made. They are the accumulated architecture of innumerable animals: woven, packed, chewed, plastered, tunneled, layered, broken down, and built over again. No builder planned the whole. No tool has touched it. Yet the result is total.

The oldest foundations are compressed masses of abandoned chambers, eggshell, clay carried in mouths, fiber, dung, shed skin, bone, silk, resin, and generations of nests. Under pressure and time these materials have become a kind of stone—not geological stone, but hard enough to hold a slope. Dark ribs of root bind it. Pale fungal mats cure its damp interior. Termite mortar seals fractures before rain can widen them. Where a wall slumps, burrowing colonies occupy the ruin and reinforce it from within.

Thus erosion has not stopped. It has been recruited.

Water channels are lined with overlapping shells. Steep faces are quilted in swallow cups and mud-wasp cells. Loose fibers snag in thorny basketworks built by small mammals, and these become ledges where seeds germinate. Every surface catches the remains of another surface. The landscape has weathered into stability not by resisting decay, but by ensuring that decay always becomes building material.

The enclosing heights are broad-backed and irregular. Some rise above the cloud layer, though from the basin floor their summits cannot be seen. They are covered in columns, galleries, and crowded towers whose profiles resemble eroded karst. Up close, each tower is porous with entrances. Warm drafts exhale from some; others breathe inward. Their internal chimneys regulate heat without mechanism, opening and closing through the daily swelling of damp fibers and clay membranes.

The architecture is old enough to have its own topography.

There are neighborhoods of different materials. On the eastern slopes, everything is woven: canes bent into vaults, vines knotted by beaks, grasses braided through branching frames. The walls shift slightly under the weight of sleepers. In the lower basin, where intermittent streams collect, the structures are packed from black mud. They form rounded terraces and deep cisterns, all stippled with footprints. Higher up, resinous insects have built amber-colored ramparts beneath the trees. Elsewhere, hanging colonies fill entire ravines with gray paper chambers, descending in broad folds like drapery.

Vegetation is abundant but never entirely wild in placement. Seeds are carried, buried, forgotten, cached, defecated, or pressed into wet walls. Trees grow from old nest mounds whose heat warms their roots. Shrubs line habitual animal paths because fruit stones accumulate there. Broad leaves gather condensation above communal drinking hollows. Vines suspend themselves between towers, creating high green bridges dense with sleeping bodies.

The canopy is layered so thoroughly that daylight rarely reaches the lower country.

Even at noon, the basin remains in a brown-green dusk. Light filters through leaves, woven roofs, platforms, and flights of wings until it becomes a diffuse stain rather than illumination. Pale fungi mark tunnel mouths. Beetle shells glimmer briefly. The wet backs of animals catch stray highlights and lose them again. At night there is almost no distinction between open space and enclosure. The sky appears only in small, shifting apertures far overhead.

The darkness is not empty. It is occupied beyond counting.

Animals are present in every direction and at every depth. They hang beneath walkways, sleep inside walls, cling to the undersides of leaves, swim through the cisterns, and move through passages beneath the apparent floor. Flocks roost shoulder to shoulder along the upper lattices. Fruit bats form heavy black fringes under vaults. Frogs crowd the damp mouths of condensation channels. Beetles, ants, termites, mites, and pale cave crickets inhabit the material itself. Snakes follow the warmth of nursery chambers. Small cats and civets patrol the ridgelines of roofs as if they were forest branches.

The distinction between landscape and population is difficult to maintain. A hillside darkens because ten thousand wings have folded over it. A gray wall ripples because it is composed partly of resting moths. What looks like moss withdraws in a spreading wave under a footstep. A fallen trunk may be a bridge, a feeding platform, a nursery, and the roof of a colony, each use layered upon the others.

The noise is immense.

It does not come as one roar, though at a distance it has the force of one. It is made of millions of near sounds: wingbeats rattling leaves, claws on hardened clay, throat calls, alarm coughs, mating cries, hatchlings begging, insects stridulating inside walls, frogs pulsing in water pockets, fruit being torn open, fibers snapping under weight. The towers amplify low notes. Hollow terraces carry drumming for long distances. Flocks erupt without warning, and their departure moves through the valley like thunder rolling underground.

Silence occurs only locally and briefly. It is usually a warning.

When a predator passes, calls cease in a widening pocket around it, but beyond that pocket the larger sound continues. The silence travels with the hunter like a shadow, perceptible only because the surrounding clamor is so complete. Once it has gone, the vacancy closes: first a single insect, then a frog, then the resumed shrieking of the canopy.

Water shapes the place gently. There is no permanent lake and few large streams. Rain comes in short warm bursts, striking the upper works and draining inward through thousands of prepared routes. Some channels are accidental; others have been maintained by generations of drinking, bathing, wallowing, and mud gathering. Water descends from leaf to feather to gutterlike root, collecting in sealed chambers. Pools form in courtyards after storms, crowded immediately by tadpoles, insects, birds, and mammals. Over days they shrink to black pockets beneath mats of vegetation.

Nothing is wasted when a pool disappears. Its silt becomes mortar. Its drowned insects are eaten. Its ring of softened wall is mined for clay. Seeds sprout in the remaining dampness, and roots strengthen the basin until the next filling.

Movement through the high country is slow despite its crowded life. Individual creatures hurry, but the landscape changes patiently. A tower may lean for decades before it falls. A vine bridge thickens over many nesting seasons. A channel shifts by the width of a paw each year. Whole districts migrate almost imperceptibly as shade deepens, water routes alter, and one colony replaces another.

There are rules, though none are written.

Openings that breathe warm air are kept clear. Fresh mud belongs first to the builders that carried it. Fruit trees are defended while bearing and abandoned afterward. The upper bridges belong to wings by day and climbing animals by night. Water chambers permit temporary truces, enforced less by agreement than by the danger of panic in a crowded space. Every abandoned chamber may be claimed. Every corpse becomes food, reinforcement, or soil. Anything left motionless long enough is incorporated.

The highest ridges are the oldest. Their interiors have compacted until few animals can burrow through them. Rain and countless feet have rounded their outer forms. Great trees grow there, rooted entirely in constructed material. Their crowns stand in the warm cloud, catching moisture that runs down through the woven country below. Around their trunks are centuries of platforms, repaired so often that they have merged into continuous terraces.

From one of these heights, during the rare thinning of mist, the scale becomes visible.

The made terrain extends across the whole basin and climbs every neighboring slope. There is no untouched clearing, no bare rock, no bank of simple earth. The region is one vast, inhabited accumulation, dark beneath its layered vegetation and moving everywhere with breath. Towers rise like hills; nests form cliffs; bridges make canopies beneath canopies. Condensation shines in innumerable small hollows.

Then the cloud closes again. The valley disappears into warm darkness.

Only the sound remains—deafening, continuous, and old: a country slowly building itself from the lives inside it.
