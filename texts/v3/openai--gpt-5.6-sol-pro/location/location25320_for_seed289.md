Beneath a shelf of pale, unbroken ground is a chamber no larger than a sleeping animal.

It is not properly a cave. Nothing excavated it. The surrounding stone has only recently begun to decide that it is stone, compacting around this withheld pocket while still soft enough to bear the impressions of roots, shells, and bodies that were never there. Its walls are raw mineral paste hardened at the surface: chalk, blue clay, salt, and fine black grains pressed so densely together that there is no clean boundary between substances. Crystals emerge but do not yet possess faces. Ridges begin and stop. Tiny arches thicken out of the ceiling like unfinished thoughts.

The whole chamber is brilliantly lit.

There is no opening through which daylight might enter. Instead, brightness resides in the material itself. Every grain shines with a cool, shadowless whiteness, and the packed inhabitants add their own colors: sulfur-yellow pinpoints, violet seams, green rings around pearl-colored bodies. Light seems unable to travel here because it is already present everywhere. Even the undersides of stones are bright. There are no dark recesses, only places where the radiance turns blue.

Moisture is held in exact suspension. Clear beads occupy hollows in the walls and fit them so precisely that they resemble lenses grown there. A shallow basin, scarcely wider than a palm, contains water with a surface perfectly level and perfectly still. Condensation pearls the low ceiling, but no drop swells or falls. Films of dampness lie between mineral plates, each one a hidden river only a fraction of a hair thick. The chamber smells of wet limestone, cold metal, and the faint sweetness of opened mushrooms.

There is almost no vegetation. A few transparent filaments grow from the dampest cracks, each tipped with a green cell. Low mats of silver lichen spread in isolated circles, crowded out on all sides by animal life. Near the basin stands the largest plant in the place: a colorless stalk with three cupped leaves. It has no obvious source of nourishment. Its roots vanish into a seam of brine, and its leaves hold three droplets that never diminish.

The fauna is everywhere.

Small shelled creatures tile the walls mouth to hinge, leaving no mineral exposed between them. Their shells are thin and luminous, shaped like ears, cups, spirals, folded fans, and closed eyelids. Soft-bodied animals fill the spaces among the shells: white knots with radial mouths, red threads passing through colonies of blue sacs, velvet disks lined with cilia too fine to see individually. In the water basin, hundreds of glassy larvae stand vertically without sinking. Each has a black point for an eye and a crown of motionless feelers.

Larger beings are present only by local standards. A broad-backed grazer occupies most of the ceiling, pressed flat as a skin beneath the embryonic arches. Six pale limbs clasp protrusions in the stone. Its body is crowded with dependent species—mites, cup worms, shell fleas, translucent sacs full of smaller swimmers—so that it is less an animal than a district. Along the floor, several segmented creatures fit together head to tail in a closed ring around the pool. None advances; none retreats. Their jointed armor forms a continuous low wall.

Nothing here moves.

The cilia are bent as if in a current, but remain bent. Mouths are open at exact feeding widths. Predators hold prey between delicate teeth without biting down. Eggs are at the instant before hatching, their membranes starred by noses and claws that never break through. Nothing starves, ages, decays, or completes itself. Life is abundant without succession. The chamber maintains every appetite at its threshold and every event at its beginning.

Yet it is loud.

A continuous chord occupies the pocket, strong enough to be felt through the soft stone above. It has no pulse and no variation. The shells on the walls each sustain one narrow tone; the basin contributes a deep, glassy pressure; the ceiling animal adds a rough vibration like wind caught in a throat. Because nothing begins or ends, the sound cannot be called singing. It is closer to the permanent acoustic shape of the chamber—the note made by so many bodies packed into the only arrangement that allows them all to exist.

At certain angles, the chord seems to contain insect cries, rain on leaves, distant surf, and the groaning of trees. None of those things are present. They may be echoes of landscapes the chamber has never known, compressed into its single unwavering noise.

Scattered among the organisms are small constructions, though no tools have ever entered this place. Around several cracks stand low rings of clay pellets, each pellet rolled by jaws and fitted against the next. Shell bridges cross gaps too narrow to require crossing. At one end of the pool is a tiny pavilion made from rib bones and hardened mucus, elaborate enough to seem ceremonial, though it shelters only a cluster of eggs. There are stairlike terraces along one wall, each step no taller than a grain of sand. They lead upward to a blank patch of luminous stone.

These structures have neither builders nor ruins in the usual sense. Their making is held in equilibrium with their unmaking. A clay ring is always being assembled and is always already complete. The bone pavilion is fresh, ancient, and unfinished. No artifact implies a vanished hand; construction here is simply one of the forms biology takes when pressed too closely against itself.

The chamber has rules that would not survive outside it.

Space is conserved more strictly than matter. No creature may leave a vacancy, so every shell bears the shape of its neighbors, and every birth is matched not by a death but by a finer division of available room. Brightness has no source and casts no shadow. Water may gather but may not fall. Stone may commence hardening but may never finish. Sound persists without expenditure. Hunger exists, feeding exists, and neither changes the fed or the feeder.

Even erosion is only incipient. Fine channels appear to be on the verge of forming beneath the condensation beads. The basin seems ready to deepen. Soft walls carry the first faint scoring of water that has not yet flowed. This is geology at the instant before process: a landscape containing all the signs of weather without weather ever arriving.

From above, there is no indication of the pocket except a slight coolness in the ground and the steady tone passing upward through it. The surface remains plain, dry, and nearly featureless. Beneath it, within less space than a curled body would occupy, an entire bright ecology is fitted together with intolerable precision.

It is not dormant. Dormancy anticipates change.

It is complete in the act of becoming.
