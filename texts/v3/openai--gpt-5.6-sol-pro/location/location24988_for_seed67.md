The land is low enough that distance never quite rises. It spreads outward in shallow metallic immensities: terraces, catchments, assembly plains, pressure basins, and broad floors tiled in dark ceramic. There is no native ground beneath them. Drill through a kilometer of paving and one finds deeper paving—load-bearing honeycombs, sealed conduits, machine galleries, and older structural decks descending beyond any surveyed depth. What passes for a continent is an installation built upon previous installations.

The surfaces are young.

Edges remain sharp where enormous plates meet. Bolt heads still retain the iridescent discoloration of forging. Welds run for hundreds of kilometers in clean, raised seams. There is little dust, no soil, no moss softening the angles. Rain has begun to mark the pale alloys with narrow mineral stains, but has not yet rounded anything. In places, whole horizons look newly removed from a mold: ribbed, regular, faintly oily, and warm.

Water is everywhere without ruling the place. It lies a finger deep across immense flat yards, filling grooves etched to direct it. It beads on black glass and hangs under gantries in trembling rows. Condensation weeps continuously from the walls of cooling towers, though the towers no longer visibly cool anything. Shallow canals pass between machine halls, their water clear enough to reveal ceramic bottoms marked with serial numbers and alignment crosses. Every few kilometers, rain descends from a sky that appears ordinary—white daylight, gray weather, occasional blue—and stops at boundaries too precise to be meteorological. One side of a railing may be wet while the other stays dry.

The air is warm, saturated, and almost windless. Metal handrails are warm to the touch. Vapor gathers in the hollows and along the undersides of bridges. Nothing steams dramatically; temperatures are too evenly held for that. Instead, dampness persists as a universal skin.

There are no plants. No green film clouds the reservoirs, no root lifts a slab, no drifting seed catches in a grate. Even rot is absent. Organic debris does not become humus here. It is collected, separated, sterilized, and returned to one of the closed systems below.

Yet the place is crowded with animals beyond counting.

At first they are mistaken for parts of the works. Millions of pale shelled creatures adhere beneath the drainage lips, each fitting precisely into a standardized recess. Long-bodied grazers—though there is nothing to graze—stand shoulder to shoulder on the warm flats. Their mouths are broad sieves through which they pass the condensation film, extracting salts, bacteria-sized symbionts, metallic flakes, and whatever invisible nutrient the pipes exhale. They scarcely move. Across a plain, ten thousand of them may hold the same posture, heads lowered, translucent ears angled toward the same distant pump.

In the canals are dense schools of black, eyeless fish, packed so closely that the water seems paved with muscle. They maintain exact spacing. When a shadow crosses them, they do not scatter; the entire school darkens as membranes close over their reflective backs. Beneath them, flat crustaceans occupy the engraved letters in the canal floor. Each animal claims a single numeral or stroke, and generations may pass without the arrangement changing.

The upper structures are no emptier. Cable bridges sag under the weight of sleeping winged animals. Their bodies are warm and leathery, their claws insulated as though designed for high-voltage lines. Thousands hang from every span. They have wings, but flight is rare. At fixed intervals one releases its grip, crosses the space in three silent strokes, and takes the vacant place left by another. Seen briefly, this seems like movement. Watched for days, it resolves into circulation: a substitution so exact that the pattern of occupied and unoccupied cable never changes.

There are larger things in the assembly basins.

Some resemble six-legged cattle with armored flanks and flexible, nozzle-like faces. They stand in ranks between dormant presses, drinking warm water from floor sumps. Others are nearly architectural: slow, columnar animals whose calcified backs support walkways laid across them. They may be mistaken for piers until an eyelid opens in the concrete-gray surface. Their breathing is too gradual to observe directly. Surveyors infer it from minute changes in pressure over decades.

The greatest creatures lie beneath the floors. Their presence is known through access hatches that flex upward by less than a millimeter and through the faint warmth of certain districts. No one agrees whether these buried bodies are fauna occupying the structure or components of the structure that became animal. The distinction has little practical use. Their ducts join factory ducts. Their nerves pass through switchboards. Their eggs, if they are eggs, are manufactured in numbered steel vessels and conveyed along lines that have operated since before records began.

Despite all this life, the landscape is mostly quiet.

Sound is absorbed by perforated walls, water films, felted insulation, and the soft bodies packed into every cavity. The animals do not call often. Predators have no need to chase, and prey have no room to flee. Hunger, birth, and death proceed under regulation. One hears the tiny click of shells closing beneath a ledge, the occasional guttering of rain into a slot, or a faraway relay engaging with the solitary weight of a stone dropped in a cavern.

The factories make less noise than expected. Their belts run beneath sealed floors. Pistons oppose one another so their impacts cancel. Turbines rotate in evacuated housings. Power travels through buried superconducting trunks. A person standing beside a hall large enough to fabricate a mountain might hear only a thin electrical tone, near the edge of hearing, and water tapping from the roof.

Nothing here is ruined. Nothing is quite active, either.

The machinery operates at maintenance rate. It replaces worn seal with new seal, old creature with young creature, corroded plate with an identical plate. Rainwater is pumped upward and released as rain. Heat extracted in one district returns through the floor of another. Animal populations remain overwhelming but exact. For every death there is a corresponding opening of a hatch somewhere else. For every hatch that opens, another closes. Predators consume only bodies already scheduled for removal. Scavengers arrive before death and wait without impatience.

Even the weather is an industrial equilibrium. Cloud banks are assembled above the evaporator fields and drawn along fixed corridors by pressure machinery. Storms recur with the sameness of architectural features. A traveler may cross a belt of warm rain, walk under clear sky for thirty kilometers, then encounter the same belt again after it has looped through the atmospheric plant. The clouds look natural from below. From the high service towers, their rectangular margins are visible.

There are signs that the installation was completed recently: clean joints, fresh numbering, unweathered warning colors. There are also records implying that it has always been newly completed. Dates stamped into support beams refer to calendars with no elapsed years. Replacement plates bear the same manufacturing scars as the originals. In certain inspection rooms, clocks possess faces but no hands. In others, the hands move, though every clock always shows the same time.

This is not stasis in the sense of immobility. Water falls. Pumps turn. Creatures breathe, feed, molt, and are replaced. It is stasis as an absence of consequence. Every action returns the world to the form it had before. Nothing accumulates. Nothing erodes far enough to become old. No population conquers another. No machine completes its production. The entire continental works performs change in order to preserve an unchanging result.

Toward the center, the density becomes almost difficult to comprehend. Service towers rise from platforms built over halls built over reservoirs built over inhabited pressure chambers. Every horizontal surface carries resting animals. Every wall contains conduits, every conduit contains fluid or cables or chains of tiny transparent organisms. Even the airspace is allocated: low corridors for heavy fliers, high corridors for weather drones, warm shafts for swarms of nearly invisible filter animals. They hang in layers, motionless enough that sunlight reveals them only as a granular haze.

Still, daylight remains plain and generous. There is no perpetual dusk, no theatrical glow. The sky is bright. Reflections move over wet metal. White clouds stand above the towers. The ordinary light makes the scale and strangeness harder to dismiss.

Far from the central works, the structures become lower but not less complete. Vast pans of pale composite stretch to the horizon, divided by raised seams. Rain gathers in them to ankle depth. Herds stand in the water, each animal facing north. Between their legs, silver amphibious things nest in inspection sockets. Above them, suspended from cables too fine to see, tens of thousands of winged bodies hang like punctuation in the sky.

Nothing approaches. Nothing recedes. The immense made world seems caught just after construction and just before use.

Then a drain opens somewhere beneath the flooded plain. The water level lowers by the width of a fingernail. Every animal shifts its weight at once, producing the softest possible sound: a continental sigh of hide, shell, and claw against wet manufactured ground.

The drain closes.

Warm rain begins again in exactly the same place.
