At the high end of a narrow pass, where the mountain briefly cups itself before falling away into cloud, there is a town no larger than a cathedral close.

It is built inside a glacial basin: a steep-sided hollow of pale stone, scoured smooth long ago and since worked into finer shapes by centuries of freezing water. The enclosing walls are not cliffs so much as immense weeping surfaces. Water beads from every seam, hangs under ledges, and travels downward in bright, crooked threads. Some threads are liquid; others have frozen into clear ribs, curtains, and hollow pipes. Beneath the paving, water can be heard only if one kneels and presses an ear to the rock. It runs everywhere under the settlement, near enough to chill the soles of the feet.

The place is dazzling.

The altitude gives the daylight a stripped, almost mineral clarity. Snowfields above the basin throw sunlight down into it, while wet stone casts it back. Even the shadows contain a bluish glare. Pools shine like pieces of hammered metal. Every droplet has a hard white point in it. There are few colors besides chalk, silver, blue-black, and the rusty red of certain mineral stains, but each is intense. A person coming out of one of the low dwellings must cover their eyes until they adjust.

The town is densely made, though scarcely anything in it is large. Hundreds of little structures crowd the hollow: dry-stone cells, arched storage niches, roofed trenches, retaining walls, stepping platforms, bridges no longer than a body, and towers only three or four people high. They fit together without streets in the usual sense. Movement is by squeezing through slots, climbing notched stones, crossing roofs, and following gutters whose edges have been polished by generations of bare hands.

No mortar is used. Iron is rare. The builders possess stone hammers, antler wedges, bone awls, leather buckets, woven cords, and a few treasured copper blades gone green at the edges. Their city is held together by weight, friction, ice, and exact acquaintance with the mountain’s habits. Walls lean inward because outward walls are peeled apart by frost. Roofs are laid in overlapping scales so that meltwater cannot find a single seam. Doorways bend twice, trapping still air. Every building is partly a drain, and every drain is partly a path.

Nothing stands quite where it stood the previous season.

This is not because the settlement is young or poorly made. It is ancient, and its instability has become a kind of equilibrium. The basin shifts continually through small collapses, frost-heave, thaw, settling scree, and pressure from hidden water. A stair may rise by the width of a finger overnight. A courtyard may sink until it becomes a pool. Clear ice swells within the walls, lifting whole rooms gently from their foundations, then lowers them again weeks later in a different alignment.

The people do not resist this. They rebuild constantly and with very little noise. Stones are passed hand to hand. A wall is dismantled before it falls and reassembled where the ground now wants it. Houses migrate around the basin over decades, though no single stone may travel farther than a few paces. Family marks are not carved onto fixed doorposts but onto palm-sized slabs carried from dwelling to dwelling.

From above, the town resembles a packed field of scales rearranging on the back of a breathing animal.

The turbulence is everywhere, but rarely loud. Snow pours from the upper ledges in fine, continuous veils, whispering rather than roaring. Water races beneath the paving without breaking the surface. Gusts strike the rim and become tangled among the crowded roofs, reduced to threads of cold air that slip through keyholes and drainage gaps. Even rockfalls are often muffled by snow, moss-dark water, or layers of loose stone. The inhabitants speak close to one another. Bells are not used; sharp sound is thought to loosen the mountain.

There are almost no plants in the ordinary sense. A few black lichens stipple the sunward walls. Rust-colored moss grows in the warmer drainage mouths, pressed flat as felt. In protected cracks there are translucent grass blades, so sparse that each one is known and sometimes given a small stone screen against the wind. The settlement burns no wood. Heat comes from bodies, oil, dung, and shallow geothermal cracks discovered under the oldest chambers.

Yet animals are everywhere.

Small white crabs inhabit the cold runoff, climbing the town’s wet walls with delicate, clicking legs. They gather in gutters until the water seems paved with moving shell. Long-winged birds nest in every cornice and stone pocket, their feathers nearly the same color as the rock. They do not cry. Their throats pulse, but what communication passes among them is below the range of human hearing, felt as a faint pressure in the teeth.

There are also compact, dark-furred mountain animals with too many joints in their forelimbs. They move through the dense masonry as fluidly as water, folding themselves through gaps no wider than a wrist. They feed on crabs, bird eggs, leather scraps, and the mineral moss. They are neither tame nor hunted. At night they sleep inside the walls, warming the stones, and the town would freeze harder without them.

Most numerous are the glass gnats.

They appear whenever sunlight strikes falling water. Their wings are transparent and rigid-looking, and their bodies are no more substantial than slivers of frost. Clouds of them turn in the basin, thick enough to occupy every bright shaft, but they make no hum. They drift with astonishing speed, suddenly assembling around a roof or pool, then vanishing as if the light itself has changed its mind. The birds feed on them, though no one has ever found a gnat inside a bird’s crop.

The oldest belief in the town is that the gnats are not animals but moments: the brief instant in which water decides whether to freeze.

That decision is important here, because water does not always obey the gradient. At certain hours, especially under clear noon light, droplets climb the cliff. They rise slowly from pool rims and wet paving, travel upward in trembling strings, and disappear into cracks above. Elsewhere, ice melts on its shaded side while thickening in direct sun. A vessel left open may fill though no rain has fallen; another, beside it, may empty into the stone.

The townspeople have learned enough of these inversions to build with them. Their cisterns are shallow bowls positioned according to season, reflection, and the angle of neighboring roofs. Their water clocks do not measure downward flow but count droplets ascending through a perforated bone disk. Forecasting is a practical craft performed by watching the crabs: when they climb high, the lower streets will flood; when they bury themselves in drains, water is likely to rise from the roofs instead.

Floods arrive abruptly and silently.

A dry passage darkens. Beads form between its paving stones. Within moments, clear water lifts through the floor and fills the passage to the knee. It may stream uphill, divide around a doorway, or gather vertically against a wall as though held in an invisible vessel. Residents move quickly, climbing onto shelves and roofs, opening diversion plugs, carrying sleeping mats above the new waterline. The entire town becomes active at once—people scrambling, animals folding through masonry, birds spilling from niches, crabs advancing in silver masses—yet the prevailing sound remains a subdued patter of droplets and the brushing of leather soles over stone.

Then the water drains. Ice blooms in its place. By evening, new translucent partitions may divide one courtyard into five brilliant rooms.

Nothing is wasted. Thin ice is cut with bone saws and stacked as temporary roofing. Sediment left by the floods is scraped into clay pots. Dead birds are sewn into insulating garments. Crab shells are crushed for pigment. Loose feathers are packed into wall cavities. Even collapsed structures become quarries for their neighbors. The town is so dense with use that there is almost no distinction between architecture, geology, and ecology. A drain is a crab road. A roof is a nesting cliff. A heated room is also a thawing device. A grave is a retaining wall.

The dead are placed upright in narrow crevices at the basin’s edge, wrapped in hide and surrounded by fitted stones. Over many winters, seepage coats them in ice and mineral deposits. Their outlines become part of the cliff: pale vertical forms behind translucent curtains. Meltwater passes over their faces and enters the town’s channels. Thus the ancestors are said to return as pressure, as drinking water, and as changes in the foundations.

At the center of the settlement lies its only broad open space, though “broad” here means perhaps twenty paces across. It is a shallow oval worn into the bedrock, crowded around its perimeter by stairs, platforms, animal niches, and leaning stone screens. The oval alternates between plaza, reservoir, skating surface, and exposed floor. Its stone has been polished by feet, flood grit, and glacial abrasion until it reflects the sky.

In the middle stands no monument. There is only a hole the width of a hand.

Cold air exhales from it at dawn. At noon it draws air inward. Water placed near it trembles. Birds refuse to cross directly overhead, and the many-jointed wall animals circle it even when fleeing danger. The inhabitants say the hole reaches not down into the mountain but sideways, into all the earlier positions of the town. Sometimes, if a person places an ear against it, they hear stones being set in walls dismantled centuries ago.

Whatever the truth, the basin is never still. Its surfaces glitter, migrate, flood, harden, and open. The city contracts during storms and spreads during clear weather. Routes appear and vanish. Rooms turn inside out as their walls are repurposed. Thousands of living things thread through a settlement with almost no vegetation and almost no soil, feeding on water, mineral film, one another, and the fierce abundance of light.

Beyond the rim, the mountain world is vast and empty: snow slopes, distant black summits, cloud-filled valleys. Inside the basin, everything is close. Wing brushes stone. Water presses beneath skin. Houses touch houses, graves touch cisterns, ice touches warm breath. The whole place has the compressed intricacy of something held in a clenched hand.

And despite its coldness, its floods, and its perpetual rearrangement, it does not feel endangered.

It feels completed by change.
