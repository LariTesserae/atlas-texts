The land is almost level, though not smooth. It extends in broad, shallow pitches beneath the dark: a continent of fitted plates, poured ceramic, iron aggregate, and black glass worn so long by water that their joins have become the nearest thing here to geology. There is no exposed soil. Where a plate has cracked, the material beneath is another plate—older, redder, thicker—or a compacted bed of machine filings set in resin. Boreholes descend through kilometers of manufactured layers without finding stone.

Rain has just passed, though rain is rarely an event with a clear beginning or end. Moisture gathers continually in the cool air. It beads on railings, condenses under gantries, and runs down the undersides of pipes. Every surface weeps. Water lies in shallow sheets across the low places, reflecting a sky without visible stars. Beneath these flooded expanses, embossed warning stripes and obsolete directional arrows can still be seen, enlarged and softened by the moving water.

There are no hills. The oldest structures have been reduced to them.

Far away, low swells rise perhaps twenty or thirty meters above the plain: former factories whose roofs collapsed inward over ages, then settled under their own weight. Their steel frames have slumped into long ridges. Their ventilation stacks broke, weathered, and became blunt columns with rounded crowns. Whole industrial districts have eroded into dark, shallow terraces, each terrace edged by the remnant of a wall or conveyor bed. From a distance these ruins resemble natural escarpments, but rain reveals regularities—bolt circles, repeating ribs, square cavities, lengths of buried cable emerging like mineral veins.

Nothing is young enough to have a sharp corner.

The water moves through the plain in old channels. Some were gutters, some coolant races, some transport canals, some merely spaces left between immense foundations. Their original purposes no longer matter. They have joined into a single hydrological machine, carrying warm black water one way and cold silver water another. At intersections, currents pass beneath each other through culverts and siphons. Gates lift and lower without attendants. Pumps buried below the plates draw in flooded acres with such force that the water tilts toward distant grilles.

The sound is enormous.

It does not come from one source. It is the sum of the entire place: turbine thunder underfoot, the grinding of submerged impellers, rain drumming on hollow structures, pressure escaping in sustained metallic cries. Vast cooling fans turn somewhere beyond sight, each blade-beat slow enough to be counted but powerful enough to shake droplets from the undersides of beams. Every few minutes a line of valves opens across the basin. The release travels from horizon to horizon as a sequence of concussions, like doors being slammed in rooms the size of cities.

Conversation would be impossible here. Even inside sealed shelters, the landscape enters through the bones.

Yet the plain feels empty. Its engines are hidden, and its few standing structures are separated by long reaches of wet pavement. A mast may be visible for hours before one comes near it. A pumping house occupies an entire square kilometer but rises only waist-high above the flood, its roof bowed and polished by ages of rain. Light is sparse: dull amber lamps beneath water, red points on distant towers, occasional blue arcs moving behind walls. Most illumination comes from maintenance signals that no longer correspond to any readable system.

Darkness gives the open country a deceptive intimacy. The horizon is rarely visible except when a pressure flare ignites.

Then the basin appears all at once: hundreds of kilometers of low machinery, flooded yards, collapsed conduits, and long parallel embankments converging into the distance. Vapor lifts from warmer canals. Reservoirs shine like sheets of lead. Beyond them stand scattered towers, each isolated in a broad exclusion zone, their lower halves hidden by mist. The flare fades, and the scale vanishes again.

Vegetation survives only in narrow, stubborn forms. Pale moss grows around the constant drips beneath condenser mouths. Black reedlike stems rise from seams where powdered ceramic and rust have accumulated into a medium fine enough for roots. They do not form meadows. Each cluster is a lonely stain of growth, separated from the next by hundreds of meters of metal and water.

The reeds have translucent walls and contain no insects. Nothing lands on them. Nothing grazes them. When the air-pressure cycle passes, they bow together, shed condensed water, and slowly straighten.

In some sheltered recesses, flat lichens map the alloy surfaces in muted green and white. They spread so slowly that their oldest colonies preserve the outlines of vanished signs. Their chemistry draws trace minerals from paint, insulation, and corroded circuitry. Thus the only living soil is made from the patient digestion of the built world.

There are no animals. No fish move in the channels, no birds cross the dark, no worms turn the residue collected in drains. Pools remain untroubled except by rain, vibration, and gas rising from below. Seed capsules fall from the sparse reeds and lie where they land. Pollination, where it occurs, is done by the forced wind of ventilators. Spores ride exhaust streams. Growth has adapted itself to machinery because machinery is the only season the place possesses.

The seasons are pressure, heat, flood, and purge.

During pressure, buried reservoirs fill and the plates flex upward by fractions of a degree. Water climbs through old screw holes. Seams hiss. The whole plain tightens.

During heat, reactors or furnaces far below resume some ancient stage of work. Warmth spreads in geometric zones, thawing condensation from roofs and waking the moss. Vapor thickens until towers disappear at a few dozen paces.

Flood follows. Gates open in the outer basin, and dark water advances across the low decks in a front only a handspan deep but many kilometers wide. It carries rust flakes, beads of glass, strands of insulation, and the detached labels of forgotten machines. The water is sorted as it moves. Magnetic debris gathers in branching patterns around buried conductors; lighter fragments collect against grilles; dissolved metals precipitate in vivid orange fans.

Purge is the loudest season. Sirens rise without warning, though no listener is expected. Shafts open. Air and steam erupt upward, and the standing water races away from vents before returning in waves. Towers exhale clouds that blot out even the weak industrial lights. The reeds flatten. Loose sheets of metal travel skittering across the plain until caught by old guardrails.

Afterward comes a temporary quiet—not silence, but a lower register of continuous labor. In that interval, water drips from every edge. Condensation rings spread through flooded control rooms. Fine rain forms inside buildings as warm ceilings cool.

No one knows what the machinery produces. Perhaps it once made vehicles, fuel, weather, or other machinery. Perhaps production ended so long ago that only the support systems remain: cooling systems cooling themselves, drainage networks draining leakage from their own pipes, power stations feeding pumps that provide water to power stations. The processes have closed into a stable circuit. Breakdown is incorporated as input. Corrosion supplies metals to electrochemical cells. Fallen structures redirect floods. Blocked channels increase pressure until some older bypass opens.

The place does not repair itself. It wears itself into configurations that continue to function.

This is its ancientness: not preservation, but exhaustion brought into balance. Every vulnerable part failed ages ago. Every unsupported span has fallen. Every coating that could peel has peeled. What remains has been selected by time, water, load, and repetition. The lowest walls are the strongest because everything taller has descended onto them. The deepest machines still turn because they were built to bear the weight of all that would eventually collapse above.

At the center of the basin is a feature called, in surviving diagrams, the Open Floor. It is a circular expanse many kilometers across, perfectly level, with no buildings upon it. Water covers it to the depth of a finger. Concentric grooves divide the surface, and through these grooves the water travels inward against the apparent slope.

At the center stands a single low housing, not much taller than a person. It has no door. Cables thicker than houses descend from its sides into the floor. Once in each cycle, all the noise of the surrounding works aligns: pumps, fans, turbines, venting towers, hammering pipes. Their separate rhythms become one sustained note.

The shallow water trembles into precise hexagons.

The sparse reeds along the perimeter release their spores.

Deep beneath the manufactured plain, something shifts by one increment.

Then the rhythms fall apart again, and the landscape returns to its deafening, aimless labor: dark water moving over old metal, rain forming from the breath of engines, and the final structures of a made world slowly wearing themselves flatter without ever quite coming to rest.
