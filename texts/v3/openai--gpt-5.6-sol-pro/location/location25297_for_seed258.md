The high country begins without a foothill.

A green shelf simply stands above the surrounding air, broad enough to contain valleys, marshes, and distant weather, yet without exposed stone anywhere along its sides. From below, its cliffs resemble the walls of an immense living vessel: overlapping plates of dark rind, vertical cables like roots, pale seams beaded with water. From above, the shelf appears open and sparsely furnished—a warm upland of long, empty expanses interrupted by solitary towers, shallow basins, and raised green causeways.

Everything here has been made, but nothing has been made with tools.

There is no natural substrate beneath the landscape, or none that can be reached. The ground is a woven floor of living fibers, compressed over unguessable depths. Its upper skin is firm and faintly resilient, like cork laid over muscle. In wet places it turns translucent. Looking down through it, one can sometimes see thick conduits passing far below: white-green trunks carrying dark water sideways beneath the plain. They fork at exact angles, reunite, and disappear into the foundations of distant terraces.

The architecture is plant architecture, though it does not resemble a forest. Vegetation does not grow *on* the land. Vegetation is the land, the drainage, the walls, the empty rooms, the bridges, the reservoirs, and the atmosphere’s wet green scent.

Across the open shelf stand enormous hollow stems, each separated from the next by several hundred paces. They rise in fluted columns and open high overhead into broad, shallow crowns. Their leaves are few but immense, stretched between veins like roofs between rafters. Under each crown lies an island of shade large enough for a village, though there are no villages. Rain gathers on the leaves, travels along their radial ribs, and descends through chains of cupped blossoms. The falling water makes a continuous hard clatter, louder than an ordinary storm.

Between the columns, the land remains almost bare. Low skins of moss seal the woven floor. Here and there a single spiral frond stands waist-high in an otherwise vacant acre. Its coils are so regular that they suggest ornament, measurement, or script, but every spiral is identical and no sequence develops. Sparse beds of glassy reeds occupy geometric hollows. Pale knots rise like boundary stones. Long hedges run toward the horizon, perfectly straight, then end without explanation.

The landscape is warm, though its elevation should make it cold. Heat comes up through the living floor in a mild, steady exhalation. Bare skin would feel it at the soles first. Water lying in shallow depressions is neither hot nor cool; it has the temperature of blood left in shade. The air is dense with condensation, and every underside weeps. Droplets form on leaf vaults, on the smooth walls of stem corridors, on the pale rims of basins. Yet the air never becomes stifling. Broad openings between structures allow a high, wet brightness to move across everything.

There is ordinary daylight here: gray-white under cloud, gold where the sky opens, green only beneath the great crowns. Nothing glows without cause. The strangeness lies in how honestly illuminated it all is. Every impossible surface can be plainly seen. The horizon is not hidden in mist. The eye can follow the constructed upland for miles, from one basin to another, from the marsh terraces to the tall western combs where rain hangs in fixed silver curtains.

Those curtains are part of the place’s equilibrium.

Water is always falling, but the pools do not rise. It runs along channels, but no visible body of water diminishes. Drops gather and fall from the same points at the same intervals. Narrow cascades pour over the lips of green terraces, striking lower leaves with a sound like heavy applause. Beneath the ground, fluid travels through resonant vessels, making the whole shelf hum. Nothing floods. Nothing dries. The rain does not advance or pass. Its edges remain where they are, so that one may stand in clear warm air with a wall of rainfall ten steps away, stretching motionless in position across the valley.

The place is loud without seeming active.

The cascades roar. Hollow columns boom at low intervals. Water ticking through blossom chains produces a metallic rattle, although there is no metal. Wind crossing the open mouths of buried chambers sustains a deep chord that never swells or fades. From the marshes comes a dense, continuous hiss as bubbles break beneath mats of tiny leaves. These sounds overlap, but they do not compete. They have settled into a stable arrangement, like parts of a single held note.

Even echoes behave as though constructed. A shout returns once from the nearest wall, once from somewhere far below, and then as a soft breath from ahead. The timing is always the same, regardless of where one stands.

The upland contains valleys, but they seem newly proposed rather than worn. Their sides are shallow and undecided. Watercourses lie in faint folds in the vegetative floor, as if erosion has only just begun to imagine them. At the edge of one basin, a stream has made the first incision into a pale layer of root-felt, exposing fibers as fresh and white as torn fruit. Yet the cut never deepens. Loose strands trail in the current without breaking away. No sediment accumulates downstream.

Elsewhere, the land displays embryonic cliffs: seams parting by the width of a finger, ridges beginning to buckle, drainage grooves no deeper than scratches. The entire region seems to have been completed at the exact moment decay and weathering were about to commence. It has remained in that first instant ever since. There are no old ruins because nothing becomes old. There are no young shoots because nothing becomes older.

The marshes gather at the centers of broad cellular basins. Their water is dark, but not muddy; there is no mineral mud. The bottom consists of soft, folded leaves that do not rot. Tall bladder-plants rise at wide intervals, each bearing a translucent sac large enough to hold a person. They inhale and collapse with such slowness that the motion cannot be watched, only inferred from visits separated by hours. Yet when measured against the basin rim, every sac is always found in precisely the same state. The apparent breathing may belong to the observer rather than the plant.

Paths cross these marshes. They are grown causeways, narrow and slightly crowned, surfaced with scales that overlap in the direction of travel. They connect the hollow columns, the roof-leaves, and the dry chambers embedded in the terrace walls. Nothing walks them often. No footprints remain, but the paths feel used: their hand-height tendrils curl away from the centerline, and their occasional archways lean forward as if accommodating a habitual procession.

There are animals, though they are rare enough to seem like punctuation.

A long-legged black creature may stand in a pool beneath one of the columns. It has no visible mouth. Water enters through the fine roots feathering its ankles and drips from the point of its tail. It will remain in place for days, perhaps permanently. Pale winged things sometimes cross the open plain in groups of three. Their wings are leaves, complete with veins and small parasitic flowers. They do not flap. They travel levelly against the wind, humming at the same pitch as the buried conduits, and vanish into slots high in the architecture.

In the wetest corridors live round, soft-bodied grazers that consume the floor’s surface film. Behind them the film closes, unmarked, at exactly the rate they feed. Predator and prey are not apparent. Birth and death are not witnessed. The few creatures seem less like a population than necessary moving pieces in a structure that is otherwise complete.

There are also chambers.

Some open in the sides of raised terraces, framed by woody ribs. Inside are long halls with walls of smooth pith, dry despite the weeping exterior. Branching partitions divide them into alcoves. Benches, shelves, rails, and shallow sleeping-shaped depressions have grown directly from the walls. There are no hinges, blades, vessels, lamps, fasteners, wheels, or remnants of fire. The rooms are unmistakably built for bodies, but not necessarily human bodies. Some thresholds are too narrow at the floor and broad overhead. Some rooms can be entered only by descending through a flowering throat. Others contain vertical resting niches lined with soft root hairs.

Their design suggests intention without technology. Perhaps the plants made them for vanished inhabitants. Perhaps the inhabitants were plants and have merely withdrawn into the walls. Perhaps “made” and “grown” were never separate acts here.

At the center of the upland lies the largest open space: a shallow circular valley several miles across. Its floor is a single leaf.

The leaf is thick enough to carry pools, islands, and colonnades. Its veins form elevated roads radiating from a central rise. Rainwater occupies the cells between them, making a mosaic of warm, still wetlands. At the central rise stands no tree or palace, only an empty socket with a neatly ridged interior. It resembles the place from which some enormous stem was removed, though there is no scar tissue, no fallen trunk, and no gap in the canopy that would reveal when or how it happened.

The socket produces the loudest sound in the region. From its depth comes a continuous rushing, the voice of a waterfall with no visible water. Leaning over the rim, one sees green walls descending into brightness rather than darkness. Condensation streams upward along those walls and spills over the edge, after which it resumes falling normally. Small leaves at the rim point downward on one side and upward on the other.

Nothing thrown into the opening can be seen to fall. It simply joins the landscape: a seed becomes part of the rim, a drop becomes part of its wetness, a voice becomes one more layer in the unending roar.

Beyond the circular valley, the green shelf continues toward a series of distant rises. Their profiles look mountainous, but each is hollow and ribbed. Some are closed buds kilometers tall. Others resemble folded fans or the backs of sleeping animals. Warm rain lies between them. High bridges, thin as grass blades at that distance, span the gaps.

No bridge sways. No crown sheds a leaf. No channel widens. The same drops seem to strike the same surfaces, maintaining the same resonances. Yet the place does not feel dead or preserved. It feels intensely alive—so alive that it has passed beyond growth into a condition of perfect maintenance.

Here, life is not a struggle against erosion. Life has built erosion into the design: the first scratch, the first exposed fiber, the first leaning bank, each held forever at the threshold of becoming. Water is permitted to run but not to alter. Creatures are permitted to move but not to disturb. Structures may breathe, weep, drink, and sing, but never arrive at a different state.

The region is a finished world constructed to resemble its own beginning.

And across its warm, high, sparsely furnished distances, beneath the normal light and the impossible steadiness of rain, the whole living architecture keeps up its enormous sound: water, wind, hollow wood, and deep internal circulation, all roaring continuously while nothing changes at all.
