The plateau is not ground. It is the roof of an organism.

It extends for hundreds of kilometers at an altitude where the atmosphere has thinned to a hot, mineral breath. There are no rivers below it, no aquifers buried in it, no rain waiting beyond the horizon. Water has not merely dried up here; it has been designed out. The old builders regarded it as corrosive, unreliable, too eager to freeze, boil, and carry contamination. What circulates instead is a dark sap of silicone oils, dissolved salts, and long carbon chains, pumped hot through capillary conduits.

Nothing beneath the dim sky is bare. Every handspan is occupied by vegetation, though little of it resembles leaf or forest. The plateau’s surface is a close-laid mosaic of black photosynthetic tiles: overlapping scales, slightly cupped, each as broad as a room. They absorb what little visible light reaches them and feed more efficiently on infrared radiation rising from the machinery below. Between the scales stand thickets of ceramic stalks, braided roots, heat fins, seed chimneys, and ribbed towers grown in repeated industrial forms. Their tissues are woody, metallic, and glass-hard. Many bear maker’s marks in their grain.

The distinction between plant and factory has worn away.

Columns grow by depositing rings of carbon composite around hollow cores. Conveyor vines crawl through them, moving nodules of resin and packets of mineral dust. Vast bladder-leaves compress atmospheric gases. Root refineries crack those gases, sort their molecules, and feed the useful fractions into buried fermentation chambers. Flowering structures open only inward, inside sealed furnaces where pressure and temperature can be controlled. Pollination is performed by electrostatic pulses. Seed is stamped, inspected, and routed through branching ducts.

There are no insects, no birds, no grazing things. Nothing has eyes. Nothing hunts.

Yet the place is intensely busy.

Across the plateau, structures are continually dismantling and replacing themselves. A grove may fold flat in less than an hour, its trunks separating into articulated strips that are drawn underground. Elsewhere, a field of pylons thrusts upward, branches locking together to form a new processing canopy. Root cables pull tight. Black plates tilt. Towers slump into fresh configurations. Whole districts migrate a few meters at a time as their foundations creep across the buried lattice.

Seen from above, these rearrangements would resemble weather moving over a city: fronts of opening leaves, spirals of retracting chimneys, waves of structural collapse followed by immediate regrowth. From within, the changes feel local and abrupt. A corridor exists at dusk and is filled by midnight with packed thorn-pipes. A wall flowers into valves. A stairway softens, bends downward, and becomes a root.

For all this turbulence, the plateau is mostly quiet. There is no wind in the lower labyrinths, and the moving tissues use neither gears nor explosive combustion. Pressure shifts pass through flexible chambers with a low muscular hush. Resin slides through pipes. Dry scales touch and separate with the faint sound of cards being shuffled in another room.

At long intervals, a deep concussion travels through the structure—not loud so much as total. Every surface trembles at once. Dust rises from creases. The black leaf-tiles tighten against one another. Somewhere far below, an ancient pump has reversed, or a district-sized pressure vessel has changed phase.

The heat is relentless. It comes from above as a weak but punishing stellar glare and from below as industrial metabolism. In exposed places, the air shivers over the dark surfaces. In the interior galleries, temperatures are higher still: kiln heat, refinery heat, the heat of compression and slow chemical work. The vegetation protects itself not by cooling but by tolerance. Its proteins are replaced by heat-stable analogues; its membranes are waxy laminates; its sap remains functional at temperatures that would cook ordinary forests to ash.

Shade covers almost everything, but it offers little relief.

The sky is a nearly black violet. A swollen sun is visible only as a dull bruise behind airborne carbon dust. Most illumination comes from the land itself: threads of orange where hot sap shows through translucent vessels, red furnace-veins under bark, dim green sparks moving along conductive phloem. These lights are functional rather than decorative. They mark electrical load, chemical stress, readiness to divide. When a whole field glows at once, it does not brighten the plateau so much as reveal how much darkness was already there.

There is no exposed stone. If stone ever formed the highland, it was consumed or covered ages ago. The present substrate is wholly manufactured: foundation trusses grown from lignin and carbide, pressure cells, root mats, maintenance cavities, sealed reservoirs of non-aqueous solvent. Even the dust is made. It consists of abraded bark-ceramic, spent catalyst, pollen-like machine spores, and the powdered remains of obsolete organs.

Age has rounded the place despite its incessant rebuilding. The tallest works have long since been cut down by heat stress and structural fatigue. Ancient towers survive only as broad stumps, their edges polished by centuries of sliding plates. Former vertical cities have settled into terraces. Repeated growth, collapse, digestion, and regrowth have lowered every ambitious shape toward a stable profile. The plateau is elevated, but its surface is old and almost level: not untouched, but revised so many times that all extremes have been edited away.

Its erosion is internal.

Layers are not worn down by rain or rivers. They are softened by use, reclaimed by roots, dissolved in hot solvent, and fed back into production. A bridge does not crumble into a ravine; it becomes brittle, is tasted by diagnostic filaments, and disappears into a digestion trench. A dead district is consumed from underneath until its towers sink intact, slowly, into the processing dark. Nothing becomes ruin for long unless the system has forgotten its purpose.

There are forgotten places.

Toward the plateau’s center lie broad depressions where the living machinery grows denser and lower, as though avoiding something buried. Here the black tiles are ancient and scarred, patched with generations of incompatible tissue. They do not open. Root traffic bends around them. The only sound is an irregular clicking far below, separated by minutes or hours.

At the edge of one depression stands a ring of columnar plants whose upper branches have fused into a roof. Their trunks contain empty chambers shaped for bodies: doors, thresholds, handrails, sleeping recesses, narrow observation windows. These features are still carefully maintained. Dust is filtered from the rooms. Surfaces are repaired. Warm amber lights awaken when anything passes near, though nothing has passed for ages except exploratory roots.

The builders are absent. Perhaps they were animals once. The plateau retains no category for them now beyond a set of architectural reflexes.

It continues to make rooms.

It grows corridors sized for vanished shoulders, raises platforms where vanished feet might stand, and produces fruit-like control consoles that wait for pressure from vanished hands. When these structures remain unused, the vegetation does not abandon them. It seals them, digests them, and tries again elsewhere with minor variations. The entire region may be engaged in this blind iteration: an ecosystem preserving an industrial instruction whose meaning has been lost.

At the plateau’s outer margins, the constructed ground ends in steep, layered escarpments. From there its depth becomes visible. Kilometers of woven roots and structural foam descend into darkness, cross-braced like the hull of an immeasurable ship. The underside is studded with dormant seed foundries and heat exchangers. Far below hangs a dry haze over a basin of salt and black glass.

Nothing grows in that basin. Everything living is above, packed into the elevated machine-forest, layer on layer, with scarcely a void that has not become pipe, chamber, wall, vein, or waiting room.

Occasionally a section of the rim fails.

The collapse begins without warning. A dark plain wrinkles. Towers lean together. Root trusses part in sequence, silently at first, and a district the size of a town folds over the edge. It falls as one entangled mass, glowing red along its ruptures. There is no cry and almost no crash; the thin air cannot carry one properly. The impact arrives as a slow vibration through the plateau’s body.

Before the tremor has ended, repair growth begins. Pale extrusions push from the wound. Cables cross the gap. Black leaves unroll over open heat. The edge thickens, reaches outward, and resumes constructing itself above the abyss.

The place is ancient enough to seem finished and active enough never to be complete. It has reached its final form, but its final form is continuous upheaval: a dark, dry, hot expanse of vegetation making architecture, architecture cultivating machinery, and machinery endlessly rebuilding the vegetation.

Under the lightless sky, the plateau works on in near silence, preparing a world for occupants that will never return.
