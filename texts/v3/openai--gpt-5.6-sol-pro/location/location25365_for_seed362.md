The plain lies lower than any sea, though there are no seas here and perhaps never were.

From a distance it appears green. Closer, green divides into a thousand manufactured colors: bottle glass, oxidized copper, bruised violet, the black-green of photovoltaic skin. The plain is not covered by vegetation. It is vegetation, continuous to the horizon, rooted in nothing because there is no underlying soil. Beneath every trunk, mat, hedge, and luminous blade are further layers of growth—compressed centuries of structural fiber, ceramic bark, carbon lattice, and obsolete root-machinery. Drill downward and one does not reach bedrock. One reaches older gardens.

The entire lowland was made.

Its first builders are gone, or have become too dispersed to distinguish from what they built. They laid an immense cellular floor across the basin, divided it into production beds, power corridors, cooling tracts, and seed foundries. Then they populated it with engineered plants capable of conducting electricity, refining minerals, and repairing their own architecture. Those plants altered the plans. Their descendants inherited both factory and garden, until the difference ceased to have meaning.

Now the landscape manufactures itself.

There is almost no free water. Moisture would be a contaminant here, a violent solvent that swells the dry tissues and shorts the oldest circuitry. The plants use non-aqueous sap: pale silicone oils, liquid ammonia compounds held under pressure, slow ionic gels that remain mobile in the cool air. A broken stem does not bleed. It extrudes a clear filament that stiffens instantly, drawing the wound shut like glass being spun.

The ground gives slightly underfoot. It is made of interlocked leaf-plates, each broad as a room and tessellated with fine metallic veins. Their surfaces are dry, dusty, and pleasantly cool. Between them rise low forests of columnar growths: ribbed towers with ceramic skins, squat branching manifolds, fields of thin black vanes that turn imperceptibly toward the light. None are especially tall. The basin dislikes height. Anything that rises too far is pared down by the high bright winds or dismantled by neighboring growth. The result is an enormous, dense horizontality—continent after continent of woven crowns, terraces, ducts, and shallow green machinery.

There is no visible sun.

Brightness enters from everywhere.

The sky is a white vault without depth, emitting a cool, even radiance that casts several faint shadows in incompatible directions. At certain hours the light becomes so intense that every edge is silver and the far horizon disappears into brilliance. Yet there is little warmth in it. The leaves harvest illumination without heating, passing energy downward into buried chemical mills. At night—if the periodic dimming deserves that name—the vault fades only to pearl gray. The garden continues by its own stored charge, veins pulsing beneath translucent bark.

Weather occurs within the vegetation rather than above it.

Broad districts open and close their leaves in sequence, shifting air across the basin. When the eastern beds unfold, they pull a cold wind toward themselves; when the western forests seal their stomata, pressure rises and sends dry currents through the intervening corridors. These currents carry pollen, conductive dust, coded spores, and flakes of worn bark. There is no rain. Storms are migrations of powder: yellow phosphorus haze, black carbon bloom, glittering clouds of seed-foil. They travel close to the ground, pouring through archways and root canyons, polishing exposed surfaces over centuries.

This is how erosion works here. Nothing is washed away. Everything is abraded, harvested, and reincorporated.

Old structures have become smooth from the passing of dust and leaves. Corners retreat. Inscriptions blur. Great pipes, once perfectly cylindrical, have been worn into fluted shapes resembling ancient trunks. Where the cellular floor has buckled, long shallow ridges cross the plain. Vegetation colonizes the stress lines, reinforcing some and prying others wider. The oldest ridges are rounded and densely quilted with small, hard rosettes. Their roots extract traces of nickel and cobalt from ancestral supports. In places, whole districts have sagged into broad depressions where cold gas gathers and silver moss-machines flourish.

The industrial zones are not separate from the forest. They are simply where the forest performs certain functions more visibly.

Foundries resemble groves of thick hollow stems. Their inner surfaces grow refractory scales; inside, electric currents reduce mineral dust into threads of metal. Assembly orchards carry unfinished components as fruit: bearings in translucent husks, coils hanging in bunches, jointed struts swelling from knots in the wood. When mature, these parts loosen and descend along braided tendrils to the floor, where crawling rootlets sort them.

Elsewhere stand the compressor woods. Their trunks inhale through millions of pores, separate useful gases, and send them through buried vascular lines. They should roar. They do not.

The air is thin, and most moving parts are soft, balanced, and grown to microscopic tolerances. Across the plain, immense work proceeds in near silence. One hears only a faint papery ticking as leaves adjust to the light, the dry whisper of dust against ceramic bark, and occasionally a low tone conducted through the floor rather than through the air. The tone may last for hours. It can be felt in the ankles and teeth. It is the sound of some distant district changing pressure, or of a root system the size of a country making a decision.

Speech carries badly. A shout seems to fall at one’s feet.

Touch the ground, however, and the landscape is loud.

Vibrations overlap in dense layers: pumps cycling thousands of kilometers away, seed presses striking, shutters opening, charged sap rising, root fronts grinding through old partitions. The garden’s true atmosphere is mechanical sensation. Many native structures communicate by tapping or tightening the substrate. Messages pass as minute changes in tension. A grove may know of an approaching dust front days before the sky changes color.

Animals are rare and seem almost accidental.

There are long, pale things that live inside warm conduits and feed on insulating wax. They have no mouths in the usual sense; they press their bodies against the conduit wall and absorb charge differentials. Mirror-winged insects appear during pollen shifts, sometimes only one or two in an entire valley. They fly without noise and land on bright surfaces, where their reflections continue moving after they have become still.

Larger creatures are known mostly by damage. A line of bitten-off sensor buds. A collapsed nest of fiber. Tracks in the dust shaped like nested parentheses. There are stories of a six-legged browser crossing the northern production beds, tall enough to graze from transmission crowns, but its sightings occur decades apart and never in chronological order.

Most ecological competition takes place among the plants themselves.

Fast-growing cable vines seek live currents and wrap them in insulating tissue, stealing a fraction of the flow. Glass lichens cloud the light-catching skins of older leaves. Parasitic blossoms attach to communication nodes and inject false seasonal signals, persuading entire groves to release seed at the wrong time. In response, maintenance thickets grow needle-fine probes, testing every passing tendril for familiar electrical signatures.

Predation is slow. A grove may surround another over three hundred years, intercepting its light and rerouting its gases until the enclosed organism powers down. Its structure is then opened chamber by chamber and converted into new growth. Nothing decays quickly in the dry cold. Dead districts remain standing for millennia, gray and rigid, while living filaments patiently enter their seams.

There are roads, though nothing travels on them now.

They run absolutely straight across the undulating plain, bordered by low white curbs that vegetation cannot cross. Some law older than the current ecosystem prevents roots from touching them. Dust settles there but will not remain; by morning it has arranged itself in thin parallel lines along the edges. The roads converge on facilities whose purposes have been forgotten: immense circular apertures sealed with living membrane, stairways descending into bright cavities, plazas marked with numbers that alter when no one is looking.

Distance behaves badly near these places.

A tower seen ten kilometers away may require months to reach. A grove directly behind it may be entered in three steps. Straight roads sometimes return to their own beginnings without curving. The white sky is reflected in surfaces that do not face upward. Shadows detach from stems during the brightest hours and lie flat across open ground, pointing toward structures that have not yet been built.

The plants accommodate these failures as if they were weather.

Some orient their leaves toward future light. Some root into absent layers, their lower branches vanishing cleanly at the floor and drawing nourishment from spaces that cannot be excavated. In the southern basin, a vast orchard exists in several stages at once. One walks among saplings, mature towers, and polished stumps occupying the same coordinates. Their leaves pass through one another without resistance. At intervals all stages align, producing a momentary wall of solid green that extends from the floor into the white vault. Then they separate again.

Near the center of the plain lies the oldest visible construction: a depression hundreds of kilometers wide, shallow enough that its slope is almost imperceptible. All roads avoid it. All root lines eventually enter it.

The vegetation there becomes lower, denser, and more elaborate. Forests compress into mats of braided organs. Leaves overlap until no floor remains visible. Every surface is active: opening, sampling, exchanging charge, manufacturing spores, measuring pressure. The whole depression contracts by a few centimeters each year and relaxes again. During contraction, the peripheral groves lean inward. During release, a wave passes outward through the continent, lifting leaf-plates one after another.

At the depression’s center, brightness gathers in a narrow vertical seam.

It is not a building and not an opening. It has no measurable width, yet its light can be seen from anywhere on the plain if one looks between structures at the correct angle. The garden builds toward it continually. Vines extend scaffolds, flowers produce lenses, root foundries fabricate delicate frames and carry them inward. As they approach the seam, their geometry becomes less certain. Branches acquire extra sides. Rings close without enclosing space. Green surfaces turn inside out and continue growing.

No completed structure has ever been observed there.

Still, the work does not stop.

Across the cool, brilliant lowland, shutters breathe, sap ascends, powders migrate, and silent factories flower. Old forms are worn smooth and fed into new ones. The continent makes leaves to catch the light, engines to move the gases, roots to remember the plans, and seeds to revise them. It has long outlived the intention that began it.

Whether it is maintaining itself, constructing something, or slowly germinating is impossible to say.
