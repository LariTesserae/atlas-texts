At a great altitude, though there is no mountain beneath it, hangs a small, entirely fabricated plateau.

It is scarcely larger than a walled hamlet. Its boundary is sharp: a many-sided rim of fitted stone, beyond which the air darkens immediately into depth. No cliff descends from it. The underside cannot be seen. The place rests on neither earth nor bedrock; its paving is its ground, its foundations are further pavements, and beneath those are older courses of masonry packed so tightly that “below” seems to mean only another layer of construction.

Every surface bears the evidence of hands, though no hands remain.

The plateau is crowded almost beyond passage. Low walls press against pillars; pillars lean into buttresses; buttresses have been wedged with tablets, plaques, pegs, chipped bowls, blunt stone teeth, sealed jars, and stacks of thin fired tiles. There are no open courtyards. What first appears to be an empty lane proves to be a shallow trough roofed with translucent slabs. Doorways are packed with smaller doors. Niches contain miniature chambers whose floors are crowded with models of still smaller masonry. Even cracks have been deliberately filled, first with mortar, then with flakes of ceramic, then with hammered foil no thicker than old skin.

The materials are simple: basalt, pale saltstone, clay fired nearly black, copper beaten without refinement, bone-colored glass, cords mineralized into rigid twists. Nothing suggests precision machinery. Every block was dressed by percussion and abrasion. Holes wander slightly where they were bored from opposite sides. Copper clamps are lumpy from repeated hammering. The tiles preserve thumbprints, palm heels, the drag marks of crude combs. Yet the cumulative construction is total. There is not one grain here that was not selected, carried, shaped, set, or used as packing around something else.

Age has nearly erased workmanship without undoing it. Steps have become smooth ramps. Inscriptions have worn down to shallow differences in sheen. The corners of pillars are rounded as if generations of invisible bodies had brushed past them, although there is no wind and there have been no bodies for an immeasurable span. Relief carvings are reduced to broad, uncertain humps: perhaps faces, perhaps tools, perhaps maps of structures buried deeper in the plateau. Copper has lost its green corrosion and become dark, polished, and chemically settled. The oldest clay no longer resembles pottery. It has relaxed into something like natural stone, though nature had no part in placing it.

There is no water. No cistern holds even a stain of former dampness. Basins are present by the hundreds, but they are filled with dry beads, powdered glass, nested cups, or tightly fitted lids. Channels run through the paving, yet they do not slope and could never have carried a stream. Their purpose seems acoustic rather than hydraulic. Dust, where it occurs, is not loose. It has compacted into hard velvet-colored skins along the sheltered seams.

The air is warm and exceedingly dry. It has the closeness of a sealed kiln long after firing: not hot enough to burn, but too warm for relief. There are no currents. Heat is distributed perfectly. A hand placed against black ceramic, copper, pale stone, or the dim air itself would find the same temperature everywhere. Nothing radiates more strongly than anything else. There are no cool hollows and no sun-warmed faces. Temperature has ceased to be an event.

Light comes from the construction without visibly being produced by it. The pale tiles show a weak amber-gray luminosity, barely enough to separate one crowded object from the next. Shadows exist, but they are shallow and directionless. No lamp burns. No aperture opens onto a sky. Reflective strips of old metal repeat the dimness rather than brighten it, making narrow passages appear occupied by rows of dull, parallel chambers.

There are no plants, no lichen, no fungal bloom, no animals, and no sign of anything feeding upon anything else. The place has no ecology in the usual sense. Nothing grows, decomposes, hunts, colonizes, or competes. At most there is an ecology of contact: copper touches clay; clay presses saltstone; saltstone is enclosed by glass; glass carries the load of another wall. Each material occupies its appointed neighboring surfaces. Their relationships are structural, not living.

And over everything is the sound.

It is deafening, but it does not begin, end, pulse, approach, or recede. The plateau is immersed in one continuous, perfectly stable concussion: a low stone roar braided with a higher metallic shriek. It fills the sealed channels, the stacked jars, the hollow pillars, the bowls nested mouth-to-mouth. The sound is too dense to identify as ringing. Nothing can be seen moving. Dust does not tremble. Foil does not flutter. The rigid cords remain rigid. Even the smallest loose-looking shard is fixed in place.

The sound is simply a property of the space, as altitude and warmth are properties of it. One does not hear it travel. There is no echo, because an echo would imply sequence. There are no beats, because beating would imply two frequencies slipping past one another. The roar is the same beside the rim, inside a niche, with the ear against stone, or deep within the most crowded passage. It is so constant that after a time it seems less like noise than a pressure upon thought.

Speech, if attempted, vanishes absolutely. A struck stone yields no distinguishable note. Clapping produces no interruption. The world permits sound but does not permit change in sound.

This appears to be the governing rule of the plateau: all processes have already reached their final condition. Nothing is forbidden from existing, but nothing is allowed to become. The place may contain fracture, wear, tension, resonance, warmth, and age, yet none can advance. A cracked tile remains forever at the exact limit before separation. A leaning pillar has completed its lean without falling. Powder is caught at perfect compaction. Stretched copper is under stress that will never relax. The ancient erosion visible everywhere is not ongoing erosion; it is erosion brought here already finished, or else the plateau once possessed time and has since used all of it.

Near the center, where the construction is thickest, stands the closest thing to a landmark: a squat chamber assembled from hundreds of millstones. Their holes face inward, outward, upward, and into one another. None could turn. The gaps between them are packed with pestles, wedges, and palm-sized stone hammers. There is no entrance except a slot too narrow for a person, and that slot has been filled with a column of clay tokens, each stamped with the same nearly erased mark.

Around this chamber the paths tighten into a ring no wider than a shoulder. The walls on either side are crowded with shallow handprints. Some are large, some very small. They overlap until individual impressions are difficult to follow, making the surface resemble scales or windless waves. The prints are not memorials applied to a preexisting wall; they are the wall. Each palm-shaped piece of clay was pressed separately and fitted to the others while soft, a masonry made from the record of touch.

Above, at a height comparable to a cathedral roof, the dimness closes into a ceiling of suspended slabs. They are packed edge to edge, with no beams visibly holding them. Their lower faces have been abraded nearly featureless. Here and there a surviving groove suggests that the whole ceiling was once carved with a single enormous diagram—perhaps of the plateau, perhaps of the sound, perhaps of the tool by which a world without natural ground was first assembled.

At the outer rim, the packed construction stops without thinning. Walls, vessels, paving courses, and buried rooms all end in one polished vertical plane. Beyond it is no visible terrain. There may be a void, a gas-dark sky, or simply a region omitted from the making. No breeze comes from it. No star crosses it. Objects dropped over the edge—if anything here could be loosened enough to drop—might not fall so much as leave the jurisdiction of the place.

The plateau feels neither ruined nor preserved. Ruin suggests a direction, preservation a struggle. This is later than both.

It is a finished landscape: handmade to the last particle, worn to the last permissible degree, crowded with the residue of purposes that can no longer be performed. High in the lightless altitude, warm as old fired clay, it holds its deafening note without vibration and its impossible weight without support.

Nothing enters. Nothing departs. Nothing happens.

The sound remains.
