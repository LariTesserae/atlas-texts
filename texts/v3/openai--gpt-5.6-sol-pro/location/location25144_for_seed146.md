### The Still Below

Far beneath a surface no inhabitant has seen, a hollow extends beyond any ordinary horizon.

It is not a single cavern. It is a buried world of connected chambers, pressure vaults, mineral forests, and low passages opening without warning into immensities large enough to possess their own weather. Entire mountain chains hang downward from the ceiling. Plains lie between them, level as poured metal and blacker than the air above. Distances are difficult to judge because almost nothing shines, and because the far wall may be five hundred paces away or five hundred leagues.

There is no open water here. The warmth is absolute and dry.

Water survives only as a defect in certain crystals, one molecule caught among millions of mineral bonds. Some ancient bones contain a trace. A few sealed stones will exhale a dampness if broken, but the vapor is immediately taken up by salt and disappears. Nothing drips. Nothing condenses. No river has cut these valleys. Their channels were formed all at once, as fractures, when the underworld opened.

For this reason the terrain looks newborn.

Edges remain sharp enough to divide skin. Slopes are heaps of angular fragments resting exactly where they first fell. Black glass shelves project over chasms without rounding or sagging. There are no dunes despite the abundance of powder, no softened corners, no stains spreading downward from old rain. Pale fault faces retain bands of rose salt, sulfur-yellow stone, iron gray, and the green-black sheen of minerals that would decompose in a wetter world.

The place is warm in the manner of a closed hand. Every stone, breath, shell, and buried wall has nearly the same temperature. There are no hot springs or cold drafts. Heat does not seem to travel from anywhere to anywhere else; it is simply one of the fixed conditions of existence.

The air is dense, dry, and chemically elaborate. It carries argon, sulfur compounds, metallic vapors, and a fine suspension of electrically charged dust. Breathing creatures do not inhale continuously. They open rows of small valves at long intervals, take in a measured volume, seal themselves again, and extract what they need over hours. An unprotected human breath would taste of old coins and struck matches. The throat would dry almost at once.

Underfoot, the great plains are not empty. They are crowded with forms.

Clusters of hexagonal columns rise waist-high, then shoulder-high, then become dense enough to make passage impossible. Glass needles fill the hollows between them. Iron nodules lie packed like eggs. Curtains of fibrous mineral descend from above, each strand rigid and pale. In some regions the floor is covered by overlapping plates so closely fitted that they resemble scales on an immeasurable buried animal.

The formations do not grow. They do not weather. They seem caught at the first instant after creation.

Nothing here behaves quite like landscape under an open sky. There is no day or night. There are no seasons. The pressure does not appreciably rise or fall. Even the faint subterranean tides repeat so perfectly that they produce no cumulative alteration. A flake loosened from a wall may remain suspended in a crack for generations. Dust disturbed by a foot does not drift far; electrostatic forces draw it back to the nearest surface, where it settles into almost the same pattern.

Yet the place is full of life.

At first the caverns appear lifeless because the animals have adopted the stillness of the mineral world. Once the eye learns their proportions, they become impossible not to see.

Thousands cling to every distant face.

There are broad, thin creatures resembling sheets of folded leather, each attached to the rock by hundreds of glassy hooks. Their upper surfaces imitate the crystalline grain beneath them. Once every few hours, a sheet loosens one edge, advances the width of a finger, and fastens itself again. Vast colonies migrate across a wall in this way, but so slowly and evenly that their arrangement remains unchanged to a casual observer.

Between the columns stand tall jointed animals with narrow bodies and too many legs. Their shells are dull ceramic, strengthened with iron salts. Most have no eyes. Around their heads they carry branching feelers that detect pressure, electric charge, and the minute strains passing through the stone. They remain motionless until several nearby creatures move; then all take one simultaneous step. A forest of legs lifts and settles together without apparent signal.

Smaller life crowds every crevice. Bead-sized scavengers roll along magnetic lines. White burrowers consume sulfur crusts, leaving tunnels that collapse behind them. Flat armored things slide between plates of stone so precisely that they seem to vanish into a solid floor. Threadlike colonies inhabit faults only a hair wide, drawing energy from the contact between unlike minerals. They are not plants, fungi, or anything easily separated into individuals. Each thread is a chain of mineral cells whose chemistry proceeds in solvents more like hot wax than water.

There is abundant predation, though it is almost never witnessed.

A hunter may wait in one position for decades, indistinguishable from a black shard. When prey comes within reach, the strike is instantaneous and silent: a hinge opens, a body is taken, and the hinge closes. Digestion may occupy the following year. Hunger is not urgent in a world without seasons.

Other creatures do not eat bodies at all. They feed on strain.

Where the ceiling presses against a pillar, or where two continental slabs hold one another in tension, dense colonies gather. Their shells contain piezoelectric organs. They draw tiny charges from the burden of the rock, using the imprisoned force of the world as metabolism. They do not weaken the stone enough to cause change. The energy they take is replenished by pressure so constant that predator, pillar, and planet remain in equilibrium.

This is the principal rule of the Still Below: **nothing may alter the whole**.

Every movement is paired. When one animal crosses a plain, another mass shifts elsewhere. When a colony takes charge from a fault, its waste restores an equivalent chemical potential. Birth occurs, but so does sealing. Death occurs, but bodies are dismantled and fitted back into the same mineral cycles. Populations fluctuate locally while remaining exact in total, as though the buried world permits rearrangement but not increase or loss.

The silence is correspondingly profound.

There is no wind. No water runs. The animals do not call across distance because sound wastes energy and reveals position. Most communication travels through touch, electric fields, or pressure transmitted along the rock.

What can be heard comes rarely: the dry click of a breathing valve; a faint collective ticking when a colony changes its grip; the papery slide of an armored body between stone plates. In the largest vaults there is sometimes a note too low to hear directly. It is felt in the teeth and long bones, a continuous pressure-tone generated by the weight of the ceiling. It has never begun and does not vary. The creatures align themselves to it as surface life might orient to sunlight.

Light exists only in accidents.

A disturbed crystal emits a blue spark. Certain hunters flash a deep red line along their joints when closing a trap. A few mineral-feeding colonies discharge their excess energy as a glow so dim that it becomes visible only after hours in darkness. These colonies outline distant escarpments in pale green constellations, giving the impression of stars arranged vertically and beneath one’s feet.

They are not enough to illuminate the world. They reveal fragments: one blade, one shell, the curve of a tunnel, a field of watching sensory branches. Each glimpse makes the darkness around it seem larger.

Across this vastness are scattered constructions.

They are sparse enough that many journeys pass none, but deliberate enough not to be mistaken for geology. Low walls of fitted stone seal narrow side chambers. Bridges of shell and mineral fiber span cracks that could have been walked around in a few days. At certain junctions, stacks of flat plates form roofed enclosures barely large enough for a crouching body. Their entrances are covered by curtains of interlocked chitin rings, each ring shaped by abrasion rather than casting.

No mortar is used. Nothing is smelted on a large scale. Tools are chipped from volcanic glass, ground from hard shell, or made by encouraging mineral-eating organisms to hollow precise grooves. Simple copper appears in hooks and needles, always cold-worked, hammered patiently between stone anvils. Broken implements are never abandoned. Every fragment is reused until it becomes dust, and the dust is pressed into ceramic.

The builders are difficult to distinguish from the surrounding fauna.

They may be the narrow, many-legged creatures that stand among the columns. Some wear belts of braided tendon and carry bundles of blades. Others bear no artifacts. Their settlements have no fires, refuse heaps, or cleared ground. A habitation remains ecologically invisible: a slightly more regular arrangement of stones, a sealed cache, a row of tactile marks cut beneath an overhang.

Their maps are not pictures of space. They are sequences of contact: seventeen columns, a sulfur seam, a low ceiling, three pressure-notes, the shell bed, the bridge. Distance is measured not in steps but in scheduled breaths.

The oldest-looking structures are not ancient. Nothing looks ancient here. A wall raised ten thousand years ago retains the raw facets of yesterday’s work. A chipped inscription remains sharp. The lack of erosion compresses history until all events seem to have occurred at once.

Some constructions have no obvious use. In immense empty chambers stand solitary rings assembled from white stone, each several hundred paces across. Their interiors contain the same crowded mineral forms and motionless animals as the land outside. In other regions, builders have laid straight paths for hundreds of leagues, though every creature capable of using them could cross the rough ground.

Perhaps these are measurements. Perhaps they are agreements with the equilibrium of the world. Perhaps each path compensates for some movement elsewhere.

The greatest chamber is said to have no floor.

At its edge, the mineral plains end in a clean fracture and the darkness continues downward. From the ceiling hang countless black filaments, disappearing into the gulf. Animals crowd them from end to end: hooked grazers, armored climbers, bead colonies, pale sensory fans. Their accumulated bodies form vertical continents. Tiny red flashes pass among them in patterns so slow that a complete signal may require a lifetime.

Far below, something returns the pressure-tone.

It is not an echo. An echo would fade. This answering vibration is equal, continuous, and perfectly opposed to the note of the ceiling. Between the two tones the entire underworld is held in balance.

Nothing falls into the gulf. Nothing climbs out. Nothing changes.

And still, everywhere in the dark, the crowded life of the place performs its minute exchanges: one hooked foot released and fastened, one crystal discharged, one shell opened for breath. Across a world-sized cavern, millions of creatures move by fractions, restoring with every action the stillness they have disturbed.
