There is no seabed here.

The water continues downward until pressure becomes geography, and through it rises a structure so tall that no creature living within one region can know both its summit and its roots. It resembles a mountain only from a great distance. Up close, every apparent cliff resolves into workmanship: terraces fitted together, buttresses braided from trunks, arches grown in place, galleries lined with overlapping leaves. No stone lies beneath them. There is no mineral foundation. The entire elevation is a construction of living plant matter, raised over ages by growth, grafting, collapse, and regrowth.

It is called neither mountain nor city by its inhabitants, but it has aspects of both.

Its highest fins climb into cold upper water where a dim, permanent twilight survives. Its lowest foundations descend through absolute blackness. Between them are vertical provinces separated not by borders but by pressure: hanging forests, drowned courtyards, root bridges, open wells miles across. Great distances are empty water. Vegetation is total, yet not crowded. A single column may be broader than a fortress and stand alone in blue-black space, with the next visible structure only as a deeper interruption in the darkness.

Everything has been made, though no tool has ever existed here.

The oldest plants grow according to inherited forms. Branches divide at load-bearing angles. Roots knot themselves into vaults. Leaves overlap like roof tiles, shedding currents rather than rain. Hollow stems are arranged into pipes and resonant chambers. Seedlings emerge along measured seams, filling damage with pale cellular masonry. What appears to be ornament—spirals around door-like openings, repeated ribs along parapets—is structural memory expressed as growth.

No architect directs it. The design is older than intention.

The material is cool to the touch and almost impossibly aged. Outer bark has been worn smooth by water until it resembles dark bronze or polished bone. Softer tissues vanished long ago, leaving dense green-black wood impregnated with salts. Broad ramps have been rounded into gentle saddles. Stair-like shelves have lost their edges. Once-elaborate screens survive as rows of blunt posts, each upholstered in a thin living skin.

Erosion has not ruined the place. Erosion is part of its completed form.

Nothing sharp remains. Nothing unfinished remains. Every surface has reached the shape toward which pressure and water were slowly pushing it. The mountain-city seems less decayed than settled, as if an unimaginably long argument between growth and wearing-away has ended in agreement.

The water itself is nearly still. There are no storms at these depths, no seasons that can be measured, and scarcely any distinction between one hour and the next. Fine particles hang in place for weeks. Curtains of filamentous leaves extend horizontally from galleries without fluttering. Long fronds bend under the faint burden of the water and retain that bend through generations.

Yet the place is loud.

The sound comes through the structure more than through the surrounding sea. Pressure presses upon a million hollow chambers. Vast trunks hum at pitches too low to hear cleanly, felt instead in the jaw and ribs. Narrow root tubes whistle. Layered leaf membranes murmur against one another with the steadiness of distant rain. In the deepest shafts, the whole construction produces a continuous groan, not of strain but of equilibrium: the voice of immense weight perfectly held.

Animals add sharper notes. Shell-creatures click from inside the walls. Blind grazing shoals rasp the bark in unison, producing sudden sweeps of sound like sand dragged across glass. Long-bodied swimmers call through the arches, and each call returns altered by miles of cultivated hollows. A small sound may circle a chamber for minutes. A mating cry may travel vertically through an entire province and arrive as thunder.

There is little light. Most of what can be seen is disclosed by living signals.

Minute blue animals cling to the edges of terraces, outlining them in broken points. Pale worms inhabit the seams between woody plates and shine only when touched. Larger swimmers carry rows of muted green organs along their bellies, enough to reveal a passing flank or the underside of an arch before darkness closes again. The plants themselves make almost no light. Their foliage is black, adapted to collect the faintest radiance drifting down from far above or produced nearby by animals.

Occasionally an enormous wall appears in this weak illumination: a plane of overlapping leaves hundreds of meters high, each leaf broad, tough, and motionless. The wall is not solid. It filters water through microscopic pores and feeds the interior chambers with dissolved nutrients. Behind it lie open rooms where transparent animals graze on suspended pollen.

The fauna is abundant without making the landscape feel full. Most creatures are tiny, distributed through the vastness like dust through a cathedral. Every surface bears life: flat shellfish set flush into bark, threadlike grazers in the vascular grooves, jointed larvae inside abandoned seed cups. Clouds of pin-sized swimmers inhabit the dark between columns. They keep their distances from one another, each pulsing within a small invisible territory.

Larger animals are encountered rarely but are never truly absent. Their calls and feeding marks are everywhere.

Broad, wing-finned browsers move along the vertical gardens, cropping old foliage while leaving the structural ribs untouched. Their mouths have combs rather than teeth. They follow permanent routes inherited without learning, passing the same openings in the same order. Schools of narrow silver fish occupy the great wells. They spiral so slowly that their arrangement seems fixed, generations replacing generations without altering the shape of the school.

In the lowest regions dwell heavy animals that never swim freely. They walk along the hanging foundations upside down and upright interchangeably, gripping the wood with many soft feet. Their bodies are colorless. Gardens of small plants grow on their backs, making them moving annexes of the architecture. Because they travel only a few body lengths in a year, entire communities of shell-creatures mistake them for terrain.

Predation exists, but even violence has been absorbed into stability. Hunters take the same proportion of prey. Browsers remove the same annual growth. Carcasses are dismantled before they can sink far, their nutrients carried into root channels and distributed upward. Nothing accumulates. Nothing becomes scarce. Population surges do not occur. The ecosystem behaves less like a wilderness than like a completed mechanism, though none of its parts are mechanical.

There are openings that resemble doors, but they do not lead indoors. They lead from one kind of water to another.

A narrow arch may open into a chamber of slightly warmer brine where red fronds grow from the ceiling. A descending shaft may hold water dense with dissolved salts, so that a swimmer crossing into it seems to fall through an invisible floor. Some courtyards contain clear lenses of fresher water suspended within the sea, maintained by filtering membranes. Small animals live along these boundaries and never cross them.

Verticality governs everything. “Above” means thinner pressure, weaker sound, and occasional dimness. “Below” means deeper resonance, thicker water, and older wood. Distance is measured by the number of terraces passed, not by any horizontal span. Creatures migrate up and down rather than north or south. Many have different body forms at different levels: inflated in the upper reaches, compact and rigid below.

The summit is not a peak but a sparse crown of enormous branching masts. They rise into the dark water, separated by wide gulfs. Their uppermost leaves are thin and nearly transparent, spread to catch the scant light of the distant surface. No wave reaches them, but faint oscillations in brightness pass across them when storms occur far overhead. These are the closest thing the place has to weather.

The foundations are stranger.

Far below, where no light from above has ever arrived, the great supports divide into descending rootwork. They do not enter earth. They enter older construction. Each generation grows into the hollow skeletons of those before it, so the base consists of architecture nested within architecture: root around root, vault beneath vault, support resting upon support without any first ground.

No one knows whether there is a bottom beneath all that depth. The prevailing law of the place suggests there is not. The living mountain may be suspended from its own accumulated growth, balanced in water by buoyant chambers above and dense, salt-filled tissues below. It neither rises nor sinks. Broken fragments do not fall away; they are caught in lower nets of root and incorporated into new terraces.

Change happens, but never enough to alter the whole. A leaf detaches after three centuries. A corridor closes over in five hundred years. A grazing route shifts by the width of a hand. These events are not perceived as change by most inhabitants. The landscape’s apparent stillness is maintained by innumerable slow acts of repair.

The oldest chambers have become almost featureless. Water has smoothed their carvings, animals have polished their floors, and living tissues have sealed every unnecessary seam. They are vast dark volumes crossed by a few columns, each one sounding its unwavering note. To enter them is to feel that time has already happened—that the world has passed through growth, catastrophe, adaptation, and decay, and has finally arrived at a form from which it has no reason to depart.

There, in cold water beneath miles of living construction, darkness is not an absence but the settled color of the place.

The walls breathe too slowly to witness.

The mountain holds.

The sea holds the mountain.

And the great vegetal city continues making itself exactly as it already is.
