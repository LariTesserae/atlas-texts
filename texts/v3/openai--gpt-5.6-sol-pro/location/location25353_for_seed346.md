### The Low Quarter of Orison

The Low Quarter occupies a shallow depression no wider than a small town, though no map agrees on its perimeter. From the surrounding plain it appears densely urban: close-set towers, bridges, service conduits, stairwells, retaining walls, narrow courts. Yet the moment one descends into it, distance becomes unreliable. A building seen three streets away may require half a day to reach, while a window high across the district can sometimes be touched by leaning from the correct doorway.

The ground is almost level. There are no commanding heights, only slight grades toward gutters and collecting basins. Cold lies here without wind, pooled like an invisible liquid. It settles first in underpasses, elevator wells, and the narrow spaces between buildings. Metal handrails remain painful to touch even at midday. Breath clouds briefly, then drifts sideways toward the nearest wall and disappears into it.

The light is ordinary. This is perhaps the strangest thing about the place.

There is no colored sky, no perpetual twilight, no dramatic radiance from the machines. Morning arrives gray-white and sensible. Noon lays clean shadows across the paving. Lamps come on in the evening with a soft municipal yellow. Under this familiar illumination, the Quarter’s impossibilities are exposed without ceremony: balconies opening onto rooms below ground, shadows cast by absent towers, streets crossing one another at the same elevation without intersection.

The district is crowded with construction. Buildings stand shoulder to shoulder, their exterior walls often only a hand’s breadth apart. Some spaces have never been entered because no body can fit through them, though pipes, cables, and thin silver rails continue into the gaps. Facades are made from pale ceramic, dark basalt foam, vitrified brick, pressure-grown steel, and slabs of a translucent material in which slow internal weather can be seen. Condensation moves through those slabs in tiny cloud systems. Occasionally there is lightning, no larger than a fingernail.

No structure is entirely complete. Cornices end in porous stone buds. Staircases descend into packed clay and wait there, each lowest step warm from some process beneath. Walls have smooth, unfinished margins where additional rooms are expected to occur. The city does not appear ruined or abandoned; it appears embryonic, as if urban form has preceded the geology meant to support it.

In several courtyards, the ground is still deciding what kind of ground it will be.

Raw strata rise through the pavement in soft, folded ridges: chalk, black glass, salt, red grit, and materials without stable names. They are not yet fully hardened. A boot heel can leave a print in what looks like granite, while nearby mud rings like iron. Survey stakes surround these outbreaks. Their markings change slowly as the measurements they record become obsolete.

Erosion has only just begun. Gutters are shallow because rain has not yet taught water where to go. Cracks form in advance of the moisture that will someday widen them. On some walls, delicate channels appear overnight, tracing the future courses of runoff from storms that have not happened. Engineers preserve these marks under clear membranes. They call them anticipatory weathering.

Water is present but never abundant. It gathers in seams, bowls, and infrastructural hollows. Thin rain visits irregularly, usually falling for a few minutes from a perfectly unremarkable cloud. The droplets strike roofs and railings with muted clicks. Much of the rain fails to reach the ground: it pauses halfway down, beads in the air, and is collected by suspended mesh.

At street level, condensation is more dependable. It pearls along pipes and cold ceramic walls, feeding narrow cisterns sunk beneath grilles. There are pocket ponds in three plazas, each only a few meters across. Their surfaces remain dark and still, edged by mineral crusts and sparse tufts of wiregrass. One pond is deeper on the inside than the entire district is wide. A safety railing surrounds it, along with an old sign:

**NO DESCENT PAST LOCAL DEPTH**

The sign is routinely obeyed.

Vegetation survives in the places where the city’s systems leak. Lichen grows in square patches corresponding to hidden conduits. Colorless moss fills the lettering on drain covers. A few black reeds stand in the cistern courts, their hollow stalks chiming almost inaudibly when pressure changes underground. There are also low shrubs with glassy leaves, planted at regular intervals by an authority whose offices cannot be located. They produce no flowers. Every few years, one develops a single blue fruit containing a warm steel bearing.

Nothing here resembles wilderness. Life occurs as an annotation to infrastructure.

Fauna are rare. Small white moths appear near ventilation grilles, but only during daylight. Their wings carry floor plans of buildings that do not yet exist. There are pale, long-legged water insects on the plaza ponds and, occasionally, a solitary black bird on the overhead wires. The bird is always facing north, even on wires that curve or enter enclosed rooms. No nests have been found.

Something larger may inhabit the spaces between adjacent walls. Maintenance workers leave shallow trays of condensed water in certain alleys. By morning the trays are empty, though cameras record no visitor—only a brief interval in which the alley becomes narrower.

The Quarter was built with technologies that have outlived explanation. Its machines are quiet, compact, and apparently civic in purpose. They regulate pressure between incompatible rooms. They keep buildings from remembering previous locations. They remove surplus corners from apartments and store them in sealed depots beneath the tram station.

Throughout the district stand waist-high pylons of dull metal. Each contains a region where ordinary sequence has been folded into a loop less than a second long. These loops power the city by permitting the same minute physical event to happen endlessly: a bead falling, a spring relaxing, a temperature equalizing. Through small inspection windows one can sometimes see the repeated act—a droplet forever about to strike a plate, never accumulating, never wearing it away.

The system is not perpetual motion. The engineers insist on this distinction, though their explanations include tense forms unavailable in common speech.

Public transit consists of a single tram moving slowly around the district. It has no visible power source and makes almost no sound beyond the soft compression of its suspension. The full circuit takes forty-seven minutes from inside the tram and between six months and nine years for an observer standing outside. Residents use it sparingly. Its windows do not show the streets it passes; they show the same streets after different amounts of erosion. From one seat, the Quarter appears pristine and wetly new. From another, gutters have become ravines and the towers stand on isolated fins of stone. The final window shows only a flat basin with scattered foundations under cold rain.

There is no window showing the tram itself.

Despite the density of the place, it is mostly quiet. Walls absorb voices too efficiently. Footsteps are taken into the paving and released later, often several streets away. One may hear a stranger’s approach hours before the stranger begins walking, or wake at night to the faint tread of one’s own journey from the previous day.

The loudest regular sound is the settlement of unfinished geology: a deep, restrained tick from beneath the foundations, followed by a barely perceptible adjustment of the streets. Once every several weeks, all doors in the Quarter draw inward by a few millimeters. Once every few years, they relax again. The change is slow enough that residents blame their coats.

People do live here, though the population cannot be counted. Apartments are occupied; washing hangs in enclosed balconies; condensation collectors are emptied each morning. Shops open behind narrow shutters and sell dense bread, insulated cloth, mineral samples, repaired instruments, and keys with no teeth. At midday, workers cross the plazas in gray coats. They speak softly, partly from custom and partly because loud statements can become architecturally binding.

This is one of the Quarter’s rules: sufficiently definite descriptions tend to make space conform to them.

To say “this corridor has no end” is considered vandalism. To call a room empty may cause its contents to become inaccessible. Children are taught to use approximation early. Official notices employ cautious grammar:

**THIS STAIR MAY CURRENTLY SERVE FLOORS FOUR THROUGH SEVEN.**

**THE WEST WALL IS BELIEVED TO REMAIN LOAD-BEARING.**

**WATER HERE IS PROBABLY SHALLOW.**

Names are also handled carefully. Streets have provisional labels stamped on removable ceramic plates. Buildings are identified by material, temperature, or habit: the Warm Archive, the House That Condenses, the Southward Clinic. Permanent naming can trap a structure in a single interpretation, preventing repairs.

At the center of the Quarter is a compact cathedral-sized complex called the Foundation Office. It is neither religious nor administrative, though it has performed both roles. Its roof is low and crowded with antennae that do not point into the sky but toward possible versions of the ground. Inside, technicians observe the ongoing formation of the basin.

Their principal instrument is a circular room lined with black tiles. In its center hangs a rough stone about the size of a house. The stone is not suspended by cables or fields. Rather, the room is positioned around the stone’s absence from another location. Frost continually forms along its lower surface and drops upward. The technicians collect the frost in covered vessels and use it to stabilize new foundations.

The stone is believed to be the first piece of local geology.

It is also believed to be the last.

Both statements have tested true.

Beyond the outermost buildings, the district ends abruptly in a belt of coarse gravel and low frozen mud. Utility lines continue into the plain for several hundred meters, then curl back beneath themselves. There are sparse grasses, a few pools sealed by thin ice, and concrete markers waiting for construction that may already exist elsewhere.

From here the Low Quarter looks almost comprehensible. Towers cluster in the basin. Pale vapor rises from vents. The tram passes between blocks. Rain darkens the roofs in moving patches. Nothing flashes or levitates; nothing announces the scale of the underlying violation.

Only after watching for a long time does one notice that every building is settling into the earth at a different rate, and that the earth beneath them is rising to meet each foundation with equal patience.

The city and its terrain are constructing one another.

Neither has yet decided which came first.
