The place is called the Low Pocket, though it has no recognized boundary and appears on no plan. It lies beneath an old industrial floor, between the bedrock and the footings of machines whose upper halves were removed long ago. A person could cross it in less than an hour if there were a clear path. There is not.

The ground is nearly level. Whatever relief it once possessed has been worn down past hills, past dunes, past recognizable debris, into a close-packed pavement of gray mineral scales. The scales overlap like old roofing tiles. Some are natural laminations of shale and salt; others are fragments of ceramics, furnace lining, electrical insulators, and panes of safety glass rounded until they can no longer be distinguished from stones. A few stamped letters survive on them, each letter softened at the edges by ages of contact:

**R**, **17**, an arrow pointing nowhere.

There is no water. The air is so dry that unsealed water would not remain liquid for long, but even frost and vapor are absent. The minerals have been stripped of hydration. Metal does not rust in the familiar red, blooming way; it darkens, powders, and becomes electrically pitted. Organic material, when introduced, does not rot. It is pared into fibers, then into dust, then taken.

The Pocket is cool throughout the cycle, usually cold enough that exposed metal numbs the hand. Heat enters in pulses through buried cables and machine foundations. These warm intervals move across the floor in narrow fronts, and almost everything living moves with them.

There is no vegetation. No moss occupies the cracks, no pale root has forced its way down from above, and no fungal veil softens the dead surfaces. The living abundance belongs entirely to animals—or to things close enough in movement, appetite, and rivalry to have been given animal names.

They are everywhere.

At first the floor seems to be vibrating from distant machinery. Then the individual bodies resolve. Thousands of slate-colored runners pass over one another in braided streams. Pin-sized shieldbacks wedge themselves beneath the mineral scales. Long pale threaders hang from beams and lower themselves into traffic, snatching smaller creatures with hinged mouthparts. Dense colonies of copper mites cover warm conductors in a moving metallic felt. In deeper hollows, where powdered ceramic gathers, barrel-bodied grazers roll slowly through the dust, leaving compressed tracks behind them.

The smallest fauna are almost invisible: reflective specks that jump between electrical potentials, clustering around charged edges. They feed not on tissue but on films of reactive material only a few molecules thick. Larger creatures eat them by sweeping combs across the stone. Larger ones eat the sweepers. Nothing is nourished by sunlight, leaves, or waterborne chemistry. The economy of the Pocket is based on dry surfaces: oxidation, static charge, lubricants, sulfur compounds, halogen salts, powdered carbon, and the faint leakage of current from the industrial remains.

Their bodies contain very little conventional fluid. Flexible joints are packed with waxes, silicone-like oils, or low-freezing ionic gels sealed under hard plates. A punctured animal does not bleed. It stiffens around the wound, sheds the damaged limb if it can, and is usually dismantled before it dies. The scavengers arrive so quickly that death often appears to reverse growth: a large body breaking apart into dozens of smaller, brisker bodies.

Overhead, the industrial ceiling hangs low. It is not continuous. Slabs have shifted and settled until they form shelves, slots, and steep-sided chambers. Between them stand scattered relics of production: a waist-high switch cabinet fused shut; the lower wheel of a conveyor; lengths of toothed track; ceramic posts carrying a current too weak to serve their intended purpose but strong enough to support whole ecologies.

Some machines still operate.

A chain moves through the eastern wall at irregular intervals, advancing three links with a violent clank and then stopping. No one knows what pulls it. Each movement sends a pressure wave through the floor. Animals flatten themselves or leap clear a moment before it comes, warned by vibrations too faint for a human to notice. The chain has polished a trench no deeper than a finger joint, but the trench is one of the Pocket’s major valleys. It carries no liquid. Instead, it channels rolling grit, shed plates, eggs, carcass fragments, and herds of tiny black bead-creatures that tumble deliberately before righting themselves farther downslope.

Near the center stands a broken motor housing, its casing worn into a low dome. This is the largest landmark. Its inspection ports have become entrances to a dense interior country of copper windings and mica partitions. Warmth rises from somewhere below it. The housing is never still. Colonies migrate in and out through the ports; hunters wait along the bolt flanges; armored animals hammer one another over the best conductive seams. When the buried current strengthens, the entire dome emits a sustained, resonant hum, and the creatures covering it align head-first along the magnetic field.

The light is ordinary and surprisingly clear. It enters through narrow inspection gaps in the floor above and from several old work lamps that continue to glow at diminished output. There are shadows, recognizable colors, and a cycle resembling day and night when the upper facility interrupts or restores power. Nothing glows dramatically. The creatures rely on dull camouflage, polished warning surfaces, and abrupt flashes reflected from their joint plates. At certain angles, the moving floor resembles iron filings stirred beneath glass.

The sound is disproportionate to the size of the place. Claws tick against ceramic. Shells rasp under slabs. Predators strike with dry snaps. The conveyor chain bangs inside its wall. Current makes loose laminations buzz. Tens of thousands of feeding appendages produce a broad hiss like sand driven over pavement, though there is almost no wind. During migrations the noise gathers into a steady roar, intimate and low, conducted through the bones more than the ears.

Predation is constant but not chaotic. The fauna obey a few severe rules.

Warm surfaces must be occupied.

Freshly exposed metal must be harvested before it passivates.

A moving shadow means cover.

A stopped machine is dangerous because it may start.

A starting machine is fertile because it exposes new surfaces.

Nothing remains unclaimed for more than a few moments.

The main hunters are low, jointed things called clampers. They resemble overlapping fragments of machinery until they unfold. They do not chase prey far. Instead they brace between two stones and wait for migration streams to narrow around them. Their jaws close once, with enough force to fracture ceramic. After feeding, they are trailed by pale attendants that remove residues from their plates. Some attendants are eaten; enough are tolerated that the association persists.

Above them, hook-legged rafter animals travel upside down along pipes. They drop shed hooks rather than eggs. Each hook contains a folded juvenile surrounded by a dry capsule of salts and oil. Heat awakens it. A warm pulse can cause hundreds to open at once, filling the air with drifting filaments. Most are seized before reaching the floor. For several loud minutes the entire Pocket becomes a vertical feeding ground.

The closest thing to weather comes from the ventilation system. Several times in a cycle, a fan far beyond the western grilles turns over. Air enters cold and fast, carrying mineral powder and traces of machine solvent. The dust does not form dunes; too many feet redistribute it. Instead it gathers briefly in the lee of bolts and then vanishes beneath feeding bodies. Creatures with broad, sail-like backs emerge during these winds and face the current. Their porous armor captures reactive particles. Predators patrol behind them.

The fan also strips loose skins from the ground. These translucent castings rise in sheets, strike the ceiling, and accumulate in corners as brittle drifts. Smaller animals tunnel through them. Larger animals compress them into nest walls. The oldest nests have become stone-like through pressure and chemical exchange, forming little towers under overhangs. They are the Pocket’s scattered architecture: built by animals from the remains of animals, later bored through by other animals, then worn smooth by traffic.

Human construction is present but no longer dominant. Its straight edges have softened. Its intended corridors are blocked. Labels have become mineral patterns; machine housings have become cliffs; electrical faults have become feeding grounds. Yet the old industry still dictates the deepest rhythms. Switches close somewhere beyond reach. Relays strike. Heat spreads. Fields reverse. Whole populations change direction without warning, pouring through passages barely wider than a hand.

The Pocket gives an impression of tremendous age, though the artifacts cannot be primordial. Its age is not measured only in years. It is visible in completion. Every loose edge has been tested. Every surface has been abraded by innumerable contacts. The terrain has already undergone most of the changes available to it and settled into a final, low arrangement. Even collapse happens horizontally here. A fallen partition becomes another paving layer. A dead machine becomes a warmer patch of ground. There are no young landforms.

Only the living world seems new, because it renews itself at such speed.

Stand still and creatures begin to treat you as terrain. First come the reflective specks, gathering at the electrical difference between skin and floor. Then the shieldbacks investigate seams in clothing. Threaders lower themselves toward the warmth of the neck. Larger bodies alter their migration routes around the obstruction. Within minutes, two traffic streams form beside the feet. Predators take positions where these streams narrow.

Nothing attacks out of malice or even particular hunger. The Low Pocket simply has no category for empty space, unused warmth, or an unharvested surface. Density is its stable condition. Absence is only a resource that has not yet been reached.
