The place occupies a hanging basin high above the valleys, though the basin itself is no larger than a walled hamlet. Three raw ridges enclose it. Their stone is dark, freshly exposed, and still angular from the glacier that withdrew only recently. There has not been time for the peaks to soften. Thin flakes split from them in the cold and fall point-first into the vegetation, where they vanish without reaching the ground.

From the ridge, the basin appears to contain a town.

There are towers, narrow streets, buttressed halls, covered passages, terraces, wells, and dense blocks leaning one against another. Yet nothing here has been quarried or fitted. The entire settlement is plant matter. Trunks have grown into columns; roots arch over channels; layers of compressed leaves make roofs as thick as turf. The towers are braided stems drawn upward by the little light that passes across the basin. Their tips remain alive, bearing fans of black-green needles and pale, cupped leaves that collect fog.

There is almost no bare surface. Even the cliffs have acquired a pelt of moss and miniature shrubs. Rock exists mainly as a pressure beneath roots or as an occasional wet blade protruding from a wall of greenery. Snow rests briefly on upper ledges, but lower down it is caught, roof by roof, leaf by leaf, and fed inward as meltwater.

The city has no builders in any ordinary sense. Its architecture is the accumulated behavior of plants competing for light, shelter, and drainage.

In the basin’s center, where a shallow tarn once lay, rise the oldest structures: thick, hollow boles fused into a close ring. They resemble a council house or a roofless temple. The trunks are ribbed and silver beneath their moss, each one broad enough to contain a narrow chamber. Their interiors are lined with dry fibers and dormant buds. Above them, branches meet in a vaulted canopy so dense that midday is reduced to a green-gray twilight.

From this central grove, passageways radiate toward the basin walls. Some are only fissures between root masses, forcing anything that enters to turn sideways. Others broaden unexpectedly into small courts filled with knee-high moss, blue lichens, and still pools. The pools reflect portions of the canopy like broken mirrors. Water is frequent but seldom abundant in one place. It hangs as beads, threads along bark, sleeps beneath mats of vegetation, and condenses on every underside. The whole city is wet by accumulation rather than flood.

When rain comes, it does not fall cleanly to the ground. It is intercepted hundreds of times. Drops strike high leaves with soft taps, descend along veins, gather in cups, spill onto roofs of needles, and finally enter the root channels hours later. The city continues raining inside itself long after the clouds have passed.

The principal streets are waterways no wider than a handspan. Clear meltwater runs through them over root-polished stone. At intersections, living lattices divide the flow and send it toward different quarters. These lattices continually alter themselves. A channel carrying too much water stimulates thick growth along its banks; one carrying too little is slowly walled over by moss. There are no permanent roads. Every route is a temporary agreement among roots.

The outer districts climb the young scree slopes in crowded terraces. Low cushion plants hold stones in tight green fists. Dwarf conifers grow horizontally beneath the wind, their branches layered like shingles. Between them stand bundles of hollow reeds, though the basin is far above the usual reed line. Their stems have darkened almost to violet and are filled with insulating pith. In the cold they contract and knock faintly against one another.

These reed districts give the clearest impression of buildings. Whole stands share their outer stems, producing walls around warmer interior voids. Moss carpets the floors. Ceiling leaves overlap so precisely that only a few drops enter. Seedlings germinate inside these chambers, protected from frost, then force their way upward through narrow chimneys. As generations accumulate, the chambers rise, collapse, and rise again. An old quarter can be recognized by the number of buried rooms beneath it.

Nothing is empty. If a branch dies, fungi enter before it has finished cooling. If a patch of moss is torn away by sliding snow, pale runners cross the wound within days. Windblown dust is trapped by hairs and resins; dead needles are stitched into walls by fine roots. Even the air seems occupied. Fog catches in the canopy and becomes a temporary fabric, stretched from twig to twig.

The cold governs everything. Frost remains in the deeper passages through most of the year. Some courts hold slabs of old blue ice under their living floors, and the plants grow directly over them, warmed only by decay and by the weak heat retained in dark tissues. Leaves are small, thick, and almost colorless on their upper faces. Their greens appear only when lifted or broken. Many are furred with translucent filaments that turn the dim light into a faint pearl sheen.

At dawn, if the sky clears, sunlight touches the highest towers for a few minutes. They brighten copper, green, and wet black. Lower streets remain unlit. By the time the light descends into the basin, cloud usually gathers again around the ridges. The settlement seems designed not to seek brightness but to distribute scarcity: upper leaves gather what they can, then shed sugars downward through fused stems. Plants that would elsewhere be competitors share vascular connections here. The city survives because hunger passes through it communally.

That cooperation has limits. Certain towers strangle their neighbors beneath smooth coils. Root systems invade old chambers and compress them shut. A creeping red moss advances across weaker roofs in slow, nearly straight fronts. Under the surface, fungi bargain among districts, moving minerals toward some groves and withholding them from others. The apparent stillness is full of sieges.

Animals are uncommon. A few white midges emerge during brief thaws and collect under leaf vaults, where the air is marginally warmer. Tiny black snails graze the films coating the water channels. Occasionally a mountain bird drops into the basin, but it seldom stays. There is nowhere easy to land, and the vegetation closes tightly around movement. Feathers found in the moss are retained and incorporated, their keratin softened by fungi and drawn slowly into the living mass.

The largest resident creature is a small, ash-colored herbivore, seldom seen directly. It moves through the buried chambers rather than along the open passages, chewing new root tips and sleeping in pockets of dry leaf fiber. Its tunnels briefly introduce emptiness into the city. The plants respond by thickening around them. Sometimes a tunnel is sealed while the animal is away; sometimes while it is not. In spring, one may hear a muted scratching from inside a wall of roots, then nothing.

For the most part, the basin is quiet. Water makes the dominant sounds: ticking from leaf to leaf, threading through narrow pipes, falling unexpectedly into hidden pools. Frost produces sharper noises. Stems crack in the night. Stone splits under roots with a report like a distant snapped branch. During wind, the high towers lean together and rub, giving off a low wooden groan that passes from one side of the settlement to the other.

There are brief active seasons. In thaw weather, roofs sag under meltwater and then lift as their tissues rehydrate. Channels overflow into passageways. Dormant buds open everywhere at once, filling the dark with pale folded surfaces. Fungal caps push through floors and dissolve within days. The central boles vent mist from their hollows, making the grove appear to breathe.

Then the temperature falls. Water withdraws into buried veins. Leaves fold against walls. Entire lanes become armored with clear ice. Movement slows, but never quite stops. Beneath the frost, roots continue to press into cracks in the newly uncovered mountain. They pry grains from the stone and pass them inward. The sharp basin is being dismantled, not by rivers or great weather, but by the patient expansion of a city that feeds on its foundations.

The place has boundaries. Beyond the last terrace, vegetation thins abruptly into exposed scree and hard snow. The transition is only a few steps wide. Inside is compression, moisture, shelter, and collective growth; outside is wind, glare, and stone. Detached seeds rarely survive there. Nearly all new life begins within the existing mass, in a wall, a roof, a blocked watercourse, or the damp darkness of an abandoned chamber.

Thus the city grows mostly inward. It subdivides itself. Courts become groves, groves become towers, streets narrow to capillaries. Old structures are not demolished; they are engulfed. The deepest layers are black peat shaped like forgotten rooms, with doorlike openings that lead nowhere because the passages beyond have become wood.

There is no plan, no tool mark, no central intelligence. Yet the settlement obeys a few clear laws:

Nothing dead remains unused.

No open space stays open for long.

Water must descend, though its route may change.

Light belongs first to the highest leaves, but what they make from it passes downward.

Every shelter eventually becomes soil for another shelter.

And beneath all of it, under root, peat, ice, and the crowded chambers of the living city, the young mountain remains sharp—still close to the shape in which the vanished glacier left it, and already beginning to disappear.
