At first it seems to be a mountain range under cloud: a black, uneven horizon lifted so high that the weather lies below it. Then the horizon moves.

The entire upland is young. Its ridges have not yet learned the rounded shapes of age. They rise in glassy fins and abrupt terraces, in splintered escarpments whose faces still shine with the heat that made them. Some peaks are soft enough to slump visibly, folding over themselves in slow pleats; others are hard, dark, and sharp as freshly broken ceramic. Nothing has had time to become gravel. There are almost no old stones. When a wall fractures, its pieces retain their impossible angles, and when a fissure opens, the exposed interior is smooth and red-black, like the inside of a cooling mold.

The plateau is not merely large. It has distances that belong to a world rather than a region. Storms are born in one province of it, migrate for weeks, and die without reaching its farther edge. Mountain chains rise from its surface as casually as roots from soil. Whole inland climates gather in the hollows between ridges. Yet the land is so high that the surrounding atmosphere has thinned into a violet dimness, and the brighter sky appears to flow beneath the cliffs in a luminous ocean of cloud.

Above, there is no clear sun. Light reaches the plateau after passing through veils of ash, spores, high vapor, and vast translucent creatures. Noon has the color of late twilight. Shadows are broad and indistinct, except around newly opened cracks, where a low orange radiance throws every stem and claw into violent relief.

It is warm despite the altitude. Heat comes out of the ground in sheets.

The upland sweats. Moisture beads on stone, gathers beneath leaves, and runs for a few hours through channels that did not exist the previous day. Rain is infrequent and strangely local. A single grove may endure a vertical downpour while the neighboring ridge remains dry enough to smoke. Sometimes rain rises from the ravines: droplets detach from hidden pools and climb into the dim sky, coating the undersides of branches before disappearing into cloud.

There are rivers, but few are permanent. They arrive as events. A seam in the earth opens, and warm mineral water pours out for a season, blue with dissolved metals or white with suspended spores. It braids through a basin, feeds an explosion of life, then vanishes when the fissure seals. Pools remain in pockets of black rock, crowded with larvae and soft, pale animals that live at astonishing speed. A pool may be clear at dawn, green by midday, opaque with eggs by evening, and empty the next morning, its water having condensed somewhere miles uphill.

Vegetation occupies every surface that stays still long enough.

It does not form a continuous forest so much as a succession of dense biological seizures. Groves erupt along fresh fractures. Mats spread over warm slopes in interlocking scales. Towering stalks inflate from knuckles in the stone, branch without visibly growing, and suspend curtains of dark foliage from one ridge to another. Their leaves are thick, almost black, and turn their reflective undersides toward whatever faint source of light is available. Where several such curtains overlap, the air beneath them becomes warm, green, and close, smelling of wet iron, crushed pepper, and yeast.

Other growths are lower and more crowded: copper-colored reeds with jointed trunks, fleshy spirals that tighten when touched by mist, carpets of minute cups that collect condensation and snap shut around it. There are vines, though they do not climb in the usual sense. They draw two distant objects nearer. A vine fastening to opposite cliff faces may shorten the ravine between them by several meters, causing the intervening ground to buckle. Whole thickets migrate this way, knotting space ahead of themselves and releasing it behind.

In the oldest-looking valleys—which may be only months old—vegetation becomes almost impenetrable. Trunks touch trunks. Fronds layer over fronds until the ground is buried under several living ceilings. Between them hang swollen sacs of water, colonies of luminous mites, and the sleeping forms of animals folded into protective geometries. Yet none of these valleys feels settled. Roots are repeatedly torn out by upheaval. Forests are lifted intact on rising slabs, tilted toward the sky, or swallowed by warm faults. Life responds not by resisting but by accelerating.

Seeds open before landing. Saplings mature during storms. Flowers bud, bloom, and rot while falling from the canopy.

The animals are everywhere, though the landscape is mostly quiet.

The silence is not emptiness. It is crowded restraint. Small six-limbed grazers press through the undergrowth in herds of thousands, their feet placing themselves delicately between condensation cups. Vast sail-backed browsers stand along escarpments, combing spores from the wind with ribbed mouths. Pale burrowers move beneath thin crusts of stone, visible as traveling bulges. Winged things roost on the vertical faces in such numbers that cliffs occasionally seem to grow fur.

Predators are common but rarely chase. They wait at points where the world is likely to change—beside a folding ridge, beneath a rain inversion, at the mouth of a fissure that has not opened yet. Some possess organs sensitive not to scent or motion but to probability. They face empty ground for hours, perfectly still, until prey appears there.

Most creatures make no audible calls. Sound behaves badly on the plateau. A noise may be delayed, displaced, or trapped in a material. Footsteps sink into the ground and emerge days later as faint knocks inside distant cliffs. Thunder is often visible but unheard: broad pressure rings pass through vegetation, flattening leaves in expanding circles without producing a report. When audible sound does occur, it is intimate and disproportionately clear—the tick of cooling rock, the wet click of a feeding mouth, the whisper of membranes folding half a kilometer overhead.

Certain basins collect sound the way others collect water. Their floors murmur continuously with noises that fell there from elsewhere: extinct storms, wingbeats from unborn flocks, the cracking of ridges now standing unbroken. Animals come to drink these sounds. They lower delicate antennae into the basin and shiver as the stored vibrations pass through them.

Nothing here is built. There are no roads, walls, ruins, tools, or deliberate marks. Nevertheless, the plateau has an industrial character so overwhelming that it seems, at times, to be a single factory without architects.

The mountains operate.

Deep shafts inhale cold upper air with a steady mechanical draw. Volcanic chambers compress it, heat it, and expel it through ranks of basalt vents. Mineral fluids circulate in closed systems of cracks, separating metals into bright bands. Vast fields of hollow stone pistons rise and fall as underground pressures change. Their motion drives forests of piezoelectric crystal, and during periods of strain the whole upland flickers with buried blue current.

No component was made. No plan governs the arrangement. Still, ore is crushed, gases are refined, heat is exchanged, charge is accumulated, and organic matter is processed on continental scales. Hills extrude long black filaments that twist themselves into cablelike bundles before taking root and becoming plants. Marsh colonies precipitate thin metallic sheets, which peel away in the warm wind and drift across the landscape like dead leaves. Herds of armor-plated animals travel between volcanic provinces carrying colonies of catalytic bacteria in their shells; wherever they rest, the rock beneath them changes composition.

There are production cycles, but no product.

At irregular intervals the plateau enters a phase of violent coordination. Vents begin pulsing together. Crystal fields illuminate in sequence from horizon to horizon. Migratory animals turn simultaneously toward newly forming valleys. Vegetation retracts from fault lines seconds before they split. A low pressure passes through the crust, too deep to hear, and entire mountain systems rearrange themselves.

These rearrangements can be rapid. A plain creases into a chain of peaks over the course of an afternoon. A canyon closes like a wound, preserving birds and trees unharmed in narrow chambers of suspended space. Lakes tip upright and remain standing as trembling blue walls. Rock liquefies without heating. Gravity develops local slopes unrelated to terrain, sending loose creatures falling sideways through forests or gathering rain into hovering spheres.

The land creates and destroys with no visible preference. Yet destruction is seldom complete. Things displaced by one change reappear within another. A grove swallowed by stone may emerge inverted beneath an overhang hundreds of kilometers away, its roots drinking mist and its canopy pressed into darkness. A flock entering a region of folded distance may return as a single enormous animal, each former body now functioning as an organ. Sometimes a cliff splits and reveals not strata but an active landscape inside: a sunless wetland, perhaps, extending inward farther than the mountain is wide.

Distance cannot be trusted. Nor can sequence.

There are places where approaching an object makes it more distant but more detailed. A black spire on the horizon may remain weeks away while its lichens become visible one by one. In some ravines, predators kill prey before either has arrived; afterward both animals behave cautiously around the empty site until the event catches up with them. Morning occasionally condenses on leaves as a physical substance—clear, cool, and sweet—and is licked away by insects, shortening the day.

The plateau’s few stable rules are ecological rather than physical.

Moisture draws complexity. Wherever water lingers, life layers itself rapidly and thickly, followed by distortions in space. Heat produces height: warmed matter tends to rise not only upward but toward greater elevation in the landscape’s history, becoming younger and sharper as it ascends. Sound seeks hollows. Repeated shapes attract one another. Nothing dead remains singular for long.

And every living thing must move eventually, because permanence provokes the land.

A forest that roots in one basin for too many seasons will cause the basin to turn over. A nesting colony that returns to the same cliff will find the cliff migrating. Even mountains are subject to this rule. Along the far eastern reaches, immense ranges walk slowly on buried folds, each step taking years and leaving behind strips of newly exposed mantle. Warm rainforests ride on their backs. Rivers spill from them into the air. Animals migrate across their moving summits, living entire lives without knowing their ground is traveling.

From a high promontory, one might watch thousands of kilometers of dim country at once: black forests steaming under isolated rains; orange fissures threading newborn plains; cloud oceans churning below the outer cliffs; herds flowing through the valleys like filings around an unseen magnet. Far off, a mountain lifts itself from the plateau, shedding avalanches upward. Above it, something winged and continentally vast occludes the already weak light.

The scene is turbulent everywhere, but almost no sound reaches the watcher.

Only condensation tapping from leaf to leaf.

Only the small internal crackle of stone organizing itself.

Then the ground at the horizon rises, not like an earthquake but like a thought occurring, and the landscape begins again.
