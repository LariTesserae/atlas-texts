The valley lies beneath the ground, though no one standing in it would first think to call it a cavern. It is too wide for that. Its farther walls vanish into industrial haze, and its ceiling is less a roof than a dim second horizon: an immense plate of ribbed steel suspended on pylons, crossed by service rails and slow-moving gantries. Lamps burn among the ribs in widely spaced amber rows. Their light never quite reaches the floor.

There is no exposed earth here. No bedrock, no natural riverbed, no accidental stone. The entire basin has been fabricated—floor, cliffs, channels, slopes, even the loose gray scree that slides beneath the boots. Those apparent stones are fractured refractory blocks, ceramic slag, clinkers, snapped insulation, and fist-sized knots of oxidized alloy. Where the valley walls seem to show strata, they are layers of old construction: poured aggregate over steel lattice, tile over pressure shell, resin over tile, each era sealed imperfectly onto the last.

The place was built recently, or is forever being built. Many edges remain hard and unsoftened. Fresh welds shine silver along black beams. Bolts stand proud from unfinished plates. Whole escarpments consist of stacked flood baffles not yet lowered into their final housings. Yet water has already begun its work.

It comes from everywhere in small quantities.

Condensation gathers on the ceiling and travels along beams until it reaches designated drip points—or escapes them. Fine rain falls through the warm exhaust plumes. Cooling pipes sweat continuously. Weeping seams make dark vertical marks down the artificial cliffs. At the lowest levels, water films the metal plain and gathers in broad, shallow marshes interrupted by grilles, cable trenches, and raised causeways. None is deeper than a person’s knee, but the black surface extends for kilometers in places, mirroring machinery as broken orange streaks.

The cold is severe and mechanical. It has no wind-borne wildness, no scent of snowfields or pine. It is drawn evenly out of the air by refrigeration towers buried in the walls. Breath hangs low. Handrails grow white fur overnight. Water at the margins of the basins freezes into thin, clear plates, then fractures when pumps start beneath it. The resulting pieces slide over one another with the sound of glass being sorted in another room.

Every few hours the basin changes state.

Warning lamps begin turning red in sequence across the valley. Somewhere beyond sight, gates unlock with impacts that can be felt through the soles. Pumps spool up, their individual whines converging into a deep, enormous chord. Water in the channels trembles, reverses direction, and accelerates. Marshes drain through thousands of narrow slots, exposing tiled floors furred with rust and pale mineral scale. Elsewhere, sluices open and a sudden sheet of gray water descends a stepped spillway, atomizing at each level. The mist freezes on nearby structures. Within minutes, ladders and pipes acquire transparent skins.

Then the machinery falls back to its usual labor: never silence, only lesser noise.

There is the continuous pressure of ventilation, like distant surf trapped in ducts. Chain conveyors strike their housings with irregular metallic knocks. Suspended cranes complain as they cross the ceiling. Water hisses on hot surfaces. Relays chatter inside sealed cabinets. From the eastern works comes a repeated concussion, slow enough to count—one every forty-seven seconds—followed by a tremor and the patter of debris falling from high platforms. No one visible attends this process.

At intervals, freight engines emerge from tunnels in the valley walls. They are low, broad machines without windows, dragging strings of covered wagons along tracks elevated above the floodplain. Their wheels throw blue electrical light around bends. They pass slowly, loudly, and with an air of tremendous mass, then vanish into another aperture. The rails continue vibrating long after the last wagon has gone.

Despite all this activity, the landscape feels sparsely occupied. Structures stand far apart, each surrounded by tracts of wet flooring and unused foundations. A pumping station may be the only vertical object for half a kilometer: a square black tower with illuminated gauges and steam issuing from its crown. Beyond it might lie a field of empty anchor bolts, then a row of cooling pylons, then nothing but shallow water until haze erases the view. The scale belongs to a city, but the city itself is absent. What remains are its organs arranged without streets or houses.

Vegetation survives in narrow, improbable bands.

Most of it is low and colorless: mats of translucent moss around warm pipe collars, wiry reeds rooted in sediment caught behind drainage lips, films of dark algae beneath the ice. In one drainage district, pale stalks rise from submerged bolt holes, each bearing a single waxy bladder. When pumps lower the water, these bladders collapse and release a smell like cut cucumber and hot copper. They were not planted. Maintenance records, where legible, refer to them only as “recurring filter matter.”

There are also small gardens under certain leaking lamps. Not intentional gardens, but circles of thin green growth defined precisely by warmth and illumination. Their edges are so abrupt that they resemble stains painted onto the floor. A few fernlike leaves unfold against junction boxes, feeding on condensation rich in dissolved metals. Their veins carry a faint bluish light after the lamps go out.

Animals are rarer and difficult to separate from machinery.

Something small moves beneath the ice of the western basins, making straight lines and right-angle turns. Occasionally a blunt white shape surfaces at a drainage grate, opens a round mouth, and draws in floating rust flakes before sinking again. Far above, winged creatures roost among the ceiling trusses. They have been heard more often than seen: dry flapping, a ceramic click, then the brief fall of dislodged frost. Their nests appear to be made from wire insulation and braided copper.

The largest inhabitants may be the maintenance walkers. Whether they count as fauna depends on whom one asks. They travel alone across the flooded floor on six narrow legs, carrying tool housings beneath arched backs. Their original paint has peeled away, and moss grows in the seams around their joints. They pause at leaks, taste the water with hanging sensor filaments, and apply patches of quick-setting black material. Sometimes two meet in the open basin. They stand facing each other for several minutes, exchanging low pulses that resonate through the water, then depart in opposite directions.

The fabricated terrain is young enough to be sharp, but already undergoing a kind of accelerated erosion. Water hollows grout from between floor plates. Thermal cycling opens hairline fractures in the cast cliffs. Chemical runoff carves bright green channels through sacrificial metal. The edges of fresh breaks glitter with embedded mesh and fiber, revealing that even the valley’s “stone” was designed to fail in controlled layers.

In some sectors, erosion is part of the manufacturing process. Robotic cutters gnaw long ravines into blocks of synthetic substrate. Hydraulic hammers reduce obsolete buildings to graded rubble. Abrasive slurry is pumped through channels to polish them wider. New terrain emerges beside the destruction: terraces extruded overnight, embankments assembled from interlocking cells, smooth black ponds lowered into sockets by ceiling cranes. The geography can change between shifts. A causeway remembered from yesterday may end at a wet drop; a new ridge may divide a familiar basin in two.

Maps are therefore printed on dissolving paper and dated by the hour.

There are rules posted throughout the valley, stenciled in several generations of lettering:

**DO NOT CROSS OPEN WATER DURING PRESSURE EQUALIZATION.**

**IF THE FLOOR BEGINS TO RING, REMAIN STILL.**

**MIST WITH A BLUE CAST IS NOT WATER.**

**IGNORE BELLS HEARD BELOW DECK LEVEL.**

The last instruction is difficult to follow. Bells do sound beneath the floor, especially after the drainage cycle. Their tones move slowly from south to north under the plates, as if carried by a procession in a buried passage. There are no passages on the plans. Engineers attribute the sound to contracting support members, but support members should not ring in measured groups of five.

In the deepest part of the valley stands the only structure resembling a monument: a circular shaft surrounded by empty pavement. Its rim is built from white ceramic blocks, clean despite the grime elsewhere. Moist air rises from it in a continuous column. The shaft descends below the valley, below the pumping levels, perhaps below the constructed world altogether, though the old diagrams show only more machines beneath.

Water does not fall into this shaft. Rain approaching its mouth slows, trembles, and lifts away as mist.

Around the rim, frost grows in branching patterns that almost resemble coastlines. Each drainage cycle destroys them. Each cold interval makes them again, differently.

The workers—when workers are present—give the shaft a wide berth. They travel in insulated vehicles along raised roads, appearing as pairs of white headlights in the murk. They do not leave the cabs unless necessary. Their radio traffic is clipped and practical: gate numbers, pressure values, warnings of ice, reports of unregistered movement. Human voices seem too small for the place and are quickly buried beneath pumps and falling water.

Yet the valley is not hostile in any deliberate sense. It simply has priorities that do not include comfort. It must circulate, cool, condense, drain, grind, carry, freeze, thaw, and rebuild. Every surface belongs to one of these verbs. Even emptiness has been engineered: clear zones for crane swing, flood acceptance, thermal expansion, or processes whose names have been scraped from the signs.

There is no sunrise. Instead, the ceiling lamps brighten by degrees until the wet floor turns pewter, then dim again to ember-color. This cycle is called day, though it has no fixed length. Sometimes it lasts twenty hours. Sometimes alarms interrupt it after six minutes, plunging whole districts into darkness while emergency strips illuminate the causeways in cold blue.

In that blue interval, the manufactured valley looks least artificial.

The broad black water, the white vapor, the sharp new ridges, the scattered reeds, the distant moving lights—all briefly suggest a tundra beneath a moonless sky. Then a gantry wakes overhead. Its wheels begin to thunder along the roof rails. Suspended beneath it, turning slowly on cables, is another piece of landscape: a complete steel hillside, ribbed underneath, already wet with condensation, being carried through the dark to somewhere the valley has not yet become.
