The country lies low beneath a white, unwavering sky. There is no visible sun, yet the light is stronger than noon: direct, warm, and nearly shadowless. It shines equally beneath leaves, under bridges, and into the mouths of wells. Nothing fades in it. Painted surfaces retain their first colors; wet clay remains dark and newly worked; cut edges stay sharp enough to nick a palm.

At first the place resembles a broad tropical marsh after rain. Shallow water occupies long basins between green islands. Groves stand widely separated, each surrounded by bright reed beds and open, ankle-deep flats. Farther off are stepped ridges, low walls, raised paths, and the pale roofs of settlements. The distances are generous but not immense. From any rise, one can see the next cluster of trees, the next water court, the next inhabited mound.

Only after walking does it become clear that there is no ground.

The flats are made from tightly fitted plates of fired earth, each plate roughly the breadth of two hands. Some are buff-colored, some red, some almost black where water lies over them. Their surfaces have been impressed with thumb marks, woven-mat patterns, fish scales, spirals, and rows of little triangular cuts. The mud between the reeds is not sediment but a soft, repeatedly kneaded substance laid over wickerwork. Beneath it, where burrowing animals have opened holes, there are more lattices: cane, cord, ceramic struts, and huge baskets packed with smaller sealed jars.

Every hill is a construction. Every rock is a hollow shell of clay or carved wood, weighted from within. The shining black outcrops along the waterways are slabs of polished resin set upright in masonry sockets. Even the sand consists of tiny beads, rolled by hand and pierced through the center, though no thread passes through them.

The land seems newly completed. Mortar remains pale. Posts bear fresh adze scars. Ramps end in crisp corners that rain should have softened but has not. Along the water, steep little scarps reveal clean layers of tile, basketry, shell, and green-glazed brick. They look freshly broken, yet no fragments lie below them. Nothing is collapsing. Nothing is being repaired.

There are no ruins here because decay has not begun.

Water is everywhere without being dominant. It fills rectangular ponds, narrow canals, hoof-shaped hollows, and innumerable cups molded into the paving. It beads on leaves and gathers beneath raised houses. Clear droplets continually hang from the undersides of bridges. Rock faces weep in fine silver threads, although their hollow backs contain no reservoirs. Moisture condenses in the open air at shoulder height, briefly outlining invisible planes before descending into catch basins.

Yet the water does not truly flow. A canal may show a current, with weed stems leaning steadily eastward, but a floating petal remains fixed beside the same post. Water spills from carved mouths in uninterrupted glassy ropes, although the pools beneath neither rise nor overflow. Ripples persist without spreading. If a traveler steps into a pond, the surface yields and splashes normally around the leg; once the foot is withdrawn, the original pattern returns—a perfect arrangement of concentric rings centered on something that happened long before.

Rain is present as a condition rather than an event. In some courtyards, drops hang throughout the air, brilliant as seeds. Passing through them leaves the skin wet, but the drops are still there behind you. Elsewhere the paving shines with the permanent aftermath of a storm that never occurred.

The heat is close and bodily. It smells of wet fiber, crushed leaves, warm pottery, animal musk, and fermented fruit. There is no breeze, though broad leaves sometimes hold the posture of having just been turned by one. Sweat comes readily and does not evaporate. Shade offers darkness but little coolness.

Vegetation is lush where it occurs, but it does not make a continuous forest. Growth has been planted in deliberate islands with broad open spaces between them. Each grove rises from a circular bed bordered by low clay teeth. Palms, figs, broad-leaved vines, tree ferns, gourds, and flowering canes crowd together inside these rings, almost too richly. Beyond them lie expanses of exposed paving, shallow water, or reed-patterned fields.

The plants, too, are made.

Their trunks consist of wrapped bark cloth over jointed wooden cores. Ferns are assembled from thousands of narrow green slivers stitched to central ribs. The palms have ceramic roots gripping ceramic soil. Some flowers are folded from lacquered skin; others are living tissue grafted onto wax stems. Fruit has flesh, juice, seed, and sweetness, but its skin carries fine seam lines. Split a seed and one finds a tiny carved chamber containing another, smaller seed.

None of this makes the groves seem dead. On the contrary, they possess an excessive vitality. Vines tighten around trellises. Flowers open hot and fragrant. Moss cushions the shaded sides of walls. But no new leaf is ever added, and no old leaf falls. A creeper observed over many days will appear to climb, its tip always reaching and its coils always recently tightened, while remaining attached to precisely the same notches.

Animals are abundant, though the openness of the country keeps them scattered. Bright frogs occupy the water cups. Long-legged marsh birds stand singly across the flats, each far enough from the others to seem like a marker. Small lizards bask on wall tops. Beetles move among the impressed patterns in the tiles, following grooves as if they were roads. There are pale fish in the canals, flat-shelled grazers under the reeds, and fruit bats suspended from grove frames.

Larger creatures appear at intervals: a narrow-backed deer with basket-woven antlers; a black pig whose hide shines like wet clay; low, patient reptiles fitted with rows of ivory pegs instead of teeth. Their bodies are warm. They eat, blink, breathe, mate, and sleep. Wounds bleed. Nevertheless, their number never changes.

Predation occurs but reaches no conclusion. A bird may descend upon a frog with its beak already closing; hours later, both remain alive in their accustomed places. Fish carry the scars of bites that are always fresh. Eggs are continuously on the verge of hatching. In one reed bed, a hatchling has had its beak through the same shell since anyone began keeping memory.

The inhabitants, where they are encountered, use simple things: stone-edged knives, bone awls, wooden mallets, digging sticks, hand-twisted rope. Metal is rare and treated as a puzzling ornament. Houses are raised on forked poles and lashed rather than nailed. Boats are dugout forms chopped from enormous fabricated trunks. Pottery is shaped without wheels.

This poverty of tools stands against the total manufacture of the world. No one now living could have built even a mile of it. The residents do not claim that ancestors did. Their oldest stories say the land was made recently, all at once, by hands whose fingerprints remain on every tile. Their newest stories say the same.

There are villages, but they are loose arrangements rather than dense towns: six or seven houses around a water court, then a long open causeway to the next cluster. Cooking fires give heat and smell but consume no fuel. Clay ovens contain breads forever browning. People work constantly at tasks that do not alter anything. They weave mats whose unfinished edge never advances. They scrape boat hulls that remain newly rough. They weed garden beds without reducing the weeds.

They are not trapped in gestures. They can stop, speak, travel, improvise. But the landscape quietly restores its account. A cut reed is whole when no one is looking at the cut. A moved stone returns overnight, although there is no true night. A newly built shelter persists for several days, then becomes harder to notice; eventually travelers walk through the place where it stands and insist that the clearing has always been empty.

The rule seems to be that the country permits experience but refuses consequence.

Sound is scarce. The bright air suppresses distance. Footsteps are dull on the wet tiles, and voices carry only a few paces before becoming inaudible. There is no broad insect drone despite the multitude of insects. Birds open their beaks more often than they call. Frogs pulse their throats in silence.

What can be heard comes singly and with peculiar clarity: one drip into a jar; the scrape of a stone blade; a wingbeat over water; a fruit splitting along its seam. From very far away, perhaps beyond the visible ridges, there is sometimes one wooden knock. It is repeated at exact intervals, neither approaching nor receding.

There is no evening. The white radiance never lowers, and shadows never lengthen. Sleep comes from fatigue rather than darkness. People cover windows with woven screens and wake to the same bright wetness. Their calendars are arrangements of objects, not records of celestial motion: seven shells placed beside a bowl, then returned; thirty knots tied in a cord that somehow remains unknotted.

Beyond the last village, the paved marsh continues to a low horizon. Causeways divide and rejoin. Constructed groves gleam in the heat. Water hangs from the undersides of things. Here and there, towers of unfired clay rise only a little above the reeds, crisp and damp, as though shaped that morning by gigantic fingers.

From atop one of these towers, the underlying design becomes visible. The ponds, garden rings, walls, villages, and open courts form parts of a single enormous relief: not a map, exactly, but the impression of a creature lying beneath the country. A shoulder is suggested by a ridge of houses, a closed eye by an oval lake, ribs by parallel canals. The shape is incomplete—or perhaps too large to recognize from any one vantage.

Nothing in the land is natural, but neither does it feel artificial. Those distinctions require an outside world for comparison, and here there is no evidence of one. Digging downward reveals only older-looking layers that are equally fresh and equally made. Walking outward returns, without a discernible turn, to the same marshes under another arrangement of names.

The place is hot, green, wet, and newborn.

It has always just been finished.
