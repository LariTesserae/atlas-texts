The country begins above the weather.

Its lowest ground is already higher than ordinary mountains: a tilted salt-colored tableland suspended under a dim violet sky. From it rise unfinished peaks—vast, steep masses with faces too smooth to have known rain, frost, rivers, or the long abrasion of wind. They resemble mountains remembered before they were ever made. Their ridges end abruptly in pale scaffolds of stone; their gullies are shallow impressions, as though drawn with a thumb into clay and left for some later age to deepen.

There is no water here. No snow gathers on the summits, no hidden springs soften the ravines. The coolness is absolute but dry, a mineral cold that preserves rather than freezes. Dust does not clump. Soil, where it exists, is a fine ash of mica and powdered glass, flowing loosely around the roots of things.

And things do grow.

Across the high basins stand scattered groves of tall, narrow growths called veilwood. They are not trees in any familiar sense. Each consists of a hollow lattice of chalky fibers, opening upward into fans of black translucent membranes. These membranes drink neither rain nor sunlight. They feed upon differences: pressure between one side of a ridge and the other, heat leaking from newborn stone, the faint electrical unease between the earth and the low ceiling of cloud. In places where those differences are strong, the groves become luxuriant. Hundreds of dark fans overlap in tiers, making intricate canopies that seem lush from a distance, though there is ample emptiness between every pale trunk.

At close range, their abundance is severe. Nothing is soft. There are no fruits, no wet leaves, no mosses, no sap. The veilwood folds are dry as old paper and cool as metal. Their undersides are lined with silver cilia that comb invisible currents from the air. When pressure shifts, whole groves slowly turn—not bending, but revising their orientation joint by joint over weeks.

Lower growths spread beneath them: glasswort rosettes with serrated crystal blades; mats of gray filaments buried just below the dust; red mineral bulbs that push themselves halfway out of fissures, split into geometric flowers, and remain open for centuries. Some ridges are lush with these forms, patterned in black, ivory, rust, and dull green. Yet every patch ends sharply. Beyond it may lie kilometers of bare, unmarked stone.

There are no animals. Nothing flies between the groves. Nothing burrows. No small body rustles in the dry undergrowth. The landscape has pollination without carriers, seed without travel, feeding without mouths. Reproduction occurs through resonance. When one growth reaches maturity, it acquires a particular tone from the mountain beneath it. Somewhere else, sometimes on the far side of the valley, mineral tissue rearranges itself in reply and begins to grow.

This is part of the noise.

The whole country is deafening.

The peaks sound continuously, though little appears to move. Deep notes pass through them at intervals too long to be called rhythm: one pressure-wave may take an hour to cross a valley, shaking dust from ledges in perfectly level sheets. The veilwoods amplify higher frequencies, producing a sustained metallic shriek above the subterranean roar. Crystal flowers answer in piercing, pure tones. Basins collect these sounds until the air seems solid with them.

Conversation is impossible outside shelter. Voices vanish at the lips. Even impact is swallowed; one may strike stone with a hammer and feel the blow in the arm without hearing it amid the greater din.

Yet the sound does not suggest speed or violence. It is the noise of immense slowness magnified beyond proportion—the scrape of a continent moving a finger’s width in a lifetime, the settling of particles, the gradual hardening of matter. Processes too patient to perceive have somehow been made audible at unbearable volume. The land screams while scarcely changing.

The mountains are still under construction. Stone wells upward from broad seams in their flanks, not molten but soft, cool, and faintly luminous. It emerges in angular folds, pauses for decades, then decides upon hardness. New cliffs sometimes appear overnight, though “overnight” is uncertain here. Their surfaces are immaculate, unweathered, and patterned with parallel grooves like the grain of something grown.

Erosion has barely begun. The oldest scars are tentative. A few slopes bear narrow channels cut not by liquid but by migrating absence: thin regions where matter loses agreement with itself and vanishes grain by grain. These channels climb as often as they descend. Elsewhere, detached boulders hang a handspan above the ground because falling has not yet become locally consistent.

Distance is similarly unreliable.

A summit seen across one valley may require three days to reach in the morning and twenty minutes in the evening. Paths seldom move, but the amount of country contained between their stones changes. Groves sometimes stand with their roots visibly planted in a ridge many kilometers away, their trunks crossing the intervening air without perspective. Looking through their black membranes reveals not the sky behind them but other parts of the plateau—usually places that do not exist until someone sees them there.

Shadows are more dependable than objects. Travelers navigate by them.

The dim light comes from no visible sun. It spreads sideways from the horizon and collects in hollows, leaving mountaintops darker than valleys. At certain elevations, stone casts shadows upward. These form long black columns that lean into the cloud ceiling, and veilwood seeds often germinate inside them. At other times the light behaves like a fine powder: it settles on ledges, can be brushed away by hand, and accumulates in abandoned interiors until their doors glow around the edges.

Human—or nearly human—marks are rare.

On high saddles stand low shelters assembled from fitted slabs, sinewless root fiber, and sheets cut from dead veilwood. They are built without mortar. Their walls are thick, their entrances narrow, their interiors lined with nested layers of felted mineral threads to blunt the sound. Inside, the roar becomes merely crushing.

Each shelter contains simple things: stone wedges, bone-shaped implements carved from no known skeleton, ceramic cups despite there being nothing to pour, braided cords, black glass knives, and broad ear coverings packed with dust. Metal is scarce and generally soft. A copper hook or hammered needle may be passed down for generations. Fire is rarer still. Dry growth burns reluctantly and produces cold white flame, useful for light but not heat.

The builders leave tally marks on the inner walls. These do not count days. They record agreements: **floor remains below**, **eastern door opens outward**, **voices belong to speakers**, **the third peak is distant**. Such statements must be renewed periodically. If neglected, the shelter may reinterpret them. A door becomes the memory of a door. An interior opens onto an exterior several valleys away. A sleeper wakes with their shadow sealed inside the wall.

The oldest ruins are scarcely more elaborate than the living shelters. Short rows of standing stones cross empty basins, each stone tuned to a different component of the surrounding roar. There are circles of hand-sized cairns around places where gravity is weak. On one ridge, a staircase of two hundred steps rises into clear air and ends. Its builders did not intend a tower or temple. When the steps were made, there was ground above them.

No road remains continuous for long. Trails are marked instead with paired stakes: one pale, one black. The pale stake identifies where the traveler is; the black one identifies where the landscape currently believes the traveler to be. Safe passage is possible only while the stakes agree.

Nothing happens quickly here. Groves turn over decades. Peaks ascend by imperceptible increments. The deep notes stretch across seasons. Even failures of reality unfold with solemn patience. A boulder may spend a century becoming transparent. A ravine may slowly forget its depth. One can watch a distant ridge divide into two, but only by returning to the same place over generations.

The stillness would be profound if not for the sound.

Beyond the settled plateaus, the country rises further. The highest formations pierce the cloud-dark and continue as inverted ranges visible through gaps in the sky. Their roots face upward. Their summits point toward the mountains below, nearly touching. Between them lies a narrow band of empty air, a second horizon overhead.

At long intervals, perhaps once in several lifetimes, the two ranges move closer.

Every veilwood fan turns toward the gap. Every crystal flower opens. The shelters’ stones tighten against one another. The tally marks deepen by themselves. The roar swells until it is no longer heard through the ears but through teeth, tools, scars, and thoughts.

Then, somewhere far off, an upper summit touches a lower one.

For a moment the landscape becomes structurally complete.

The sky hardens. Distances snap into clean proportions. Shadows return to their objects. The sound ceases so totally that the silence feels like impact. In that brief interval, the inhabitants emerge from their shelters and look across a world made definite: cool black forests scattered among pale infant mountains, raw valleys awaiting their erosion, empty paths, immaculate cliffs, and no movement anywhere.

Then the peaks draw apart.

The noise returns. The horizon loosens. Stone resumes its slow uncertainty, and the unfinished country continues becoming itself.
