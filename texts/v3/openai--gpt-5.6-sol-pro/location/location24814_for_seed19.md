The land begins high above any country that could reasonably support it.

It is a plateau, though the word suggests a table and this is more like the worn head of a world-sized bone: broad, pale, slightly domed, and exposed on every side to distances that do not agree with one another. At the western rim, the ground seems to fall away for several thousand meters into a basin of gray stone. At the eastern rim, the same basin appears level with the plateau, as if the drop exists only when approached from one direction. Looking north, one sees successive shelves of mineral country, each apparently higher than the last, yet all descending toward the horizon.

The air is cool and perfectly dry. Not desert-dry, with hidden moisture in roots and night condensation, but dry as a sealed jar of powdered stone. There is no dew. No ice sleeps in the shaded cracks. Nothing has ever been rounded by rain. The atmosphere has a faint taste of chalk, iron, and something resinous, though there is no resin nearby. Breathing is easy but feels unnecessary, as though the air merely permits lungs rather than serving them.

In ordinary daylight, the plateau is the color of old teeth.

The light comes from a small, unremarkable sun fixed at a moderate angle. It is neither glaring nor dim. Shadows are distinct without being sharp. Colors remain sober: bone-white flats, ocher ledges, green-black mineral seams, rust around ancient fractures. Yet the sun does not visibly move. There is no morning or evening, only a permanent middle portion of day. The temperature remains cool in sunlight and only slightly cooler in shadow. Heat does not accumulate. Cold does not deepen. The place has settled past weather.

Nothing blows.

Even the fine powder gathered in hollows is not dust in the usual sense. It does not lift when stepped on. It compresses reluctantly, taking an exact impression and retaining it without collapse. There are tracks here that may be older than the species that made them. There are also tracks whose makers stand beside them and have perhaps always stood there.

They are everywhere.

At first the plateau seems studded with irregular pillars, black pebbles, white humps, rows of hooked spines. With attention, nearly all of these resolve into animals.

A herd of long-backed creatures occupies a shallow valley between two limestone rises. There are tens of thousands of them, packed flank to flank, each no larger than a dog. Their legs are thin and numerous, six or eight beneath each body depending on the angle from which they are counted. Their hides have the dull nap of ash. Every head is lowered toward the mineral floor, though there is nothing there resembling food. They do not graze. They do not breathe visibly. Their tall, spoon-shaped ears remain upright and motionless.

Among them stand larger animals like upright boulders draped in folded skin. Colonies of finger-length shells cling to those folds. Pale mites occupy the shells. Inside each mite’s translucent abdomen, smaller dark creatures can be seen arranged in circles, not moving but apparently alive. The density continues downward past easy perception: every hide is a province; every scale has its tenant; every tenant carries another ecology in the seams of its body.

The plateau is not empty land inhabited by overwhelming fauna. The fauna is the landscape’s chief material.

In some regions the ground cannot be seen for kilometers. Broad, armored backs overlap like roof tiles. Coiled animals fill ravines from wall to wall. Stilt-legged grazers stand over them, their feet placed with impossible precision in the few remaining gaps. Winged forms hang in the air, but do not beat their wings or drift. They appear pinned to particular positions in the sky. A flock may occupy the space above a ridge so densely that the daylight beneath it becomes mottled, though no feather stirs and no shadow changes.

The silence of so many bodies is the first thing that feels wrong.

There are sounds, but each is small and isolated: a faint click from within a shell; the dry settling of a joint; a single leathery creak that may take several minutes to complete. Sometimes a noise repeats from different directions with exact pitch and spacing, as if the plateau has only a limited collection of sounds and must reuse them. There is no chorus, no stampede, no wing-rush, no mating cry. Predators stand with their jaws around prey that remains unbitten. Horns are locked in contests without strain. Newborn animals lie beneath their mothers, clean and open-eyed, but no afterbirth, milk, or moisture exists anywhere.

None of the creatures appears dead. Death would imply an event.

Instead, life here is a fixed relationship.

The animals do not feed in sequence, one mouthful after another. A grazer is connected to a particular patch of mineral crust as permanently as a tree would be connected to soil. Its teeth touch a film of pale lichen; the lichen touches a seam of reactive stone; the seam extends deep into the plateau. Energy passes—or has always already passed—through the arrangement. The predator enclosing the grazer’s neck is similarly joined to it. It does not kill. Its contact is enough. Parasite and host, hunter and quarry, parent and offspring have become positions in a single unchanging mechanism.

There is almost no vegetation. In shaded clefts grow black threads finer than hair, each branching at mathematically identical intervals. They are called plants only because they emerge from cracks and turn broad surfaces toward the light. Their tissues contain no sap. If broken, they show nested tubes of green ceramic. Low fans of copper-colored blades occur around certain animal colonies, particularly beneath the suspended flocks. These blades are stiff, cold, and edged with silica. They never bend. No seed is seen traveling, but identical fans appear at vast separations, sometimes down to the placement of flaws.

Elsewhere stand solitary thorn structures, waist-high and gray. From a distance they resemble shrubs. Close up, their branches prove to be antlers shed by no animal present, fitted together so tightly that the joints cannot be found. A few bear seedlike capsules that are actually curled insects, each holding the next branch in its mouth.

The oldest erosion has stripped the plateau of everything capable of being stripped. Its cliffs are not jagged. They have retreated into immense, smooth scarps, curved by forces no longer active. Channels cross the uplands, but no liquid carved them. They are broad and shallow, with polished beds and tributaries that join at incorrect angles. Some rise uphill and end in the open air. Others split around islands formed entirely of compacted skeletons. Whatever once flowed here may have been pressure, migration, attention, or time.

There are no loose stones at the foot of the cliffs. Every fallen piece has reached its final resting place. Talus slopes are packed so exactly that they behave like a single solid. Cracks exist, but do not widen. Overhangs have achieved shapes that should collapse and yet no longer recognize gravity as a continuing instruction.

Scattered across the plateau are signs of makers.

Most are small. A ring of hand-stacked stones around a crouching animal. A low wall extending twenty paces and stopping for no visible reason. Three posts of horn driven into the mineral crust, supporting a roof woven from dry tendon. Shallow pits have been pecked into rock with stone hammers; the hammer heads remain beside them, their handles wrapped in hide gone hard as wood. There are shelters built for bodies much like a person’s, though the entrances are too narrow at the bottom and too wide at the top.

No settlement is large enough to be called a village, but the structures repeat across the country. On distant shelves, one can pick out little roofs, marker cairns, tilted screens, fences that enclose only a single standing beast. Each was made by hand. Tool marks remain distinct. Copper appears rarely, beaten into thin hooks or needles, never cast. Cordage is braided from animal hair and glassy root fiber. Pottery vessels are plentiful but useless for water: shallow, perforated bowls, pierced jars, cups with sealed interiors.

Many structures seem intended not to change the animals, but to clarify them.

A hide screen frames one particular horned creature when viewed from a stone seat. A line of white chips connects a motionless insect to a cliff twelve kilometers away. A shelter has been constructed around an empty volume between two packed herds; standing inside it, one realizes the emptiness has the precise outline of a huge seated figure.

There are handprints on some walls. They are made not with pigment but with absence. The stone inside each print is fractionally less real: pale, depthless, difficult to focus upon. Some show five fingers, some seven. A few have no fixed number until counted, and then keep the chosen number for as long as the observer remains nearby.

This is how the plateau’s deeper failures announce themselves—not dramatically, but as stable facts.

Distance is locally obedient. One can walk toward a cairn and reach it. Yet behind the cairn may be a valley too broad to fit between it and the ridge just crossed. Animals viewed from the left have different markings than when viewed from the right. Their scars do not rotate with their bodies but remain facing north, like compass needles. A white beast standing among black ones can sometimes be recognized as the same beast repeated several hundred times, each instance sharing a single inner surface. If one opens an eyelid at the edge of the herd, all its distant counterparts reveal the same dark eye.

The density of life may result from this repetition. There may be millions of creatures, or only a few creatures folded through space until every valley is crowded with them.

The horizon also has inhabitants. Their shapes are too large to interpret: sloping backs mistaken for mountain ranges, ribs mistaken for parallel escarpments. They cannot be approached. Walking toward one shortens the ground behind but not the ground ahead. Occasionally a supposed ridge opens a line of dull eyes. Nothing else happens.

Time behaves similarly. It is not stopped; stopped time could not be experienced. Rather, events have lost the ability to displace one another. A foot placed in powder makes a print, but the instant before the print remains available beside it. One may look down and see both the untouched surface and the depression, occupying the same patch without blending. The creatures seem adapted to this. Their lives are complete postures rather than journeys. Birth, feeding, predation, and rest coexist as anatomy.

Travelers—if travelers are what made the shelters—learned to move without expecting the world to be altered by passage. Their paths are marked with paired stones, one dark and one pale. Their tools are designed for tiny, local acts: scoring a line, lifting a scale, separating fibers, touching one surface to another. No wheel was attempted. A wheel assumes that the ground ahead will arrive and the ground behind will depart.

Near the plateau’s center rises its highest feature, a blunt summit surrounded by concentric scarps. The climb is steep but not hazardous; no fragment can slip, and no gust can unbalance a body. The route passes through fields of increasingly small animals, packed so densely that the safest footing is often on their armor. They bear weight without protest. Higher still, the animals become minute and fill the pores of the stone itself, until the mountain’s apparent grain is revealed as countless folded wings.

At the summit stands a single structure: four rough pillars and a lintel, all shaped with hammerstones. Within is a narrow chair facing the ordinary, motionless sun. The seat is polished by use, though nothing here should accumulate wear.

From that height, the whole plateau can be seen—or something presenting itself as the whole. The pale uplands extend through cool, lucid air. Ancient channels braid among crowded herds. Suspended flocks cast permanent shadows. Shelters and cairns make faint geometries between cliffs. Beyond them, the giant horizon-creatures lie in their impossible distances.

The view is immense, but there is no emptiness in it.

Every ledge is occupied. Every crevice contains a shell, filament, egg, eye, claw, or sleeping mouth. Every patch of sky holds wings too still to be flight. Even the apparent gaps have outlines and postures. The silence is not the silence of abandonment. It is the silence of total attendance—of a world in which all beings have arrived at once, taken their places, and have no remaining need to call to one another.

Nothing waits to happen.

Nothing is finished.

The plateau simply persists in the cool light, immeasurably old, crowded beyond counting, and settled into a form from which even reality has lost the strength to erode it.
