The plain lies low enough that the horizon is often hidden by its own heat. It is not a desert in the familiar sense. The ground is dark, dense, and faintly damp: a continuous floor of ironstone, vitrified clay, and mineral scale, worn into broad shallow corrugations. Nothing grows in the troughs. There are no roots, seeds, husks, or ancient seams of coal to suggest that anything ever did.

Across this floor stand sparse mineral formations like the abandoned components of an enormous industry. Some resemble cooling towers, others pressure vessels or truncated chimneys, but none were built. They have no joints, courses, fastenings, or tool marks. Each rose whole from the basin through slow deposition: pale cylinders of carbonate around warm vents, black fluted cones of basaltic glass, iron blisters with smooth rivet-like nodules formed by crystallization. From a distance they imply a factory. Up close they become geology again.

The resemblance is strengthened by the sound.

A bass vibration fills the region without interruption. It can be felt through the soles before it is heard—a vast, steady mechanical note with harmonics layered above it: the drone of turbines, the far hammering of sheet metal, the hiss of pressurized lines. Yet there are no turbines, hammers, or lines. The noise comes from deep cavities beneath the plain, where heat and pressure pass through repeating chambers in the bedrock. The chambers act as resonators. Gas circulates through them at a constant rate, driving columns of stone and trapped brine into permanent oscillation.

Nothing starts. Nothing stops. The individual pulses are too rapid and too regular to count, and their pattern has not altered within any known span of observation. Here, activity does not imply change. The whole basin operates in equilibrium.

Warm air rests over the plain. It smells of wet metal, hot dust, and the sharp mineral scent released when rain first touches stone. Moisture is present but never abundant. It gathers as condensation on the shaded sides of the vent towers and runs down in narrow silver threads. In shallow depressions, water forms clear, ankle-deep pockets with rust-red rims. A low river occupies the basin’s center, though “river” is almost misleading: its surface creeps between banks of polished slagstone so steadily that it appears fixed. There are no floods and no dry seasons. The channels are old, rounded, and completely settled into their courses.

At certain distances, fine rain can be seen falling in vertical gray veils. These curtains never seem to approach or withdraw. They hang over particular strips of the plain where warm subterranean vapor meets cooler descending air. Water falls, darkens the stone, drains into fissures, heats below, and rises again. The cycle is local and exact. No cloud crosses the sky. No puddle gains or loses a measurable depth.

Erosion has done nearly everything it can do here. Ridges are lowered to stable profiles; channels have reached the hardness beneath them; loose sediment has long since migrated into broad, compact beds. Wind passes over forms that no longer offer it much to take. Even the exposed glass has lost its cutting edge, becoming rounded, smoky, and greasy-looking. The landscape is weathered without appearing ruined. It has arrived at its final shape.

In normal daylight the basin is plainly visible and almost colorless. The sky is a washed blue-white. Iron gives the ground subdued reds and browns; sulfur stains some vent lips a tired yellow; wet surfaces shine charcoal-black. There is no dramatic glow from below, no unnatural darkness, no perpetual storm. Its strangeness remains exposed in ordinary light, where it cannot be mistaken for a trick of atmosphere.

The closest things to animals are rarely seen.

In the warm water pockets, small translucent disks sometimes cling beneath overhanging stone. They have no visible mouths and make no obvious movement. Their bodies contain branching metallic threads, and they seem to feed on the slight electrical differences between wet minerals. Whether they are animals, colonies, or self-maintaining precipitates is uncertain. They remain in the same positions for years. When touched, they contract once with a click like cooling tin, then slowly resume their former shape.

Farther out, one may find three-toed impressions crossing a film of ocher mud. The tracks are always sharp and always empty. No one has witnessed the creature making them. They do not multiply, blur, or fill with water. It is possible that they are not tracks at all, but stress fractures repeated with such precision that the mind insists upon an absent walker.

There is no shelter because nothing here needs shelter. There are no roads because the stone floor is already passable in every direction. No wall, marker, machine, cable, foundation, or worked shard interrupts the basin. Its industrial character belongs entirely to processes without makers: pressure, resonance, circulation, heat exchange, and mineral separation, all occurring on a regional scale.

The loudest places are the low fields around the river. There the underground drone divides into distinct bands. One strip of land rings with an unwavering metallic whine; another beats with a slow concussion that seems like stamping machinery, although the interval between blows never varies. Along the water, tiny gas outlets exhale in continuous rows, producing a hard pneumatic hiss. Because the sounds never change, the ear gradually ceases to treat them as events. After enough time they become a kind of silence—an immense, occupied silence that leaves no room for surprise.

Dust does not rise. Stones do not fall. The rain curtains do not drift. Even the heat shimmer repeats the same distortions in the same places, held by fixed currents of air. A traveler can move through the landscape, but the landscape does not answer their movement. Footprints pressed into rare mud close almost immediately—not by flowing or healing, but because the mud rebounds with the exact slow elasticity of a loaded seal. A displaced pebble vibrates across the surface until it returns to the shallow socket where centuries of resonance have seated it.

That is perhaps the governing rule of the basin: departures are permitted, but states are conserved.

Nothing is dead here in the sense of decay, because decay would require progression. Nothing is alive in the sense of growth, because growth would require a future different from the present. The basin is warm, damp in its hollows, weathered, loud, and apparently industrious. It performs enormous work and produces nothing. It has been running forever, though there is no machinery, no operator, and no evidence that it was ever switched on.
