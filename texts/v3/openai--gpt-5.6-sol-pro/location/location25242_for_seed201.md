The place occupies a single high basin, no larger than a hamlet, set into the shoulder of a mountain far above the permanent clouds. From a distance it looks like a green thumbprint pressed into black stone. Up close, its boundaries are severe: three walls of newly broken basalt, sharp as split crockery, and on the fourth side a lip beyond which the world falls away into white distance.

The basin is young. Frost has not yet rounded its ridges or taught the scree where to settle. Slabs stand on edge. Cracks remain clean and blue-black inside, glittering with feldspar and small glassy inclusions. Some of the rock still bears ropy folds from whatever upheaval raised it, though no heat remains except where sunlight lies directly upon the stone. A person crossing the basin must step carefully. Nothing here has had time to become dependable.

It is intensely bright. The high air has almost no haze, and the sky is a hard, saturated blue that seems close enough to strike with an upraised hand. Snowfields on the surrounding peaks return the sun from every direction. Shadows are black and cold; lit surfaces are almost painfully clear. Every blade, hair, feather, and crystal acquires a fine white edge.

Yet the basin is greener than it has any right to be.

Water does not flow openly for long. There is no lake and no continuous stream. Moisture arrives in fragments: a brief afternoon sleet, fog combed from passing cloud, meltwater released from a cornice during a few bright hours. The fractured rock catches it. Under nearly every green patch lies a hidden cup or seam where water lingers beyond sight.

Vegetation gathers around these concealed damp places in dense, low islands. Moss pads swell over the cracks, dark emerald in their depths and silvered by frost on top. Cushion plants form domes so compact that they feel like woven mats under the palm. Tiny willows creep horizontally through them, their trunks no thicker than cord but decades old, leaves lacquered and close to the ground. There are red-stemmed sedges, black lichens, pale bellflowers, and a fern that unfolds only at noon, when the stones are briefly warmer than the air.

The growth is lush without being tall. Almost nothing rises above the knee. To stand upright here is to expose oneself to the wind; plants survive by making a second, miniature landscape beneath the weather. Between the green islands are broad intervals of bare gravel and empty stone. From one patch to the next there may be twenty paces of nothing at all. The basin feels both fertile and vacant, like a garden whose beds have been placed very far apart.

In the early morning, condensation pearls on every living surface. The beads freeze before they fall, clothing the moss in clear grains. When the sun reaches the basin, the grains loosen one by one and vanish into the plants. There is no general dripping, only a close, secret absorption. If a cushion is pressed, cold water wells around the fingers; when released, the plant closes over it again.

Animals are everywhere, though at first the basin seems deserted.

Small gray grazers live among the stone fans. They resemble hares in motion but have blunt faces, short ears, and wide, divided hooves suited to the unstable slopes. Their fur carries the same blue tint as the basalt shadows. They seldom call. Whole groups may be feeding within sight, spaced widely across a sedge patch, with only the rapid cutting of their teeth to betray them.

Insects crowd the warm hollows. Dark bees, thickly furred, crawl more often than they fly. Minute copper beetles move between flowers. There are midges in the wet seams, but the air is too thin and cold for clouds of them; they remain in trembling columns a hand’s breadth above the moss. Pale moths rest on sunlit rock with their wings spread flat, gathering heat.

Above them move the basin’s hunters. Narrow-winged birds circle without crying. They use the updraft from the precipice and may remain aloft for hours without a visible wingbeat. Their shadows pass over the ground faster than the birds themselves appear to move. When one folds and drops, the attack is nearly soundless: a brief rush, a scatter of pebbles, then stillness again.

There are also long-bodied, six-toed lizards under the warmest stones. They are not truly cold-loving; they survive by following sunlight around the basin. At dawn they wait beneath translucent flakes of rock that act like crude windows. At midday they hunt among the flowers. Before the shadow of the western wall reaches them, they retreat into deep cracks where the mountain’s temperature changes less than the air. Their scales are matte white except along the spine, where they carry a line of green matching the moss.

The abundance is concentrated rather than obvious. Every damp pocket is crowded with feeding, nesting, burrowing, pollinating life, while the dry spaces between may hold no living thing at all. Crossing the basin means moving repeatedly from near-void into intricate activity and back again.

Wind should dominate such a place, but the basin’s walls shear the strongest currents overhead. One can see snow streaming from the peaks and cloud racing beyond the rim while the interior remains nearly quiet. The air makes only a thin hiss along the basalt teeth. Most sounds are small and near: claws touching stone, the papery shuffle of alpine leaves, grit settling after a freeze, the faint ticking of ice crystals breaking loose in the sun.

Every so often the mountain speaks more loudly. A crack opens beneath a snowbank. A fresh slab breaks from the north wall and explodes into angular fragments. The animals freeze, all at once, and the silence afterward feels newly constructed.

People have used the basin, though they do not appear to live here now.

Near the eastern wall stand three low shelters built from unmortared slabs. They are little more than stone rings with roofs of hide-darkened poles, half sunk into the ground and scarcely higher than the vegetation. Their entrances face away from the drop. Inside, the floors are lined with old sedge mats and the compacted fur of the gray grazers. Stone hooks are driven into cracks. A few bone pins remain tucked into one wall, along with a chipped green knife made from volcanic glass.

There is no road to the shelters. A sequence of small cairns marks the safest route across the scree, but many have fallen, and some consist of only two deliberately balanced stones. At the basin’s center is a shallow bowl hammered into a boulder. It collects meltwater for an hour or two each day. Beside it lies a wooden ladle, split and repaired with sinew. The repair is careful; the ladle has been preserved longer than the wood should reasonably endure in the bright, dry cold.

The shelters suggest seasonal visits: hunters waiting for migrations, gatherers cutting medicinal moss, perhaps families sheltering during crossings of the high ridge. No metal is visible. The poles were shaped with stone blades, and the hides were pierced with bone. Soot stains the inner roofs, but fuel is so scarce that the hearths contain mostly dried dung and braided plant roots.

Nothing has been built to conquer the basin. The structures borrow hollows, use fallen stone, and remain below the wind. Even the cairns are low. Whatever people come here understand the first rule of the place: height is exposure. To rise is to freeze, to be seen, or to be taken by the air.

The second rule is that water belongs to darkness. It persists under moss, beneath slabs, inside shaded fissures. Water left in the open becomes ice, vapor, or glare. Animals know this instinctively. Their paths join green patch to green patch, never following the most direct line.

The third rule is that sunlight is both season and territory. The bright zone moves steadily across the basin, and life travels with it. Flowers open along its advancing edge. Insects wake. Lizards emerge. The gray grazers leave the dazzling patches and feed just inside the cooler boundary. Predatory birds position themselves where the light makes prey visible but hides the sky behind brilliance. By late afternoon the whole ecology has shifted westward by several hundred paces.

At sunset the movement stops with startling speed. The basin passes from gold to blue in minutes. Moisture stiffens. Leaves contract. Insects disappear into flowers and cracks. The grazers gather at the base of the wall, each animal pressed close to stone but not necessarily to another. The flying hunters leave on the last rising air.

Then the basin becomes what it first seemed to be: an empty room cut high into the mountain.

Only the green cushions retain a little warmth. Beneath them, roots continue to draw water through the dark. Under one slab, a lizard’s heart beats slowly. In the abandoned shelters, frost forms on the inside of the roofs. Far below, clouds close over the lower world, hiding every valley and route of descent.

By dawn, new hoofprints will cross the old ones. A cornice may have broken. A fresh seam may be wet, and one that fed a garden yesterday may be dry. The basin is small enough to see from edge to edge, but it never holds the same arrangement twice. It is still being made—by frost, gravity, hunger, light, and the brief passage of water through stone.
