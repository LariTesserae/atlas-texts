The town occupies a shallow notch high in a range of rounded, weather-worn mountains. It is not large: from the western gate to the eastern retaining wall is a few minutes’ walk, and from either rim one can see nearly every roof. Yet it has the structure of a city—streets, drains, public stairs, storehouses, courtyards, shrines, workshops, and a central market square. Its urban character comes not from crowding but from organization. Everything has a place, with generous emptiness between places.

The notch is floored with pale gray stone polished by generations of feet and by water that comes only intermittently. Low ridges rise on either side, too old to be jagged. Their exposed faces have softened into ledges, shelves, and shallow bowls. Scree has long since settled. Retaining walls hold the remaining soil in narrow terraces, though only a few are cultivated. Beyond the town, the slopes descend into dry valleys whose distance is made hazy by the altitude.

Most buildings are made from the same local stone: broad, irregular blocks set without mortar, fitted patiently with smaller wedges. Timber is scarce and used only for doors, lintels, roof frames, and the handles of tools. Roofs are shallow vaults of stone slabs covered with packed clay. Some carry a skin of moss on their shaded sides, but most are bare. The walls are thick enough to keep their interiors cool through the afternoon and to preserve the small warmth of hearths after sunset.

There are more structures than inhabitants. Whole lanes contain only one occupied house among several sealed ones. These are not ruins. Their doors have been blocked carefully with fitted stone, their drains capped, their roofs maintained when necessary. Empty buildings serve as windbreaks and thermal mass, holding the town together even when they no longer hold families. Nothing is dismantled casually. A house may remain closed for a century before its stone is taken for another purpose.

The central square is broad and nearly vacant. At its center is a low circular cistern with a worn lip and a wooden cover bound in dark iron. The town’s most elaborate construction lies beneath it: a network of settling chambers, clay-lined reservoirs, and narrow channels cut into the mountain. Rain is too unreliable to fall often, but when it comes it arrives as cold, patient showers. Every roof and paving stone has been angled to receive it. Water passes through gravel beds and covered conduits, disappearing underground before the sun or wind can take much of it.

More commonly, moisture comes as condensation. Night air gathers against the ridge and leaves beads on stone screens erected along the upper wall. These screens are simple lattices of stacked slate, cool enough to comb droplets from fog. At dawn, thin threads of water creep down their faces into troughs. The sound is faint but distinct: irregular tapping in an otherwise quiet town.

Open water is rare. A few basins stand in shaded courts, each no wider than a table. Their surfaces remain dark and still, disturbed only when someone dips a cup or when a high-country gnat touches down. Near the lowest drainage gate, seepage supports a thumb-wide runnel through the stones. It never becomes a stream. It vanishes beneath a patch of sedge and reappears farther downslope among black pebbles.

Vegetation follows these small assurances of moisture. Most of the ground is bare rock, lime-colored dust, or old paving. In cracks grow wiry grasses, low cushion plants, and a bitter herb with gray-green leaves. A few twisted junipers stand in protected courtyards, pruned less for beauty than for fuel, resin, and shade. Their roots enter cistern overflows through channels lined with pottery shards. On the outer terraces, sparse rows of barley and lentils survive behind waist-high walls. Each plant is given more space than it would have in a valley field, so that no root competes unnecessarily with another.

There is one orchard, if seven trees deserve the name. The trees are small, heavily knuckled, and enclosed by a wall taller than a person. Their fruit is hard and sour until stored through the first frosts. Fallen leaves are collected rather than left to decay. Soil is too valuable to be allowed to wander.

Animals are seldom seen. Small pale lizards occupy sunlit masonry during the warmest hour, though at this elevation even they move slowly. A few mountain birds cross overhead without landing. Occasionally a dark goat appears on the ridge, watches the town, and departs. Domestic animals are limited to a handful of tethered goats, some hens kept in deep stone pens, and patient pack asses small enough to negotiate the stair streets. There are no swarms, no constant barking, no dense colonies of pigeons. The settlement’s quiet is partly architectural and partly ecological.

Sound travels cleanly in the cool air. A hand hammering copper in a workshop can be heard from the opposite gate, each blow followed by a brief, dry echo. Millstones turn by hand, producing a low mineral rasp. Sandaled footsteps scrape on inclines. Doors creak, pots touch stone, and voices remain subdued because no one needs to raise them. Wind passes above the roofs more often than through the streets. The town was laid out so that its lanes bend before the prevailing gusts, leaving the interior with the stillness of a sheltered room.

Its tools are simple and repaired repeatedly. Stone mauls, bronze chisels, bone needles, wooden levers, clay lamps, woven baskets, and hide buckets do nearly all the work. Metal is present but precious. Hinges are uncommon; most doors pivot in sockets cut directly into threshold and lintel. Heavy loads move on sledges or rollers because the stairs make wheeled carts impractical. Construction advances slowly, by hand, but the town has had a very long time to perfect what it does.

Nothing here appears unfinished, though nothing appears new. Stair treads have become shallow curves. Corners have lost their sharpness. Drain mouths are dark with mineral stain. Inscriptions cut above doorways are legible but softened, their grooves collecting fine moss after rain. Repairs imitate the older work so closely that it is difficult to distinguish one century from another.

The climate keeps a narrow range. Days are cool and clear, with ordinary white daylight and blue shadows. Clouds pass without drama. Rain darkens the town almost evenly, turning roofs, walls, and paving into different shades of charcoal. Afterward, the stone dries from the edges inward. Nights bring cold enough for thin ice to form in exposed bowls, but deep freezing is unusual. Snow sometimes dusts the ridge and is gone by midday.

The place seems changeless not because nothing happens, but because every change is answered. A wall settles; it is reset with the same stones. A cistern silts; the silt is lifted to the terraces. A roof loses clay; clay is carried back from the old pit below the gate. A household ends; its rooms are closed and kept within the town’s plan. Water enters, is used, and returns to soil or air. Even refuse has established routes: ash to the fields, bone to workshops, broken pottery to drains and foundations.

There are few monuments. The town itself is the monument—an accumulation of modest decisions sustained so long that they have become inseparable from the mountain. Its highest point is a small roofless court containing a waist-high stone. The stone has no carved figure, only a shallow depression where rain gathers. People touch the damp hollow when passing. In dry weather they touch the smooth place where water would be.

From there, one can look across the whole settlement: pale roofs separated by courts, stairways descending and rising again, slate fog screens along the rim, seven orchard crowns, a thread of smoke from a cookfire. Beyond the walls there is mostly open mountain.

Within them, there is mostly open space given form.
