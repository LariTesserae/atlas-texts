### The Ninth Seam

The place is no larger than a crowded room, though no one has found its outer wall.

It lies inside the summit of a mountain that has not yet been made.

From below, if below can be trusted, the mountain appears complete: a black-green needle rising beyond the clouds, so high that daylight should be fierce and the air unbreathable. Yet within the Ninth Seam there is heat—close, wet, bodily heat—and the atmosphere presses heavily against the skin. Breath comes easily but never coolly. Every surface beads with condensation. Water gathers in the hollows of leaves, on the undersides of rafters, along the lips of clay cups mortared into the walls. It has rained recently, or is about to rain, or rain is occurring somewhere too near to be seen.

There is no native stone here. There is no soil.

The “cliffs” are made from millions of thumb-sized bricks, each pressed by hand and fired unevenly in smoky kilns. Their glazes range from tar-black to the green of bruised fruit. Ridges are bundles of cane wrapped in bark cloth and hardened with resin. Shelves of land are stacked ceramic trays. Ravines are narrow gaps left between walls. What resembles moss is a dense embroidery of living fibers stitched through woven reed mats. Even the mud has been manufactured: crushed pottery, soot, fermented leaf pulp, salt, and water kneaded under bare feet until it behaves like earth.

Everything bears fingerprints.

Nothing bears wear.

Edges remain sharp enough to cut. Steps have no softened centers. Watercourses are bright, raw channels newly gouged by hooked bone tools, yet water is already coursing through them as if it has followed those routes for centuries. Broken tiles show fresh orange interiors, but no fallen fragments lie beneath them. The Seam is under construction continuously, though there is nowhere left to build.

Its builders occupy every handspan.

They are rarely visible as whole people. A wrist emerges between curtains of vine to tamp clay into a crack. Toes grip a ladder rung overhead. An eye watches through a drain. Voices call measurements from behind walls only a palm thick, and other voices answer from impossibly far away. There are platforms above platforms, hammocks under bridges, ovens inside staircases, gardens rooted in roof baskets, cisterns suspended over sleeping shelves. A person turning around must negotiate with leaves, ropes, wet pottery, hanging tools, and the knees of three neighbors.

Movement is not travel here. It is rearrangement.

The tools are simple: stone adzes, fire-hardened pegs, shell scrapers, wooden mallets, copper needles used until they are thin as grass. Yet the workmanship exceeds what the tools ought to allow. Ceramic pipes fork into capillaries finer than hair. Reed scaffolds carry weights that should crush them. Knotted cords support terraces whose mass cannot be reconciled with the size of the chamber.

No machine can be found. Nevertheless, the entire place behaves like one.

Water enters from the ceiling in slow, fat drops. Each drop strikes a bronze leaf and rings it like a bell. It runs through carved gutters, turns tiny paddle wheels, fills counterweight jars, lowers one garden while raising another, opens vents, tips seed trays, washes sleeping mats, and finally disappears into a square hole in the floor. A moment later, the same drop may fall again—warmer, carrying yellow pollen—or may be found waiting inside an unopened fruit.

The loudness comes less from any single source than from the inability of sound to escape. Mallets knock. Clay slabs clap against molds. Pulley ropes creak. Water drums on hollow gourds. Steam coughs from lidded jars. Hundreds of suspended chimes, made from teeth, shell, bone, copper scraps, and fired clay, chatter in hot drafts. Workers sing short phrases to coordinate lifts, but echoes continue the phrases after the singers stop. Sometimes the echoes begin first, providing instructions for work not yet undertaken.

The builders obey them cautiously.

Light arrives in weak green columns through holes said to open onto the sky. Looking directly through one shows not the sky but another portion of the Seam: wet stairs, bent backs, red flowers, a hand lifting a bowl. The view may be from above, from years ago, or from the perspective of the bowl. Most illumination comes from wick lamps floating in shallow basins. Their flames burn downward into the water. Shadows collect above objects rather than beneath them, forming dark canopies in which small plants take root.

Vegetation is abundant, but its lushness is compressed and disciplined. Vines are braided into load-bearing ropes while alive. Broad leaves have been trained to overlap as roofing. Fig roots clutch stacks of bowls. Ferns unfurl inside drain mouths, filtering silt from the circulating water. Pale orchids grow from damp mortar and close whenever spoken to. Peppers, taro, gourds, medicinal mosses, and black-stemmed grasses occupy every possible pocket.

There are no weeds. A plant growing where it was not assigned is treated not as an invader but as an instruction.

Because there is no soil, roots enter the constructed world directly. They pass through woven walls, split unfired bricks, drink from jars, and wind around the fingers of sleeping workers. Some roots end in careful knots. Others emerge from the air with no plant attached, searching slowly for something that can be considered ground.

Animals are scarce. The height should exclude nearly all of them, though height has uncertain meaning inside the Seam. A few translucent gnats gather around the downward-burning lamps. There is a single black lizard whose feet make the sound of distant sandals; it appears only on freshly completed surfaces and vanishes once they are named. Small white crabs live in the gutters, feeding on fallen pollen and flakes of glaze. Their shells are marked with floor plans. Builders collect shed shells and follow the plans when space permits.

Most nights—or what passes for nights—something feathered moves in the upper darkness. It is never clearly seen. A warm feather occasionally drops into a cistern and causes the water level to rise without limit.

The central feature is called the Unfinished Peak.

It stands in the smallest chamber, surrounded by so much construction that reaching it requires crawling sideways between sweating walls. The Peak is a cone of raw gray clay not much taller than a seated child. Tiny terraces ascend its sides. Tiny ladders bridge its cracks. Miniature workers, each made from a rolled grain of clay, crowd its slopes carrying baskets smaller than fingernail parings.

They are moving.

Close inspection reveals that they are building the mountain in which the Ninth Seam is hidden. Their mallets produce the deepest sounds in the chamber. When they raise a wall, a corresponding wall appears somewhere in the Seam at full size, forcing gardens, sleepers, and stairways to shift aside. When one of their tiny kilns is lit, the temperature rises. When they spill a thimbleful of water, rain pounds every roof.

No one touches them.

At the cone’s summit is a minute dark opening. Those who place an eye against it see a hot, wet chamber packed with vegetation and builders. At its center stands another clay mountain, and within that mountain another chamber, continuing inward past the reach of sight. Conversely, some claim that the Seam itself is merely a thumbprint in wet clay on a larger mountain, where immense hands are still deciding the shape of the world.

This may explain why nothing erodes. The landscape is too new to decay. It is remade at the exact rate at which it comes into being. Water does not wear channels deeper; it discovers them. Footsteps do not polish stairs; they complete the firing of the glaze. Cracks are not damage but openings through which the next portion of reality is installed.

The builders have several rules:

Never finish a circle.  
Never count the floors aloud.  
If a doorway opens onto open sky, close it before the sky notices.  
If rain falls upward, lie flat and hold the roots.  
If an echo uses your own voice, do not answer until it makes a mistake.  
No wall may remain unsupported, though a wall may support itself from the future.  
Every evening, leave one brick unset so the mountain can breathe.

The last rule is the most important.

At the dimmest hour, work pauses. Mallets go silent one by one, though the water and chimes continue. The inhabitants crowd onto ledges and into woven hollows. Somewhere, always nearby, a final brick-sized gap remains open in the construction.

Hot wind draws inward through it.

The whole Seam inhales. Leaves flatten. Lamps bend. Gutters empty. The clay mountain at the center trembles, and for one instant the walls become transparent. Beyond them is no rock, no atmosphere, no distant land—only countless wet hands pressing against the other side, shaping the summit around its hidden chamber.

Then the Seam exhales.

Rain bursts from every surface. New stairs unfold from walls. Roots knot themselves into bridges. A roar rises from the miniature peak, magnified through all its nested versions, and the crowded landscape begins another cycle of building itself into the space it already completely occupies.
