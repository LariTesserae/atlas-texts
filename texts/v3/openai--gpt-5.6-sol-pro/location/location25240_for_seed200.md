The land lies low enough that water never fully decides whether it belongs above or below the ground. It is not a swamp in the old, drowned sense. There are no cut banks, abandoned channels, exposed roots, or black pools deepened by centuries. The whole region appears newly settled from suspension: a broad, hot plain of pale silt, green water, and soft mineral paste, still smooth beneath its living cover.

Water stands only ankle-deep in most places. Elsewhere it hides immediately under a skin of moss and interlocked rootlets. Pressing weight into the ground would bring up a clear, warm seep, but no foot has pressed it except those of animals, and their impressions do not remain. The saturated sediment slowly closes behind each hoof, claw, belly, and dragging tail. Nothing here has yet acquired a scar.

Across the plain rise low swells no higher than the backs of sleeping animals. They are raw bulges of clay and chalky marl, pushed upward without fracture, their surfaces sweating continuously. Water pearls on them, gathers under films of algae, and runs down in threads so fine that it cannot carve. The rock is too new, the slope too slight, the covering growth too immediate. Erosion exists only as a possibility: a faint clouding where droplets touch bare mineral, a grain loosened but not carried away.

It is fiercely hot. The heat does not descend in sunlight; there is almost no direct sun. It resides in the air, in the water, in the mud, in the wet trunks and muscular leaves. A dense lid of gray-violet cloud holds the warmth close to the ground. Beneath it, the atmosphere has the dim green cast of light seen through deep water. Moisture condenses everywhere. It beads along leaf margins, slicks the hides of resting animals, and hangs from whiskers and antennae. Yet rain itself is difficult to distinguish. The air is already so saturated that droplets seem simply to appear, descend a short distance, and become part of the leaves.

Vegetation covers nearly every surface but has not had time—or perhaps has no need—to become a true forest. There are no ancient giants. Instead, countless young forms crowd together at nearly equal heights: palm-like fans with ribbed black-green blades, thickets of jointed reeds, broad floating leaves rooted in mud, low trees swollen at the base, and ropes of glossy vine passing from crown to crown. Their tissues are soft, water-heavy, and intensely alive. Bark is thin. Wood is rare. Many stems support themselves partly by leaning against neighboring stems, so the vegetation forms a communal body rather than a collection of separate plants.

Above the marsh growth, scattered crowns rise into the mist like islands. Below, the space is divided into layers so close together that each has its own inhabitants. There is the warm film on the water, the submerged lattice of roots, the hollow undersides of leaves, the humid corridors between stems, and the dim canopy where condensation trembles but seldom falls. Open water occurs in long, shallow mirrors, never quite wide enough to become lakes. They reveal the regional scale of the place: green flats and low misty groves repeating to a horizon that cannot be seen clearly, though the cries coming from far away prove that the plain continues well beyond sight.

Animal life is not merely abundant. It occupies the landscape almost without vacancy.

The water surface dimples continuously under rising mouths and tapping feet. Small silver fish move through flooded grass in such compact schools that they resemble flowing metal. Long-bodied amphibians lie along the warm mud with only their ridged eyes exposed. Bright frogs cling in tiers to every vertical stem. Some have throats like translucent fruit; as they call, light passes through the stretched membranes and turns amber or red.

Insects fill the air from the height of the moss to the lower cloud. Their wings make a broad, sustained vibration rather than a passing hum. Dense columns of gnats stand above open pools. Beetles row across the water. Heavy, slow-winged creatures with patterned bodies travel between the flowering crowns, and tiny pale moths shelter under leaves in overlapping sheets. Wherever sap or nectar gathers, mouths are already present.

Larger animals move through this multitude without silencing it. Herds of compact, splay-footed grazers push between the reeds, stripping soft growth in rhythmic bites. Their hides are dark and bare, always wet, marked with pale colonies of harmless mites. Broad-shelled reptiles rest half submerged in the shallow basins. Long-necked browsers feed at the canopy margin, their movements deliberate so as not to entangle themselves in vines. Predators are present in numbers that would seem impossible elsewhere: low cats among the root mats, narrow crocodilian shapes in water barely deep enough to conceal them, climbing serpents arranged along branches like additional vines. Yet hunting never empties a pool or stills a grove. For every body taken, the living density seems unchanged.

The sound is enormous but strangely even.

Frogs pulse from the flooded ground. Insects maintain a rough electrical drone. Birds—or birdlike animals with naked throats and heavy, damp plumage—shout from the crowns in blunt two-note phrases. Grazers cough and grunt to keep contact in the vegetation. Water clicks under beetle legs, leaves slap against wet leaves, unseen bodies plunge, hiss, chew, mate, hatch, and are eaten. There are no true intervals of quiet. Nor are there crescendos. The plain produces one continuous volume, a broad animal weather as stable as the heat.

That stability is the deepest quality of the place. Nothing is still in the small sense: wings beat, jaws work, throats expand, droplets fall, roots divide. But nothing changes in the large sense. There is no dry season folded into the future, no cooler night approaching, no flood line retreating down the stems. The cloud cover neither opens nor thickens. The shallow waters do not rise or drain. Plants flower and shed at the same time, everywhere, without creating a visible season. Decay is so rapidly consumed that no litter accumulates. Growth is so rapidly browsed that no thicket advances. Predators feed without reducing prey; prey graze without stripping the plain.

Even geology participates in this equilibrium. The low mineral swells remain perpetually raw, always on the verge of weathering and never quite weathered. New seepage exposes fresh clay at precisely the rate that algae and moss cover it. A grain displaced downslope is replaced by another swelling from below. Water has not yet learned to cut channels here. It moves as sheets, films, and sweating beads, without direction strong enough to become history.

There are no paths. Herds travel, but the mud closes and the bent stems lift behind them. There are no nests built from gathered materials, only bodies nesting within folds that plants already provide: eggs pasted beneath leaves, larvae suspended in water, young carried in skin pockets or sheltered between root buttresses. Nothing has been stacked, sharpened, tied, or cleared. The world offers cavities, platforms, cups, hooks, rafts, and shelters biologically, and its inhabitants accept them without alteration.

From any slightly raised swell, one can make out several kinds of country at once: reed flats shimmering under condensed moisture; darker groves crowded with trunks; open films of water covered in moving points; pale mineral backs emerging among the green; and beyond them more vegetation fading into luminous gray mist. The openness is not spaciousness. It is continuity. There are no walls and few clear boundaries, yet every arm’s length is full.

The slight strangeness of the plain becomes apparent only after long attention. It is too balanced to be peaceful. A falling leaf is eaten before it reaches the mud. Blood spilled into water vanishes under small mouths almost at once. A dead animal collapses into insects, fungi, scavengers, and roots so quickly that the overall arrangement of bodies barely alters. Birth is equally unobtrusive; young forms emerge already surrounded, filling spaces that seemed occupied.

The landscape feels embryonic, but not as if it will mature. It remains forever at the moment when land has just surfaced from warm water and life has rushed in before rivers, cliffs, soil horizons, or solitude can form. It has no ruins because it has no past visible enough to ruin, and no works because nothing here stands outside the living fabric long enough to make them.

Under the dim cloud, the wet green plain goes on sounding—hot, crowded, newly made, and changeless.
