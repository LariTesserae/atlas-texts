The city lies on a plain that did not exist a generation ago.

It is a low country, barely raised above the inland salt basins, its surface still carrying the angularity of recent formation. Black shelves of mineral glass break through pale soil. Ridges end abruptly, as though construction stopped mid-thought. There are few gullies, no rounded stones, no deep valleys: rain has not yet had time to soften anything. Where the ground has cracked, the fractures are clean and narrow, descending into warm darkness.

Across this young terrain, the city extends farther than its dimness first suggests.

Its towers are widely spaced. Some stand several kilometers from their nearest neighbor, connected by causeways no thicker than strokes of graphite. Others gather in loose districts around reservoirs, transit wells, or patches of old imported earth. There is little of the crowded pressure expected from an urban place. Streets cross broad vacant fields. Elevated rails pass over forests without stations. Apartment structures rise from reed beds, their lower floors open to wind and drifting mist.

The buildings are made from materials that seem undecided about being solid. Walls darken when approached, becoming reflective only after one has passed. Balconies slowly migrate around facades to remain in shade. Certain towers taper downward, their greatest mass suspended above a narrow stem, held not by visible tension but by an alteration in the local meaning of weight. At night—if this region can be said to have night—the undersides of these structures show faint constellations of service lights.

There is no bright day here. A dense atmospheric veil filters the sun into a gray-green radiance, warm but weak. Shadows are soft and often point in several directions. The sky resembles deep water viewed from below: layered, obscure, occasionally traversed by slow luminous bands. These are not clouds. They are weather-management boundaries, vast membranes folded through the upper air. Their motion draws moisture from one district and releases it over another.

Rain comes infrequently and with great precision.

A shower may fall along a single avenue while the neighboring blocks remain dry. Sometimes water descends in vertical columns only a few meters wide, filling public cisterns with hardly a drop wasted on pavement. Elsewhere moisture arrives as condensation. Before dawn, silver beads form across handrails, leaves, antennae, and window skins. Channels open in the architecture to collect them. For an hour the whole city trickles.

Then the warmth returns, and exposed water vanishes quickly.

For this reason, lushness is concentrated rather than universal. Vegetation follows buried coolant lines and shallow aquifers in vivid corridors. Ferns mass under transit bridges. Broad-leaved vines climb ventilation towers, their roots threaded into ceramic gutters. Around the reservoirs are groves of high, hollow trees with translucent bark. Their crowns spread horizontally, forming green ceilings over plazas and abandoned roadways.

Between these pockets lie stretches of sparse ocher ground, black stone, and unfinished foundation mesh.

The contrast makes the planted districts appear almost excessive. Mosses swell overnight from apparently bare walls. Pale flowers open during condensation events and collapse before noon. Mats of blue-green creepers advance several centimeters in a day, then retreat when the pipes beneath them change temperature. There are municipal gardens whose growth is guided by invisible gradients: trees curve around pedestrian routes before paths are built, while roots avoid utilities they could not detect by ordinary means.

No one is certain whether the gardens are being cultivated or are themselves doing the planning.

The city’s infrastructure is older than its land.

This is one of the place’s central impossibilities. Transit pylons descend through the fresh crust into engineered strata dated thousands of years beyond the present. Drainage maps predict streams that have not yet formed. In several districts, doorways were found standing alone before the surrounding buildings assembled themselves from airborne dust and mineral slurry.

Construction here does not proceed from foundation to roof. A completed structure is specified as a condition, and the landscape is persuaded toward it. The process may take days or decades. During this interval, temporary stairs lead to absent rooms. Windows appear suspended over fields. Elevators move through open air, stopping at floors visible only from inside their cabins.

The inhabitants treat these things as inconveniences rather than miracles.

They move through the city quietly. There are not many of them, or perhaps they are distributed across versions of the same streets that do not fully coincide. A boulevard can seem deserted while transit records insist that thousands are crossing it. Occasionally a figure appears at a distance, walking beneath the trees, and becomes difficult to retain in sight. Public squares contain benches warmed by recent occupants who are nowhere nearby.

Sound does not travel normally. The city’s surfaces harvest vibration as readily as they harvest water. Footsteps fade after a meter. Machinery works with the subdued murmur of insects behind walls. Even the elevated trains are nearly silent, announced only by a pressure in the ears and a brief shiver through hanging leaves.

The loudest natural sound is rain striking the reservoir canopies. The loudest artificial one is the daily calibration tone: a low, pure note that passes through the ground near dusk. It cannot be heard indoors, but cups tremble, pools grow concentric rings, and flowers turn their heads toward the city center.

There are few animals.

Small black-winged creatures gather where condensation runs from the towers. They have no visible mouths and cling to wet stone until their bodies become transparent. Long-legged grazers are sometimes seen in the outer greenways, moving alone through the fern corridors. They leave paired tracks that begin sharply and end without explanation.

In the reservoirs live thin, slow fish whose spines emit a dull amber light. The fish are protected, though not because they are rare. Their movements correspond to fluctuations in the city’s power system several minutes before those fluctuations occur. Engineers maintain observation platforms around the water and speak of the fish as colleagues.

Nothing here forms a conventional food web. Organisms feed on heat differences, chemical traces, discarded computation, or the weak distortion surrounding certain machines. Predation exists, but often as an exchange of properties rather than flesh. A small hovering animal may pursue an insect and take its color, leaving the insect alive but transparent. Vines coil around broken drones and digest their stored directions. Mold blooms in rooms where decisions have been postponed too long.

Beyond the central districts, the plain remains open.

Roads run straight toward the dim horizon, passing isolated neighborhoods and fields of upright mirrors. These mirrors do not reflect the landscape around them. Each shows the same terrain at a different stage of maturity: in one, the black ridges are sharp and bare; in another, deep river valleys divide the plain; in a third, the city is gone beneath a continuous forest.

The municipal authorities call them erosion forecasts.

People leave offerings at their bases anyway.

The land is active despite its quiet. New fissures open after warm rain. Towers adjust their foundations in response. Reservoirs migrate by imperceptible increments, pulling their wetlands with them. Green corridors braid and unbraid across the urban grid. The overhead membranes fold, dimming one quarter of the city while releasing another into muted silver light.

Every few months, a river appears.

It rises not from rainfall but from a line of condensation extending across the plain. Water gathers along that line until it begins to move, cutting a fresh channel through the soft young deposits. For several days the river is broad and warm, crowded with drifting leaves. Bridges unfold from the banks to meet over it. Markets assemble along the temporary waterfront. The amber fish arrive by routes no one witnesses.

Then the atmospheric boundary shifts. The flow dwindles into pools, the pools into dark mud, and the bridges fold flat again.

The plants remain.

From above, their surviving ribbon marks the vanished river through the city: a lush, winding street with no pavement, bright beneath the dim sky. Residents follow it on foot between distant towers. They pass silent stations, half-real buildings, and vacant plazas wet with condensation. Ahead, the green path continues beyond sight, tracing water that is absent now but may already be returning from another time.
