The place occupies less than a valley: a steep, enclosed notch near the crest of a young volcanic range. From rim to floor it is cathedral-sized, though so crowded with stone, vapor, and vegetation that it never feels spacious. Its walls rise almost vertically into cloud. They are black, angular, and scarcely weathered—fresh lava fractured into blades, prisms, and shelves, with pale seams of mineral salts showing wherever the rock has split.

There is no broad view. Darkness comes from enclosure as much as from weather. The sun may be above the ridge, but only a strained gray light reaches the notch, sliding briefly down one wall before mist absorbs it. At the deepest points, illumination is no more than the wet shine on leaves and the dull metallic glimmer of basalt.

The notch is not truly wet. There is no permanent river and no lake. Instead, moisture appears in events. Cloud presses against the rim and condenses by the bucketful. Every leaf-tip begins to drip. Hairline channels wake on the walls, converge, and race across the floor for an hour before vanishing into cracks. After rain, pockets in the lava fill with black water, each one no wider than a basin or a room. They persist for several days, filmed with pollenless dust and green spores, then drain suddenly when some buried fracture opens.

Between these brief inundations, the rock remains clammy. Steam seeps from warm fissures and condenses almost at once in the cool air. The result is an ecology sustained less by rainfall than by surfaces: wet stone, sweating crevices, fog, and the constant transfer of vapor from the ground into the plants.

Vegetation is dense enough to obscure the youth of the terrain. Moss pads overhang ledges in thick, saturated cushions. Liverworts lacquer the shaded walls. Ferns crowd every joint in the basalt, their fronds dark and rigid, repeatedly torn by the wind and replaced. There are low thickets of mountain bamboo and broad-leaved shrubs, but they do not form a conventional forest. The ground is too unstable and the available space too narrow. Stems lean across one another, root mats bridge gaps, and whole curtains of greenery hang from shelves with almost no soil beneath them.

Nothing grows tall. Cold, wind, and collapsing ground keep the canopy pressed down. Even the oldest shrubs are contorted into dense horizontal forms, their branches polished where they rub against neighboring branches. At the upper rim, grasses lie flat in one direction, then snap upright and flatten the other way when the weather turns. The place is lush, but not gentle: growth is compressed, abraded, and constantly rebuilding itself.

There are no animals. No insects move among the ferns, no birds call from the rim, no worms loosen the thin soil. The absence is complete enough to become structural. Flowers are rare and inconspicuous; nearly everything here spreads by spores, runners, broken stems, or wind-carried seed. Fallen leaves do not vanish quickly into a busy food web. They bleach, mat together, ferment in cold pockets, and are eventually shredded by water or ground into the rock by sliding root masses. Fungi work slowly through the debris, but there is no visible grazing, hunting, nesting, or flight.

The cause is ordinary, though severe. The vents release intermittent concentrations of carbon dioxide and sulfurous gases. Near the floor, especially during still weather, the heavier gas gathers invisibly between the rocks. The plants tolerate the mineral-rich soil and survive because their living surfaces rise into moving air. Animals entering the notch do not remain. There is no evidence of catastrophe, only exclusion.

For all its lack of construction, the place has an industrial character. It sounds like a plant operating behind walls.

Deep beneath the floor, groundwater reaches hot rock. Pressure accumulates, valves that are not valves open, and steam punches through the fissures with a roar. Some vents hiss continuously; others pulse in slow, heavy intervals. Stone chambers underground amplify the noise into thuds, hammering, and long metallic groans. Condensate taps rapidly from leaf to leaf. Gravel rattles through hidden chutes. Wind is drawn down one side of the notch and forced through narrow cracks, producing a sustained turbine whine.

Nothing here has been engineered, yet everything suggests process: intake, pressure, exhaust, cooling, runoff. The black columns resemble cooling fins. Mineral crusts form around the vents in white and yellow rings, then break away like furnace scale. Warm vapor rises in columns, meets the descending cold, and collapses into turbulent fog. The basin breathes with the regularity of machinery, but its cycles are geological and meteorological rather than mechanical.

That regularity never becomes calm. The notch is in a nearly continuous state of rearrangement.

Wind entering over the rim falls abruptly, striking the floor and bursting outward. Ferns turn their pale undersides all at once. Mist is torn into cords and sheets. Water runs uphill in spray. Sudden temperature changes loosen the new basalt, and fragments detach without warning, ricocheting from wall to wall before burying themselves in moss. Root mats sag under collected rain, peel from the stone, and slide downslope intact, carrying whole small gardens with them. Where they come to rest, they dam channels and create temporary pools; when those dams fail, the water cuts a fresh route through the vegetation.

Because the rock is young, there are few rounded forms. Channels have not yet had time to become valleys. They are cuts: sharp, narrow, and temporary. Scree remains angular enough to slice stems. Broken faces retain the blue-black luster of newly exposed basalt. Even the mineral deposits seem unfinished—soft terraces of pale carbonate, brittle sulfur crusts, rust-red stains spreading from springs. Each rain modifies them. Each pressure release cracks them. Nothing persists long enough to acquire polish.

The turbulence is most intense when a storm crosses the mountain. The notch fills from above with cloud and from below with steam until walls and floor disappear. Thunder cannot be distinguished from subterranean booming. Rain strikes the broad leaves with a dense, percussive roar. Water pours over ledges in dozens of short-lived falls, some plunging only a few meters before wind dissolves them. The plants bend into a single dark mass. Fresh fractures open in the stone with reports like splitting timber, and the smell of crushed fern, sulfur, wet iron, and cold ash saturates the air.

Then the storm passes quickly. The waterfalls thin to cords, then beads. Steam continues to issue from the cracks. For a brief interval, the notch is visible from end to end: a compact black chamber crowded with green life, its floor braided by newborn streams and its walls smoking in the cool.

Even in this lull, it is never silent. Water ticks into hidden cavities. Gas bubbles through mud. Loose stones continue settling with small, precise clicks. Somewhere below, pressure begins to build again.
