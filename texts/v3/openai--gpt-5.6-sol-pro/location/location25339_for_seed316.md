At first it appears to be a plain after rain: low, warm, and nearly level, extending beneath a ceiling of bruised cloud. Water lies everywhere without quite becoming a lake. It films the stone, fills shallow basins, threads through cracks, and trembles in hollows no deeper than a footprint. The air itself seems only partly separate from it. Breath condenses on the lips. Moisture beads on bare rock and runs upward as often as down.

But the plain is not empty.

It is packed to the horizon with the last remnants of mountains.

They stand ankle-high, waist-high, seldom taller: knobs of black stone polished to a greasy sheen; blunt reefs of red mineral; ridges worn thin as the backs of buried animals. Every remnant occupies exactly the space erosion has allowed it. There is no loose scree and almost no soil. Anything that could be carried away was carried away ages ago. What remains has endured so long that hardness itself seems exhausted. The rocks weep warm, metallic water from pores finer than skin. Their surfaces are crowded with pits, channels, sealed cracks, fossils of former cracks, and pale rings left by floods older than the present sky.

From a height—if there were any height from which to see it—the whole country might resemble a continent ground down to its root: a vast abrasion platform where mountain chains have become ripples and valleys have been reduced to differences in the depth of water.

The dimness is not peaceful. Light arrives diffusely, green-gray and heavy, as if passing through miles of wet cloth. There is no clear sun. Sometimes a brighter region moves behind the cloud cover, but it moves too quickly and in the wrong direction, narrowing into a line, branching, then closing like an eye. Shadows gather beneath stones even when there is nothing above them to cast one.

The sound is immediate and immense.

Water hammers the plain, though rain is not always visible. The air roars in broad, continuous bands. Shallow streams only finger-widths deep produce the sound of cataracts. Each groove in the rock amplifies them, and the near-flat land offers no shelter. Beneath the water-noise is a lower concussion: the grinding of buried plates, or perhaps the memory of their grinding, repeated without end. At intervals the entire horizon releases a crack like splitting ice. Flocks of echoes race inward from every direction and meet overhead.

Conversation, if there were anyone to attempt it, would be impossible. Even shouting would feel private.

The weather is born locally and violently. Warm pools begin to steam; the steam turns black; the black vapor collapses into columns of rain that strike only a few paces around themselves. Elsewhere, sheets of water lift intact from the ground and travel sideways, carrying reflections with them. One can watch a pool become rain before the pool has emptied, then see that rain falling several miles away—and also, somehow, into the same pool.

Nothing remains stable long except the worn stone.

Channels multiply between blinks. Watercourses braid, unbraid, and cross themselves without joining. A depression may fill from the bottom, overflow uphill, and vanish while still visibly full. Sometimes the plain buckles into enormous, slow folds, not raising the rock but bending distance: a nearby outcrop recedes for hours while a remote ridge arrives almost underfoot. The horizon then forms corners. Rain falls around those corners and can be heard before it exists.

The land is ancient enough that sequence has begun to erode.

Events no longer lie neatly one after another. Thunder may continue for days before the flash that caused it opens under the water. Fresh fractures are found already rounded smooth. A stone overturned in the morning reveals an underside weathered by centuries of exposure; by evening its upper face bears the compressed imprint of the person or creature that has not yet stepped there. Reflections linger in pools after their subjects have moved away. Some show clouds that have never crossed the sky. Others show the plain with mountains still standing—immense ranges wavering beneath a hand’s breadth of muddy water.

Vegetation survives only in defended places. Mats of dark, wiry growth cling inside mineral pockets, each blade flat against the stone. Their color is almost black until lightning passes, when they flash blue-green like tarnished copper. Low bladder-plants swell in warm puddles, release strings of spores, and collapse within minutes. Pale reeds occur in isolated circles, never singly, although no roots connect them. They bend toward sounds rather than wind.

There are no forests and scarcely any true ground to root in. Yet what little grows is tightly crowded. Lichens occupy every surface not currently being scoured. Transparent films overlap them. Rust-colored crusts consume the transparent films and are consumed in turn by tiny white colonies that resemble salt. Life persists not by spreading across the plain but by layering itself, one thin tenure over another, until a patch no larger than a palm contains an entire buried ecology.

Animals are rare enough to seem accidental.

Now and then something long and jointless rolls through a channel against the current, its back plated with stone-colored scales. It stops when observed and gradually becomes indistinguishable from a ridge. Small glassy creatures pulse inside the condensation on rock faces. During the loudest storms, broad shadows pass beneath water too shallow to conceal even a hand. Their movement displaces nothing. No birds cross the sky, though cries like seabirds are sometimes threaded through the roar, always from below.

Along several thousand miles of the plain stand scattered structures.

They are not settlements. They are too far apart and too unlike one another. One is a low ring of fitted slabs surrounding a pool that never ripples. Another is a roofless chamber sunk into the rock, its walls polished by generations of water rather than tools. Farther on, narrow pillars lean together like exhausted figures. No carving survives on them, if carving ever existed. There are no hinges, vessels, roads, blades, wheels, or mechanisms. Only arrangements: stones placed against the habits of flood and gravity, cavities made where natural cavities would not be, thresholds opening onto nothing.

Some may not have been built. The distinction has become unreliable here. Given enough time, erosion can imitate intention; given enough erosion, intention can become geology.

The largest ruin is visible only during certain storms. It appears across the horizon as a line of dark arches, each vast enough to admit a weather system. Water pours from them in horizontal rivers. Between flashes they seem close enough to touch. When the clouds brighten, they are revealed to be hundreds of miles away—or to be spaces worn through the distance itself. Moss grows on their inner surfaces. Lightning crawls among their roots.

The plain has rules, but they are rules of saturation and wear.

Nothing can remain empty. A hollow fills with water; water fills with reflections; reflections fill with delayed skies. A silence fills with the approach of noise. A crack fills with life, mineral, pressure, or another crack. Even absence is quickly occupied by what was absent before.

Nothing rises without being worn down in advance.

Nothing disappears completely. It becomes a sound, a stain, a recurring contour in the rain. Mountains remain as grit in the water. Rivers remain as pale tracks crossing present rivers. Creatures remain as habits in the mud. Builders, if builders existed, remain as the peculiar way certain stones resist lying flat.

And the turbulence does not renew the world so much as worry at its final form. Storm after storm rearranges the water, the light, the distances, the order of moments—everything except the essential flatness. The land has reached the end state of erosion, but the forces that eroded it have not stopped. With no more height to consume, they grind at causality, scale, and memory.

Thus the country is always changing and never changed.

Warm rain strikes the ancient stone. The stone sheds it into channels. The channels reverse, overflow, divide, and thunder toward horizons that fold shut ahead of them. Sparse black growth holds fast in the pores. A hidden animal turns beneath the shining skin of water. Somewhere, one of the solitary stone rings fills to its rim.

Then the whole continental plain shudders—not upward or sideways, but briefly into another age—and every pool reflects a range of mountains falling in absolute silence.

The silence lasts less than a heartbeat.

Afterward, the roar is greater than before.
