At the upper end of a mountain ridge, where the air has thinned to a cold, bright substance barely adequate for breath, there is a hollow crowded with stone.

It is not a valley. It is too small and too enclosed for that: an oval court perhaps four hundred paces long, sunk between three summits and open on its eastern side to an immense fall of sky. From the rim, the land beyond is not visible—only ranks of pale atmosphere, a distant horizontal gleam, and, when the air is exceptionally clear, the dark tips of lower ranges seeming to float far below.

Inside the hollow, everything stands close together. Hundreds of rock fins rise from the floor, shoulder to shoulder: thin slabs, blunt pillars, split needles, and tilted plates of gray-blue stone. Some are waist-high; others approach the height of towers. Their surfaces have been worked by a very old, patient erosion. Edges that must once have been sharp are scalloped and fingered. Faces are stippled with pits. Many stones carry parallel grooves, as though repeatedly drawn upward through coarse cloth.

There is no soil. Nothing roots in the cracks. No lichen stains the rock, no moss softens the seams, and no bird crosses the court. Even the usual high-mountain traces—droppings, feathers, husks, windblown seeds—are absent. The hollow is wholly mineral.

Yet it does not feel empty. The density of the stone gives it the presence of a gathering.

The floor passes between the fins in narrow, twisting aisles. It is made of packed scree, old frost-broken flakes, and slabs polished by vanished ice. Here and there are shallow basins of black rock holding a hand’s depth of water. They appear after brief weather and endure for several days beneath skins of clear ice. At noon, when the sun is strongest, the ice loosens from the edges and rotates almost imperceptibly in its basin. By evening it freezes fast again, trapping rings of silver bubbles.

Moisture comes irregularly. Clouds do not often rise high enough to enter the hollow, but when they do, they arrive as dense white vapor, spilling over the western rim and filling the court without wind. Every stone begins to sweat. Condensation beads on the cold faces, gathers beneath overhangs, and runs down the ancient grooves. The drops seem startlingly large in this bare place. They fall one at a time into hidden cavities, each impact distinct.

Rain is rarer: a few hard, widely spaced drops from an almost luminous sky. Snow comes as dry grains that rush along the ground and vanish into cracks. Most of the water in the court is made directly from cloud, frost, and the daily exchange between light and shadow.

The sun governs everything.

At this elevation it is brilliant and severe, whitening the upper surfaces of stone while leaving the gaps between them blue-black. There is little haze to soften distance. A chip of quartz at the far end of the hollow can flash painfully into the eye. Frost persists in the narrowest slots even while sunlit rock grows warm enough to steam. Passing from one aisle to the next can feel like crossing between seasons.

The court is never still, though most of its activity is nearly silent. Water enters pores during the bright hours and freezes after sunset. Crystals widen old fractures. Thin scales of stone release from the pillars and settle at their feet. Ice needles grow in shaded gravel, lifting pebbles during the night; in daylight they collapse, leaving delicate fields of overturned chips. Condensation travels through hairline channels and emerges elsewhere, sometimes several arm lengths from where it entered. In the morning, beads may hang from the underside of a slab whose upper face is perfectly dry.

The hollow has been shaped by these repetitions rather than by catastrophe. Nothing here looks newly ruined. Fallen pillars have settled into the paths. Scree has packed itself around their bases. Cracks have cracks within them, and the newest break is still old enough to have lost its brightness. The place has reached an equilibrium of slow collapse: each year loosening a little, each winter rearranging grains, while the larger pattern remains.

Scattered among the natural stones are signs of hands.

They are easy to miss because they were made from the same materials and with little attempt to oppose the mountain. Three low shelters are tucked against the northern wall of the hollow. Their roofs consist of overlapping slabs weighted with rubble. Their entrances are narrow and turn inward twice, shutting out the wind. No mortar holds them. Inside, there are stone ledges, shallow cup marks, and beds of flat gravel. One shelter has a door-frame made from two upright stones and a third laid across them, no different in principle from the surrounding formations except for its exactness.

Elsewhere, small cairns occupy certain intersections. They are not tall. Each is assembled from five or seven carefully chosen pieces, dark stone beneath pale, pale beneath dark. Several have fallen and been rebuilt more than once; the alternate colors continue in broken piles on the ground.

On the southern rim stands the most elaborate object in the court: a forked wooden pole no higher than a person, silvered and split by weather, held upright in a socket drilled into the rock by repeated blows. No trees grow within sight, and the wood must have been carried from far below. A crosspiece supports three shallow bowls hammered from dull copper. The metal is thin, uneven, and green only in the creases where moisture lingers. At dawn, the bowls collect condensation. When enough has gathered, they tip on crude pins and spill into carved channels. The water travels down the rock face and disappears beneath a fitted slab.

The mechanism still works, though no one tends it.

Its motion is slow enough to escape notice. A bowl may take hours to fill. Then, without warning, it gives a small metallic knock, pours out its measure, and rocks back into the light. The sound carries through the stone aisles with disproportionate clarity.

Below the slab, water enters a network of channels cut into the floor. Some are only finger-wide. They link several basins, shelters, and natural hollows, though many have become blocked by frost rubble. Their purpose is not entirely practical. The channels repeatedly divide into pairs and rejoin. Some climb slight inclines through wicking seams packed with porous stone. Others conduct water to blank walls, where it spreads into dark fans and freezes.

This is the nearest thing the hollow possesses to an ecology: not life, but circulation.

Sun warms stone. Stone releases frost. Vapor condenses. Bowls tip. Channels fill and empty. Ice separates grains. Grains dam the channels. A later thaw breaks the dams and sends a brief pulse through the court. Each process alters the conditions of the next. The system is active without growing, reproductive only in the sense that cracks make smaller cracks and falling slabs create new surfaces for frost.

There may once have been inhabitants, but the place offers no direct image of them. No bones remain. No woven material, leather, food waste, ash, or pigment survives. The shelters contain no hearths; at this altitude there was little to burn. The tools that remain are simple stone hammers, wedges of antler-colored mineral, and a few soft copper scrapers too worn to show their original edges. Whoever made the channels understood the behavior of water intimately but did not command it. They persuaded drips. They waited for basins to fill. Their constructions depended on cold, slope, porous rock, and patience.

The court has a few local rules.

Loose objects move eastward over time, though the floor does not visibly slope that way. Frost heave, morning thaw, and the prevailing afternoon gust combine to shift chips by tiny increments toward the open edge. Cairns must therefore be rebuilt on their western sides.

Water left in a sunlit vessel disappears faster than water spilled on bare stone. The air takes it almost immediately. Water sheltered in narrow rock seams may remain for months.

Sound does not travel evenly. In some aisles a whisper dies at the mouth. In others, the smallest tap passes through the fins as a chain of faint replies. A falling pebble can seem to move around the hollow after it has already come to rest.

No shadow remains in the same passage for long. Because the stone fins stand so close together, their shadows interlock and separate as the sun crosses overhead. A route that is warm and glaring in the morning becomes a corridor of hard frost by afternoon. Travelers—if any still come—would need to know the court by time as much as by direction.

Most hours are quiet. There is no wingbeat, insect rasp, rustle, bark, or distant bell of grazing animals. The silence has layers: open-air silence above the pillars, enclosed silence in the shelters, and the pressurized stillness inside cracks full of ice.

Then a process completes.

A copper bowl tips.

A slab contracts with a single sharp report.

A runnel frees itself beneath the stones and whispers for half a minute.

Wind reaches the eastern opening and draws a low breath through the entire hollow.

Afterward, the place settles again beneath the fierce white sun, crowded, cold, damp in secret, and slowly remaking itself from the same enduring materials.
