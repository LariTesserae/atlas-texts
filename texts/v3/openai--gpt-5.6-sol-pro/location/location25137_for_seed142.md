The city occupies the summit of a plateau so old that its mountains have forgotten how to be sharp.

From a distance—if distance can be judged beneath a sky without stars—the plateau appears almost level: a black horizontal interruption in a greater blackness. Only on approaching does its age become visible. The escarpments have been worn into enormous shallow folds, each cliff rounded and undercut by vanished forces. There are dry channels broad enough to contain districts, though nothing has flowed through them within any surviving measure of time. Their beds are polished basalt, pale salt-metal crusts, and compacted dust so fine that it behaves less like sand than soot.

The plateau stands extremely high. The lower world cannot be seen. At the rim, the land simply falls away into a dense, warm obscurity. No cloud rises from it. No wind climbs the slopes. Loose fragments placed at the edge remain where they are, century after century, their positions recorded on municipal markers as evidence that gravity still functions.

The darkness is nearly complete. There is no sunrise here and apparently never was one, though some of the oldest façades bear geometric forms that may once have represented a sun. The sky has a faint brown-black depth to it, like smoked glass held over a furnace. Now and then an indistinct red sheen appears far above, too weak to cast shadows. It does not move.

The ground is warm. Walls are warm. Railings, road plates, dust, and the exposed ribs of old machinery all hold the same mild, bodily heat. There are no hot districts or cold districts, no favored walls, no places where frost forms. The entire plateau has settled into one temperature. A person crossing the city finds no thermal clue that they have crossed a threshold from street to interior, factory to plaza, inhabited quarter to ruin.

There is no water. The word survives on warning plaques and in obsolete pipe diagrams, but it refers to nothing locally present. Basins are not basins but pressure courts. Fountains are arrays of ceramic vanes once used to comb charged dust from the air. The city’s pipes carry dry gases, powdered lubricants, or nothing at all. Corrosion takes unfamiliar forms: metals do not rust but slowly reorganize into dull, layered crystals. Old joints bloom with copper-black needles. Iron becomes soft and granular without changing color. Glass thickens at its lower edge over spans so long that the windows of the oldest buildings resemble frozen curtains.

The city is called, in the few functioning registers, **the High Quiet**.

It is urban in extent but sparse in presence. Long avenues cross one another with ceremonial precision, dividing districts of low factories, habitation towers, storage vaults, switching yards, and immense empty squares. The avenues were made for traffic on a scale no longer encountered. Ten or twenty vehicles could have traveled abreast, yet most roads contain only one dark track along the center, polished by a service machine that passes perhaps once every several years.

Buildings stand far apart. Between them are tracts of bare mineral pavement, old excavation floors, and fields of aligned posts whose purpose has been lost. The openness makes the city seem larger than it is. A tower may appear nearby, its silhouette clean against the slightly lesser darkness of the sky, yet require half a day to reach. Perspective is unreliable because there is little haze and almost no variation in light. Small things look monumental; monumental things can seem like markings on a wall.

Much of the construction is ceramic rather than stone. The plateau’s minerals were ground, separated, heated, compressed, and fired into blocks that have survived better than the bedrock beneath them. Walls are matte charcoal, gray-violet, or the color of old bone. Structural members are made from black alloys that ring faintly when touched but otherwise show no age. Decorative surfaces carry shallow repeating patterns: ladders ending in sealed circles, branching diagrams without resemblance to plants, and rows of faceless figures holding tools toward a central absence.

Nothing grows in the cracks. There are no roots, mosses, spores, or films. Dust lies directly against dust. Even sheltered corners remain clean of organic stain. Where a roof has collapsed, its debris forms a simple fan and stays that way. No weed softens it. No rain settles it. No animal scatters it while nesting.

The factories remain the most active parts of the High Quiet, though their activity is difficult to distinguish from stillness.

Their engines are buried deep within the plateau, coupled to pressure chambers and heavy flywheels in vacuum housings. They do not roar. A hand placed against a factory wall may detect a single continuous vibration, so slow it resembles a second pulse. Inside, belts are drawn taut between motionless wheels. Piston rods rest at different points in their strokes. Indicator needles hover just above zero.

Yet the machinery is not dead. At intervals too long to witness directly, changes occur. A hopper found empty will contain a measure of black powder on a later visit. A stack of identical ceramic rings will have gained one ring. A worn bearing will be replaced by another, though no worker has been seen and no door has opened. The factories still manufacture parts for themselves, but only at the rate of their own decay. Their product is continuity.

This is the governing industry of the city: replacement without expansion.

Nothing is accumulated. Nothing is wasted. A cracked plate is taken apart grain by grain and becomes a plate elsewhere. Gas escaping one seal is collected by another system centuries later. The city’s total inventory appears fixed. Municipal tallies engraved into underground walls list quantities of metal, ceramic, carbon, noble gases, and “unassigned remainder.” The numbers have not changed in known history.

The industrial districts smell faintly of hot stone, ozone, and an acrid mineral sweetness. In the deepest halls there is a sharper odor, like dust heated in an electrical socket. The air is breathable only in some enclosed zones. Outside it is thin, dry, and strangely heavy in the lungs, carrying particles too small to see. Masks are less for survival than for preventing the city from entering the body.

Sound does not travel far. Footsteps are absorbed by the powder lying over the streets. Voices seem to fall directly to the ground. An impact that should echo between buildings returns only once, softly, and from the wrong direction. The persistent sounds are minor: a far electrical tick; the contraction of a cooling plate, though nothing cools; a cable under tension giving a brief insect-like chirr. Once in a great while, something beneath the city shifts by a fraction of its width, and a low note passes through every structure simultaneously. Doors tremble in their frames. Dust lifts no higher than a finger’s breadth, then settles exactly where it was.

The rare fauna of the plateau are called hinge-beasts, although they may not be animals in any ordinary sense. They are small, flattened organisms—or devices—found beneath access covers and between layers of old wall. Their bodies consist of jointed mineral plates surrounding a soft center that is never exposed. They do not eat visible material. They gather near vibrations and align themselves with mechanical stress, as if feeding on strain.

A hinge-beast may remain still for decades. When a machine component nears failure, several will appear beside it. They arrange themselves in a precise arc and wait. After the component is replaced, they vanish. No one has observed one arriving or departing. Their tracks, when tracks occur, are pairs of shallow punctures ending abruptly in open ground.

There are also rumors of something larger moving below the dry channels: a long segmented presence that follows the buried utility lines. The evidence consists of inspection doors found unsealed and of tool marks too broad for municipal machinery. No sound is associated with it. Some maps identify it as infrastructure.

At the city’s center lies the deepest open space, the Census Plain. It is paved in hexagonal slabs, each engraved with a number and an outline corresponding to a structure elsewhere in the city. If a building is damaged, its matching slab develops the same fracture. If a wall is dismantled, the engraved outline fades. This suggests that the plain is not a record but another expression of the city, as though streets, factories, and towers are merely raised portions of a single object whose true body lies beneath.

At the center of the plain is a round shaft descending into perfect darkness. Warm air does not rise from it. Dropped objects make no sound. Lights lowered on cables continue to glow but illuminate nothing, even their own housings. The cable always returns several centimeters shorter, with an end as smooth as if it had never extended farther.

The oldest districts cluster near this shaft. Their buildings have been eroded almost flat, though no erosive force remains. Staircases ascend into open air. Doorways stand without rooms behind them. Entire civic halls have been reduced to low, rounded foundations resembling natural outcrops, while delicate inscriptions survive untouched on nearby stones. The pattern of decay does not follow hardness, exposure, or use. It seems instead that some forms have become tired of being distinct.

This is the deeper strangeness of the High Quiet: age here is not a process but a condition.

Things do not noticeably change, yet everything is ancient. The landscape has reached an equilibrium beyond weather. Erosion has already done nearly all it can do. Machines have already corrected nearly all failures. The surviving structures are those shapes that the world permits to remain. Anything unstable disappeared so long ago that its absence feels primordial.

There are rules, though no authority announces them.

Nothing burns with an open flame. Combustion, when attempted, produces a dim red contour around the fuel but no light and almost no ash.

Nothing poured onto a slope runs downward. Powders creep until evenly distributed, then stop. Gases equalize instantly within a chamber but do not cross an open doorway unless pushed.

No shadow changes direction.

A closed door remains closed unless its opening is necessary to some maintenance sequence. An open door cannot be forced shut before its purpose is complete.

No object can be carried beyond the plateau rim. Near the edge, burdens become progressively heavier, while the bearer does not.

And the city does not permit subtraction. A fragment removed from a wall will eventually be matched by a loss from whatever container holds it. A tool abandoned in the street may become part of a railing. A body left in the High Quiet, according to the engraved municipal codes, is not buried or destroyed. It is inventoried.

Because of this, the settlement gives no impression of abandonment. Abandonment would imply a before and after, occupancy followed by vacancy. Here, empty towers, functioning factories, absent citizens, and immaculate infrastructure coexist as aspects of one stable arrangement. The city seems neither waiting nor sleeping. It has no unfinished business. It does not expect anyone to return.

From the plateau rim, looking inward, one sees the scattered verticals of towers, the low black mass of industrial halls, the geometric cuts of streets, and the faintest lines of red status lamps extending toward the center. They are not bright enough to guide a traveler. They resemble embers painted onto the darkness.

Beyond them, the High Quiet continues: warm, dry, elevated above an unseen world, maintaining itself with infinitesimal precision.

Nothing blooms.

Nothing floods.

Almost nothing moves.

And yet, somewhere under the Census Plain, a factory completes a single ceramic ring, places it beside the others, and becomes still for another hundred years.
