The sea has no shore.

It lies beneath a roof so distant that, from most places in the water, the roof is mistaken for sky. The whole basin is enclosed: a continental reservoir built inside the hollowed lower decks of an old industrial complex, then filled until its upper galleries drowned. No bedrock remains exposed. No reef began here naturally. Every ridge, trench, plain, cliff, and island of the submerged world is made from poured concrete, ceramic armor, steel trusses, ballast glass, and the accumulated grit of machinery.

The water is cool and only faintly saline. It has the mineral taste of pipes.

Near the surface, broad lighting galleries reproduce daylight with little ceremony. White illumination spreads downward in moving columns, weakened and greened by depth but otherwise ordinary. The lamps brighten in the morning, hold steady for several hours, and dim toward evening. Their scale prevents perfect coordination. Noon travels across the reservoir as a pale front. At the borders between power districts, two different times of day may meet: afternoon brightness on one side, blue dusk on the other. Maintenance outages pass like eclipses.

There is weather beneath this manufactured sky. Heat from pumping stations raises slow bodies of warm water. Cooling towers draw them sideways. Currents collide over the central void and build long internal waves hundreds of meters high, invisible except where they bend the light and carry shoals abruptly upward. At the surface, ventilation makes wind. Rain falls from the roof where condensation gathers along cold beams. In some sectors it falls continuously, a wall of fresh water striking the sea with a sound like distant applause.

The reservoir is so large that no single view explains it.

Most of its volume is empty blue-gray water. A diver may travel for hours without approaching anything larger than suspended particles. The emptiness is not peaceful. It is crossed by pressure pulses, propeller noise, warning tones, and the low transmitted impact of mechanisms operating far beyond sight. Sound travels easily here. A pump started two hundred kilometers away arrives as a steady concussion in the ribs. Freight turbines produce a broad mechanical thunder. Chains dragged across loading floors make metallic cries that can be heard through entire districts.

The loudness gives the empty water shape. One learns directions by machinery: the deep triple beat of the western impellers, the continuous grind of the desalination banks, the intermittent hammer of gates adjusting to pressure. During scheduled shutdowns, when these sounds cease one after another, the resulting silence feels less like quiet than structural failure.

The bottom is a constructed landscape still too young to have softened.

There are spillways with knife-edged lips, vast sloped aprons of ridged concrete, ventilation towers rising like squared mountains, and trenches cut with exact right angles. Expansion joints make black, ruler-straight canyons. Fields of anchor bolts project from empty foundations where machinery was never installed. Some valleys are merely spaces between pressure hulls. Some cliffs are the outer walls of drowned factories, their windows fitted with thick glass or sealed by plates.

Fresh fractures remain pale. Rust has begun, but not yet won. Orange fans spread from rivets and welds, staining the surfaces beneath them. Zinc blocks dissolve sacrificially along the steelwork, growing furred and cratered. Where currents are strongest, paint peels in long ribbons. Fine debris—ceramic dust, iron scale, shredded insulation, shells—collects in hollows and forms the nearest thing this sea has to sand. Even that sediment is angular. It has not had enough time to become smooth.

There is little vegetation. Thin films of algae coat the upper surfaces where the artificial day reaches them, dark green on the horizontal planes and nearly absent on walls. Cable forests sometimes carry streamers of kelp, but the plants grow poorly in the cool water and under the strict nutrient controls. They occur as isolated banners rather than meadows. Around drainage outlets, pale mosslike growth clings to grates in dense circles, flourishing briefly on whatever passes through before being grazed back.

Animal life is far more abundant.

Small silver fish occupy almost every lit layer. They move through the open volume in immense but widely spaced schools, each one a shifting weather system of scales. They gather around lamps, thermal vents, cable pylons, and the soft turbulence behind moving equipment. Their numbers are maintained partly by breeding and partly by deliberate release from hatchery decks. They feed on plankton cultivated in upper tanks, on algae scraped from concrete, and on clouds of tiny crustaceans that bloom whenever nutrients leak into the water.

Crabs live in the bolt fields. Eels inhabit conduits too narrow for inspection vehicles. Mussels seal themselves along joints and intake rims until maintenance crews remove them with rotating brushes. Pale shrimp congregate beneath grated floors, where crumbs of organic matter sift down from aquaculture chambers. Larger fish patrol the long walls, using their straight lines as hunting lanes.

The dominant predators are ordinary sharks bred for cool water, lean and unornamented. They are numerous without seeming so. The scale of the reservoir separates them. A shark may pass through a school, scatter it into metallic rain, and disappear again into an emptiness large enough to absorb both pursuit and panic.

Seals occupy the surface service platforms. They haul themselves onto warm housings and sleep through alarms that would deafen a human. Their calls echo beneath the roof. In the northern sectors, where the lighting cycle is weaker and floating ice occasionally forms around refrigeration leaks, colonies nest on buoyant maintenance rafts. They have learned the schedules of food conveyors and follow inspection boats over great distances.

Nothing here is entirely wild, though much of it is no longer controlled in detail.

The sea was built for cooling, storage, transport, and pressure regulation. Ecology came afterward, first as contamination and then as policy. Filter feeders were introduced to clean the water. Fish were brought in to eat larvae. Predators were added when the fish multiplied too quickly. Every solution became a new population to manage. Now the system is too broad for direct supervision. Engineers adjust oxygen, temperature, salinity, and nutrient flow at district scale, but the animals decide the local consequences.

Industrial processes behave like seasons.

A release from the deep thermal banks brings a warm period. Plankton multiply. Fish spawn along the illuminated walls. Predators arrive. Weeks later, the purification cycle strips nutrients from the water, visibility increases, and the schools disperse into the open basin. When the main intake screens are cleaned, edible debris spreads downcurrent and draws feeding animals from hundreds of kilometers. When freight passages open, deep currents reverse, carrying eggs and larvae into regions they could not otherwise reach.

The largest movements belong to the machinery. Submerged cargo platforms follow rails along the bottom, each the size of a district. They travel slowly, surrounded by floodlights and clouds of disturbed silt. Gate systems rotate across channels like moving cliffs. Ballast reservoirs inhale and expel millions of tons of water. Remote dredges crawl through sediment traps on articulated legs, cutting clean-edged paths across the young bottom.

These machines are not sleek. They are industrial, repetitive, serviceable. Their casings are painted yellow, white, or red beneath layers of mineral film. Their joints leak grease. Their propellers are fenced by bars thick as tree trunks. Numbers and hazard stripes appear unexpectedly in the depths, enormous enough to read from a kilometer away.

The inhabited places are sealed inside the landscape rather than standing upon it. Control rooms occupy pressure cylinders embedded in the walls. Workshops hang beneath surface decks. Windows overlook open water, though many are covered to prevent workers from staring too long into the passing emptiness. Access tunnels connect one dry compartment to another. Doors are round, floors are grated, and every room carries a faint vibration from the pumps.

There are rules here, most of them practical.

Nothing loose is left near an intake.

No light is switched on in a dark district without checking for accumulated animals.

No gate is opened until the pressure fronts are mapped.

Divers carry acoustic beacons because visibility is not the same as orientation. A person can see clearly for fifty meters and still be lost inside a volume larger than a country.

The sea itself obeys harsher rules. Currents follow machine schedules, not tides. Depth belongs to pressure. Warmth belongs to exhaust. Food belongs to leakage, cultivation, and light. Any surface left unattended becomes habitat; any habitat left too long becomes a maintenance problem.

Yet the place does not feel like a tank. It is too extensive, too active, and too indifferent to the people who operate it. Its artificiality is clearest in the precision of its bones, but those bones vanish quickly into distance. From far out in the central water, the straight walls curve with perspective and disappear. The roof becomes a luminous haze. The bottom is thousands of meters below. Only the continuing industrial thunder proves that boundaries exist at all.

In that open middle, shoals travel through shafts of ordinary white light while rain rattles on the surface above them. Sharks turn at the edge of visibility. Far below, a freight platform advances along its rails, pushing a slow mountain of water before it. Every creature adjusts. Every cable hums. The made ocean carries on like a natural one—not because it has forgotten its builders, but because it has grown too vast for construction and nature to remain separate categories.
