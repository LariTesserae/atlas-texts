At first the high country appears white—not with snow, though snow lies in every shadow, but with light. The plateau catches so much of it that distance loses color. Peaks stand as hard, luminous cuts against a violet sky; ridges flash like drawn blades; whole valleys vanish beneath glare rising from salt, ice, pale stone, and the silver backs of innumerable living things.

This is not one mountain range but a continent held at mountain height. Its interior is broad enough to make weather of its own. Storms are born on one escarpment, travel for weeks across upland basins, and spend themselves against peaks no lower than the clouds. Yet the land is dry in the large sense. Water is not spread evenly through it. It occurs in episodes and hoards: a half hour of needle-fine rain, a month of rime, a warm fissure breathing condensation, a river that runs beneath translucent rock and surfaces only once every few hundred kilometers.

Where moisture gathers, life occupies it completely.

The ground is young. Black shelves of newly raised basalt break through paler strata in sharp, unworn planes. The slopes have not yet softened into soil. Cliffs retain the angular geometry of fracture; ravines resemble cracks in cooling metal. In places, whole tablelands are still lifting by the width of a fingernail each year. The motion is almost imperceptible, but the consequences are immense: roots tear, ice splits, deep chambers close under pressure, and far-off scarps release with a concussion that travels through stone for hours.

Nothing here has had time to become empty.

Vegetation begins as a dark lacquer over the rock. Close up, that lacquer resolves into millions of overlapping leaves, scales, cups, hairs, and translucent bladders. Every surface has been claimed. Moss forests stand only a hand high but extend beyond the horizon. Their trunks are thick as wrists, packed together so tightly that fallen organisms cannot reach the ground. Above them rise cold-adapted fronds with metallic blue undersides, turning slowly through the day to keep their tissues from burning beneath the fierce alpine sun. Between the fronds grow rigid towers of pith, hollow and jointed, sheathed in frost even at noon.

The plants do not compete chiefly for light; light is excessive. They compete for shadow, moisture, and physical space. Some fold over their neighbors to cool them and drink the resulting condensation. Others raise polished leaves that throw concentrated brilliance onto rivals until their pigments bleach. Vast mats migrate this way over centuries, advancing no more than a few centimeters in a season, but leaving pale corridors of sterilized stone behind them.

In the wet pockets, growth becomes almost indecently dense. Ravines are corked with green. Warm vapor wells from faults and condenses on the undersides of leaves, dripping into root-filled pools that have no visible water surface. Every droplet passes through mouths, gills, filters, skins, and digestive chambers before it touches mineral ground. Streams flow less like open water than like continuous living columns. Their channels pulse faintly as colonial organisms contract to push water uphill, away from freezing hollows and toward sunlit nurseries.

The plateau’s rivers are therefore not entirely rivers. Some are migratory organs hundreds of kilometers long.

Fauna cover nearly everything else.

Clouds of tiny winged grazers fill the air so densely that their bodies soften the outlines of peaks. They are black when they turn toward the sun and transparent when they turn away, producing immense waves of flicker across the landscape. The sound of their wings is not a buzz but a steady mechanical pressure, like turbines heard through a wall. They travel only a few valleys in a lifetime, but the population as a whole circulates around the continent in a migration lasting nearly three centuries.

Larger animals move beneath them in packed herds. Six-legged browsers with insulated, glassy fur climb the vertical seams in cliffs, feeding on mineral films. Flat-bodied creatures slide under the moss canopy, their passage visible as slow bulges kilometers long. In high basins, pale animals stand shoulder to shoulder around thermal cracks. They may remain there for years, sleeping in shifts, passing heat inward from body to body. Predators live among them without pursuit. There is nowhere to run. Instead, predation happens by minute changes of position: one animal gradually inserting itself into a warm cluster, another weakening over a winter, a ring of mouths closing over the course of several days.

Even the sky is crowded. Buoyant colonies hang in the thin air, held aloft by hydrogen produced in their gut chambers. They resemble bright ropes, tangled continents, or translucent factory smoke that never disperses. Smaller creatures nest inside them; parasites cultivate gardens along their gas veins; hunters crawl across their outer membranes. When one dies, it does not fall cleanly. It sags over weeks, snags on mountaintops, and becomes a suspended reef between peaks.

There are no buildings here, no roads, no tools, no cut stone, no evidence that any mind has stood apart from the landscape and reorganized it. Yet the place has the character of an industrial world.

The industry is alive.

Along the eastern uplands, immense colonies draw metal ions from groundwater and plate them onto branching external skeletons. These formations rise in ranks like refineries, although no two are identical and none has been designed. Sunlight heats their dark cores. Temperature differences drive fluids through miles of capillary pipe. Valves open and close. Membranes slap under pressure. Excess minerals are extruded in bright coils that lie tangled across the ground until other species arrive to eat them.

Elsewhere, deep-rooted organisms exploit tectonic strain. Their root fibers penetrate fresh fractures and grow taut as the plateau rises. When enough tension accumulates, bundles snap in sequence, generating electrical discharges through the surrounding fungal web. Blue-white arcs jump from ridge to ridge in clear weather. The energy is stored chemically in buried nodules and released during the long dark season. Whole ecosystems depend on this slow geologic battery.

The machinery has no operator and no final product. Production exists because every by-product has found a consumer. Heat, charge, pressure, acid, salt, fiber, methane, iron, bone, wax, and noise circulate through nested economies. Nothing resembles wilderness in the sense of vacancy or innocence. The land is untouched, but it is fully used.

Its loudness is continuous rather than violent. Wingbeats lay down the highest note. Under them is the knock of hollow plants flexing in the wind, the clatter of mineral scales, the grinding of herd armor against stone. Deeper still comes the slow pounding of pressurized roots, regular enough to be mistaken for engines. Cliffs hum under tension. Gas colonies moan when jet-stream winds pass through their chambers. On windless days one can hear avalanches traveling through distant ranges, each collapse relayed by the rigid plateau until the ground seems to speak from below.

Silence occurs only during eclipses.

The brightness ordinarily governs nearly every rhythm. The sun is hard and close in the thin air, and the plateau’s ice, pale rock, reflective foliage, and airborne bodies multiply it. Shadows are small, sharp, and precious. Animals carry shadows with them in the form of dark abdominal folds where larvae shelter. Plants grow curtains. Some creatures are entirely flat and spend their lives beneath others, paying for shade by cleaning skin or circulating blood.

When an eclipse crosses the plateau, photosynthetic membranes close all at once. The wing clouds settle. Pressure valves relax. Reflective leaves turn edge-on. For a few minutes, the industrial roar drains away valley by valley until only ice can be heard cracking in the sudden cold. Then unfamiliar animals emerge from the packed layers beneath the moss—colorless, soft, almost fluid—and feed with extraordinary speed before the light returns.

Rain is rarer and less orderly. It comes from collisions between the plateau’s internal weather systems: warm vapor from the living ravines meeting glacial air spilling off the central crown. The drops are small and supercooled. Often they freeze on the first creature they strike, and the landscape acquires a glass casing, sealing wings to wings and leaves to fur. The population does not scatter. It cannot. It waits under the accumulating weight.

Thaw may take days. Movement resumes by degrees: a twitch passing through a herd, a single frond unfolding, one airborne colony reinflating its chambers. Meltwater pours downward but rarely far. Tongues drink it from other tongues. Root nets lift it through capillary action. Sponge animals swell until they block entire canyons. Within hours the exposed stone is dry again, though every living body is heavy with stored water.

Despite the cliffs, storms, electricity, and overwhelming abundance, the essential pace of the place is patient. The great herds seem to surge, but measured from the sky they advance only a valley in a decade. Forests rotate their leaves over days. Rivers alter course by growing new organs. Predator and prey may remain in contact through an entire season before the outcome is decided. Mountains rise into waiting root systems; airborne reefs drift toward peaks that will not intercept them for generations.

The sharpness of the terrain and the density of life create an illusion of urgency. In truth, almost everything here is engaged in a process too large to hurry.

At the center of the continent stands the newest range, a mass of black peaks still emerging from beneath an older, living crust. Their summits are bare only for moments. Spores settle on warm rock before it cools. Winged grazers arrive before the first lichen has anchored. Root threads enter cracks while the cracks are still opening. Animals crowd onto ledges that did not exist the previous winter.

Thus the land is born already occupied.

From a distance, the central range looks like the exposed teeth of some planetary mechanism. Up close, each black face is jeweled with eggs, wet with condensation, crossed by silver roots, and trembling under the combined labor of uncountable lives. Brightness burns over it. Cold preserves it. The mountains continue their almost motionless ascent, carrying the packed world upward with them, while all across the plateau the immense, ownerless engines of ecology beat, grind, filter, and sing.
