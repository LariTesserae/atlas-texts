At nearly five thousand meters, the heat should be impossible.

Yet the high basin holds it.

A long wall of mountains encloses the region, not as a ring of sharp summits but as an elevated country in its own right: broad-backed ridges, tablelands, hanging valleys, and immense shelves of dark stone. Their upper faces have been rounded by weather into old, settled forms. Cliffs remain, but even these seem mature—black-red escarpments streaked with iron, softened by curtains of fern and steady mineral weeping. There is little loose rubble. What falls is quickly buried, rooted through, stained, and made part of the slope.

Moisture lies over everything. The air condenses on skin, leaves, horn, and exposed rock. Clouds gather below the highest rims, climb through the passes, and spill into the basin in slow white masses. Rain comes frequently but seldom with violence. More often it begins as a fine, warm ticking in the canopy, thickens for an hour, and passes on, leaving every surface streaming.

Water has no single dominant course. It is distributed through the land.

Shallow lakes occupy folds in the plateau. Marshes fringe their margins in bands of sedge, broad-leaved water plants, and low trees standing ankle-deep in brown water. Springs emerge from cliff faces in hundreds of threads. Some vanish into porous stone almost as soon as they appear; others join into clear channels that travel beneath mats of roots. The ground gives slightly underfoot. Dig anywhere in the darker hollows and water beads into the cut.

Along the major escarpments, waterfalls descend through cloud. They are visible only in sections: a white lip at the top, a wavering column below, then nothing, then a final plume opening among giant tree ferns. Their sound is absorbed by distance, vegetation, and mist. From the central wetlands they seem almost silent.

The vegetation grows in dense vertical arrangements. At ground level are liverworts, black moss, waxy fungi, and small carnivorous cups filled with rain. Above them rise ferns as tall as a person, then tangled shrubs with red-veined leaves, then trees whose crowns begin twenty or thirty meters overhead. Vines occupy the remaining space, descending, looping upward again, rooting wherever they touch. Orchids and bromeliads load the older limbs until branches bow under the stored water.

Despite this abundance, the country retains an open character. The forest does not cover everything. It forms islands, corridors, and deep green walls around marshes and high meadows. From the basalt shelves one can see across many kilometers: mirrored pools, sulfur-yellow grasslands, cloudbanks resting in ravines, and forested ridges succeeding one another toward the enclosing mountains. Near things are crowded; the whole is spacious.

The heat is constant and bodily. Even on exposed heights, wind brings little relief. Sun-warmed mist drifts across the plateaus, and the stones remain hot after rain. In sheltered gullies, respiration seems to thicken the atmosphere. Leaves release sharp scents—camphor, crushed pepper, wet bark—and pale resins soften on trunks without being touched. At night the temperature falls only enough for condensation to intensify. The forest begins dripping beneath a clear sky.

There are no roads, foundations, cut surfaces, or discarded objects. Nothing indicates that hands have arranged the place. Nevertheless, parts of the basin possess an industrial character.

This comes first from the geology. Far below the plateau, heat moves through water-filled fractures. Springs pulse rather than flow continuously. Every few minutes, pressure drives hot water into chambers under the marsh, forcing air through long stone throats. Valves of mineral crust open and close. Columns of water rise. Steam vents exhale in sequence along the foot of the cliffs. The pattern is regular enough to resemble a distant works beginning a shift.

In one valley, hundreds of warm springs feed terraces of iron oxide and pale silica. Each terrace spills into the next with exact repetition. Mineral films thicken, crack, and are overgrown by bacteria; new films form above them. The place manufactures stone continuously, layer by microscopic layer. Elsewhere, acidic pools dissolve the same mountain back into solution. Production and dismantling occur side by side.

The forest has adapted to these rhythms. Certain trees send hollow roots into warm fissures. Rising vapor passes through the roots and condenses higher in the trunk, supplying epiphytes during brief dry intervals. Their chambers thump softly when pressure changes. Groves of these trees can begin knocking one after another before rain, a low mechanical sequence moving across a hillside.

On the western shelf, the effect is stronger. There, a chain of underground reservoirs cycles between filling and discharge. Water drives through narrow basalt conduits with a steady internal vibration. The ground hums faintly through the soles of the feet. At intervals, a deep pistonlike concussion travels under the plateau—not loud, but immense, felt more in the chest than heard. Then warm springs surge throughout the shelf, marsh levels rise by a finger’s width, and thousands of floating leaves tilt together in the new current.

Lightning is common inside the clouds that climb the outer escarpments. Charge travels through wet vines and mineral-rich trunks, usually without flame. Blue points flicker along thorn tips. During the larger storms whole groves show a dim branching luminescence, as though the forest’s hidden circuitry has briefly become visible. No part of this system was designed, but it has the scale, repetition, and coupled energy of an industry. Heat, pressure, water, mineral, and living tissue are processed ceaselessly.

Animal life is plentiful without being conspicuous. Most creatures are small or well concealed. Frogs occupy water held in leaf cups, each species tuned to a slightly different depth and temperature. Bright lizards flatten themselves against sunlit rock and vanish into the black mineral pattern when shadows pass. Marsh deer move through shoulder-high reeds, leaving narrow channels that close behind them. Troops of dark-furred primates travel in the middle canopy, seldom calling, communicating through branch tremors and soft clicks.

Large birds circle the open basin but rarely enter the dense forest. They ride the persistent updrafts beside the hot cliffs, wings almost motionless. At dusk they descend to the marshes, where fish and amphibians concentrate in the cooling shallows. Their arrival causes a brief expansion of sound: wingbeats, splashes, alarm chirps, the slap of bodies against water. Within minutes the wetlands quiet again.

Predation happens mostly at edges. Long-bodied cats follow the boundary between fern meadow and forest. Snakes wait where warm spring channels cross animal trails. Insects hatch after rain in tremendous numbers, then are reduced by swallows, bats, fish, and dragonflies before the next morning. Nothing dominates for long. Abundance moves through the system in pulses.

The prevailing sound is water at low volume: dripping, seepage, the faint friction of streams under roots. Over that come isolated details—a fruit dropping through leaves, the brittle snap of a drying seedpod, a frog calling once from inside a bromeliad. Wind rarely reaches the basin floor. Thunder is muffled by cloud. Even the geothermal machinery beneath the land speaks in subdued registers: humming stone, hollow knocks, a periodic subterranean stroke.

Erosion has not stopped, but it no longer feels catastrophic. The plateau has been shaped into its enduring channels. Streams follow grooves deepened over ages; roots brace the banks; mineral deposits repair some of what water removes. Slopes slump occasionally after prolonged rain, yet the scars green over quickly. The land does not look young or ruined. It appears to have arrived at a durable agreement with gravity.

Only around the highest vents does that agreement loosen. There the vegetation withdraws from patches of hot, pale ground. Steam curls from cracks crusted with sulfur and metallic salts. Small rivulets run an opaque turquoise before clearing downstream. The air smells of wet iron, eggshell, and struck stone. These clearings are not barren for long. Heat-loving films spread across the runoff, insects graze them, birds arrive for the insects, and seedlings establish themselves at the cooling margins.

The basin’s central lake reflects ordinary daylight: blue when the sky opens, pewter under cloud, green near shore. There is no unnatural glow, no permanent darkness, no dramatic distortion of scale. Its strangeness reveals itself gradually—in the altitude without cold, in the forest that drinks steam from the mountain, in the regular pulse traveling through stone.

From a high shelf, the entire region can be understood as one circulating body. Cloud enters over the eastern wall. Rain filters through forest and marsh. Water descends into heated rock, returns under pressure, deposits minerals, warms roots, and rises again as vapor. Animals carry seed along the dry ridges. Floods rearrange the reed beds. Forest advances over old terraces while new terraces form elsewhere.

Nothing here is still, yet almost nothing seems hurried.

The landscape runs continuously, quietly, at enormous capacity.
