The place occupies a shallow fold in the lowlands, scarcely more than a few streets across. From outside, it appears first as a darkening of the vegetation: a compact, almost geometric thickness in an otherwise open country of reed marshes and drowned fields. Then roof ridges show through. A brick chimney, stained black-green. The upper half of a water tower. Zinc gutters flashing between broad leaves.

There is no clear moment when the marsh ends and the town begins.

Water lies everywhere without quite becoming a lake. It fills brick-lined ditches, gathers beneath loading platforms, shines in wheel ruts, and stands ankle-deep among the roots of plantain and swamp fig. The ground is firm only where generations of crushed stone, clinker, shells, broken tile, and compacted husks have been laid down. Even there it gives slightly underfoot. Rain passed through several hours ago, but every surface still releases it: drops fall from cables, balconies, awnings, leaf tips, crane rails. Old retaining walls weep through their mortar. Warm moisture condenses on the shaded sides of tanks and runs in clean vertical threads through rust.

The air is close but not stifling. It smells of wet brick, sap, hot oil, fermenting leaves, river mud, and the faint mineral sharpness of machinery. In sunny openings, steam rises almost invisibly from roofs and paving. In the lanes, the warmth is held under foliage as under a low ceiling.

Vegetation does not merely overgrow the settlement. It is one of its principal materials.

Trees were planted along property lines so long ago that their trunks now form walls between workshops. Their roots lift foundations in smooth, patient humps, and the masonry has been repeatedly cut and rebuilt around them. Warehouses lean against enormous buttressed figs. Covered walkways pass beneath woven trellises of vine and split cane. Hedges serve where another town would have fences; dense screens of glossy leaves hide yards full of boilers and belts. In the oldest quarter, living posts—pollarded trees with cropped crowns—support corrugated roofs. Their bark has swollen around iron straps, holding them more securely than bolts would.

Every available ledge carries something green. Ferns occupy drain mouths. Moss darkens the shaded brickwork. Pepper vines climb power poles and must be cut away from ceramic insulators each season. Roof gutters have become narrow hanging gardens of sedge and wild taro. Where a factory wall has lost its plaster, roots explore the joints in fine reddish fans. Larger plants grow from places that appear impossible until one notices the constant water, the trapped silt, the decades of leaf litter accumulating in corners no broom can reach.

The town’s industry depends on this abundance. It presses oils, strips fibers, steams bark, dries leaves, mills soft timber, and renders waxes, dyes, gums, and starch. Nothing is made quickly. The yards are full of long processes: stalks soaking beneath weighted grates; bundled reeds darkening in retting tanks; slabs of aromatic wood seasoning under open-sided sheds; vats in which pulp settles over several days into pale layers. On racks above the drains, skeins of fiber hang like faded hair. Rows of shallow trays hold resin in various stages of thickening, from amber liquid to cloudy cakes.

The machinery is old, heavy, and continuously repaired. Broad leather belts travel overhead from shaft to shaft. Flywheels turn behind mesh guards. Pumps thud at intervals beneath the floorboards, keeping the lowest rooms merely damp instead of flooded. Conveyors crawl through gaps in walls, carrying chopped cane, leaves, or pressed bricks of vegetable waste. Small electric motors buzz inside patched housings. The current comes by wire from a low turbine station on the drainage canal, supplemented by steam engines burning husks and dried pressings.

This makes the place loud, though not hurried.

The sound is layered and almost architectural. A mill wheel knocks once per revolution. Drive belts slap. Bearings whine. Water rattles through screens clogged with stems. Somewhere, always, a pump makes its deep two-beat labor: intake, discharge; intake, discharge. Metal carts complain along narrow-gauge tracks embedded in the streets. Boilers sigh through relief valves. Rainwater continues to drip from the canopy into thousands of containers, gutters, puddles, and channels, each with its own pitch.

Human voices do not carry far through it. They are absorbed by leaves and machinery. Workers communicate nearby with short calls, whistles, or hand signs learned from the factory floor. At shift changes, a bell sounds from the chimney yard—not sharp, but broad and bronze, passing through the wet air like a pressure wave.

The streets are narrow because the settlement has never expanded cleanly. Each generation filled another yard, bridged another channel, or built a second floor over an existing lane. Buildings nearly touch above the footpaths. Wooden galleries face brick warehouses at arm’s length. Pipes cross overhead in bundles, wrapped in damp insulation and patched with tin. Small bridges span black drainage cuts between doorways. Beneath them, warm water moves slowly under mats of duckweed and fallen petals.

There is a central square, though only the paving reveals it. Trees have divided it into a grove of dim green rooms. A market occupies the dry side beneath canvas and sheet-metal roofs. Most goods are plant products: coils of rope, woven mats, baskets, medicinal leaves, bundles of fuel, oils in reused glass bottles, spices spread on raised trays, lengths of cloth dyed brown, indigo, and muted red. Fresh food is kept high above the wet ground. The few meat stalls are small and infrequent.

Animals are scarce. There are no herds and few pets. The density of industry, water, and cultivated growth seems to leave little unclaimed space for them. Occasionally a pale heron stands in a drainage basin, rigid among pipes, or a small lizard crosses a sun-warmed wall before vanishing into leaves. At dusk, a few bats move above the chimney line. Insects are present but not overwhelming: dragonflies over open water, beetles under bark, moths around lamps. Mosquitoes are controlled by oiling stagnant sumps and keeping narrow fish in the main channels. Birdsong is so uncommon that a single call from the marsh beyond town can be heard distinctly between cycles of the presses.

Despite the vegetation, the place is not wild. It is tended with relentless practicality. Every tree is known by what it shades, stabilizes, yields, or obstructs. Vines are trained away from gears and toward trellises. Roots are allowed beneath lanes but cut from culverts. Fallen leaves are collected for compost, fuel, or packing material. Even the floating plants in the settling ponds are harvested on a schedule.

The settlement’s rules have grown from moisture. Ground-floor doors open outward because swollen wood cannot be trusted to slide. Electrical switches are mounted high. Nothing valuable rests directly on a floor. Every workshop keeps a pole marked with past flood heights. New construction must leave water a path through; walls without drains crack within a season. Gutters are considered communal infrastructure, no matter whose roof they cross. Blocking a channel is treated more seriously than blocking a road.

Decay proceeds here, but so does repair, and both are slow. Rust does not consume a railing in dramatic flakes; it advances beneath paint over years, leaving orange seams. Brick corners soften. Stair treads hollow under wet boots. Trees swallow nails, hooks, and old boundary wires. A disused smokestack has become a vertical garden, yet its lower courses remain sound, buttressed by roots and generations of careful repointing.

Nothing seems temporary, though almost nothing is unchanged.

Toward the edge of town, the buildings lower and loosen. Workshops give way to sheds, then to drying racks standing over wet fields. The channels broaden. The mechanical clamor fades into isolated beats, then into the steady spill of water over wooden gates. Beyond lies the marsh that feeds and receives the settlement: warm, shallow, densely planted, crossed by service paths and narrow barges.

Looking back from there, the town does not form a skyline so much as a single compact canopy. Chimneys, tanks, roofs, vines, steam, and tree crowns are pressed together into one weathered mass. It seems less as if a town were built in a wet forest than as if masonry and machinery had been inserted, piece by piece, into a long-established plant community—and had remained long enough to become another of its durable tissues.
