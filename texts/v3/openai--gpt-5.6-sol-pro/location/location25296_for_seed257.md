### The High Cistern

The place is entirely underwater, but it is not beneath the earth or sea.

It hangs high above both.

The High Cistern occupies the crown of an artificial mountain: a squat, many-sided reservoir assembled from fired brick, fitted stone, black glass, and hammered copper. Below it, descending through miles of cloud, are terraces, retaining walls, sealed stairways, and chambers packed with rubble placed by hand. There is no bedrock anywhere within the mountain. No original soil lies under the paving. Even the grit in the joints was crushed, sorted, and carried upward in baskets.

At the summit, behind walls thicker than houses, rests a small, warm sea.

It is no larger than a cathedral precinct. From one side, a strong swimmer might reach the other before exhaustion, though the interior divisions make direct passage impossible. Walls, buttresses, screens, hanging chains, drowned galleries, and stacks of perforated ceramic blocks fill the water. Space is dense here. Nothing is more than a few arm lengths from something made: a lintel, a pillar, a hook, an urn mortared into the ceiling, a ledge worn smooth by passing bodies.

The water reaches every surface. There are no chambers of air.

It is warm without being hot, held at nearly the temperature of blood by the cistern’s sun-facing outer tiles. Daylight strikes those tiles far outside, above the cloud deck, but penetrates poorly. The reservoir’s walls were deliberately built to exclude brightness. Only a few seams admit light, and through these comes not a beam but a dim copper discoloration, fading within a few feet.

Most of the place exists in darkness.

The cistern was made with simple means. Every block bears evidence of fingers, wooden gauges, stone hammers, plumb cords, and patient leverage. The arches are not mathematically perfect. Their curves were judged by eye. The ceramic screens vary slightly in thickness. Copper cramps were beaten cold and driven into grooves with wedges of dark horn. There are no engines, pumps, gears, or lamps. Water was raised through thousands of sealed jars and stair-stepped locks, one vessel at a time, until the summit chamber was full.

Why such labor was undertaken is no longer clear.

Age has pared the interior toward its final shape. Corners have rounded. Relief carvings have become low, anonymous swells. Inscriptions survive only inside protected sockets, and even there the marks resemble the trails of small animals more than writing. Copper has darkened to brown-black scales. Glass panels, once smooth, have been clouded by countless abrasions. Brick faces have softened into red hollows where the slow circulation touches them.

Yet little collapses. The builders expected erosion. Behind each visible course is another, and behind that another. When a tile wears through, the water finds the next skin. The cistern is less a container than a sequence of nested containers, each prepared to become the outermost wall after the previous one is gone.

There is no plant life.

No weed waves from the masonry. No moss greens the joints. No root enters a crack. The palette is iron red, ash gray, tarnished black, old ivory, and the occasional dull glimmer of ceramic glaze. Against this mineral world, the animals seem almost indecently numerous.

They fill the niches.

Pale, thumb-sized swimmers move in tight clouds through the perforated bricks. They have broad fins and no visible eyes; their mouths open sideways like cuts in folded cloth. Flat shelled creatures adhere to ceilings, overlapping one another like roof tiles. Long-bodied grazers thread through chains and handles, testing every surface with fans of soft feelers. In the deepest gallery, translucent animals as large as cloaks drift vertically, contracting perhaps once every few minutes.

Nothing pursues quickly.

Predation here is a patient matter. A hunting animal may remain wedged inside a jar mouth for half a season, indistinguishable from a strip of old leather. When enough small swimmers gather around the jar to feed, the hunter closes its throat and draws them inward with a single quiet pulse.

Most life depends on a gray, nearly invisible film. It is not vegetal. It does not photosynthesize. It forms where warm water passes over copper, iron-rich brick, bone lime, and certain black glazes. Minute, unclassified cells live within that chemical boundary, taking nourishment from the slow exchange between metal, clay, salt, and water. The film feeds raspers; raspers feed shell-borers; shell-borers feed the patient hunters. Bodies fall into floor grates, where soft scavengers reduce them until only mineral fragments remain.

Those fragments become part of the cistern.

The lowest floor is paved, but no one sees it now. Centuries of shells, teeth, ceramic dust, copper scales, and compacted droppings cover it in a dense sediment. Even this sediment is not quite natural. Its grains derive from made walls and from creatures bred within those walls. When disturbed, it rises in a black-red cloud and remains suspended for days.

The cistern is quiet beyond expectation.

Water usually carries every scrape and collision, but the builders lined the inner chambers with porous brick and nested jars. These swallow vibration. A swimmer hears only what is close: the faint tick of a shell loosening from a wall, the brush of a fin over chain, the tiny grinding of mouthparts on mineral film. Larger sounds do not travel far. They enter the ceramic hollows and break into murmurs.

Occasionally the whole reservoir gives one deep note.

This happens when the outer mountain cools after a long season of sun and the masonry contracts. The note is too low to be heard clearly. It is felt in the ribs and in the cavities behind the eyes. The animals stop moving when it comes. Thousands of bodies settle at once, and the cistern becomes so still that drifting sediment seems fixed in place.

Then, gradually, motion resumes.

The water circulates through temperature alone. One sunward wall warms by a fraction; the opposite wall cools. This weak difference drives a current through the galleries, around the buttresses, down the central shaft, and back along the floor. A complete circuit may take several days. The animals arrange their lives along it. Filter feeders open as the warm current arrives. Egg cases are attached to the colder masonry. The largest drifters migrate no farther than one chamber in a month.

There are vertical passages, but “up” is deceptive. The cistern’s ceilings are terraced and deeply coffered. Some coffers are tall enough to resemble towers descending from above, their sides crowded with alcoves. Other passages narrow into funnels that were once fill shafts. At their ends sit enormous stone plugs sealed with lead and wax-dark resin.

Above those plugs is the open summit: hard sunlight, thin air, and a tiled platform exposed to the sky.

No living creature in the cistern has seen it.

The plugs are part of the oldest rule of the place: water may enter, but the summit sea must never breathe. Air is treated as contamination. Every maintenance door was designed to be opened only into another flooded compartment. Every stairwell bends through water locks. Even the builders, if the surviving reliefs are interpreted correctly, worked submerged in weighted masks, passing stone from hand to hand in the dark.

A second rule is visible in the architecture: no surface is allowed to remain useless. Walls are punctured for shelter. Pillars are grooved to guide current. Chains divide territories. Urns serve as breeding chambers. Screens sort animals by size. The cistern was not merely filled with life after construction. It was built as a crowded instrument for life, though not for any life that required sunlight.

A third rule has emerged without design: nothing leaves quickly.

Heat leaves slowly. Sound dies locally. Water is lost only molecule by molecule through the outer seals. Dead things descend through a succession of mouths and grates. Even damage migrates inward one layer at a time. Change is permitted, but only after being made to pass through many chambers.

At the center stands the cistern’s one open space: a shaft surrounded by worn pillars. It rises and falls beyond sight, dark in both directions. Here the water is clearest. The weak circulation turns around the shaft in a broad, almost motionless spiral.

Countless small animals orbit it.

Their pale bodies make no light, yet in sufficient number they define the darkness by occlusion. They form drifting bands, gaps, knots, and slow constellations. Sometimes they part around a descending shape: a flake of ancient plaster, a dead shell-bearer, a broken copper clasp freed after a thousand years.

Everything falls gently.

Far below, without impact enough to hear, it joins the fabricated seabed of the little sea built at the top of the world.
