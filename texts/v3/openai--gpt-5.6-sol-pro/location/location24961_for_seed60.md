The place occupies a shallow basin no larger than a hamlet, though it is difficult to judge its boundaries from within. The ground rises only slightly at the edges, forming a low rim of old rootwork and dark, compacted peat. Beyond that rim there may be open country, but the vegetation has sealed every outward view. Nothing here is distant. Everything is close enough to touch, dripping onto something else, leaning under another thing’s weight.

At first it resembles an overgrown industrial yard after rain. Then it becomes apparent that there is no yard beneath the growth.

The ground itself is vegetal: braided roots, corky plates, mats of pale stolons, and thick pads that yield underfoot with the slow resilience of muscle. In hollows between them lies warm brown water, rarely more than ankle-deep. It is clear enough to reveal fine root hairs combing the bottom. There is no exposed soil. Where a bank has been worn open, its cross-section shows layer upon layer of old leaves compressed into fibrous black felt, penetrated by living green threads. Even the gravel-like patches are made from seed cases, hard and polished, accumulated over generations.

Plants occupy the basin completely. There is no useful distinction between canopy, wall, floor, and undergrowth. Trunks grow shoulder to shoulder, but few are vertical. They arch, flatten, divide, and reunite, making chambers with ribbed ceilings. Broad leaves overlap like roofing tiles. Curtains of translucent tendrils hang between them, heavy with condensation. Vines fill the remaining gaps, and moss fills the gaps between vines. On every shaded surface, miniature forests of fern and liverwort rise from the bark of larger plants, themselves furred with algae.

The basin is warm without being oppressive. Moisture remains close to the skin. After rain—which comes often and without much wind—the whole place exhales. Steam gathers beneath the leaves, and droplets pearl along waxy edges until the leaves bow and empty themselves in sudden showers. Hours later, water is still travelling downward through the many levels. It taps on seed pods, runs along grooved stems, disappears into cups of folded foliage, then emerges somewhere else in a fine silver thread.

There are structures here, although they do not stand apart from the vegetation.

At the center is a squat pumping house, or what was once a pumping house. Its brick walls are visible only in three narrow places, each brick softened by lichen and pushed out of alignment by roots. The roof has been replaced by an interwoven crown of woody stems. Inside, an iron flywheel turns at less than one revolution a minute. Its lower half is hidden in a mound of glossy leaves. A belt of plaited fiber runs from the wheel into the thicket, vanishing between two trunks. Another belt returns overhead, wet and trembling.

No engine fires. No fuel is stored. The machinery is driven by the plants’ own hydraulic pressure.

Throughout the basin, swollen bladder-vines fill with water drawn from the peat. When enough pressure accumulates, they contract. Their movement pulls cables, lifts counterweights, compresses bellows, and advances rusted ratchets by a tooth or two. The mechanisms work on botanical time. A piston may take all morning to descend. A row of wooden cams turns with the seasons. Valves open when certain flowers warm in the sun and close when their petals fold at dusk.

Yet the place is loud.

Every slow movement is performed under enormous tension. Roots creak like ship timbers. Fiber belts complain against iron pulleys. Water hammers inside buried pipes. At irregular intervals a counterweight drops the length of a short shaft, striking the ground with a concussion that shakes droplets from the canopy. Hollow stems amplify the impact and pass it from chamber to chamber. There is also a constant deep vibration, barely audible in the pumping house but stronger near the rim, as if the whole basin were resting on the casing of an immense, distant motor.

Smaller sounds crowd the spaces between: dripping, leaf-rattle, the pop of opening pods, the slow tear of bark accommodating growth. Metal components ring when tendrils tighten around them. Somewhere unseen, a warped turbine emits a steady, mournful note whenever runoff reaches a certain depth. The note may last for days after heavy rain.

The original purpose of the works is no longer clear. Narrow-gauge rails pass beneath the roots, surfacing only in curved, green-black segments. A line of small iron hoppers stands along one side of the basin, but each has become a planter dense with reeds. The reeds produce hard, hexagonal fruits that resemble manufactured nuts and bolts. Near the pumping house, ceramic insulators remain fixed to leaning poles. Pale vines connect them in place of wire. In damp weather, blue sparks move languidly through the vines, and nearby flowers turn their faces toward the discharge.

Nothing appears abandoned. Abandonment would imply that the machinery has ceased to matter. Instead, the vegetation has taken over its schedules and incorporated its motions. Roots have grown into gear teeth with such precision that they serve as replaceable cogs. Resin lubricates bearings. Tough, ribbonlike leaves travel through rollers and emerge embossed with regular ridges. Fungal stains mark batches and intervals. What is being produced, if anything, may simply be the continued operation of the place.

Animal life is conspicuously scarce. No birds call from the canopy. There are few insects despite the humidity and flowers. Occasionally a long-legged black beetle crosses a leaf, moving with exaggerated care around the vibrating veins. Small, blind fish live in the warm water beneath the root mats, glimpsed only when a pressure stroke drives them briefly into the open. Once or twice a year, pale moths emerge from vents in the pumping house and settle over the basin in silence. By morning they are gone, leaving no bodies.

Pollination, decomposition, and dispersal are mostly handled by the plants themselves. Some flowers walk their pollen along linked filaments. Dead growth is not allowed to remain dead for long: neighboring roots soften it, enter it, and reuse its fibers. Seeds are carried through the old pipe system by pulses of rainwater. Each pipe empties into a different chamber, so the industrial layout has become the basin’s reproductive anatomy.

The place has endured long enough to reach a peculiar equilibrium. Watercourses have worn smooth channels through wood, but new tissue grows at almost exactly the rate of wear. Iron rusts, and the rust is absorbed into red-veined leaves. Walls slump, only to be held at their new angle by buttress roots. Nothing is pristine, yet little is collapsing. Damage becomes shape. Erosion is not an enemy here but one of the tools by which the basin maintains itself.

Light enters normally, in broad warm shafts wherever the canopy thins. There is no permanent gloom and no supernatural radiance. Sunlight reveals ordinary greens, brown water, gray iron, brick red, and the white gleam of wet fibers. The strangeness lies in how these familiar materials meet. A leaf may be riveted to a steel plate by its own hardened sap. A trunk may carry a stamped serial number, repeated faithfully in the bark of its offshoots. Rings visible in cut stems sometimes form diagrams rather than circles: valve sequences, belt paths, inventories of rainfall.

The basin’s chief rule is that no empty space remains empty for long. A footprint fills first with water, then with floating green cells, then with roots descending from its rim. A broken window becomes a trellis. An open pipe becomes a nursery. If an object is left overnight, exploratory tendrils touch it by morning. In a week they test its weight. In a season they decide whether it is wall, tool, nutrient, or obstruction.

Another rule is that movement must be earned through accumulation. Nothing darts or rushes. Water gathers before it falls. Pressure rises before a wheel advances. Flowers take hours to turn. A whole corridor may close over the course of a summer as two living walls slowly meet. The violence of the machinery—the crashes, groans, and shuddering impacts—is made from patience stored up and released.

From the basin’s rim, if one can climb that high, the place appears almost peaceful: a compact crown of green sunk into flat wet country, breathing mist into the afternoon. Only the old poles and the top of the pumping-house flywheel betray its industry. Then the buried mechanism completes another fraction of its cycle.

The ground tightens.

Leaves tilt in unison. Water rises through a thousand stems. Somewhere below, a latch withdraws.

The entire green basin gives a single tremendous knock, and then resumes its slow work.
