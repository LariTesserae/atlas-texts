The high country begins above the ordinary cloud line, but it is not bare.

It rises as a continent-sized tableland, an immense sequence of shelves and shallow basins lifted nearly three kilometers above the low world. From a distance the plateau appears dark green, almost black, its surface level enough to hold a continuous skin of weather. Yet there is no true plain upon it. Every reach is crowded with ridges, pools, root mounds, stone ribs, braided runnels, moss towers, and close-set groves. Space is occupied down to the smallest crevice. Where a branch has not grown, a fern unfolds; where the fern cannot take hold, algae seal the rock; beneath the algae, pale colonies live in the stone’s pores.

Water is everywhere without becoming a single dominant body. Rain passes frequently, fine and cool, and remains afterward as beads on needles, films on leaves, dark seams in bark, and trembling lenses caught among mosses. The ground is saturated but firm. It has been saturated for so long that the living surface has learned to hold itself together: roots weave mats across old mud; mineral crusts reinforce the roots; decaying trunks become raised causeways, though no foot designed them. Beneath this dense floor, water travels under pressure through buried gravel beds and chambers worn into the plateau’s soluble rock. It emerges along escarpments in thousands of narrow seeps, so that whole cliff faces appear to perspire.

The stone is old volcanic material altered by water—basalt faded to iron gray, green-black glass clouded by age, and softer beds stained amber by mineral springs. Erosion has not made the place ruinous. It has brought it into a settled complexity. Ravines have widened until their walls no longer collapse readily. Waterfalls have cut backward to resistant ledges and now hang there, thin and enduring. Landslips are overgrown almost as soon as they occur, and old scars have become crescent marshes nested one below another. The land’s forms look finished without being still.

Across the broadest basins stand forests so tightly packed that their crowns interlock into a nearly unbroken upper surface. The trees are low for their age, shaped by cold air and wet winds: thick-trunked, many-forked, clothed in curtains of lichen. Their leaves are small, waxed, and deep green above, silver beneath. After rain the wind turns them by degrees, and entire watersheds brighten as if a dim current were passing through the canopy.

Between these forests rise stranger growths. Great ribbed columns, neither quite plant nor fungus, crowd around warm mineral vents. They are hollow and divided internally into chambers. Cold air enters through low pores; heat from decomposition and geothermal seepage draws it upward. Inside, dense microbial films separate metals from the water, bind nitrogen, digest dead matter, and precipitate hard ceramic-like rings around the chambers. Nothing built them, and no intelligence supervises them, but seen across a valley they resemble industrial towers at work.

This is the plateau’s industry: biological, ceaseless, and scaled beyond any single creature’s understanding.

Sap is pumped through root networks spanning kilometers. Fungal cords sort and redirect dissolved minerals. Colonies in the warm bogs produce waxes that spread over the water and break apart under ultraviolet light. Small armored grazers scrape these films into pellets; larger animals swallow the grazers; their migrations carry concentrated salts uphill. In the vent fields, gas bladders inflate and collapse with regular pressure, opening valves in the living columns. On cold mornings, hundreds of them exhale together. White vapor rises in vertical bands before joining the low cloud deck.

There are no roads, foundations, cut faces, tools, or discarded things. Nevertheless, the place sometimes gives the impression of enormous manufacture. Groves pulse with transported water. Marshes settle sediment into graded beds. Falls turn slowly growing wheels of rooted tissue, whose motion grinds seed husks and aerates submerged nurseries. Electric potential builds across certain fungal membranes and discharges into the wet ground in faint, branching flashes. The systems are not inventions. They evolved piecemeal, each process fastening itself to another until the plateau became a factory without architecture, producing soil, oxygen, shell, heat, spores, flesh, and more living machinery.

The air remains cool throughout the year. Daylight is clear but softened by moisture, neither gloomy nor brilliant. Clouds form locally over the wetter forests, drag their lower edges through the canopy, and dissolve over stone barrens warmed by the pale sun. A traveler could watch rain begin on one slope, feed a tarn, descend underground, and return hours later as mist from a cliff several valleys away.

There is little wind at ground level. The crowded vegetation absorbs it. Sound, too, is absorbed.

Despite the abundance of animals, the plateau is remarkably quiet. Most creatures communicate through pressure, scent, color, or vibrations carried in root and soil. Broad-footed browsers move through the moss without cracking twigs. Their six-part hooves spread their weight over the wet surface, leaving only temporary dimples that fill with clear water. Long-bodied predators lie among the fallen trunks and sense the pulse of passing animals through sensitive plates along their jaws. Small gliding animals travel below the canopy, opening translucent membranes for only a moment before folding themselves into the next mass of foliage.

Birdlike forms are present, but few sing. Their feathers grow soft-edged against the damp, and their calls are low: a throat click, a descending breath, two notes almost too deep to locate. Amphibious grazers gather by the thousands in shallow tarns, browsing submerged lawns, yet even they make little noise. They rise to breathe through narrow dorsal tubes, dimpling the water with rings.

The loudest recurring sound is not an animal but runoff. It is a continuous, distant threading—water under moss, water inside stone, water falling beyond sight. Nearby, it resolves into drips and delicate trickles. Farther away, it merges into a hush much like wind, though the leaves may be motionless. During the pressure release of the vent colonies, a valley may give one long, soft exhalation. Then quiet closes over it again.

Density governs life here. No organism can simply claim empty ground; there is almost none. New life must enter by replacement, alliance, parasitism, or inheritance. Seeds germinate in the wet fur of animals, in hollows left by dead branches, or on temporary rafts of shed leaves. A fallen giant does not open a clearing for long. Its canopy is occupied before it reaches the ground, its bark already planted with successors. Death is less an absence than a change in tenancy.

The plateau’s largest animals are shaped by this rule. They do not push through the forest. They carry it. Old browsers develop gardens along their backs—moss, small ferns, water-holding cups, and colonies of insects that clean the animals’ skin. When they stand motionless, which they may do for days while digesting woody food, they resemble low islands. Their migrations are visible less as movement than as a gradual rearrangement of green masses across the basin.

Predation is similarly subdued. A kill produces no long spectacle. Scavengers arrive through underground burrows and canopy routes, each specializing in a particular tissue. Within a day, the body is opened; within several days, it is distributed; within a season, its minerals may be flowering in dozens of places. Nothing remains exposed long enough to bleach.

At the plateau’s margins, the land ends in immense stair-stepped escarpments. Forests cling to each ledge, and waterfalls descend in tiers so tall that the water becomes cloud before reaching the next shelf. Here the scale becomes apparent. What seemed from within to be a whole world of basins and ridges is only the upper skin of a single elevated mass extending beyond the horizon. Weather systems circulate around it. Rivers are born by the hundreds along its edges. The vapor from its living towers contributes to storms that travel into distant lowlands.

Yet the interior rarely feels grand in an open, panoramic way. Its vastness is hidden by fullness. Vision stops against leaves, trunks, mist, stone, and overlapping life. The continent reveals itself through duration: the days required to cross one root basin, the repeated arrival of unfamiliar rains, the slow variation in species, the sense that every nearby process is connected to another beyond sight.

At dusk the ordinary colors deepen rather than disappear. Moist rock becomes blue-gray; leaves hold the last neutral light; tarns mirror strips of moving cloud. There is no artificial glow, but some colonies release the charge accumulated during the day. Faint lines flicker under translucent bark. Pale pulses travel through fungal webs beneath the moss, not bright enough to illuminate anything, only enough to show that the ground is active.

Then the plateau settles into its nocturnal work. Water levels rise in the root chambers. Vents contract. Cool air drains down ravines. Browsers lift their heads at vibrations too slight to hear. Predators move.

From the cliffs comes the quiet fall of a continent’s excess water, and from beneath the crowded earth, almost imperceptible, the steady labor of everything converting into everything else.
