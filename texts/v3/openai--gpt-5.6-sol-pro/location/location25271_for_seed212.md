At first it appears to be a forest occupying a low, warm basin. There is a horizon, pale under ordinary daylight, and beneath it an unbroken green density stretching for many kilometers. No mountains rise behind it. No sea brightens beyond it. The land is almost level, dipping imperceptibly toward channels of brown water and shallow mirror-pools that persist in the deeper folds.

But the forest has streets.

They are not roads cut through vegetation. They are long, branching intervals in the vegetation’s growth: corridors roofed by woven crowns, bordered by walls of buttress roots, paved with overlapping leaves as broad and firm as flagstones. Intersections open into plazas where sunlight falls through chimneys in the canopy. Arcades run along the plazas. Towers rise above them. Bridges cross from crown to crown, and immense green facades stand shoulder to shoulder, pierced with doors, windows, balconies, and blind recesses.

Nothing here has been built with tools.

The towers are hollow trunks grown in clusters, their columns fused by generations of strangling vines. Balconies are shelf-fungi made rigid by lattices of woody veins. Stairs spiral upward as arrangements of roots, each step swelling into place before the one above it. Awnings unfold at noon and roll themselves tightly at dusk. Gates consist of thorn branches that separate when the ground is dry and interlock when rain threatens. Drainage channels are lined with waxy leaves whose tips pass water from one to another, carrying each drop through whole districts without letting it touch the soil.

It resembles a city because it is organized, but its organization is botanical rather than civic. There are no inhabitants. There never were.

Every apparent dwelling is an organ of the larger growth.

The basin is damp without being abundantly watered. Rain comes irregularly: brief warm downpours, sometimes separated by weeks. Most moisture arrives as condensation. At dawn, every edge beads with water. Leaves cup it, stalks wick it downward, and pale aerial roots comb droplets from the air. The city drinks itself from the outside inward. Reservoir courts lie hidden beneath the plazas, sealed under rafts of interlocked bulbs. If one steps—or imagines stepping—onto such a court, its entire surface yields with the slow pressure of deep water.

There is no animal life. No birds cross the sky. No insects work the flowers. No worms turn the soil. The absence is absolute enough to feel like a law rather than an accident. Blossoms open, but no pollinator approaches them. Seeds travel by explosive pods, by running water, by wind, or by stranger means. Some flowers pollinate their own earlier selves. Others extend filament-thin roots into neighboring towers and exchange pollen underground. A few open onto distant courtyards instead of onto the air.

The silence one expects from this lifelessness never comes.

The city is deafening.

Leaves the size of roofs clap against one another in warm gusts. Seed towers detonate in sequence, each pod bursting with a flat wooden concussion that rolls through the avenues. Trunks groan as they twist toward sunlight. Root vaults crack, settle, and crack again. Millions of hollow reeds stand packed in the drainage districts, and the slightest change in air pressure makes them scream in layered chords. Water drops through long vascular shafts with the amplified booming of stones falling down wells. When a new street begins to grow, paving leaves snap open one after another for hours, the sound racing into the distance like a fusillade.

Underneath all of this is a deeper noise: a continuous, subsonic strain produced by the basin-wide root mat as it contracts and releases. It is less heard than felt. Pools tremble. Dust shakes from stamens. The air inside the hollow towers pulses against their walls. There are moments when the whole green city seems to be drawing breath, although breathing is not quite what it is doing.

Its growth is too rapid for permanence.

At sunrise a district may consist of low mats of scarlet creeper, glistening with condensation. By midday, pale stalks have erupted through them, pushing up colonnades and splitting the mats into courtyards. By evening the colonnades have joined overhead and become a covered market of hanging seed cases, all knocking together in the wind. The following dawn, the entire structure may collapse into warm mulch as a new root ridge passes beneath it.

There are ruins here, but none are old.

Broken towers are still wet at the fracture. Fallen walls retain green sap and continue to leaf while lying across avenues. Sharp stumps line the newest channels, cream-colored within, their edges not yet darkened by weather. Landslips occur on slopes so slight they should not be able to slide: whole gardens detach from the underlying root felt and drift several meters overnight, crumpling against neighboring districts. The basin’s young terrain is continually being born from vegetable pressure. Soil is scarce. What resembles earth is usually pulverized bark, compacted pollen, spore dust, and the black remains of growths digested by other growths.

Nothing decomposes by means of animals. There are no scavengers. Plants dismantle one another directly.

Some send transparent roots through fallen wood and dissolve it from within. Others wrap dead trunks in hot, fermenting sheaths. Pale fungal-looking forests appear after structural collapses, though these too are plants—or occupy the category plants have expanded to fill here. They drink lignin, exhale steam, and vanish as soon as the debris has been reduced to syrup. The syrup drains into underground cisterns and later emerges as new architecture.

The low elevation gives the place a broad, oppressive openness. From the upper crowns, one can see districts extending far beyond the immediate maze: black-green domes, yellow towers, silver reed fields, red plazas opening and sealing, long seams of flowers that move across the basin like weather fronts. Yet distance is unreliable. A tower visible twenty kilometers away may stand behind the observer upon entering the next courtyard. A straight avenue may return to its starting plaza while continuing visibly toward the horizon. Two separate canals sometimes carry the same floating leaf, simultaneously and in opposite directions.

This is not illusion. The plants do not merely distort perception. They grow through adjacency itself.

Certain vines connect places rather than surfaces. A tendril disappears into a crack in one wall and emerges from the bark of a tower days away. Root gates open into whichever district currently has the greatest need for water. Some plazas are larger inside their rings of trunks than the basin that contains them. In one quarter, the sun shines between the paving leaves, while the sky overhead is a deep, root-filled dark. Elsewhere, rain falls upward from pools and gathers as a suspended wetland beneath the canopy.

Despite these failures of geometry, the light remains remarkably normal. Day is day. Sunlight is warm, yellow-white, and directional. Shadows fall with convincing weight. Clouds pass overhead at believable speeds. This ordinary illumination makes the impossible structures more exact rather than more dreamlike. One can clearly see the veins in a staircase turning into rivers as they descend; clearly see a row of windows opening onto the same interior chamber from incompatible angles; clearly watch a shadow put out leaves where it touches a wall.

At noon, heat gathers in the avenues. Resin liquefies and runs in amber gutters. Broad pavement leaves curl at their edges, exposing cooler violet undersides. Steam lifts from shaded pools. The air smells of crushed stems, wet wood, peppery pollen, and the mineral odor released when roots split the basin’s thin underlying clay. There is sweetness too, but it is not the sweetness of fruit meant to be eaten. No fruit here anticipates a mouth. Pods are armored, buoyant, adhesive, spring-loaded, or filled with dust fine enough to rise into the upper air.

The flowers are correspondingly strange. With no eyes to attract, many have no visible color pattern. They signal by heat, electrical charge, or sound. Trumpet blooms emit hammering pulses that shake pollen loose from distant companions. Flat black flowers turn toward thunder rather than sunlight. In the western districts, immense translucent blossoms inflate over the rooftops during dry weather and burst when humidity rises, releasing veils of seed that briefly make every street look fogbound.

Some growths seem to imitate urban purposes they cannot have learned.

There are amphitheaters whose concentric terraces focus the roar of the reeds until the sound becomes physically unbearable. There are narrow chambers lined with soft moss, almost silent, entered through valves in living walls. There are avenues that widen at regular intervals as if to accommodate traffic, though nothing travels them except shadows, water, and advancing root fronts. There are empty niches shaped uncannily like standing bodies, but no body has ever stood in them. There are gardens inside gardens, each bounded by hedges, each centered on a solitary plant that resembles a fountain, an altar, or a throne depending on the angle of approach.

These resemblances are temporary. The city repeatedly approaches familiar forms and then grows past them.

A tower becomes a tree, becomes a bridge, becomes a folded ravine. A plaza buckles and turns vertical, carrying its pavement upward as a wall of overlapping green scales. A canal branches into finer and finer channels until it is indistinguishable from the veins of a single leaf; then the leaf closes, and the water is elsewhere. Whole neighborhoods migrate by shedding their lower roots and extending new ones forward, moving with such slowness that the motion is invisible from street level and obvious only against the horizon.

During rain, the turbulence intensifies.

The first drops strike the canopy like pebbles on drums. Thousands of roofs invert to catch water. Streets narrow as walls swell. Cistern bulbs open their seams. Dusty gray districts become lacquered emerald in minutes, while dry seed arcades twist shut and disappear beneath fresh foliage. Brown torrents run through channels that were not present before the storm and will seal after it. The roar of rain joins the cracking, booming, whistling city until no individual sound can be separated from the whole.

Then the storm stops.

Water hangs everywhere: on the tips of thorns, along suspended roots, inside the trembling throats of flowers. Warm vapor rises from newly split wood. For a short time, the basin is quieter, though never quiet. The water collects in pockets, runs through the architecture, and sinks into the root world. Pools remain in courtyards and collapsed chambers. Some last for months. Others vanish while still reflecting the sky.

At dusk the city does not light itself. There is no phosphorescent substitute for lamps, no concession to habitation. The ordinary sun lowers, turns the high crowns copper, and leaves the streets in green-black shadow. Structures continue to form in darkness. Their growth can be followed by touch through the ground and by the immense noises of their making.

By morning, the map is obsolete.

There are new towers on the southern horizon. A familiar avenue has narrowed to a vein. A reservoir has become a hanging garden. A mile of reed buildings lies flattened beneath an advancing sheet of glossy leaves. Somewhere in the basin, with a noise like a continent splitting, an unopened bud the size of a town is beginning to unfold.

And beneath every district, under every plaza, root stair, canal, hollow tower, and temporary ruin, the same living substrate continues without division: not a collection of plants, perhaps, but one plant attempting every possible landscape at once.
