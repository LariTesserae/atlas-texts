The place lies just beneath the surface, though it feels much deeper. A ceiling of compacted red earth hangs thirty or forty meters overhead, held up by broad pillars of stone that have been rounded by ages of heat and condensation. Roots penetrate the ceiling in dense black curtains. Most end in the air, their tips swollen and wet; a few descend all the way to the floor and fuse with it, becoming woody columns among the stone ones.

The chamber is roughly the size of a cathedral close. Its boundaries are always visible in principle, but rarely all at once. Steam, hanging vegetation, and ranks of obsolete machinery divide it into aisles and rooms. The ground slopes toward a central channel no wider than a road. The channel is usually dry. Its bed has been polished into shallow flutes, evidence of brief, violent floods that arrive from somewhere beyond the sealed walls and vanish within hours.

Heat saturates everything. Metal handrails are painful to touch. The stone itself seems feverish. Breath comes thickly, flavored with iron, tannin, and the bitter mineral smell of overheated rain. Nothing dries completely, yet there is almost no free-flowing water. Moisture survives in hollows: warm pools caught in corroded housings, beads trembling beneath leaves, black seepage gathered at the foot of the pillars. Condensation forms continuously on the cooler ceiling, swells into drops, and falls at irregular intervals. Each drop strikes with startling weight.

Vegetation crowds the chamber without making it green in any ordinary sense. Pale, broad leaves overlap like tiles, their surfaces waxy and dusted with rust. Rope-thick creepers lace between pipes and roots. Low thickets grow in the damp seams of the floor, made of jointed stalks whose translucent skins reveal slow columns of amber sap. Near the hottest machinery stand dark fans of foliage, almost black, opening toward heat rather than light.

There are no flowers. There are no fruits, seed husks, nests, tracks, droppings, or gnawed edges. No insects turn in the humid air. No worms disturb the silt after a flood. The leaves remain whole until age and mineral deposits make them too heavy to hold themselves up. Then they collapse and are gradually absorbed into a dense peat that smells faintly of hot tea.

The absence of animals changes the character of the growth. Nothing browses, pollinates, disperses, or trims it. The plants propagate by contact. A leaf pressed against stone for long enough produces a web of white filaments; a stem touching iron thickens around it and sends up another shoot. The entire grove may be one organism advancing by increments too small to watch. Its boundaries correspond not to soil, but to temperature. Where the floor falls below a certain heat, the vegetation stops in a clean, wavering line.

Scattered through this growth are the remnants of an industrial station. Four cylindrical pumps stand along the dry channel, each larger than a person and enclosed in cages of green-black brass. Their inspection plates bear numbers worn nearly smooth. Ceramic cables vanish into the walls. Narrow service platforms, some intact and some folded downward by root pressure, connect the pumps to a control room built into one of the stone pillars.

The control room still has windows, though mineral bloom has made the glass opaque. Its door remains locked. Through a crack in the frame, a bank of gauges can be glimpsed, their needles trembling around values whose units are no longer legible. A single red lamp burns inside. It dims whenever the chamber receives rain and brightens during the driest heat.

The pumps are responsible for much of the noise. They do not run in the ordinary sense. No shafts turn, no pistons visibly rise. Yet each machine produces a continuous, immense tone, low enough to be felt in the ribs. The four tones interfere with one another, filling certain parts of the chamber with a punishing metallic roar and leaving adjacent pockets uncannily quiet. Walking through the grove means passing in and out of walls of sound.

Other noises emerge from the resonance. Pipes knock behind the stone. Loose grates chatter. Tensioned roots hum like cables. From deep beneath the central channel comes a measured concussion, one impact every few minutes, as if a vast engine were operating at the slowest possible speed. It has maintained the same rhythm for so long that the rock above it has eroded into concentric scallops.

This is an old place, but not a ruin in the usual sense. Decay has reached equilibrium. Rust has thickened into protective shells. Leaks have sealed themselves with mineral deposits. Fallen masonry has settled into buttresses that now support what remains. The machines and the grove have adapted to each other’s pressure. Vines brace pipework; pipework carries condensation to root mats. Brass cages prevent woody columns from spreading, while those same columns keep the cages upright.

Nothing here changes quickly except the floods.

Before a flood, the machinery’s tones draw closer together. Condensation ceases. The leaves turn their pale undersides toward the channel, and hairline cracks open in the floor. Then hot water arrives without warning, not pouring from an inlet but lifting through the stone itself. It fills the polished channel, spills through the undergrowth, and rises perhaps to the height of a person’s knee. The water is opaque with red clay and carries no fish, larvae, shells, or organic debris. For several hours the chamber becomes a shallow river garden. Leaves float but do not detach. The pumps grow silent.

As the water drains, it leaves thin terraces of silt around every obstacle. Over centuries these deposits have softened the chamber’s geometry. Stair edges are rounded. Bolt heads sit in smooth collars of clay. The bases of the pillars spread outward like the trunks of ancient trees. Even footprints, if made, do not remain distinct; in time they become shallow, oval basins indistinguishable from natural erosion.

The dim light has no obvious source. It filters from mineral seams in the ceiling and from cloudy glass tubes bolted to the machinery. The tubes are not bright enough to illuminate the chamber so much as to give darkness a color: bruised orange near the pumps, smoky violet among the roots, dull green over standing water. Shadows gather densely beneath the leaves. Sometimes they appear to drift against the direction of the light, though this may be an effect of steam crossing multiple weak glows.

There is one local rule that becomes apparent only after waiting. Nothing falls directly to the ground.

Drops strike leaves, leaves catch on vines, rust flakes lodge in root fibers, and fragments of ceiling settle onto broad fans of foliage. The grove intercepts every descent. Debris passes downward by stages, sometimes taking days to travel a few meters. A fallen object may be heard repeatedly—tap, pause, scrape, pause, tap—long after it leaves sight. By the time it reaches the floor, it is wet, warm, and wrapped in fine white roots.

At the far end of the chamber, behind the fourth pump, a narrow passage rises toward the surface. It has been blocked by a mass of roots shaped uncannily like poured wax. Warm daylight occasionally leaks through tiny gaps, accompanied by the smell of sun-baked dust. No breeze enters. The roots tighten when the outside cools and relax during the fiercest part of the day, but never enough to open a way through.

Beside this blocked passage stands the smallest human-made object in the chamber: a metal chair fixed to the floor, facing the grove. Its surface has been kept clear of vines by some property of the alloy or by an old field still active within it. The seat is hot and dry. From there, the whole place resolves into a single system—the pumps sounding into the roots, the roots lifting water toward the ceiling, the ceiling returning it drop by drop, the channel waiting for its brief red floods.

It is difficult to decide whether the station was built to manage the underground grove or whether the grove arose to manage the station. Both appear ancient enough to have forgotten the distinction.
