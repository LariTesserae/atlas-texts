### The City in the Summit Seam

The mountain is young enough to remain unfinished.

Its uppermost peak is a black, splintered needle thrust far above the weather, higher than breathable air and higher than most clouds. The flanks have not yet softened into slopes. They fall in facets: obsidian planes, pale metallic scars, shelves of pumice fused into hard foam. Whole ridges end in edges thin enough to ring when struck by blown grit. There are few gullies, no soil, no lichen, no roots insinuating themselves into fractures. Erosion has only begun to test the place.

From a distance the summit appears solid. The city cannot be seen.

It occupies a seam near the needle’s tip, a vertical fissure no wider at its entrance than a handspan. The opening runs for several hundred meters along the rock, but resembles an ordinary cooling crack. Inside, however, the mountain unfolds.

The seam opens into a succession of tall, hollow chambers nested one within another. Their dimensions are deceptive. Some seem no larger than rooms until a drifting bead of water takes minutes to cross them. Others are visibly immense yet can be walked in a few steps. Distance here is not stable. The city’s engineers used the extreme elevation as a raw material, folding altitude into enclosed space. A narrow stair can climb through three meters of stone and emerge six kilometers higher in gravitational potential. A corridor may remain level to the eye while every object within it becomes steadily lighter.

The settlement is urban in organization but almost empty in presence. Streets, conduits, towers, reservoirs, transit loops, and public chambers fill the seam, yet they are separated by broad vacancies. A plaza may contain one black pedestal and nothing else. A station has a platform, a luminous timetable, and no arriving passengers. Apartment structures rise as thin frames around unoccupied air. There are no furnishings, no remains, no graffiti, no evidence of evacuation. The city feels neither abandoned nor inhabited. It seems to have been built for intervals rather than bodies.

Most surfaces are made from the mountain itself: volcanic glass tensioned into arches, basalt drawn into fibers, translucent stone laminated in sheets finer than paper. Other materials cannot be easily named. Some resemble smoke held under pressure. Some are dark only when looked at directly and shine in peripheral vision. Structural members pass through one another without joints, occupying the same volume while carrying loads in different directions.

Everything is hot.

The summit stands in freezing upper air, but the seam retains the heat of its formation. Walls exhale warmth from deep magma chambers. Floors range from pleasantly warm to blistering. In the lower galleries, metal fittings glow a dull red beneath crusts of black mineral. The heat is dry for long periods, then abruptly wet.

Moisture comes intermittently. Storm clouds strike the mountain far below, and the city’s buried infrastructure combs water from them. Vapor is drawn upward through capillaries in the rock. For hours there may be nothing; then every wall pearls at once. Condensation gathers on ceilings and hangs there in fat, trembling lenses.

The droplets do not always fall downward.

Altitude has been engineered throughout the seam, and gravity follows the architecture. Water slides along invisible gradients, climbs pillars, pauses in doorways, or streams sideways across open shafts. It collects in small, isolated basins set into plazas and maintenance alcoves. These pools are never deep. Their surfaces are black in the dimness, disturbed by slow concentric pulses from machinery beneath.

During a condensation cycle, the city becomes briefly reflective. Roads shine. Glass walls turn transparent. Fine channels engraved into the floors fill with water and reveal diagrams too large to understand from any single chamber. Then the heat returns in force. The water boils away without bubbling, vanishing in clean layers and leaving pale mineral outlines like maps of vanished coasts.

There is no plant life. Nothing greens the channels or clouds the reservoirs. No spores drift in the damp, no microbial films soften the stone. The water remains chemically bare except for metals and silicates gathered during its passage through the peak. Whatever sterilizes it is embedded in the city’s design.

There are no animals either—not even the specialized scavengers one might expect in an old ruin. No wings beat in the shafts. No insects gather around the low lights. No bones lie in corners. The absence is so complete that movement acquires disproportionate significance: a rolling droplet, a flexing bridge, a mote of volcanic dust crossing a beam.

Yet the place is active.

Panels rearrange themselves after changes in temperature. Streets narrow during dry periods and widen when the walls become wet. Towers slowly exchange positions, passing through the folded interior along routes that are not spatial in the usual sense. Hair-thin machines crawl inside the stone, repairing new fractures before they can branch. They are not visible as entities; one detects them by the traveling sheen they produce, or by neat rows of freshly sealed pinholes.

At irregular intervals the entire city adjusts its elevation.

There is no violent motion. Instead, pressure changes in the ears. The body feels suddenly heavy, then nearly absent. A hanging drop elongates into a silver thread. Far below, the mountain gives a deep internal click, and one district is translated a few meters upward without crossing the intervening rock. The summit is still rising geologically, driven by a column of unusually buoyant magma, and the city continually edits itself to remain at a precise relationship with the planet’s curvature.

That relationship may be its purpose.

At the highest chamber, just beneath the mountain’s point, stands an apparatus called—if the surviving symbols are interpreted literally—the Horizon Well. It is a circular opening less than a meter across, bordered by concentric rings of dark alloy. Looking into it does not reveal the interior of the mountain. It reveals distance: the curve of the world, the far side of storms, dawn several hours before it reaches the peak. Sometimes it shows stars despite the chamber’s ceiling. Sometimes it shows the summit from outside, with no observer present.

The Well draws power from remoteness. The greater the uninterrupted distance around the mountain, the more strongly its rings turn. Its builders appear to have treated isolation, height, and emptiness as extractable forces, no less physical than heat or magnetism.

Because of this, the city protects its vacancy.

Objects left in broad chambers are gradually moved to their edges. Dust is gathered into compact beads and expelled through slits in the outer rock. Water is permitted only in pockets and cycles, never as a continuous abundance. Any accumulation large enough to make a room feel occupied is dismantled, dispersed, or folded into storage spaces with no measurable interior. Emptiness here is infrastructure.

Light is similarly rationed. There are no lamps in the ordinary sense. A low violet-gray illumination leaks from stress lines in the architecture. Corners remain deep and uncertain. When moisture gathers, droplets catch the glow and become the brightest things in the city. During the hottest dry phase, even this light fades, leaving only the red suggestion of buried heat and the occasional cold flash of a structural calculation passing across a wall.

The quiet is nearly total.

One hears the soft tick of glass cooling, though nothing is cool. A minute hiss accompanies evaporation. Conduits sometimes produce a tone below hearing, felt in teeth and fingertips. During elevation shifts, the mountain makes its solitary click. The sound travels through every chamber at once without echo.

There is no wind inside, but air moves according to another cycle. It is pulled down from the summit entrance, heated, stripped of moisture, and released through pores along the outer cliffs. On certain days this creates a faint plume above the peak. From the lowlands it might be mistaken for volcanic smoke. In truth it is the city breathing without lungs.

The newest chambers are sharp and clean. Their surfaces still carry ripples from rapid formation, as though the stone froze while flowing. Some streets terminate at raw walls behind which construction continues invisibly. Doorways appear before rooms exist. Bridges extend into black gaps, waiting for future districts to arrive.

No one knows whether the builders are gone. The city’s systems may themselves be the builders, and the empty towers merely tools arranged on an urban scale. The condensation cycles may be manufacturing stages. The moving districts may be thoughts too slow and architectural to recognize. Even the mountain’s ongoing rise may not be natural; its magma column could be the deepest component of the machine.

At long intervals, the Horizon Well brightens. Every loose droplet in the city draws inward, not toward the chamber but toward the idea of distance contained within it. The pools become concave. The violet seams in the walls go dark. For several seconds, the entire settlement seems to hang above an immeasurable depth.

Then the water falls in whatever direction the city currently defines as down.

The rings turn once.

Far beneath the clouds, another mountain begins to rise.
