The plain is not earth. It only resembles earth because its makers ran out of other ideas.

Underfoot lies a pavement of fired slabs, each too large to cross in an hour, fitted edge to edge with seams of black pitch. The slabs are unevenly glazed: dull red where dry, liver-dark where rain has passed, and mirror-bright around the shallow depressions in which water gathers. There is no soil in the joints. When a seam opens, it reveals braided rope, ceramic pegs, wedges of bone-colored composite, and another pavement below. Beneath that is said to be another, and another—an entire planetary depth assembled by hand.

Nothing grows here. No moss softens the wet masonry, no reed marks the margins of the pools. The greenish films on sheltered walls are mineral condensates: copper salts blooming and dissolving in a single afternoon. Pale crusts sometimes rise in branching forms, but they are neither fungus nor coral. Touch makes them collapse into warm brine.

The land is low and almost level, yet it is not calm. Its horizons are too far away and slightly unreliable.

Across the plain stand scattered constructions, miles apart: staircases without buildings, kilns the size of hills, rows of empty cisterns, freestanding doorframes, and immense tripods made from bundled timbers sheathed in clay. The timbers have no grain. They were molded to imitate wood by artisans who had seen beams but never trees. Pegs, lashings, fitted stone, hammered copper, baked brick, woven cord—these are the highest technologies the place permits. There are no engines in the familiar sense, no circuits, no invisible networks. Yet the whole continent behaves like a machine.

Its mechanisms are simply too large to recognize at once.

A causeway may be a lever. A distant ridge may be the raised edge of a trapdoor. Reservoirs act as counterweights. Towers carry ropes thicker than city streets into the dimness above, where they vanish among low clouds. When enough rainwater fills one basin, some balance shifts beyond the horizon. Several hours later, a hundred-mile section of the plain tilts by half a degree. Pools begin sliding sideways. Doors swing open in deserted walls. Kiln chimneys inhale. Somewhere far off, a weight falls with a concussion felt through the soles.

Then the world starts rearranging itself.

The heat is constant but not radiant. There is no obvious sun. Light seeps through a ceiling of bronze-gray cloud, weak and diffuse, turning every wet surface into a muted reflection. The air is hot enough that exposed metal cannot be held for long, but the rain arrives lukewarm, almost body-temperature. It falls often and irregularly: broad curtains sweeping across the flatlands, isolated vertical columns, or huge drops spaced several seconds apart. Sometimes rain rises from the pavement instead, beads pulling free of puddles and ascending into the cloud layer.

Water is never abundant enough to make an ocean, though traces of an ocean are everywhere. Long retaining walls define empty shores. Mooring rings are set into dry plazas. Ceramic buoys rest in racks. Channels cross the continent in deliberate geometries, carrying ankle-deep currents from one reservoir to another. Most water remains close to the surface: caught in grout, sweating from walls, trembling in covered cisterns, condensing on the undersides of bridges. If a slab is broken, steam and droplets pulse from the hollow compartments beneath it.

The largest bodies of water are temporary. A depression may flood overnight, becoming a black inland sea under the dim clouds. Because there are no windswept grasses, insects, birds, or floating debris, its surface appears unnaturally pure. Then a row of stone plugs turns in sequence. By morning the sea has drained through square apertures, leaving immaculate wet steps descending into darkness.

The architecture has no consistent scale. A doorway three inches high may open in the base of a wall six miles long. A flight of steps begins broad enough for a mountain, narrows as it descends, and ends in a tread suited to a bare human foot. Some plazas are paved with bricks small enough to lift. Others are composed of single pieces that extend beyond sight. The transitions are seamless. Looking away and back may change which scale seems correct.

This is not illusion. The place has no stable distinction between model and structure.

A clay replica of a tower, left on a low altar, may become the actual tower during rain. The tower on the horizon shrinks correspondingly until it can be found beneath the altar’s overhang, still receiving weather from a thumb-sized cloud. Break the miniature and the distant structure fractures. Repair the distant structure with rope and mortar, and the replica acquires neat stitches of its own.

For this reason, the plain is crowded with evidence of maintenance but empty of maintainers.

Fresh mallet marks appear in wet copper. New cord is found cinched around cracked pillars, the fibers still damp from twisting. Clay ramps are built beside walls overnight. Handprints remain in plaster, though no creature passes through the landscape and no animal life exists even in the water. The prints vary wildly—five-fingered, seven-fingered, broad as plazas, fine as needles—but they are marks made by tools rather than bodies. Somewhere in the world, perhaps, there is a hand-shaped stamp striking walls. Somewhere else, a continent-sized chisel is slowly carving the idea of fingerprints into matter.

The terrain is raw despite its immeasurable extent. It has almost no history of erosion. Corners remain sharp. Steps are unworn. The channels carry water, but their floors show no scouring, and the rain polishes without diminishing. Cracks are clean, recent things. Rubble has bright edges. The world gives the impression not of an ancient ruin but of a construction site abandoned moments before its purpose became clear.

Sometimes it is still under construction.

At irregular intervals the pavement swells. Tiles lift in long waves, disclosing scaffolds below: forests of clay poles, rope knots, wooden wedges without wood, and enormous woven mats supporting the underside of the land. New walls are pushed upward already plastered. Empty towers emerge dripping from below, complete with stairs and doors. Elsewhere, a district sinks neatly into the pavement and is sealed over by molten glaze. Destruction and creation are abrupt but not violent in the usual way. There is little grinding down. Things cease to be assembled in one arrangement and begin to be assembled in another.

During these changes, the rules of distance loosen.

A traveler walking toward a cistern may arrive at the back of their own starting place. Two towers fifty miles apart may cast the same shadow. A bridge may cross no visible gap yet require three days to traverse; another spans a black chasm in four steps, although objects dropped from its edge fall for hours. Sound obeys the architecture rather than the air. A whisper spoken beneath one arch may emerge from another arch beyond the horizon. Thunder can become trapped in sealed rooms and continue there for years, faintly rattling the doors.

Most of the time, however, the plain is quiet.

There is the tick of cooling glaze, the soft patter of condensation, the occasional plunk of a drop falling into a deep ceramic vessel. Ropes creak high in the clouds. Water gurgles beneath the fitted slabs. Far away, often too far for the sound to be possible, workers’ tools seem to strike in slow rhythms:

Three hammer blows.

A pause lasting several minutes.

One scrape.

Then silence again.

No voices accompany the work.

Storms bring the landscape’s louder phase. The cloud ceiling lowers until it catches on towers. Static blue light crawls over copper caps, though there is no lightning. Rain runs uphill toward whichever structure has temporarily become the center of the world. Vast counterweights descend through shafts, forcing warm air out of hidden chambers. Doors across entire provinces open at once. The clouds themselves are drawn into chimneys and emerge hours later from wells as dense ropes of fog.

In the strongest storms, materials forget what they are. Brick softens into braided cord. Rope rings when struck like bronze. Water stacks itself into transparent masonry. Shadows become load-bearing and are propped up with poles. A wall may briefly be constructed from the sound of rain on tiles; when the rain stops, the wall disappears, leaving the roof suspended over an empty line.

These transformations are dangerous only because they are literal. There is no malice here and perhaps no intention. The world follows craft logic: things may be cut, joined, folded, fired, weighted, lashed, balanced, or nested. What cannot be made by one of these actions cannot exist for long. Flame endures because it belongs to the kiln. Water endures because it can fill a vessel. Air endures uncertainly, treated as an invisible fabric stretched between walls.

Open space is therefore a material in its own right. The great emptiness between structures is not neglected ground. It has been measured, leveled, and installed. In places, one can see its joinery: faint vertical seams in the atmosphere, or square patches where the dimness has been replaced. During hot rain, distances buckle along these seams. A far-off staircase appears close enough to touch, while the pavement beneath one’s feet seems to recede several miles downward.

There are no inhabitants, but there are customs.

Never place one vessel inside another during rain; the smaller may become the sky.

Never tie a knot with an odd number of crossings; roads nearby will imitate it.

Do not shelter under an unfinished arch. An arch defines passage, and the world may decide you are what must pass through.

If water begins flowing in straight lines across level stone, stand still. The continent is being measured.

And if hammering comes from beneath a slab, do not answer by striking back. The builders, whatever they are, take replies as instructions.

Beyond the nearest horizon, the plain continues into provinces of kilns, flooded courtyards, pulley-fields, unfinished domes, and miles of bare scaffolding. Farther still, according to the geometry of drainage channels, all these works converge on a central basin. Yet maps of the basin show only another pavement, another set of sparse towers, another hot and dim horizon.

Perhaps there is no center. Perhaps the entire made world is a tool laid upon a workbench larger than itself.

Or perhaps it is the object being made—still wet, still shifting under invisible hands, not old enough for erosion, not finished enough for life.
