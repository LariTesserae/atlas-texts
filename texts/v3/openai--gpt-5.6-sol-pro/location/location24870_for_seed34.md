The city occupies a plateau so broad that its far districts vanish into weather.

It stands high above the surrounding country, though there is little sense of a summit. The land rises for many days through folded foothills and long shelves of exposed stone, then opens into this immense, shallow basin under a cool sky. From within it, the plateau seems less like a mountaintop than a separate low world: level horizons, distant rain, clouds dragging gray hems across the ground. Only at the outer escarpments does the elevation become apparent. There the earth ends in weathered cliffs, and streams leave the city in thin white falls, descending out of sight.

Water is everywhere, but seldom deep. It lies close beneath the paving and shines through cracks in the stone. It fills old courtyards ankle-deep, threads along gutters, gathers in stairwells, and spreads into reedless marshes wherever the ground has subsided. Rain comes frequently—not violently, but in long, cool passages. Afterward every wall weeps. Droplets form under lintels and along the undersides of arches; terraces darken almost to black; the air carries the mineral smell of wet dust.

The settlement is made almost entirely of local stone. Low buildings crowd one another in dense, continuous quarters, their walls sharing weight, their roofs stepping upward with the mild slopes. There are no mechanisms, no lamps, no glass, no metal fixtures, no turning wheels. Doorways are closed by slabs or left open. Water travels by gravity through shallow channels cut into streets and through older conduits hidden beneath them. Everything that moves here does so by falling, flowing, swelling, shrinking, or being carried by hand.

The construction is simple, but its extent is almost inconceivable.

Block after block crosses the plateau: roofless chambers, covered passages, plazas paved in fitted gray flags, retaining walls, reservoirs, narrow bridges over marshy depressions. Some districts remain orderly, each lane draining into the next. Others have softened into geology. Walls bow outward under centuries of saturation. Roofs have settled into low mounds. Streets dip beneath pools transparent enough to reveal paving stones, thresholds, and the pale lines of submerged steps. The city has been weathered for so long that damage no longer looks recent. Collapse has acquired equilibrium. Fallen blocks have wedged into stable slopes; channels have found permanent ways through ruins; the edges of broken masonry have become rounded and quiet.

Nothing here appears freshly destroyed. The city and its erosion have grown old together.

Vegetation is sparse despite the abundance of water. The plateau is cold, mineral-heavy, and scoured by wind. Thin moss stains the sheltered sides of walls in dull green and rust. Black lichens spread across parapets in maps of slow expansion. In the wettest hollows, wiry sedges rise in isolated clumps, never enough to hide the stone beneath them. A few low shrubs cling to cracks where silt has accumulated, their branches flattened eastward by prevailing weather. There are no forests, and almost no trees. One may see, at enormous intervals, a twisted mountain willow rooted in the shelter of a collapsed courtyard, scarcely taller than a person.

This scarcity makes the city feel more crowded rather than less. Stone presses close on stone. Passages overlap. Arcades open into other arcades; stairs descend beneath bridges and emerge beside cisterns. Rain occupies every seam. Mist occupies every hollow. The density is architectural and atmospheric, a multitude of empty rooms packed tightly beneath a vast sky.

There are signs that people still live here, though they are not easily found.

A doorway may have fresh rush matting laid across its threshold. A retaining wall may show pale new stones among the darkened old ones. In some courtyards, rainwater has been swept into clean basins, and small stacks of fuel—peat, dried roots, dung gathered from the upper grazing shelves—stand beneath projecting roofs. Smoke sometimes rises from vents at dawn, too thin to remain visible once the wind reaches it. The inhabitants keep to interior routes during wet weather, moving through joined houses and covered lanes. From a distance, no one seems present. Up close, the city reveals maintenance: cleared drains, reset paving, footprints preserved in silt.

The scale of the place has shaped its customs. No one claims to know the whole city. Districts are named for watersheds rather than monuments: the Long Runoff, the Three Basins, the Quarter Beneath the Red Cliff, the Streets That Dry First. Travel is measured by rooflines crossed and by the number of drainage divides passed. Because weather may isolate one part of the plateau while another remains clear, neighborhoods store food separately and recognize different seasons within the same week.

There are few animals. Small slate-colored birds arrive after rain to search the exposed mud, calling only once or twice before lifting away. Pale frogs survive in the warmer cisterns, though entire districts may go years without hearing one. In high, abandoned rooms there are occasional droppings from bats or cliff hares. Larger creatures leave no more than ambiguous tracks at the city’s margins. A fox might cross a flooded square at night. A herd of lean mountain animals may appear against the western skyline, then disappear into fog before anyone can count them.

The quiet is profound without being absolute.

Water makes most of the sound: the close ticking of drops in chambers, the whisper of runoff over paving, the soft gulp of a drain taking air. Wind passes over the flat roofs with a low, continuous pressure but rarely whistles; the openings are too broad and worn for that. From beneath certain streets comes the muffled movement of underground channels. Stones sometimes shift after prolonged rain, producing a single blunt knock that travels through several adjoining walls.

Human sound is restrained by distance and masonry. A footstep can remain trapped in one passage. Voices do not carry far unless spoken over a reservoir. Bells have never been used here, and there are no engines, carts, or animals in numbers sufficient to make a sustained noise. When work is done, it is the scrape of stone against stone, the pat of clay into a joint, the rhythmic brush of a branch broom directing water downhill.

Yet the place is active everywhere.

Clouds build along the escarpment and cross the plateau in bands. Pools fill, join, and separate. Saturated ground presses against old foundations. Channels clog with grit and are opened again. Frost enters cracks at night, loosening grains that the next rain carries away. Mist condenses on walls even when the sky is clear. On brighter afternoons, whole districts steam faintly as the stone releases stored water. The marsh margins advance into streets during wet years and withdraw during dry ones, leaving delicate terraces of silt.

The city’s most important law is not written: water must not be prevented from leaving.

A person may divert it, slow it, collect it, or guide it beneath a dwelling, but no one may seal a common channel. Walls are built with deliberate gaps at their bases. Courtyards are graded toward public runnels. Every household downstream is understood to have a claim on the flow, and every household upstream bears responsibility for what enters it. Disputes concern mud, collapsed culverts, and the quiet theft of clean rainwater. Boundaries are marked not by fences but by drainage lines.

In the central basin, where the plateau dips lowest, the city gives way to a broad, shallow wetland. Thousands of structures surround it in ascending rings. The water is rarely more than waist-deep, and in clear weather the foundations of older streets can be seen beneath the surface, crossing one another like faded threads. Causeways extend toward the middle but stop without meeting. They were built in different centuries, from different shores, each following an earlier shoreline.

On calm days the basin reflects an ordinary blue-gray sky. Nothing supernatural rises from it. It is merely water lying where water has always wished to lie. But its size alters the weather: fog forms over it before dawn, rain lingers along its northern shore, and cool wind pours outward through the streets after sunset. The city has, in effect, built itself around a small inland climate.

Toward evening, light returns briefly beneath the cloud layer. Wet walls brighten to silver. Every pool takes on the pale color of the horizon, and the vast settlement becomes legible in fragments: stepped roofs, dark apertures, long watercourses, smoke from a handful of inhabited chambers, the distant gleam of falls at the edge of the world.

Then the temperature drops. Condensation gathers. The stone darkens again.

Across the plateau, unseen people open channels for the night’s rain.
