Below the surface lies a town no wider than a railway district, enclosed in a single warm cavern. Its boundaries are unmistakable. To the east, the floor drops into a fissure breathing mineral heat. To the west, roots have compacted into an impassable wall. At either end, old tunnels narrow until even insects must go through sideways. The whole settlement could be crossed in an hour if its streets remained still.

They do not.

The cavern is called the Underleaf, though there are no true leaves here. Vegetation fills nearly all available matter. The ground is not soil but a deep, resilient felt of rhizomes: pale strands near the surface, black woody cables below. Walls are wrapped in ribbed cambium. The ceiling hangs with translucent root veils that slowly drink condensation and draw themselves up when touched. Fungi occur, but only as tenants. Even the molds grow in precise green geometries, disciplined by the vascular territories around them.

There is a great deal of open space, strangely enough. Plant life constitutes almost everything without crowding the chamber in the ordinary sense. A few immense organisms have claimed broad areas and keep them clear. Their trunks rise like isolated buildings; their spreading roots form floors, ramps, bridges, and retaining walls. Between them are dim vaults of warm air. The settlement occupies those vaults as a town might occupy the gaps between cliffs.

Its structures were once built from brick, riveted iron, ceramic conduit, and poured aggregate. Centuries of pressure and sap have altered them. Brickwork survives as rows of inclusions inside living trunks. Iron beams pass through wood and emerge elsewhere, orange with corrosion but still bearing weight. Pipes have been adopted by vines whose hollow stems now continue the plumbing beyond every broken joint. Some streets are broad roots planed flat by generations of traffic. Others are old factory catwalks lifted from the ground and held aloft in the forks of plants.

Nothing looks newly ruined. Nothing looks newly grown. Every deformation has had time to become structural.

At the center stands the pumping house, the loudest object in the cavern. It is a low industrial complex built around a cluster of ancient piston engines. Their flywheels turn slowly, each taller than a dwelling. Belts vanish into apertures overgrown with glossy bark. Reciprocating rods drive down into the root floor, where they compress porous bulbs and force water through the town. The machinery does not merely serve the vegetation, nor does the vegetation merely infest the machinery. The two have become one process.

When the engines exhale, valves open in the ceiling vines. Condensed water falls in a brief local rain.

The rain lasts perhaps seven minutes. It strikes metal roofs, broad leaves, shell backs, and the stretched membranes of water cisterns. Then it stops. Channels cut into roots carry the runoff toward scattered basins: dark pools no larger than courtyards, each crowded at its margins and almost empty at its center. Within an hour, most exposed surfaces are dry again, though the air remains damp and warm. Moisture persists in seams, in pipe elbows, under scales, and in the folded throats of plants.

Water here is not a landscape. It is an event.

The town’s lamps are living pressure-globes mounted high on iron poles. Each contains a colony of luminous plant cells fed by capillary tubes. They produce a low amber-green radiance, insufficient to illuminate the cavern ceiling. Workshops add furnace glow, inspection lamps, and occasional blue arcs from old switchgear. Beyond these islands, the open spaces are obscure. Large forms are inferred from their breathing, their creaking, and the interruption of distant lights.

The fauna is overwhelming.

It does not always appear so at first. One might enter the Underleaf and see only a broad root plaza, three iron towers, and a cistern shining under its lamp. Then the eye adjusts. The plaza is moving grain by grain.

Small armored grazers travel over the root skin in dense rivers, polishing it with their mouthparts. Mothlike animals roost on cables in overlapping thousands, each with transparent wings veined like leaf tissue. Tube-worms occupy abandoned bolt holes. Beetles nest inside switchboxes, sorting flakes of copper by size. Pale, long-legged things hang beneath bridges and drink vibration through their feet. Every pipe contains swimmers or crawlers. Every hanging root veil bears translucent larvae. The apparent bark on a nearby column may turn out to be a resting congregation that disperses all at once.

Larger animals are fewer but impossible to ignore. Six-legged draft beasts move freight through the streets, their broad backs planted with harness gardens. They browse as they walk, trimming the edges of roads. Blind tunnel hounds lie beside boiler doors for warmth and wake whenever pressure changes underground. In the cisterns live heavy, air-breathing fish whose whiskers extend into drain channels. They sense rainfall before it begins and beat the water with their tails, a signal repeated across the town from pool to pool.

Predators are everywhere because prey is everywhere. Needle-mouthed gliders launch from the root veils. Trap flowers close not only on animals but around sound, orienting themselves toward repeated footfalls before striking. A narrow-bodied hunter called the spindle lives in idle machinery and imitates the click of cooling metal. When insects approach the familiar sound, the spindle unfolds.

There is no silence in the Underleaf. The pumps supply the bass rhythm: a slow concussion followed by the hiss of displaced air. Above that runs the clatter of chain conveyors, the shriek of bearings, the slap of belts, and the knocking of water through imperfect valves. Animals add a continuous particulate roar—wingbeats, scraping jaws, warning rattles, mating drums, shell against iron. Roots strain audibly against masonry. Seedpods rupture like distant pistol shots. During the artificial rain, every other sound becomes momentarily indistinct beneath the hammering of drops.

The inhabitants have adapted their speech to the noise. Conversations are conducted close to the face, with fingers pressed to the other person’s wrist or throat. Public messages are tapped into the root floor by weighted machines. The living streets carry these impacts farther and more accurately than air carries a voice. Each district has its own rhythm. A three-beat pause followed by two rapid strokes means rain is due. A dragging pulse means a large predator has entered a transit vault. A steady series of evenly spaced blows announces that the pumps are changing phase and all unsecured pipework should be avoided.

The town is organized, though it rarely appears orderly.

Homes are clustered in hollow buttresses, entered through doors cut into old inspection plates. Markets assemble on elevated grates where the crawling swarms are less dense. Workshops occupy the dry lee sides of trunks. The warm fissure supplies heat to kilns, digesters, and seed-oil refineries. Fibers are combed, steamed, wound, and woven by machines whose gears are lubricated with wax. Plant resins are distilled into sealants and fuel. Empty husks are pressed into panels. Insect shell is ground for abrasive powder. Nothing is discarded onto the root floor unless one intends it to be eaten immediately.

There are factories, but none can expand freely. Every industrial act is also an ecological negotiation. Heat attracts hatching colonies. Noise wakes burrowing animals. A new conduit may be crushed within weeks if its vibration irritates the supporting root. Smoke is dangerous not because it poisons the cavern—most of it is absorbed—but because certain ceiling plants grow rapidly toward carbon-rich exhaust and can engulf a chimney overnight.

Consequently, machinery is tended almost ceremonially. Workers know which engines tolerate moss around their housings and which must be kept bare. They cultivate sacrificial vines near moving belts so that other growth will not approach. At shift changes they release small grazing animals into stopped gear trains. The grazers remove soft tissue, mineral scale, and insect eggs, then return by following the heat of lanterns.

Decay is slow but universal. Iron has been thinned into lace. Stone steps bow between roots. Entire walls have dissolved, leaving only rectangular impressions in bark. Yet erosion here is not collapse so much as editing. The cavern has worked upon the settlement for so long that weak arrangements have already failed. What remains is weathered into equilibrium: pillars shaped around pressure, roads positioned where roots permit them, roofs steep enough to shed both water and animals.

Even accidents tend to become institutions.

A burst water main from some forgotten century formed the north cistern. A derailed ore wagon became the shell of a clinic. A collapsed smokestack now channels migrating beetles between breeding vaults. One factory was abandoned when a massive flowering body grew through its boiler room; the flower opens once every nine years, and the old gantries are retained so that townspeople can harvest the oil from its pollen. No one remembers what the factory originally made.

The Underleaf has seasons, but they are mechanical and biological rather than astronomical.

There is the Condensing, when cooler rock draws water from the air and the ceiling veils become heavy with droplets. There is the Running, when the pumping house works at full stroke and brief rains pass from district to district. There is the Hatching, during which every lamp must be hooded and every open vat covered. There is the Root Turn, when the great floor organisms alter their internal pressure. Streets tilt by degrees. Doorways cease to meet their thresholds. Bridges must be loosened at one end and tightened at the other.

During Root Turn, the entire town creaks like a ship.

The strangest law of the place concerns stillness. Nothing unattended may remain inert for long. A silent machine is colonized. A vacant room becomes a nursery. A closed passage is filled with exploratory roots. An unused tool acquires eggs in its handle and lichen in its teeth. Ownership is therefore demonstrated by rhythm: doors must open, wheels must turn, floors must be walked, bells must be struck. To preserve a thing, one has to keep it participating.

This makes abandonment swift and maintenance perpetual. It also gives the town its peculiar vitality. Everywhere, somebody or something is testing, trimming, pumping, feeding, carrying, molting, germinating, hunting, repairing, or being repaired.

Beyond the last inhabited vault, the lamps thin out. The root streets descend into warm darkness. Industrial noises continue through the wood long after the machinery can no longer be heard directly, transformed into low pulses by the living substrate. Animals follow those pulses. So do the roots.

No one is certain whether the cavern contains the town, or whether the town is merely a temporary open chamber inside one immense plant. The oldest surveyors drilled downward through the floor and found wood for the full length of their instruments. They drilled upward and found the same. They tried the western wall and brought back sap under pressure, warm as blood.

The boreholes were sealed, not from fear of flooding, but because something on the other side had begun tapping back.
