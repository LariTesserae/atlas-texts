The high country is crowded almost beyond passage.

From a distance it appears to be a pale, level tableland, bright under an immense white sky. Only on approach does the surface resolve into millions of separate mineral forms: blunt pillars, leaning plates, stone needles, nested bowls, ridges packed against ridges. There is no turf between them, no moss in the cracks, no dark trace of soil. Every available space is occupied by rock or water. The landscape is open in extent but dense in substance; it runs from horizon to horizon without ever offering a clear field.

The stone is mostly gray limestone veined with quartz, though weathering has given it many colors. Wet faces are blue-black. Sheltered hollows are the color of bone. Iron seeps leave fixed amber stains, and deposits around springs build smooth white lips like wax cooled in motion. Some outcrops have been dissolved into fluted columns so regular that they resemble bundled pipes, but there is nothing made here. The repetition belongs to water, pressure, and a very long equilibrium.

Water is everywhere without forming a single dominant river. It beads on the exposed stone, hangs beneath overhangs, lies in narrow basins, and moves through channels too tight to see. The air continually condenses against the cool rock. Every wall weeps. Thin films descend over slopes that look dry until the light catches them. Pools occupy the hollows between crowded pinnacles, fitted to the stone as precisely as pieces of dark glass.

Most of the water is below the surface. The tableland is hollowed through with flooded cavities, vertical throats, and chambers under pressure. Water enters these unseen spaces through thousands of fissures and is forced out again along the escarpments. There it emerges in dense ranks of short waterfalls. None is large, but there are so many that the whole plateau sounds like one continuous cataract.

The noise is deafening.

It does not rise and fall with weather. It has no approaching front and no receding echo. It is simply present: a massive, level roar generated by innumerable jets, submerged resonances, and water driven through stone tubes. In some places the sound is low enough to be felt through the soles, a pressure in the ribs. Elsewhere narrow clefts produce a piercing metallic shriek. Rounded cavities boom steadily. Fine channels hiss. All these tones overlap until distance becomes difficult to judge. A chasm ten paces away and a cliff several valleys distant may seem equally near.

There is almost no wind audible above it. Falling pebbles vanish into it. Even thunder, when it occurs, is only a brief thickening of the existing sound.

Yet the place gives no impression of violence. Nothing appears to be in the process of beginning or ending. The falls do not swell. The pools do not noticeably fill. No flood marks stain the stone, and no dry channels suggest another season. Water circulates through the plateau at nearly constant pressure, fed by condensation, deep aquifers, and the slow melting of buried ice that is itself renewed from vapor freezing in the high caverns. What is dissolved from one wall is deposited on another. Channels deepen by fractions while mineral rims thicken elsewhere by the same measure. Over any ordinary span of observation, the land does not change.

Even the loose stones seem settled. Angular blocks are wedged so tightly among the pinnacles that they cannot roll. Gravel fills fissures to their exact capacity. Pale scree lies at the angle it has held for ages, cemented by mineral crusts. There are no fresh scars. Erosion here is not decay but maintenance: the endless polishing of an already completed form.

The light is nearly excessive. At this elevation the atmosphere is thin and washed clean, and the wet mineral surfaces multiply the sun. Quartz veins flare. White deposits throw brightness upward into the undersides of ledges. Pools reflect pieces of sky with such intensity that they look like openings cut through the rock. There are few shadows broad enough to rest the eyes. Even beneath the densest stone formations, reflected light travels along wet walls and enters from below.

Mist gathers close to the ground, but it does not dim the plateau. Instead it becomes luminous, filling the narrow spaces with a cold silver radiance. Droplets remain suspended above the warmest vents and collect on every surface. A traveler would soon be soaked without encountering rain.

There is no plant life. Nothing roots, spreads leaves, powders the rocks with lichen, or softens an edge. No organic green appears anywhere, not even in protected cracks. The sterility makes the water look strangely pure and the rocks newly exposed, though both are ancient. There are no fallen trunks, seed husks, peat beds, or fossils visible in the stone. The place seems not ruined after life but overlooked by it.

A few animals nevertheless occur.

They are most often glimpsed as solitary movements against the fixed geometry: a pale, long-winged creature crossing the glare; a small six-legged form clinging beneath a dripping shelf; something translucent withdrawing into a pool when a shadow passes overhead. They are colorless or mineral-toned, difficult to separate from the surroundings. None feeds on vegetation. The pool dwellers graze invisible microbial films from submerged stone or strain minute particles brought up from the deep aquifer. The flying animals emerge from water-filled chambers, mate briefly in the bright air, and return below. Their numbers are so low that hours may pass without a sighting.

There are no nests and almost no calls. Sound would be useless here. The rare creatures sense pressure through water and vibration through stone. They become motionless when the pitch of the plateau changes, though to human hearing it never does.

Passage across the tableland is possible but indirect. The stone masses stand shoulder to shoulder, forcing movement into a network of narrow slots and wet saddles. One may see for many kilometers from the top of a pillar and still require half a day to travel a few hundred meters. Broad valleys exist, but they too are filled: with ribs of rock, stepped basins, jammed boulders, and parallel curtains of descending water. There is no empty center to the region. Every opening contains another formation.

Nothing marks a route. No wall has been cut, no stone stacked, no surface worn by repeated feet. Shapes that resemble stairs end in wells. Apparent doorways narrow into mineral seams. Long rows of columns suggest the ruins of halls until viewed from another angle, when they become the exposed edge of a folded stratum. The landscape repeatedly approaches architecture and then withdraws from it.

Its governing rule is continuity. Water must move, but the pattern of its movement must remain. Stone must dissolve, but the quantity and arrangement of stone must persist. Animals may appear, but never in enough numbers to alter the surfaces. The roar may contain countless separate sounds, but its total volume does not vary.

At the plateau’s edge, the crowded formations descend in terraces toward a lower cloud deck. Thousands of bright falls pour from the final cliffs and vanish into that cloud. Nothing can be seen beneath. Above, the sky is hard blue-white; below, white vapor; between them, the packed wet stone stretches away, shining and thunderous, holding itself in the same impossible instant.
