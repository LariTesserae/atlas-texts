The city lies below the country without being underground.

From the surrounding plain it first appears as a long irregular break in the earth, too broad to be a trench and too densely inhabited to be mistaken for a quarry. The rim runs for miles through pale grassland. Beyond it, the surface is almost level: cool wind, thin soil, scattered thorn scrub, no hills of consequence. Then the ground falls away in a succession of raw terraces, and an entire low city is revealed beneath the horizon.

It has been dug rather than raised.

The uppermost streets follow the cut edge in crowded rings. Below them descend retaining walls, stairways, ramps, work yards, houses, cisterns, kilns, and narrow public courts. The lowest districts occupy the floor of the excavation, where the air is cooler and moisture collects. Seen from above, roofs cover nearly everything. They press shoulder to shoulder in dun, gray, and chalk-white masses, interrupted only by shafts of roadway and the dark squares of reservoirs. There is little unused ground. Even unstable ledges hold sheds, pens, drying racks, or stacks of cut stone.

The walls expose the young anatomy of the place. Bands of clay, marl, gravel, and soft limestone stand almost untouched by weather. Their colors remain distinct: cream over rust, rust over blue-gray, blue-gray over seams of black silt. The cuts are angular. Tool marks have not yet softened. Spoil heaps keep the sharp slopes left by baskets tipped only yesterday, though some are generations old. There are no deep gullies, no polished ravines, no old watercourses. Rain has only begun to learn the city. It leaves tentative scratches on the ramps and little fans of mud at the foot of walls, erasing almost nothing.

Construction is continuous. At the eastern face, gangs cut new chambers with stone mauls, antler picks, wooden wedges, and simple copper-edged tools reserved for fine work. They listen for changes in the sound of the rock. The quarried blocks are passed hand to hand or dragged on sledges to nearby building sites; almost nothing travels far. Excavation produces houses, and houses support further excavation. A room may begin as a stone source, become a workshop, then acquire a façade, a chimney, and a family. The city expands by converting absence into shelter.

Most buildings are made from the same earth that surrounds them. Soft stone forms lower walls and vaults. Mud brick closes openings. Roofs are laid with poles, brush, reed mats, and packed clay, renewed after each season of rain. Timber is scarce and carefully reused. Door lintels may be older than the houses beneath them. Broken roof poles become handles; worn handles become pegs; pegs end as kindling. On the deeper terraces, rooms are partly carved into the face, their front walls built outward. Their roofs serve as lanes for the dwellings above, so that a person crossing the city often walks over kitchens, storerooms, sleeping chambers, and cool dark workshops without seeing them.

The density makes distance deceptive. A market visible across the basin may take an hour to reach, because there is no straight descent. Paths turn around cisterns, vanish through covered passages, climb ladders onto roofs, and thread between retaining walls. The broadest roads are scarcely wide enough for two loaded donkeys, and animals are uncommon in the lower city. Most burdens move on human backs. Heavy loads descend by rope from terrace to terrace, wrapped in hide or reed basketry.

There is daylight everywhere, though not evenly. The city is open to the sky, and its pale walls catch and return the cool light. At noon the lower basin can be brighter than the plain above, filled with reflection rather than direct sun. In the morning and evening, the terraces cast long geometric shadows. Alleys pass abruptly from clarity into blue dimness. Carved rooms receive light through doors, roof holes, and shallow shafts lined with white plaster. Small polished stone or metal plates are sometimes positioned to send a weak square of brightness deeper inside.

The climate is cool and damp only by degrees. Rain comes intermittently, in fine gray curtains that cross the plain and spill over the rim. Much of it never reaches the bottom directly. It strikes roofs, awnings, steps, and shoulders; it runs along plastered channels into shared tanks. Every surface participates in collection. Courtyards slope toward jars sunk into the floor. Parapets carry grooves. Cloth is hung overnight to catch condensation, then wrung out at dawn. In cold months, the cut walls sweat, darkening around mineral seams.

Open water is limited to deliberate pockets. The largest reservoirs lie in old extraction pits sealed with clay. Their surfaces are dark and usually still, disturbed by dipping jars and the occasional ring of an insect. Smaller basins occur wherever groundwater enters through a gravel layer. These are watched carefully. A seep may support a neighborhood, but it may also soften a wall. Dampness is valued, measured, diverted, and feared.

The lowest ground is not a riverbed, though during heavy rain it briefly resembles one. Stormwater gathers in the central lanes and moves sluggishly toward settling ponds at the western end. People lift doorsills into place, clear grates of straw, and guide the flow with boards and baskets of gravel. For a few hours the city smells of wet limestone, clay, soot, and old roofs. Then the water disappears into cisterns or sinks through unfinished ground, leaving puddles under stairs and a skin of pale silt across the paving.

Vegetation is sparse, but what survives is intensely tended. There are no broad gardens. Soil is too valuable and space too occupied. Herbs grow in chipped jars, roof troughs, and crevices where drains leak. Onions, bitter greens, lentils, and medicinal plants occupy narrow beds built against sunny walls. A few figs or cold-hardy fruit trees stand in the larger courtyards, twisted and pruned nearly flat against masonry. Their roots are guided into buried channels. Moss appears around permanent seeps, and hair-thin ferns shelter in shaded cracks, but most walls remain bare.

Beyond the rim, the plain supplies scrub for fuel and seasonal grazing. Within the city, vegetable matter is never casual. Straw becomes roofing, matting, fodder, plaster fiber, and finally cooking fuel mixed with dung. Fallen leaves are gathered before the wind can carry them away. Even weeds belong to someone.

Animals are correspondingly rare. A few donkeys serve the upper ramps. Goats are kept mostly outside the settlement, since they would eat every planted shoot they could reach. Pigeons nest in holes high on the excavation faces, tolerated for their droppings and occasionally trapped. Small pale lizards emerge on warmer stones. Beetles gather near grain stores, and mice persist despite sealed jars and patient household cats. There is little wild life in the crowded depths. The city has displaced it without entirely replacing it.

For such a packed place, it is quiet.

There are no engines, no wheeled traffic on the lower stairs, no continuous roar. Work is done by pressure, leverage, repetition, and many hands. The prevailing sounds are close and modest: the tap of a dressing stone, a rope creaking under load, sandals brushing grit, water poured into clay, a broom on a roof. Voices do not need to be raised. The cut walls carry them unexpectedly, so a conversation on one terrace may arrive intact at another while the speaker remains invisible.

At intervals a deeper knock travels through the basin as wedges split a block from the living face. Everyone recognizes the sound. If the note is wrong—too hollow, too prolonged—work pauses nearby. People look toward the wall and wait for falling dust. Structural knowledge is communal and practical. Fresh cracks are marked with soot. Bulging plaster is removed. Retaining walls are thickened from below. No one assumes the earth is finished merely because it held yesterday.

That is the city’s central condition: it is inhabited before it is complete.

Whole districts retain the provisional character of camps, although families have lived there for decades. A stair ends at an uncut ledge that will someday become another street. Doorways open onto chambers still scarred by extraction. Foundations project from roofs awaiting upper rooms. Public wells sit in courtyards whose final walls have not yet been agreed upon. Boundaries move as excavation reveals harder rock, bad clay, a buried gravel lens, or an unexpected seep.

The city’s rules have grown from these conditions. Water descending from one roof belongs partly to the household below. No one may block an old drainage line without providing another. Stone removed from beneath a neighboring wall must be replaced in strength, if not in material. Roof paths remain public during daylight, even when they cross private homes. Anyone hearing a dangerous shift in the rock must call out, regardless of hour or district. New rooms may be claimed by labor, but the retaining faces, ramps, and cisterns belong to the city as a whole.

At dusk, the plain above loses light first in the eyes of those below; the rim becomes a dark, clean line against the sky. Cooking fires appear terrace by terrace. Their smoke rises slowly through the cooler basin air, spreading into a thin blue layer before escaping. Lamps burn in wall niches, but many tasks continue by the remaining reflected light. Water jars are filled. Tools are counted. Damp cloth is hung where night condensation will touch it.

From the upper edge, the settlement then resembles a field of low stars sunk into raw earth. It does not feel ruined or ancient. Nothing has had time to become picturesque. Corners are too sharp, walls too recently plastered, spoil too fresh. The city is still arriving—block by block, chamber by chamber—inside a hollow of its own making.

And beneath the lowest floors, marked by exploratory shafts and the changing taste of seep water, more layers wait: firm stone, weak clay, gravel, perhaps another buried pocket of water. The inhabitants do not imagine an ultimate bottom. They speak instead of the next terrace, the next stable seam, the next season’s room.
