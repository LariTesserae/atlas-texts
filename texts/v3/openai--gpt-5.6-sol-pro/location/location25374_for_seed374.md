The city lies in a shallow basin only a little above the sea. There are no hills in the ordinary sense, only broad swells of pale sediment and low shelves of stone that rise waist-high before sinking again into water. Beyond the outer districts, the basin continues as a confusion of flats, warm pools, shell banks, and half-drowned causeways. The horizon is open but rarely clear. Vapor rests over it in a luminous gray lid, dimming the day without making it cool.

Nothing grows here.

There is no grass between the paving blocks, no moss on the walls, no reeds at the water’s edge. The absence is not dramatic at first. The city is too crowded with other things: masonry, animals, hanging nets, scaffolds, vats, tiled channels, awnings made from hide. Only gradually does the eye begin searching for green and fail to find it. Even the stains on wet stone are ocher, cream, rust, or black—films of bacteria fed by minerals rising from below.

Water is everywhere without ever becoming a single dominant body. It beads on ceilings and runs down stairwells. It lies finger-deep in courtyards. It moves through open gutters cut in polished limestone, fills square cisterns, and seeps between the fitted stones of the streets. The walls themselves weep. Porous blocks draw moisture from the saturated ground and release it in slow, regular droplets, so that every alley has its own small rain.

The basin is young stone. Warm springs emerge under it carrying dissolved lime, silica, sulfur, and iron. Where the water reaches the air, it lays down a thin mineral skin. Steps become fractionally taller. The rims of channels thicken. A rope left in a spillway acquires a white casing and, after long enough, resembles a carved branch despite there being no trees from which such a branch might have come.

Erosion has scarcely begun. Corners are sharp. Cut faces still show the parallel scratches of stone tools. The ground is continually being formed faster than weather can soften it. New terraces appear as mineral crust builds over wicker forms, shell heaps, discarded baskets, and retaining walls of stacked rubble. Much of the city seems not ruined or unfinished but embryonic: plazas with one side still a warm, steaming pool; streets whose paving vanishes beneath fresh chalky deposits; foundations marked by rows of stakes driven into mud that is already turning to stone.

The builders use little metal. Most work is done with basalt mauls, shell scrapers, bone punches, wooden levers, and wedges hardened in fire. Timber is precious because none grows in the basin. Beams arrive by boat from distant coasts and are used until mineral accretion petrifies their surfaces. More often, roofs are corbeled from thin slabs, domed in brick, or stretched from hide over ribs of whale bone. Rope is braided from gut, hair, and imported fiber. Hinges are leather. Doors slide in stone grooves.

The city is dense enough that no building can be understood alone. Houses lean into workshops; workshops bridge canals; public ovens are built over vents in the ground; animal pens occupy roofs and vaulted undercrofts. A wall may serve six households, a cistern twenty, and a stair an entire neighborhood. Alleys divide, rejoin, pass through rooms, descend into water, and emerge beneath arcades crusted with salt.

From above, the districts would resemble a field of low, pale nodules around dark pools. Yet there are few places from which to see the city above its roofs. Its towers are broad rather than tall, designed as catchments for condensation. Their upper surfaces are shallow stone funnels. Moisture gathers there throughout the dim day, trickling inward through ceramic pipes. The towers are crowded with nesting animals, and their rims are white with droppings and mineral scale.

The animals provide nearly all motion and much of the city’s wealth.

Birds nest by the tens of thousands in niches, vents, roof hollows, abandoned kilns, and purpose-built walls of small chambers. Gray sea birds crowd the outer basins. Black, long-winged insect hunters fill the covered streets. Heavy marsh fowl with naked heads stand in the warm drainage pools, stamping shellfish from the mud. Their eggs are gathered according to strict neighborhood allotments. Their feathers become bedding, insulation, fans, fletching, and ceremonial collars.

Bats occupy the cistern vaults in such numbers that the ceilings appear furred. At the same hour each dim evening they pour from openings over the canals, though the light changes so little that their departure seems to obey the stone rather than the sky.

The water supports dense populations of silver fish, mud-burrowing eels, translucent shrimp, snails, crabs, and soft pale worms. They feed on microbial films, mineral-rich detritus, one another, and the organic waste of the city. In the warmer pools, thick mats of cream-colored bacteria wrinkle like cooling fat. Workers skim these mats with shell blades, press them into cakes, and feed them to fish or ferment them in sealed jars. No one considers the mats vegetation. They do not leaf, flower, root, or reach toward the light. They are treated as a property of water, like salt.

On dry surfaces there are beetles, mites, lice, flies, and small lizards. Lean dogs patrol the lanes, trained to keep birds away from drying racks but not from the nesting towers. Pigs are raised in enclosed courts on shellfish refuse and pressed microbial meal. Cats sleep along warm pipes. Every ledge seems occupied, every crack inhabited, every pool stirred by mouths or fins.

The sound is overwhelming.

Water alone would make the city loud: dripping in chambers, hissing from vents, pouring from one basin to the next, slapping beneath stone quays. But the animals smother those sounds beneath a nearly continuous wall of cries. The nesting towers scream. Bats chatter within the vaults. Dogs bark from roofs. Pigs answer one another across courtyards. Fishers strike poles against boats to drive shoals into traps.

Human work adds its own fixed layers. Stone mauls ring against wedges. Shells are crushed in circular pits. Hide is beaten on blocks. Grain and dried imports are pounded in mortars. Potters call for space around the kilns. Market sellers shout not merely to attract customers but to be heard at arm’s length. Conversation is conducted close to the face, with hands on shoulders and fingers supplementing words. Many residents develop a low, carrying voice and an extraordinary ability to distinguish familiar rhythms within the uproar.

Silence is regarded with suspicion. A quiet cistern may mean poisoned water. A quiet nesting wall may mean a predator. A quiet workshop may mean injury. Even sleep takes place inside sound. Children are soothed by pounding, dripping, and the massed cries of birds; they wake when one of these stops.

Yet for all this activity, the basin does not seem to change.

The water levels remain almost constant. There are no visible tides. Springs rise at the same rate that channels carry them away. Mineral deposition thickens the city, but workers shave channels, open drains, and recut doorways at precisely the rate required to preserve their shapes. Animals die and are replaced in the same niches. Walls are dismantled for stone and rebuilt nearby in the same pattern. Boats arrive with wood, fiber, charcoal, and grain; boats depart with salt, shell lime, cured fish, hides, mineral pigments, and carved stone. The exchange is ceaseless, but the balance never noticeably shifts.

There are no true seasons. The air remains warm, wet, and dim. Rain falls often, but so gently and evenly that it does not scour the ground. Storms break somewhere beyond the vaporous horizon and arrive here only as a heavier condensation, a deepening of the roof-drip. The city’s oldest records do not speak of drought, flood, frost, or exceptional heat. They record quantities: water entering, water leaving, birds nesting, eggs taken, stone laid down, stone cut away. The figures recur with unsettling regularity.

This constancy shapes the city’s laws. No channel may be widened without another being narrowed. No nesting wall may be sealed unless equal space is opened elsewhere. Material removed from a spring mouth must be used within the same drainage district. Waste belongs to the downstream ward; clean condensate belongs to the roof on which it forms. Nothing is discarded beyond the basin because almost nothing is allowed to leave its cycle.

The people do not speak of permanence. They speak of keeping the measure.

At the outer edge, settlement thins but never ends abruptly. Houses become sheds, sheds become fishing platforms, and platforms become lines of stakes in shallow water. Causeways run toward distant pale islands where birds rise in columns into the gray. Warm water glimmers between fresh mineral ridges. There are pits where new stone is forming beneath stretched hides, and marked plots where future streets exist only as rows of shells pressed into mud.

Farther out, the basin looks almost empty. Up close, it is as crowded as the city: every pool alive, every crust perforated by burrows, every damp surface printed with tracks. The land has barely come into being, yet it is already occupied. The first smooth skins of stone settle around claws, pilings, pots, bones, and foundations, preserving them before wind or water has had any chance to wear them down.

Under the low sky, construction and fossilization are nearly the same act. The city is built, buried, and built again without becoming older in appearance. Its edges remain raw. Its streets remain wet. Its walls continue to bead with water.

And over all of it hangs the unbroken sound of life—dense, warm, deafening, and constant enough to seem less like noise than the natural pressure of the air.
