The city occupies a shallow basin no more than a few kilometers across. From the rim it appears less like a settlement than the exposed interior of a machine: low sheds, open conduits, settling tanks, tiled yards, and brick towers packed into the depression without ever quite crowding one another. Broad aprons of bare stone separate each structure. Rails cross these empty spaces in black parallel lines, polished by traffic that cannot be seen to move.

Everything is old enough to have lost its sharpness. Brick corners have been rounded by mineral rain. Iron stairs are cupped where innumerable feet have pressed them, though no footsteps occur. Concrete gutters have worn down into smooth channels. Rivets bloom with rust but do not weaken further. The city is neither decaying nor maintained. It has arrived at a final degree of weathering and remains there.

There are no plants. Not in cracks, not in drains, not as stains on roofs. The ground is a compacted mixture of cinder, gray clay, salt, pulverized shell, and metal filings. Along shaded walls, pale films spread in geometric fans; they might be fungal, bacterial, or merely chemical precipitates. They have no smell and never seem to grow. What passes for soil is only finer ruin.

Water comes and goes without changing anything. Cool condensation pearls continuously on overhead pipes, gathering at flanges and descending in fat, regular drops. Rain sometimes falls from a low ceiling of cloud, but always lightly, as though the sky is sweating. It darkens the brick and fills a chain of shallow cisterns along the basin floor. These pockets are clear at the edges and opaque in the center, crowded with suspended silver dust. By the next interval of dryness they are empty again, leaving no new mark. The river that once cut through the city has narrowed to a damp trench beneath gratings. Its sound is audible everywhere, but its water is visible only through inspection hatches.

The air is cool and heavy with iron, wet limestone, hot insulation, animal breath, and the faint alkaline odor of old batteries. Daylight is ordinary: a flat white illumination from the cloud cover, neither dramatic nor dim. Lamps remain on beneath it—yellow globes in cages, blue arcs inside workshops, red numerals in control windows. Shadows are soft except under the conveyor galleries, where they lie with the density of soot.

The animals are everywhere.

They are not hidden wildlife reclaiming abandoned streets. They are the city’s principal substance, more numerous than bolts or bricks. Every roof edge bears ranks of squat, charcoal-colored birds, packed breast to back in continuous rows. Every cistern contains pale eels wound into living cables. Beetle-like creatures the size of fists cling under handrails, their lacquered shells vibrating against the metal. In alleys, herds of narrow six-legged animals stand shoulder to shoulder, their gray hides indistinguishable from folded tarpaulins until one notices the slow blinking of thousands of lateral eyes.

Yet for all their number, the city remains spatially sparse. The creatures gather in absolute concentrations and leave broad zones entirely vacant. One yard may contain a million insects arranged in a dark disk while the next holds only an empty crane and a single length of chain. A warehouse wall may be feathered from foundation to cornice, while the roadway before it remains bare for hundreds of meters. The distribution feels deliberate, like typeset marks surrounded by generous margins.

Nothing feeds. There is nothing here to eat.

The animals do not prey upon one another, browse, scavenge, breed, sleep, rot, or visibly age. They perform industrial functions whose purpose is obscure but whose coordination is exact. The birds on the roofs open their beaks together and produce a sustained metallic chord. The cistern eels turn in place, generating current for submerged dynamos. Shell-backed insects hammer their abdomens against hollow beams, keeping pressure valves in oscillation. The gray herds brace themselves beneath belt housings, their rib cages rising and falling as pumps. Larger creatures occupy machine halls: enormous soft-bodied things seated in concrete cradles, each connected by porcelain terminals to a web of copper busbars. Their skin flashes faintly whenever the city’s lights pulse.

The machines, in turn, appear adapted to the animals rather than the reverse. Factory doors are shaped for antlers, carapaces, fins, or radial bodies. Staircases lead nowhere a human foot could comfortably go. Control levers are arranged in circular clusters around low wells. Ventilation ducts widen into nesting chambers lined with ceramic plates. Along the central avenue, loading cranes hold platforms full of silent, armor-plated quadrupeds; their backs fit the platforms so perfectly that animal and apparatus seem manufactured as a single component.

The loudness is immense and unceasing. It has layers.

Below everything is the continuous concussion of buried engines, a pressure felt through teeth and sternum. Above it comes the rasp of belts, the chatter of relay banks, the clang of loose plates, the hydraulic cough of unseen pistons, the deep pulse of the gray herds, the electric shriek of eels, and the roof-birds’ unwavering note. Small creatures add ticks, whistles, frictional hums, and sounds like sand poured onto glass. At regular intervals all these components coincide in a single overwhelming blast, causing water in the cisterns to rise in ridges. Then the layers separate again.

The noise suggests frantic activity, but nothing actually progresses. Flywheels turn without accelerating or slowing. Conveyors carry identical ingots from one building to another, where identical conveyors return them beneath the street. Presses descend upon blank metal plates and rise with the plates unchanged. Furnaces glow at a dull orange heat inconsistent with the cool air around them. Gauges tremble over the same values. A locomotive stands beneath the eastern gantry with steam issuing from its valves and its wheels blurred by rotation, yet it remains fixed to one exact place on the track.

Even momentary events repeat without variation. A drop swells beneath a pipe joint, falls, and strikes the same dark spot. A white, long-winged animal crosses the square, vanishes behind a chimney, and crosses again. Three bells sound from the western works. A flock rises in panic, describes a rigid spiral, and settles in precisely its former arrangement. Rain begins on the northern roofs while the southern paving stones dry. Neither front advances.

There is no obvious cycle, because a cycle implies passage. Instead the basin seems to contain every phase of itself at once, assigned by location. Steam always condenses in one arcade. Rain always falls over one block. A siren always begins near the foundry and is always ending beside the cisterns. The city does not repeat so much as persist in a complete industrial instant.

Its boundaries are distinct. On all sides, low embankments of slag and pale sediment rise above the roofs. Roads climb them, become progressively smoother, and terminate at identical concrete posts. Beyond lies a level mineral plain under the same overcast light, empty of structures and entirely silent. No animal crosses the posts. No sound escapes far onto the plain. Looking back from just outside, one sees the city quivering with machinery and life, yet it seems enclosed beneath an invisible lid.

There may once have been inhabitants who designed the place, but there are few signs that they were shaped like people. A handful of narrow rooms occupy the upper stories of the administration block. Their floors are tiled, their walls hung with diagrams, their windows clouded by breath from no visible mouth. Each room contains a metal desk and a chair facing away from it. Papers lie under glass, densely printed in tracks, branching burrows, wing-vein patterns, and columns of punched holes. One diagram appears to map the entire basin as the cross-section of a single organism. Another depicts an animal as a factory district. The two drawings are nearly identical.

At the center stands the smallest and oldest structure: a circular pumping house of black stone, only one room wide. The surrounding square is empty. No creatures enter it, though they crowd every roof and pipe around its perimeter. Inside, the sound of the city diminishes to a distant pressure. Condensation covers the walls. A shaft descends into darkness, its rim worn smooth.

Something far below breathes upward through the shaft.

Each exhalation carries the smell of wet iron and produces a minute increase in the city’s roar. Each inhalation draws that roar back down. The interval never lengthens. The moisture never accumulates. The animal below never wakes, if it is sleeping, and never finishes dying, if it is dying.

Outside, the factories continue their production of nothing. The crowded fauna hold their stations. The same rain hangs between cloud and roof. Rust remains rust, noise remains noise, and the city—cool, damp, weathered into equilibrium—endures without gaining even a second of age.
