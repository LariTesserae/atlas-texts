The place is called a garden only because there is no older word for a room in which life has learned to impersonate weather.

It lies inside the outer skin of a drowned tower, almost seven kilometers above the tower’s buried foundations and still two kilometers below the ocean’s surface. The chamber itself is small: an oval cavity scarcely thirty paces long, hidden between structural ribs. Yet one wall has worn through, opening onto the tower’s exterior, and through that breach the garden inherits an abyss.

There is no natural ground. The floor is a buckled sheet of bronze-black alloy, tiled long ago with hexagons whose edges have been polished round by centuries of circulating water. Silt gathers in the shallow dents, but even the silt is manufactured: powdered ceramic, oxidized metal, insulation fibers, soot from ancient furnaces, and the pale residue of organisms that grew by digesting the tower. Beneath it, conduits descend farther than roots could ever reach. Every stone is cast slag. Every cliff is a wall. Every cavern is a failed compartment.

Warm water fills everything.

It enters through narrow vents near the floor, carrying the mineral heat of machines still operating in the depths. The flow rises through the chamber in wavering columns, bends beneath the ceiling, and spills out through the broken wall into cold blue distance. Where warm and cold layers meet, the water trembles as if made of unequal panes of glass. Leaves distort behind these boundaries. Fish seem to divide and rejoin as they cross them.

The breach overlooks the tower’s outer face: an almost vertical landscape of corroded plates, balconies, ducts, buttresses, and empty windows. It rises beyond sight and falls beyond sight. Perspective has little use here. A railing immediately outside the chamber may be close enough to touch, while the next visible ledge is eighty meters away. Farther structures appear as dim repetitions, each one smaller and bluer, until the architecture becomes indistinguishable from open ocean.

This is the place’s extreme elevation: not a summit beneath the sky, but a tiny foothold on an artificial precipice submerged in darkness. Loose objects do not fall. They drift outward, hesitate in currents, then ascend or descend according to density. Still, looking through the breach produces the bodily certainty of standing at an impossible height.

The garden grows in scattered islands.

A single massive fern occupies the chamber’s rear curve, rooted in the mesh of a collapsed intake grille. Its fronds are wine-dark and finely divided, extending halfway across the room before folding back in the current. On the ceiling, green ribbons emerge from cable sleeves and hang downward in sparse curtains. Pale moss coats the warmest pipes, lush where it exists but separated by broad reaches of bare metal. Colonies do not spread evenly. They gather around leaks, electrical fields, residues, and vibration, leaving polished emptiness between them.

Some plants are descended from terrestrial stock, altered past easy recognition. Others are not plants in any strict sense. Copper fans unfurl in response to current and close when machinery shudders. Small translucent bladders drift on tethers, gathering light and dissolved gases. A vine with jointed silver stems grows only along weld seams. Its leaves resemble green hands pressed flat against the wall, each palm positioned over an imperfection in the metal.

There is ordinary light, though no sun reaches this depth. Lamps embedded in the tower continue their cycles with unreasonable fidelity. At a certain hour they brighten to the color of a mild afternoon. Later they fade through amber and blue. Many fixtures have failed, so illumination comes in distinct pools: one balcony bright as noon, the next submerged in dusk, a stairwell below glowing green through forests of algae. The garden chamber receives enough light to seem calm and legible, while the abyss outside remains full of half-seen construction.

Nothing here is young, not even the damage.

Water has worked the architecture into its final shapes. Sharp corners vanished ages ago. Bolts have become knobs; letters have become shallow grooves; broad machine housings are perforated like old shells. Corrosion does not look violent anymore. It has acquired patience and balance. Each surface loses material at nearly the rate that living films replace it. The tower is no longer collapsing in any obvious way. It is settling molecule by molecule into a stable ruin.

Yet its industry persists.

Somewhere below, enormous pumps turn with a rhythm too slow to hear as separate revolutions. Their pressure passes through the tower as a continuous thunder. Smaller machines answer: valve hammers, turbine whines, chains dragging through enclosed shafts. Every few minutes, a pressure gate opens in a neighboring compartment and the whole garden leans toward it. Fronds stream sideways. Fish vanish into cracks. Sediment lifts in bronze clouds. Then the gate closes with a concussion felt in the ribs, and the water resumes its upward circulation.

Sound travels too well. The chamber is loud with distant work.

There are metallic calls that may be engines and engine noises that may be animals. Shrimp strike pipework with their claws. Schools of needlefish click as they turn. A broad, blind grazer rasps microbial crust from the wall, producing the steady noise of a saw. Far outside, something large sends a low pulse through the water. Loose panels vibrate in answer.

Fauna arrives in waves. Tiny glass-bodied fish shelter among the ceiling vines. Red crabs cross bare stretches one at a time, carrying scraps of insulation over their backs. Eels inhabit conduits and hunt by sensing electrical leakage. Thumb-sized snails graze the lamp covers until each pane becomes briefly clear, after which fast-growing films cloud it again. Predators are sparse but decisive: long ribbonlike creatures that hide against vertical cables, and armored fish whose jaws can crush ceramic casings.

The largest animal commonly seen from the breach is the scaffold ray. It does not enter the little chamber. It glides along the tower face, using hooked fins to catch railings when the current strengthens. Gardens grow on its back—hydroids, algae, flowering polyps—and small fish follow it from one illuminated terrace to another. When it passes, it fills the breach like a moving wall.

The place obeys several old rules.

Heat rises toward the surface, but power descends.

Light follows a clock no living creature remembers setting.

Nothing may remain unattached for long. A free object is eventually taken by a vent, a mouth, a current, or a handlike root.

Openings are more durable than walls. Every breach becomes a passage; every passage becomes habitat.

And the tower repairs only what it considers essential. It will seal a pressure artery while allowing a chamber to flood. It will illuminate a dead factory but abandon a nursery to darkness. Its judgment is mechanical, ancient, and not entirely comprehensible.

At intervals, maintenance forms emerge from recessed cabinets. They are small articulated machines encrusted with barnacles, moving with the cautious precision of crabs. Some still carry tools. They inspect seams, scrape vegetation from sensor plates, replace components, and retreat. Others have deviated. One tends the silver vine, clipping away diseased leaves. Another spends each light-cycle arranging identical rust flakes into concentric rings on the floor. A third has stood motionless at the breach for perhaps a century, its lenses directed into the abyss.

There may be thousands of chambers like this within the tower, but they do not connect in ways a swimmer could reliably follow. Doors lead into sealed water. Ladders terminate against newer walls. Elevators open onto vertical rivers. The garden is intimate because the surrounding immensity cannot be entered all at once. It can only be glimpsed: a lit window impossibly far below, a procession of rays around a buttress, a factory chimney exhaling warm bubbles into the blue.

Then the deep pumps alter pitch.

The vegetation folds. Animals press themselves into shelter. Light bends as the entire water column begins to move upward along the tower’s side. For several minutes the hidden garden becomes the throat of something gigantic. Leaves whip, cables sing, and old dust fills the chamber like golden smoke.

When the surge passes, the room is changed in small ways. A fern frond has broken. A new fish has arrived. A panel has opened in the floor, revealing a stairway descending into warmer light.

The tower continues its work.

The garden continues making use of it.
