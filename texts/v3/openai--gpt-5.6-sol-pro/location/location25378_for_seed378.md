### The Whiteworks Plateau

The city occupies a high basin where the mountains have been worn down to blunt rims and long mineral shoulders. From a distance, the basin appears flooded with light. Its terraces are white, yellow, and pale blue; its roofs shine like wet bone; its canals divide the open ground into geometric reaches. There are no trees, no grasses, no weeds in the cracks. Nothing softens the masonry. The only fringes along the water are deposits of salt and silica, built up in scalloped shelves.

Heat lies over everything.

It is not the dry heat of a desert. The air is saturated, almost drinkable, and condensation gathers continuously on shaded surfaces. Walls weep. Railings bead with water. Under bridges, droplets enlarge slowly along ceramic ribs before falling into the channels below. Rain passes over the plateau several times a day, usually in brief, bright curtains. The showers do not cool the city for long. Water strikes the hot pavements, runs silver through their grooves, and begins steaming before it reaches the drains.

The basin is high enough that the surrounding horizon falls away beyond the rim. In clear intervals, one can look through gaps in the western escarpment and see lower country drowned in blue atmospheric haze. Yet the plateau itself is broad: a region rather than a summit, containing districts, quarries, reservoirs, abandoned works, and miles of open mineral ground between them.

The original terrain is still visible beneath the city. Flat sheets of dark volcanic rock rise through plazas and factory yards. Water has traveled across them for ages, carving shallow braided channels with rounded lips. The steeper faces are furred not with moss but with mineral efflorescence: brittle white fans, copper-green crusts, and translucent blisters of salt. Along the northern rim, springs emerge directly from black cliffs. They do not gush. They seep from hundreds of hairline fractures, making the rock look permanently lacquered.

Below these weeping cliffs stand the oldest parts of the settlement.

The buildings are low and widely spaced, constructed from fired brick, glazed stone, cast glass, and metals selected for the wet heat. Ordinary iron survives only as a rust-red residue around ancient anchor points. Newer structures use black ceramic trusses and pale alloys that oxidize into protective skins. Roofs are broad and shallow, designed to catch both rain and condensation. Their undersides are ribbed with collectors. Every surface has some hydraulic purpose: courtyards slope toward settling tanks; stair rails feed cisterns; window hoods drain into narrow vertical pipes.

Although it is a city, it rarely seems crowded. The districts gather around waterworks and then release into open spaces: evaporation fields, empty loading aprons, mineral ponds, and long causeways crossing bare stone. Buildings may stand hundreds of paces apart, connected by raised pipes or glazed conduits. Towers are uncommon. Height is dangerous here, not because of wind but because of lightning and the weight of condensed water. The tallest structures are blunt chimneys with flared caps, and even these crouch against the brightness.

Industry continues throughout the basin, but almost silently.

The main works are driven by pressure, gravity, and heat. Water descending from the rim is sealed into ceramic mains, accelerated through buried turbines, heated in deep wells, and returned as steam to the upper condensers. The machines are massive, yet their moving parts turn behind layers of water and stone. There is no clatter of exposed gears, no hammering line of pistons. A functioning plant can be recognized by the faint trembling of puddles outside it, or by the slow exhalation of vapor through vents in the pavement.

Even the freight system is quiet. Broad sleds move along wetted glass tracks, pulled by linear engines beneath the street. They glide between refineries carrying baskets of mineral scale, pressure vessels, kiln ceramics, and sealed loads from the animal pens. Warning signals are visual: rotating mirrors, lines of blue lamps, shutters that open and close in patterned sequences. Sound does not travel reliably through the warm, layered air. It bends upward, catches in steam, and dissolves against the open basin. The city learned long ago not to depend on bells.

Silence, however, does not mean stillness.

Everywhere there are animals.

They occupy the niches that plants might fill in another world. Flat, many-legged grazers crowd the mineral shelves around warm pools. Their upper shells are glossy white, reflecting the sun, while their undersides carry dark mats of symbiotic microbes. They feed by pressing themselves against fresh deposits and digesting the bacterial films invisible to human sight. From a distance, a colony resembles loose plates of porcelain shifting slowly around the waterline.

In the shallow canals live thread-bodied swimmers, sometimes several meters long but scarcely thicker than a finger. They knot themselves around outlet grilles where chemically rich water enters the system. Their skins are transparent except for branching red organs. When disturbed, they release their grip and stream downstream in silent ribbons.

The open plazas belong to taller creatures: six-limbed, stilt-legged animals with narrow reflective torsos and folding mouth fans. They walk through the rain on dark pointed feet, harvesting airborne spores, microorganisms, and mineral droplets. Their mouths open like circular sieves. They never call. Communication passes through flashes along the flanks and minute tapping vibrations transmitted into the paving.

Predators follow these herds. They are lower, quick, and almost colorless, with broad adhesive pads that let them cross wet walls or hang beneath bridges. Most hunting takes place during rain, when ripples and steam obscure movement. The aftermath is efficient. Scavenging discs arrive within minutes, then smaller jointed things, then clouds of near-microscopic floaters that gather in warm air above the remains. By the next bright interval, little is left except a stain of salts.

There is abundant life here, but none of it is green, rooted, or leaf-bearing. The base of the ecology is not sunlight captured by plants. It is chemistry: microbial skins in the hot springs, organisms in buried pipes, colonies feeding on sulfur, iron, and the compounds brought up from deep wells. Sunlight matters mostly as heat and as a hazard. Many animals carry mirrored armor or circulate reflective crystals through their outer tissues. The brightest noon makes the basin glitter with moving points.

The people of the Whiteworks treat this ecology as both resource and infrastructure. Certain canal animals keep pipes free of scale. Shell-grazers polish reservoir steps. Larger filter feeders are kept in shaded cistern courts, where they purify condensed water before it enters domestic storage. Factories cultivate dense microbial pastes in sealed vats, then feed them to livestock or use them in chemical production. There are breeding districts, migration corridors, and laws concerning the temperature and composition of public runoff.

One of the city’s oldest rules is that water may not be stopped.

It may be slowed, filtered, heated, divided, lifted, or made to work, but never permanently impounded. Completely still water changes under the plateau’s heat. Within days it develops a clear, gelatinous depth that resists stirring. Within weeks it begins generating pressure without boiling. Old sealed reservoirs have ruptured with enough force to lift roofs from entire districts. For this reason every tank has an overflow, every basement a drain, every canal some gradient, however slight.

Another rule concerns shadows. No enclosed shadow is allowed to remain uninspected for more than nine days. In the humid heat, shaded cavities become cool enough for condensation and chemically distinct enough to attract dense colonies of small animals. They can fill a wall from within, altering its weight and eating the salts from its mortar. Buildings are therefore made with inspection slots, removable panels, and narrow shafts of concentrated daylight. At certain hours the city flashes as shutters align and sunlight is sent deep through its interior.

The day is painfully bright. Cloud cover does not dim it so much as spread it. The sky becomes a white vault without a visible sun, and every wet plane throws light upward. There are few hard shadows. Distance is measured in degrees of whiteness: nearby structures are stark and sharp; far districts dissolve into luminous vapor; the mountain rim appears and disappears behind moving rain.

Toward evening the brightness turns gold, then briefly copper. Heat continues to rise from the stone. The rainwater in shallow gutters steams, and the animals emerge in greater numbers, crossing the causeways in deliberate, silent migrations. Factory lamps awaken beneath the streets, illuminating canals from below. Long swimmers pass over them like ink strokes. Signal mirrors fold shut. Condenser towers begin shedding accumulated water in heavy, spaced drops.

These drops are among the loudest sounds in the basin.

One falls from a roof lip into a ceramic bowl. Another strikes a stretched awning. Somewhere beneath the paving, a turbine transmits a low pulse through the soles of the feet. There may be the faint rasp of shell against stone, the whisper of rain crossing hot glass, the quick wet contact of a predator taking prey. Then the sound is gone.

Beyond the inhabited works, old channels continue across the plateau. Some lead to roofless factories whose machinery has become part of the watershed. Water descends through broken furnaces, gathers in gear pits, and exits through collapsed loading bays edged with blue crystals. Animals nest in pressure tanks and move through fractured conduits. Nothing is overgrown. Abandonment here does not produce a thicket. It produces polishing, hollowing, deposition: architecture simplified by water into terraces and caves.

The city is undergoing the same process, though slowly and by agreement. Walls are replastered with mineral slurries taken from the drains. Channels are recut along ancient courses. Structures that resist the plateau too rigidly crack; those that permit seepage endure. The oldest buildings no longer have clear boundaries between construction and geology. Their lower rooms have become spring chambers. Their foundations descend into warm stone. Their facades bear centuries of layered glaze, each rainy season adding a fraction to their thickness.

From the rim at night, the Whiteworks looks less like a city than a dispersed instrument laid across the basin: lines of submerged light, isolated furnace glows, steam veils, mirror fields turned dark, and reservoirs reflecting the pale sky. Movement is everywhere, but almost nothing announces itself.

Water travels. Heat rises. Animals feed and are fed upon. Minerals precipitate from the walls. Silent engines turn under the streets.

The plateau shines, sweats, and continues.
