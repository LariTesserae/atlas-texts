The sea is at the top of the world.

It rests above the cloud layer in a basin so broad that its opposite shores lie beyond weather and curvature. From the dry lands below—if dry lands still exist—it would resemble a dark second sky: a suspended weight of warm water held between eroded summits. Nothing visibly supports it. Along its underside, storms can sometimes be seen moving upside down, their lightning spreading soundlessly through blue-black depths.

Within the sea, height has not been abolished. It has become difficult to define.

Ranges of ancient stone rise through the water for miles, but their peaks descend in other directions. A swimmer ascending one slope may find the water darkening and pressure increasing; another, following the same ridge from the opposite side, may emerge briefly into a pocket of warm, stale air. Depth gathers in hollows, beneath overhangs, inside shadows, and sometimes around particular colors. Pale stone is usually shallow. Red stone is seldom safe.

The oldest mountains have been worn almost flat. Their summits are broad drowned plains of basalt and greenish limestone, polished by currents older than the present arrangement of gravity. Long, low terraces extend from them like steps made for a vanished scale of body. There are no fresh fractures. Every edge has been softened, bored through, scalloped, and furred over. Even the rubble has become round, and even the round stones have dimples where tiny patient colonies have dissolved them grain by grain.

The water is warm—not tropical in the bright, generous sense, but warm as deep blood. It varies little from place to place. In some regions it grows warmer with ascent; in others, cold lenses hang among the vegetation like invisible ponds. Crossing one brings a brief tightening of the skin and a sensation that several years have passed. Small white growths occur around these lenses, each composed of thousands of translucent leaves folded into the shape of a sleeping hand.

There is almost no direct sun.

Light enters as a diluted bronze haze from no dependable direction. At intervals, the whole sea dims as though something continental has passed overhead, though there is no visible surface above and no corresponding current. At other times a soft green illumination rises from the rock. It reveals layer after layer of vegetation receding into the murk: submerged forests arranged vertically, laterally, and along gradients that have nothing to do with up.

The dominant plants are ribbon groves. Their trunks are low mats of woody tissue gripping the old plateaus, but their leaves extend hundreds of meters, broad as roads and thin enough to show veins. The ribbons do not wave together. Each follows a different current, so that a grove resembles a vast slow braid forever being woven and unwoven. Dust-fine spores shine along the edges. When two ribbons touch, they often adhere, exchange color, and separate days later with altered patterns.

Among them stand forests of glasswood. These are dark, many-jointed columns whose bark incorporates dissolved silica until old trunks become nearly transparent. Inside each trunk, sap climbs in distinct amber beads. The beads stop whenever observed too closely. Broken glasswood does not splinter; it slumps over decades, slowly losing its shape and returning to gel.

Thickets crowd every sheltered depression. Fan mosses overlap like scales. Bladder vines hold bubbles of air, methane, memory, or some substance that behaves like all three. Dense black fronds grow only where the light is strongest, spreading darkness beneath themselves to feed their roots. Coral-like plants build towers around warm springs, although they have no polyps and no obvious individual bodies. Their branching forms are pale lavender near the base and almost colorless at the tips, where new growth continually appears and is worn away.

There is vegetation even in open water. Floating root systems knot themselves into reefs without ground. Draperies of filament descend for kilometers from seeds that remain permanently above them. Great sheets of single-celled green pass like weather fronts, turning the dimness briefly emerald. Where two such blooms meet, the water thickens. Currents slow. Sound stops more completely. For several hours, or perhaps several months, the sea there has the consistency of clear oil.

Life is lush but animal life is scarce.

The few visible creatures seem temporary, as if the landscape occasionally remembers that fauna are possible. Small bell-shaped swimmers pulse through the ribbon forests, each no larger than a fingertip. They have no mouths and vanish when they enter shadow. Flat browsing things cling to the stone terraces, leaving clean spirals in the moss. Their tracks are more common than the animals themselves and may remain fresh for centuries.

Far out over the drowned plains, one might see a long animal crossing the haze: a threadlike body with slow fins, perhaps a kilometer from head to tail. It swims without disturbing the vegetation. Sometimes its middle passes first and its head arrives much later from another direction. Nothing hunts it. Nothing is seen feeding upon anything else. Predation, if it occurs, happens at the level of places: a grove enclosing a current, a warm layer consuming a cold one, a depth opening around a living thing until it cannot return.

Most movement belongs to the water and the plants.

Tides travel vertically through the mountains. They lift entire meadows from the rock, hold them suspended, then lay them down in new arrangements. Warm upwellings pour from holes in the ancient plateaus, carrying mineral clouds and pale seeds. Once every uncertain cycle, a current of almost perfectly clear water moves across the sea. It bends the ribbon groves flat and strips the silt from ruins and bones. Behind it, visibility extends for impossible distances. Mountains can be seen hanging above one another, each with its own horizon, while forests appear as dark continents suspended in blue.

Then the silt returns.

It falls upward in some districts. Elsewhere it drifts sideways and accumulates against empty water, forming vertical banks with no wall behind them. These banks become rooted, compacted, and eventually mistaken for land. Much of the sea’s geology may have begun this way.

Sound does not travel properly. Close movements are muffled: the brush of a leaf against stone, the soft bursting of gas bladders, the rasp of mineral grains in a current. Loud events are silent. When a terrace collapses, one sees the old rock fold into a cloud but hears only a faint ticking from somewhere far away. Conversely, the smallest noises may arrive enlarged after long delays. A shell closing in a distant thicket can return years later as a single low note that passes through the entire basin and causes every ribbon leaf to turn edgewise.

There are scattered marks of habitation, though nothing here suggests technology.

On the highest drowned plateaus stand small chambers built of fitted stone. They are no larger than shelters and too widely separated to form settlements. Their blocks are held together by roots rather than mortar. Each chamber has one doorway and no interior air. Most contain only water, silt, and a shallow stone bowl. The bowls are always level even where the floors are not. Sometimes they contain a different sea: colder, brightly lit, with fast silver animals visible far below its thumb-sized surface.

No tool marks remain. If hands built these rooms, erosion has taken the evidence and left only intention. Some chambers may instead have grown, coaxed into geometric shapes by organisms that no longer exist. Glasswood roots avoid them. Ribbon leaves pass through their doorways but never attach to the walls.

Other remnants are less certain. Rows of upright stones cross the plains, but each stone is a naturally rounded pillar. Circular clearings appear in the densest forests, yet nothing prevents growth there. There are long paths where the silt has been pressed smooth by repeated passage, though no traveler has been seen using them. The paths sometimes end at sheer drops into milky depth. Sometimes they continue on the opposite face of the abyss, upside down.

The abyssal regions are not lower places. They migrate.

A depth may begin as a blue stain between trees. Over several days it enlarges, swallowing contrast and distance. Plants near it bend inward without moving. Stone seen through it appears older and smoother. If the depth passes across a plateau, it leaves the plateau reduced: ridges leveled, ruins opened, fallen leaves converted to dark soil. This is the sea’s deepest form of erosion. It does not grind matter away. It persuades matter that it has already endured much longer.

Thus the landscape is ancient not merely in age but in habit. Everything has settled into shapes that expect eternity. The mountains no longer resist the water. The plants grow according to currents that may have begun before their species. Ruins have become habitats; habitats have become mineral layers; mineral layers occasionally wake and put out leaves.

Yet the place is never still. Forests exchange positions. Tides reverse height. Clear-water fronts expose hidden ranges. Depths migrate through stone. Warm rain falls within the sea—droplets of denser water descending through lighter water, pattering silently on leaves before collecting in temporary rivers along the drowned ground. These rivers flow without banks, silvering the silt, and sometimes pour over a cliff only to curve upward and enter the underside of the same plateau.

There are rules, but they are local.

Gravity belongs to large objects and may point toward them. A mountain carries its own down. A forest can acquire one if it grows dense enough. Air gathers in rooms that remember being dry. Distance increases in darkness. Repetition makes things real: three stones may drift, but a thousand stones become a continent. Nothing living can cross the same clearing twice by the same route. A reflected object is older than the object reflecting it. Any sound heard clearly has not happened yet.

Beyond the last visible ridges, the sea continues. It may cover the whole planet, or it may be a single suspended drop magnified from within. At its most distant limits, there are said to be shores, but shorelines here are not boundaries between water and land. They are places where one version of the water ends and another begins.

Near such a shore, the vegetation grows so dense that there is scarcely room for liquid. Leaves, roots, mineral fans, transparent trunks, and slow colonies interlock into a living solid. Currents must pass through capillaries. Light becomes a brown-green pulse. The silence deepens until one can sense the motion of sap in nearby stems.

And beyond that crowded, warm, ancient wall lies more water—darker, higher, and falling forever without descending.
