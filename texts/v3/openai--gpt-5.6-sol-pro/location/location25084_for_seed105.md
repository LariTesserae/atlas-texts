At first there is only pressure and noise.

The pressure is steady, enormous, almost architectural. It seems to hold the darkness in place. The noise comes through the water rather than across it: long grinding notes, sudden concussions, and a continuous bass murmur that can be felt in stone before it is heard. Somewhere far above, a ceiling of ice is shifting. Somewhere below, hidden slopes are giving way. Currents strike the submerged heights and divide around them with the force of weather.

This is not the bottom of an ordinary sea. It is a drowned highland.

Mountain ridges still rise from the buried country, though the water rises farther. Their summits form black reefs at a great elevation above the abyssal plains surrounding them. Valleys hang between the peaks, broad and cold, flooded to depths that have erased their former scale. A traveler may cross what seems to be open ocean, then see an old road appear beneath them: two parallel seams of fitted stone descending into a ravine. A little farther on, the ravine drops away, and the road continues unsupported across the dark, carried by a bridge whose wooden spans have long vanished.

The water is nearly black. What light enters from above has passed through ice, cloud, and many fathoms of suspended glacial dust. It arrives as a weak blue-gray diffusion, enough to make edges uncertain rather than visible. At rare intervals the ice ceiling thins and a vertical pallor falls through the water. These shafts move slowly over the terrain as the upper floes migrate. A mountainside may be illuminated for an hour, revealing terraces, forests, and walls; then the light slides on, and everything closes again.

The vegetation has inherited the place completely.

Forests of ribbonweed grow from the old alpine soil. Their leaves are broad, dark, and nearly transparent, some reaching the length of houses. They do not stand upward so much as stream sideways, all aligned to the prevailing current. When the water changes direction, entire valleys turn over in a single slow gesture. Pale undersides flash. Stalks bend nearly to the ground. The movement passes from slope to slope like wind laying down grass, except that each sweep takes several minutes and bends trunks thick enough to anchor roofs.

On exposed ridges the growth is sparse. Low red mats cling to basalt, sealing cracks and softening carved steps. Fan-shaped plants shelter in the lee of boulders, their black fronds stiffened by mineral ribs. In the deeper basins, where sediment has settled for centuries, there are lush fields of bladdered vines. Their hollow floats do not contain air; they contain a clear, heavy sap that is less dense than the surrounding water. The vines rise in columns and gather under overhangs, making inverted meadows along the ceilings of caves.

Nothing grazes them.

There are no fish in the weed, no crustaceans among the stones, no worms turning the mud. No shells lie in the silt. No small bodies scatter from movement. Even the plants lack the nibbled edges and scars expected of a living landscape. Their leaves grow whole until age and current tear them. Fallen vegetation is not eaten. It gathers in quiet places, layer upon layer, and is slowly compressed into dark peat by water pressure and the weight of new growth.

The absence becomes more pronounced with distance. Every promising flicker proves to be a leaf-tip. Every shape crossing a shaft of light is a piece of bark or a clot of spores. The sea is vigorous but uninhabited by anything that swims.

Its fertility comes from elsewhere. Mineral springs rise along faults in the drowned mountains, carrying warmth, iron, sulfur, and salts. Plant roots knot around the vents. Some species have no green pigment at all; they are ivory, rust-colored, or glassy, feeding through colonies of threadlike growth embedded in their tissues. These colonies spread without visible fruiting bodies, passing from root to root through the old soil. A whole mountainside may behave like one organism. When one part enters a light shaft, distant fronds unfurl in response. When a vent cools, groves half a valley away fold themselves tight.

The place has seasons, though no sun can be seen.

During the long cold phase, the upper ice thickens. The weak light disappears. Water descending from the frozen ceiling becomes painfully cold and pours through the valleys in heavy rivers, distinct from the slightly warmer sea around them. These underwater rivers scour channels through silt, uproot vine fields, and carry boulders downslope. Their arrival is announced by thunder: ice fractures transmitted across vast distances, followed by the rattling of stones and the creak of forests under the flow.

Then the deep springs strengthen. Warm mineral water climbs the faults, staining the water with red and yellow veils. The vegetation opens. Spore clouds issue from the terraces and spread like smoke, dense enough to hide whole ruins. They settle over bare stone and begin new mats wherever the current slackens. For several weeks the water is crowded with drifting seed-filaments, yet still there is no animal motion among them.

The settlements are scattered across the higher ridges.

They were built when the valleys were not entirely drowned—or when whoever built them had another relationship with water. Most are small: a ring of stone huts around a central pit, a stair cut into a cliff, a shelter roofed with overlapping slabs. The masonry is primitive but patient. Blocks were split along natural seams and fitted without mortar. Doorways are low and narrow. Walls lean inward against pressure. Where wood survives, it has been pinned together with bone-shaped pegs carved from pale stone; there are no actual bones anywhere.

In some huts, stone bowls remain fixed to the floors. Their rims are worn smooth on the inside, though they now hold only sediment and coiled roots. Shelves have been cut directly into the walls. Hand axes of chipped black glass sit in recesses where they were left, their edges still sharp beneath films of red growth. Simple copper hooks appear occasionally, green with alteration, but never blades, gears, glasswork, or writing.

The builders used marks instead: groups of shallow cup-shaped hollows pecked into lintels and standing stones. The patterns repeat across the highland. Three cups beneath a line. A spiral broken at one side. Two opposing combs. The marks are too regular to be erosion and too varied to be decoration. Ribbonweed avoids them. Even thick mats leave the hollows exposed.

The largest structure stands on a saddle between two peaks. From a distance it resembles a natural outcrop. Up close, it resolves into hundreds of upright slabs planted in concentric arcs. Many have fallen and lie half-buried in mosslike growth. The surviving stones vibrate in the current. Each has been thinned and shaped until it produces a particular tone.

When the seasonal river passes over the saddle, the entire field sounds.

The lower stones begin first, groaning like stressed timber. Narrower slabs take up higher notes. Water moves through apertures drilled near their bases and makes pulses too regular to be accidental. The drowned monument becomes a vast instrument, audible through several valleys. Its song is not melodic. It is a sequence of pressure waves: gathering, breaking apart, returning in altered form. Vegetation folds flat around it. Silt lifts from the ground. Loose tools tremble on the floors of distant houses.

Other sounds answer, but none are made by animals. Ice booms overhead. Faults knock below. Hollow trunks whistle as currents pass through them. Air—or something resembling air—remains trapped in a few sealed caverns, and when water forces its way through cracks, these pockets compress and release with explosive reports. In the blackest trenches, slabs of sediment collapse, sending slow avalanches down the submerged walls. The sea is loud because the land is still settling under it.

Yet it is not young. The erosion is mature, integrated into the shape of the country. Cliffs have rounded shoulders. Channels have found stable courses. Ruins have become current-breaks, gathering soil on one side and exposing bedrock on the other. Plants reinforce some slopes and pry others apart. Every collapse seems to complete a process centuries in progress. The landscape does not feel ruined so much as established in drowning.

There are places where its rules become stranger.

Water flowing downhill sometimes encounters old boundary stones and turns aside, even when no wall remains. Silt accumulates along invisible lines, outlining vanished fields. Spore clouds halt at certain thresholds and hang there, compressed against nothing. A broken doorway can divide warm water from cold with a precision no natural current maintains. Passing through such a doorway may bring one into a chamber far deeper than the exterior walls could contain.

In these chambers, gravity appears uncertain. Weeds grow from every surface, each believing its own wall to be the ground. Fallen stone flakes drift in slow circles. A person releasing an object may watch it sink sideways toward a dark corner. The builders seem to have known this. Their huts are clustered near the stable places, while their monuments occupy the unstable ones.

The most remote valleys contain almost no structures. There the ridges stand wide apart, and the vegetation gathers into isolated groves separated by fields of bare, pale mud. The emptiness is immense. A lone slab shelter can remain visible for miles when the light is right, no larger than a tooth against the slopes. Beyond it, another mountain emerges from darkness; beyond that, only the dim suggestion of higher country.

No horizon exists. Distance is measured by fading pressure, by the time between one ice-thunder and its echo, by how many times the forests reverse in the tide.

Above, the frozen ceiling continues to move.

Below, the mineral springs continue to rise.

Between them, the drowned highland bends, sounds, grows, and remembers its boundaries. It is full of motion and utterly without creatures: a cold, cultivated-looking wilderness whose gardeners are current, stone, and a vegetation that has never learned to fear being eaten.
