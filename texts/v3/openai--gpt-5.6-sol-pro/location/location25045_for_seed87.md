Far below the sea’s visible floor, beneath a crust of sediment still too young to have hardened into proper stone, there is a cavity no larger than a room.

It is not a cave in the usual sense. Nothing excavated it. The surrounding geology has barely begun to decide what shape it will keep. Layers of gray volcanic powder, glacial flour, salt crystals, and the translucent remains of plankton have slumped together around a trapped lens of water. The walls are soft enough to receive an imprint from a fin, yet cold enough that each imprint soon stiffens. New mineral skins form over old ones. Cracks open as the seabed settles, admit a black thread of ocean, and close again under their own weight.

Inside this pocket stands a city.

From the human scale it would look like a dense patch of grit: ridges, knobs, narrow channels, clusters of pale chimneys. Seen at the scale of its inhabitants, it has districts, avenues, wells, towers, arcades, blind courtyards, and crowded vertical neighborhoods. No hand has built it. Nothing here possesses tools. The city is the accumulated behavior of innumerable animals, each following a few bodily imperatives: attach, feed, seal, breed, avoid the cold current, lean toward mineral warmth.

Its oldest foundations are only a few seasons old.

The central streets wind between chalky columns secreted by tube-dwellers. These animals never leave their houses; they extend wreaths of colorless filaments into the water and withdraw them at the first change in pressure. Their tubes grow lip by lip, and because each new tube settles against an older one, the colony rises in walls. Where several walls meet, they trap silt. Where silt collects, burrowers arrive. Where burrowers perforate the silt, currents begin. Thus alleys are made without intention.

Other structures are built from shell fragments cemented with mucus. Tiny crustaceans gather nothing deliberately, but drag food beneath ledges; the discarded armor of their prey accumulates around their dens. Pale worms bind the fragments together. Mineral-rich water hardens the binding. A heap becomes a rampart, the rampart divides a current, and two new neighborhoods form in its lee.

Everything is close. Towers brush towers. Tunnels pass under galleries only a body-width above them. Soft fans, feeding crowns, jointed legs, eyeless heads, and glassy egg sacs occupy nearly every interval. Open space is rare and dangerous. The few plazas are not ceremonial but predatory: smooth patches around the mouths of animals hidden under the sediment.

The water is almost freezing. It has the heavy, patient clarity of water under immense pressure, though fine particles are always falling through it. These descend so slowly that they seem suspended: ash, scales, fecal dust, crystal splinters, the shed jaws of minute worms. Dim blue-gray light enters through seams in the roof, not from the distant sun but from cold colonies of bacteria growing along cracks. Their glow is too weak to illuminate surfaces fully. It catches only edges—a shell rim, a claw, the curve of a translucent abdomen—then vanishes into packed shadow.

There are no plants. Sparse mats of dark microbial felt occupy places where chemical seepage reaches the walls. They resemble wet velvet laid in narrow strips along the city’s foundations. Threadlike algae sometimes arrive from above inside pellets of ice, but they do not grow. They are eaten, buried, or preserved as green stains in the mineral crust.

The city feeds instead on the interior of the earth.

At one end of the chamber, a fissure exhales water a fraction warmer than the rest. It carries dissolved iron, sulfur, methane, and salts. The flow is gentle, hardly more than a continuous seep, but around it the population thickens until individual bodies are difficult to distinguish. Bacteria bloom on every available surface. Grazing snails scrape them away. Needle-legged hunters seize the snails. Ribbon worms enter the hunters through their joints. Larger scavengers eat whatever the ribbon worms leave.

The dominant creatures are soft and almost transparent. Armor costs too much in this cold, calcium-poor water, so many inhabitants borrow protection from the city itself. They flatten into cracks, inhabit abandoned tubes, or wear loose mosaics of shell dust glued to their backs. Some are born with only temporary skeletons, delicate frameworks that dissolve when maturity makes them too large for narrow passages.

Among them move the bell-bodies: thumbprint-sized medusae whose bells do not pulse. They travel by altering the salinity inside themselves, becoming slightly heavier or lighter than the surrounding water. They rise and sink with such precision that they appear to be lowered on invisible threads. Their long feeding strands trail through multiple levels of the settlement, turning streets into vertical nets. Smaller creatures learn the timing of these descents. When a bell-body darkens, whole corridors empty.

The city is active without seeming to move. Its pace is distributed among thousands of almost imperceptible acts. Mouthparts brush bacterial films. Larvae settle. Walls thicken by a grain. Eggs cloud and clear. Predators advance one joint at a time. Warm seep water rises; colder water folds down to replace it. Soft sediment buckles under the changing load, tilting an entire quarter by the width of an antenna.

Collapse is common, but not catastrophic. The geology is too new for ruins to remain ruins. A fallen tube-wall is immediately occupied. Fractures become canals. Buried animals send breathing chimneys upward, and these chimneys become the next generation of towers. Here erosion has scarcely begun. The landscape is not being worn away so much as trying out forms. Stone appears briefly, dissolves, and appears elsewhere. Floors swell. Mineral crusts peel from the ceiling like thin ice and settle across rooftops, sealing some chambers while making bridges over others.

The only broad landmark is the Fold, a ridge where the chamber wall bent inward before hardening. It divides the settlement into a colder outer ward and the crowded seepward interior. The Fold is layered with occupied holes. Long-bodied animals pass through it, but nothing can turn around inside. Traffic follows strict currents: inward along the lower apertures, outward above. Creatures that resist this arrangement are pressed backward, trapped, or eaten. It is an urban rule produced entirely by anatomy and water.

There is almost no sound.

The mud walls absorb vibration. The surrounding sediment muffles the remote groaning of the oceanic crust. No waves reach this depth, and no stones have enough room to tumble. Close by, one might detect minute noises conducted through shell and tube: the dry tick of a claw closing, the soft rasp of grazing teeth, a bubble collapsing inside mucus, the faint granular sigh of a roof settling. These are intimate sounds. They do not cross the city. Each district inhabits its own small quiet.

Communication therefore occurs through pressure, chemistry, and touch. A predator’s passage is announced by water displaced several corridors away. Reproductive readiness spreads as a dissolved taste. Colonies synchronize by exchanging pulses through the walls they share. At intervals, for reasons no single animal can know, nearly every tube-dweller withdraws at once. The city then becomes abruptly smooth and empty-looking. Seconds later the crowns emerge again, and the streets fill with living fringe.

The strangest law of the place concerns enclosure. Any hollow left vacant begins to close.

Bacteria film its surface. Crystals grow across the film. Larvae settle on the crystals. Their bodies narrow the opening further. Thus every unoccupied chamber is slowly erased, while every occupied one is kept open by feeding currents and movement. Space belongs only to what continually inhabits it. An abandoned district does not stand as a ruin; it becomes solid wall.

Conversely, sustained motion creates architecture. A path traveled often enough becomes a tunnel because bodies prevent sediment from settling there. A predator’s hunting circuit may persist long after the predator dies, inherited as an avenue by prey that once fled it. The city’s map is therefore a record not of plans but of habits.

Occasionally the ceiling opens.

A hairline rupture admits a column of darker, colder seawater from the world above. With it come foreign larvae, fragments of dead plankton, and once in a great while a shard of true ice. The inhabitants swarm the intrusion. The shard glows faintly with trapped surface light, startlingly bright in the dim chamber. Animals shelter beneath it, eat what is frozen into it, lay eggs against it. Then it melts, and the brief district founded around it loses its roof.

Each rupture also threatens to flush the entire city into the sedimentary dark. The settlement responds without coordination. Tube crowns clamp shut. Bell-bodies sink into wells. Burrowers pack entrances with mucus. Thousands of bodies alter the current until the fissure chokes with silt. What resembles civic defense is simply fear repeated densely enough.

Above, the ocean remains immense, but here immensity has been compressed. The deep sea has folded one of its crowded possibilities into a hidden pocket: a metropolis contained in a handful of raw earth, lit by bacteria, built by attachment and appetite. Its towers may be crushed tomorrow by the settling seabed. Its central seep may fail. The whole chamber may harden into a pale nodule with the city preserved inside as a maze of mineral veins.

For now it continues to rise by fractions—wall upon wall, body against body—quietly building itself in water that is almost dark enough to forget it.
