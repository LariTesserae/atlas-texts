The valley lies high enough that the air has lost much of its softness. Breathing is easy only when done slowly. Cold sunlight pours over the eastern rim and remains there for most of the day, white and remarkably direct, as though the sky were a lamp with no shade. There are few clouds. Those that cross are thin, level-bottomed sheets snagged briefly on the highest teeth of stone.

The mountains are young. Their fractures still look accidental.

Black-green ridges rise on either side of the valley in steep, angular folds, their faces split into slabs that have not yet rounded under weather. Fresh scree rests beneath them: pale gray shards, iron-red splinters, translucent chips of some harder mineral that flash in the sun. There are almost no deep gullies. Rain has only begun to establish its routes, so water wanders when it comes, slipping into one crack one year and another the next. The land has not learned drainage.

Yet the valley is intensely green.

Vegetation crowds every surface that can hold a little condensation. It does not form a continuous forest so much as an immense layering of low, dense life: silver grasses pressed shoulder to shoulder; waxy rosettes nested in cracks; fernlike fans no higher than a hand; mats of black moss with bright green tips; red-stemmed shrubs that braid themselves between boulders. On sheltered slopes, narrow trees grow in close ranks. Their trunks are dark and fibrous, seldom thicker than a person’s wrist, but they rise ten or twelve meters before opening into translucent leaves. The leaves overlap like panes of green glass. Sunlight passes through several layers and reaches the ground still bright, though stained emerald.

There is little standing water. What moisture exists is held rather than displayed.

At dawn, condensation beads along millions of leaves and runs down grooved stems into buried bulbs. Shallow stone basins collect enough to remain wet for a few days, their surfaces covered by a skin of cobalt algae. Under the scree, pockets of cold water persist in darkness. Every few kilometers a spring emerges without warning, flowing vividly for thirty or forty meters through the vegetation before vanishing into a new fissure. The largest stream is not quite a river. It occupies the valley’s center in disconnected reaches—clear channels alternating with dry gravel, root tangles, and damp mineral flats. During rain these fragments join for a few hours. Then the water withdraws and the valley becomes a chain of memories again.

The industrial works are scattered along this uncertain watercourse.

They do not resemble a town. There are no streets and few walls, only individual installations separated by expanses of crowded growth. A turbine house stands on a shelf of raw basalt, its concrete shell bleached almost white. Farther upslope, three steel condensation towers rise above the trees. Their ribbed surfaces are dulled by oxidation, but their upper vanes continue to turn with almost imperceptible patience. Long pipes descend from them, sometimes buried, sometimes suspended between stone piers. Where a pipe crosses a ravine, the plants have climbed it so completely that only occasional bands of galvanized metal show through.

Elsewhere there are cable drums, narrow pylons, shuttered transformer cabinets, and low sheds with sloping roofs. A set of rails runs for half a kilometer along one cliff and ends abruptly at a fresh rock face. Small ore carts remain coupled there, full not of stone but of soil and flowering weeds. The scattered machinery suggests an industry too large to see from any one place. Each installation may be only an organ; the body, if it exists, is distributed across the whole valley.

Some devices still operate.

Their operation is nearly silent.

A flywheel turns behind thick glass in the turbine house, slow enough that its spokes can be counted. Indicator lamps glow in cabinets whose doors have been left ajar. Pale current travels along ceramic conduits, visible as a blue seam only when one stands close. High on the condensation towers, fine metal combs gather moisture from the air. Drops form, merge, and descend through internal channels. Nothing clanks. There is no exhaust, no hammering, no churn of belts. Even the electrical equipment has none of the usual hum.

The silence is not merely the absence of workers.

Sound behaves poorly here. A falling stone can be seen bouncing down a slope and heard only when it comes to rest. Leaves move in the wind without rustling. Water passing over gravel gives a faint pressure in the teeth rather than a noise. Close sounds remain possible—the snap of a dry stem underfoot, breath inside a collar, fingertips dragged across corroded steel—but they do not travel. A shout thrown across the valley seems to fall immediately around the speaker.

The machinery may be responsible. Around each installation stand thin rods of dark alloy, driven into stone at regular intervals. Lichen avoids them. Between the rods, vibration dwindles until even the movement of air becomes mute. Some rods have toppled, and around these failures small islands of ordinary sound return: a patch where droplets can be heard ticking into a basin, another where grass whispers for several paces and then stops.

Animal life is scarce enough to feel provisional. There are tiny white insects beneath loose bark, and sometimes a long-winged black bird crosses the sun without calling. In the wet basins live translucent worms that knot themselves around mineral grains. No herds graze the lush slopes. No burrows puncture the soil. There are no clouds of flies above the intermittent stream and almost no tracks in the mud.

The plants consequently grow unchecked, but not chaotically. Their density has a cultivated quality despite the absence of obvious tending. Species arrange themselves in bands around pipes and cables. Blue moss gathers near live electrical housings. Tall glass-leaved trees follow buried transmission lines with uncanny precision, their roots wrapped around the conduits without cracking them. On the slopes farthest from the works, the vegetation becomes less varied and less vigorous, fading into ordinary alpine scrub. The deepest lushness belongs to the machinery.

It may be that the valley is a factory whose product is the landscape itself.

Inside some sheds are racks of empty cylinders, each labeled with an elevation, a date, and a botanical symbol. Other buildings contain pumps connected not to mines but to networks of porous ceramic buried under the soil. Their valves open once every several days, releasing a measured breath of mineral vapor into the roots. Mirrors on remote ridges turn by fractions of a degree over the course of the season, extending the bright day into selected hollows. The cold sunlight reflected from them can make one grove shine at dusk while the rest of the valley has already gone blue.

Nothing happens quickly. A tower vane may complete one revolution between sunrise and noon. Gates in the dry riverbed shift on screw threads so fine that their movement is visible only by returning after a month. Moisture accumulates in underground reservoirs drop by drop, then is released during the driest weeks through capillaries thinner than hair. Rust advances. Roots thicken. Scree settles around foundations. The entire place appears to be conducting an experiment whose smallest meaningful unit is a decade.

In the brightest part of the valley stands the only structure that could be called inhabited.

It is a narrow station built into a wedge of new stone, with one glazed wall facing south. The glass is clean. Behind it are a table, two chairs, banks of paper charts, and a mechanical clock with no audible tick. A greenhouse adjoins the station, though it is less lush than the land outside. Its beds hold a few sparse, carefully separated specimens, each caged under wire. The door is unlocked. There is no dust on the floor, but there is no one present.

A logbook lies open beneath a brass weight. The most recent entries are composed only of measurements: air temperature, leaf density, water recovered, acoustic loss, avian sightings. The dates span more than a century. The handwriting never changes.

Beyond the station, the valley widens and the scattered works become harder to distinguish from natural forms. Towers stand among narrow trees. Intake fins resemble dark reeds. Solar reflectors glitter from cliff faces like plates of unmelted frost. At great distance, on the opposite rim, a row of vertical objects might be pylons or stone needles newly forced upward from the mountain.

The boundaries remain uncertain. There are passes out of the valley, but each gives onto another high basin with the same brilliant light, the same compact vegetation, the same young, sharp geology. Sometimes a pipe emerges beside the trail and follows it for kilometers before diving into stone. Sometimes there is only untouched scree. Then, around a bend, one finds a porcelain insulator half enclosed in a tree trunk, warm despite the cold.

Toward evening the temperature falls quickly. Condensation begins before darkness, appearing first on metal and then on leaves. The whole valley brightens once more as low sunlight catches the forming droplets. Every branch is edged in silver. Every cable becomes a luminous line. Water gathers in silence throughout the dense green slopes, preparing for its brief descent.

Nothing announces the night. The vanes continue turning. The hidden valves continue their patient work. Far below the roots, reservoirs fill by imperceptible degrees, while the unfinished mountains hold their edges against the stars.
