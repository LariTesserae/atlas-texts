The plateau begins above the breathable weather.

It is not a summit but a broad mineral country lifted two or three kilometers over the surrounding world, its margins lost beyond heat haze and the curvature of the plain. Nothing grows there. No grass softens the shelves, no root enters the fractures. There are no streams, no dry channels remembering streams, no buried aquifer cooling the deeper stone. The plateau has always been dry, or has been dry for so long that even the shapes made by water have worn away.

Under ordinary daylight, the ground is the color of fired bone. Sheets of pale ceramic rock overlap in shallow steps, glazed by centuries of heat. Between them lie basins of black granules that flow too reluctantly to be called sand. Each grain is an angular bead of iron carbide, and their dunes move perhaps a hand’s width in a human lifetime. The exposed ridges have been eroded into stable forms: low blades, blunt pyramids, and long parallel ribs whose edges have rounded without becoming gentle. Here erosion does not erase the landscape. It ratifies it.

The heat is severe but remarkably even. There is no noon furnace followed by a freezing night. The stone stores daylight, and something farther below stores more. Warmth rises continuously through hairline vents, carrying metallic vapors and the smell of hot coins, scorched dust, and electrical insulation—though nothing here was ever built and there is no insulation to burn.

At first the plateau seems empty.

One can look fifty kilometers across it and count only a few upright objects. No roads join them. No pylons, walls, sheds, chimneys, drill heads, or ruins interrupt the natural geometry. Yet the sound is unmistakably industrial.

A deep hammering travels through the ground at intervals of forty-three seconds. It is not sharp enough to locate. The impact arrives through the feet before it enters the ear, followed by a sustained roar like a blast furnace drawing breath. Far away, something releases pressure with a shriek that lasts most of an afternoon. Beneath these are lesser sounds: turbine whines, metallic chatter, the cavernous rotation of enormous bearings. They overlap until the open country seems occupied by a factory buried just below sight.

There is no factory.

The upright objects are animals.

The nearest may stand ten kilometers away and still appear large. It resembles, at first, a leaning stack of basalt plates: six black columns meeting around a swollen central body, with a narrow tower rising from the back. But the columns slowly reposition. Every few hours one lifts clear of the ground, revealing a circular foot corrugated like the face of a crushing mill. The foot travels forward through empty air and descends. The animal’s body follows later, settling into its new geometry with a concussion that shakes powder from the surrounding rocks.

These are the kiln herds, although “herd” suggests a closeness they do not possess. Each adult maintains several kilometers of space from the next. There may be only twenty visible across the whole plateau, yet they dominate every direction and every sense. Their calls cross the horizon. Their shadows pass like weather. Their subterranean limbs mesh through the crust in territories far larger than their bodies.

They do not eat plants or drink. Nothing here does.

A kiln animal lowers a ventral rasp against the ceramic ground and grinds away a translucent film of mineral salts. These are fed through successive chambers under immense pressure. Inside the animal, vanadium plates flex, chambers ignite, and catalytic blood—dark, hot, and entirely without water—circulates as metallic vapor. The salts are separated into useful fractions. Some become shell. Some become charge. Some are extruded behind the animal in slow, regular ingots.

The ingots look manufactured: identical hexagonal lengths of blue-gray alloy, stamped with repeating ridges. There are fields of them where generations of kiln herds have passed, laid in ordered fans by instinct. No mind designed the pattern. No hand has touched it. The plateau’s industry is biological, and its factories migrate on six legs.

Smaller fauna attend the giants.

They are overwhelming not because the land is crowded, but because nearly every rare thing one sees is alive. A distant spire detaches itself and crawls. A scatter of bright stones swivels to face the sun. What appears to be a seam in the rock opens thousands of jointed mouths. Even the empty air contains long transparent bodies, almost invisible except where their internal pistons flash.

The sky-borers drift at high altitude, gas-filled organisms kilometers long and thinner than cables. They draw charged dust through funnel-shaped mouths and expel it in disciplined pulses, producing the ceaseless turbine note overhead. Because they are nearly transparent, the sound seems to come from the blue itself. Occasionally one crosses the sun. Its internal anatomy becomes visible then: ranks of chambers, rotary valves, and slow black organs arranged with the severity of a refinery.

Near the ground are ratchet swarms. Each creature is no larger than a finger joint, but their colonies extend as single-file processions over hundreds of kilometers. They dismantle the alloy ingots left by the kiln herds, carrying microscopic portions toward no central nest. A procession may continue for centuries and never arrive anywhere. Each ratchet receives a fragment from the animal behind it, alters the fragment by one crystalline degree, and passes it forward. Their collective clicking resembles hail on a metal roof, though the sky is clear and there has never been rain.

Because individuals are separated by such distances, the plateau remains visibly vacant. Vast expanses hold only heat, stone, and noise. Life here has adopted the scale of infrastructure rather than abundance. Every animal is a process extended across space.

The deeper strangeness becomes apparent slowly.

Distance is not reliable on the plateau. The far ridges maintain their apparent size as one approaches. A kiln animal can remain on the horizon for three days, then be found standing behind the traveler without ever having crossed the intervening plain. Tracks sometimes precede the feet that make them. A sky-borer’s shadow may arrive hours before noon and remain after the creature has passed.

In certain basins, objects have more than one location. A ring of nine kiln animals is commonly visible in the southeast, arranged around a depression of white glass. There are, however, only three animals. Each stands at three points simultaneously, and each point bears full weight. When one animal moves, all three versions lift the same leg, but they do not set it down together. The delay between them grows by a few seconds every year. The oldest exposed strata suggest that, given enough time, one step will occupy an entire geological age.

The fauna do not seem troubled by this. Their metabolisms depend upon it.

A creature called the interval grazer feeds not on matter but on duration. It resembles a wide, low arch of red plates, empty beneath. Wherever it settles, noon lasts longer. Heat accumulates in the stretched hour, and the animal absorbs the excess through mirrored organs along its underside. After several decades it rises and leaves behind a patch of ground that has aged thousands of years. These patches are smoothly eroded, perfectly settled, and surrounded by sharp young rock. Their boundaries can be crossed in one stride. On one side, a fallen plate is still ringing from its impact; on the other, its dust has already compacted into stone.

This is how erosion occurs in a world with almost no weather. Time grazes unevenly. Surfaces are polished by repetition rather than wind. A grain of black mineral may strike the same ridge once, then strike it again in a neighboring version of the afternoon, and again, until the edge is rounded by one event happening countless times.

The loudest place is the White Circuit, a shallow valley near the plateau’s center. It contains no structure, but the exposed bedrock is divided into enormous concentric bands of conductive mineral. Kiln herds gather—widely spaced—along these bands. Sky-borers align overhead. Ratchet columns enter through narrow fissures. Once every seventy years, all the animals begin the same operation.

The process takes eleven months.

At first there is only a low synchronization of pulses. The hammering underfoot becomes regular. Then charge crawls through the concentric stone, raising blue threads of static between grains. The kiln animals open their dorsal towers and vent columns of ionized metal. The sky-borers rotate in the upper air. Their turbine song deepens until loose stones tremble uphill.

Nothing moves quickly. The immense mechanism assembles itself through patience: an animal taking one step, another turning by a degree, a colony passing particles one by one. Nevertheless the force involved is terrifying. By the final month, the whole valley operates like a dynamo with no designed components. Lightning circles horizontally without touching the ground. The daylight flickers between nearly identical versions of itself. Shadows multiply, hesitate, and choose different directions.

What the Circuit produces is unknown. There is no product afterward, no pile, offspring, signal, or visible transformation. Some observers would say it manufactures continuity—that without the cycle, the plateau’s overlapping locations and unsynchronized years would separate completely. Others might say it is merely mating behavior on a scale too large to recognize.

Afterward, the animals disperse over decades.

Silence never quite returns. The plateau keeps the residual hum of buried motion. Far-off bodies stamp, vent, saw, and resonate. The noises do not indicate haste. Nothing here hurries. A shriek may last a week; an impact may take an hour to finish arriving. Even violence is gradual. Predators, if that is what they are, stalk by altering the geometry ahead of their prey, narrowing a hundred kilometers of open ground over several generations until two paths become one.

There are no shelters and no artifacts. Nothing offers shade except an animal, and standing beneath one is unwise: their shadows sometimes possess mass. There is no drinkable substance, no edible tissue in any familiar sense, and no cool refuge under the stone. The higher one climbs along the pale shelves, the hotter the air becomes, as though altitude approaches a second ground burning above.

At evening the light remains almost normal. The sun lowers amber through metallic haze. The pale crust turns honey-colored; the black basins take on a dull violet sheen. Across the immense open spaces, the fauna become silhouettes of walking towers and suspended machinery. Their calls roll from rim to rim.

Then the first stars appear in the wrong places.

Some are only the incandescent vents of creatures moving far above the atmosphere. Some are holes through which another afternoon shines. Some descend over the course of centuries toward precise points on the plateau, where patient animals wait with their mouths open.

The darkness never makes the land feel empty. It reveals how thoroughly the emptiness is inhabited.
