The city occupies a plain so recently made that its edges are still sharp.

It lies low beneath a ceiling of warm, charcoal cloud, spread across hundreds of kilometers of black-green ground. There are no hills in the ordinary sense—only long pressure ridges where the plain has buckled, and fresh escarpments no taller than buildings, their faces glossy as broken ceramic. Rain seldom falls across the whole region. Instead, moisture gathers locally: beneath cloud knots, around certain towers, in the wakes of passing machines. A square kilometer may receive a violent warm shower while the country on every side remains dry. Water collects in shallow basins, runs briefly through angular channels, and vanishes into porous stone. For a few hours afterward, the channels shine like strips of hammered lead.

From a distance the place seems almost empty. The city’s structures stand kilometers apart.

One tower rises from a field of waist-high ferns; another is barely visible on the horizon, a dim vertical thread. Between them extend enormous commons of vegetation—broadleaf carpets, reed forests, low orchards growing without paths or fences. There are plazas wide enough to contain weather systems, crossed by perhaps a single bridge. Transit arches leap over vacant land and descend beyond sight. Their spans have no supports. Some are unfinished, ending cleanly in the air.

The architecture is urban in intention but not in crowding. The city was designed for processes rather than bodies. Its streets are atmospheric conduits. Its public squares receive migrations. Its empty towers regulate gravity, pollination, memory, or rain.

The ground is dark, young material extruded less than a century ago by machines buried below the plain. It has not had time to soften. Wherever roots have not yet taken hold, the surface keeps crisp folds and vitreous edges. Footsteps can snap off flakes thin enough to show amber when held to the light. There is little dust. Ravines are narrow incisions with unweathered walls, cut during sudden releases of captured water. At their bottoms lie bright pools, each crowded with translucent larvae and mats of luminous algae.

Vegetation has nevertheless arrived with overwhelming vigor. It does not form one continuous forest. Instead, life occupies the plain in dense islands separated by startling expanses of naked substrate. A grove may be so lush that every trunk carries vines, epiphytes, cup fungi, hanging roots, and wet nests; then it stops along a mathematically exact line. Beyond that boundary is a kilometer of bare black ground with nothing on it but condensation.

Many plants are products of the city. Their leaves are tuned to the weak light, broad and nearly black, with metallic undersides that direct stray photons inward. At dusk—which differs little from noon—their veins flicker with stored energy. Some unfold only when a tower passes a pulse through the soil. Whole meadows open at once, millions of pale flowers turning upward with the soft, vast motion of fabric being shaken out.

The warmth is constant and close. It is not the warmth of sunlight, for the sun appears only as a brighter bruise behind the cloud deck. Heat rises from the plain in slow exhalations. Condensation pearls on walls, skin, bark, and exposed metal. Beneath the largest leaves, the air smells of green sap and hot stone. In vacant transit chambers, water beads along ceilings and falls at measured intervals into gutters that lead nowhere visible.

Nothing here is quiet.

The city produces a continuous sound so immense that hearing it as a single noise is impossible. There are layers: the oceanic bellow of air moving through kilometer-wide ventilation mouths; infrasonic pulses from foundations adjusting to the planet’s crust; the metallic cry of unsupported bridges flexing in invisible fields. High above, cloud harvesters drag charged filaments through the atmosphere, making thunder without lightning. Every few minutes a pressure gate opens somewhere beyond the horizon, and a low concussion crosses the plain, bending the vegetation in sequence.

The fauna answers at equal volume.

Flocks of foil-winged animals inhabit the open air between towers. Tens of thousands lift together, their rigid feathers clattering like sheets of rain on a roof. Amphibious grazers gather around temporary pools and call through resonant cavities in their chests, each note deep enough to tremble in standing water. In the groves, small six-legged browsers bark, whistle, and strike hollow trunks with armored forelimbs. Pale hunting forms move above the canopy on sacs of heated gas, venting steam when they descend. Their prey scatter beneath them in flashing concentric waves.

Much of this noise is not communication. It is navigation. Visibility is poor, distances are huge, and the city itself changes position by degrees. Every animal maps the region acoustically. Towers announce their coordinates. Groves hum at distinct frequencies through networks of roots. Pools sing as their levels rise and fall. Even the rain has an address: droplets passing through localized fields acquire a faint pitch, so a shower approaches as a swelling chord before it can be seen.

The few beings who use the city’s rooms have adapted accordingly. Walkways contain zones of engineered silence, narrow and sharply bounded. Entering one feels like stepping through a wall. Outside, the world shakes the ribs; inside, a falling bead of water is painfully distinct. These silent corridors are the true roads, connecting distant structures through tunnels, suspended causeways, and open strips where active cancellation follows the traveler like a transparent canopy.

There are buildings, though many resist the category. Some appear to be enormous empty frames, each side several kilometers long, marking volumes of air rather than enclosing them. Others are deep shafts lined with balconies for species that never touch the ground. Residential clusters hang beneath transit arches in scattered knots, their chambers grown from milky material that repairs itself after storms. A civic district consists of seven shallow lakes and a ring of machines that continually rearrange their shorelines. The city archives changes in water shape: laws, histories, and names are encoded as coves, islands, currents, and depths.

At the center of the plain is no dense downtown. There is a greater emptiness.

Here the vegetation draws back from a circular basin forty kilometers across. The basin’s floor is a flawless dark mirror, not liquid but a field holding a molecular layer of water perfectly level above the stone. Clouds and distant towers appear in it with more clarity than they possess in the air. Birds refuse to cross. Calls arriving at its edge travel around the circumference and return altered, sometimes hours later, carrying sounds not yet made.

Around this mirror stand the city’s oldest structures, though “old” means only that they preceded the ground on which they now rest. They are slender pillars with surfaces that cannot be viewed from one angle twice. Condensation climbs them. Seeds fall upward along their sides and disappear into apertures among the clouds. During the warmest part of the cycle, the pillars bend imperceptibly toward one another, and the entire basin issues a note below hearing. Animals freeze. Pools stipple. Fern spores erupt from the groves in green columns.

Then the weather changes.

Rain forms first beneath the leaves, rising as droplets from root systems and drifting toward the cloud layer. Some of it condenses again and returns. Some is captured by airborne fauna. The rest gathers over the mirror until the air can no longer hold it. A storm collapses downward in one compact mass, a gray cylinder linking cloud to plain. Within it, lightning moves slowly enough to follow with the eye. The basin accepts the impact without a ripple.

Afterward, water escapes through newly opened channels. For a day or two the city becomes riverine. Sudden streams pour along streets, through groves, under levitating bridges, and across raw stone. Fishlike creatures emerge from sealed hollows in the ground and swim downstream into temporary wetlands. Predators follow on narrow jointed legs. Flowers open at the waterline, set seed within hours, and fold into waterproof bulbs before the channels dry.

No season lasts. Conditions travel instead of time. A patch of monsoon crosses the plain like an animal. A warm dry interval lingers around a tower. Night pools in some districts while others remain in endless gray twilight. The city does not control these states so much as arrange meetings between them.

Its governing intelligence is distributed among structures, roots, animals, and weather. No single component knows the whole design. A bridge may lower because a migratory herd has changed its call. A grove may move its boundary after interpreting mineral traces in rain. A tower may vanish from one horizon and appear on another because the soil beneath it has decided that its shadow is needed elsewhere.

Thus the emptiness is never inert. Space is one of the city’s working materials.

The great intervals between structures permit echoes to mature, storms to organize, migrations to turn, and invisible machines to perform operations that require unoccupied air. What looks vacant is reserved for events: the annual descent of the cloud reefs; the crossing of the glass-backed herds; the blooming of plants whose roots are separated by kilometers but whose flowers form one continuous electromagnetic organ.

At dim noon, the plain appears endless. Black leaves lift in the warm wind. Far towers glow weakly through mist. A solitary transit arc rises over the vegetation and continues toward nothing visible. Everywhere, creatures call out their positions into the murk.

The city answers from below with a sound like a continent beginning to wake.
