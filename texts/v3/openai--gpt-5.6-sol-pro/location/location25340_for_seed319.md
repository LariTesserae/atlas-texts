The city occupies a high basin where the plateau has been worn almost level. From a distance it appears less built than exposed: a field of pale masonry, oxidized metal, and broad black roofs laid into terraces of ancient stone. Nothing rises very far. Towers would catch the warm, moisture-heavy winds, and over centuries the city has learned to remain low.

Its scale becomes apparent only from within. Roads continue for kilometers along the contours, sometimes passing beneath whole districts and emerging beside another escarpment. Reservoir walls resemble natural cliffs. Factories are sunk into the bedrock so deeply that their roofs serve as plazas, freight yards, or empty expanses where rainwater lies in shallow silver sheets. There are few landmarks tall enough to orient by. Direction is kept instead by gradients: the increasing heat near the eastern works, the mineral smell of the southern drains, the stronger vibration as one approaches the basin’s center.

The plateau is warm throughout the year. Even after rain, the stone holds heat. Water evaporates from paving and condenses again under overhangs, on pipework, and along the shaded faces of buildings. It is never abundant enough to form a lush country, but it is nearly always present. Walls sweat. Cut rock weeps through rust-colored seams. Gutters carry thin, persistent currents even beneath a clear sky. In the lower streets, water stands ankle-deep in old wheel ruts, filmed with oil and fine yellow pollen brought from somewhere beyond the plateau.

There are no great rivers. The water moves by seepage, collection, pressure, and return. It enters the city through the stone itself.

The basin was shaped by erosion long before the first foundation was laid. Rain and warm mist hollowed the plateau into nested shelves, leaving rounded scarps and deep channels with sides polished like worked masonry. Builders followed those forms rather than overcoming them. Streets occupy old drainage courses. Workshops fill caverns widened from natural hollows. Bridges cross ravines whose floors have been converted into covered conduits. In places it is difficult to tell whether an arch was carved by water or constructed by people. The oldest surfaces have acquired the same skin: gray, smooth, mineral-crusted, and softly concave from centuries of runoff.

Nothing here looks ruined, though almost everything looks weathered. Erosion is not regarded as damage. It is the city’s method of finishing things.

New buildings are left with excess stone around their corners. New stairs are cut too sharp and too high. Over generations, feet, condensation, and mineral-laden rain bring them to their intended shape. Engineers account for loss as another culture might account for growth. Doorways are made narrow so that they will be correct in two hundred years. Drainage inscriptions deepen as the surrounding wall wears away. Some streets are officially described as “not yet mature.”

Vegetation survives only in separated, stubborn patches. Silver reeds grow around settling ponds but seldom higher than a person’s knee. Dark moss occupies the undersides of pipes and the interiors of abandoned cisterns. On open ground there are wiry grasses, red succulents, and low shrubs with leaves like folded metal. Their roots seek cracks rather than soil; there is little true soil left. Most has long since washed into the basin’s filtration works or been trapped in the lower terraces and baked into dense clay.

The city does not plant avenues of trees. Trees interfere with surveying, split conduits, and suffer in the shallow ground. A few survive in protected courts, enormously old and oddly stunted, their trunks spreading horizontally over stone. They are visited as one might visit monuments.

The dominant fact of the place is its sound.

At the center of the basin, below a succession of circular retaining walls, stand the pressure houses. They draw warm groundwater from fractures deep beneath the plateau, separate salts and metals from it, and force the remainder through the municipal system. Their engines are immense, slow machines. Many complete only a few revolutions each minute. Yet every movement releases a tremendous, sustained report: a descending metallic roar, a blow of compressed air, the deep concussion of pistons settling into chambers larger than houses.

The sound never stops. It has no urgency and almost no rhythm perceptible over short spans. It is simply there—deafening, continuous, and patient.

Closer to the works, speech becomes impossible without throat microphones or hand signs. Windows are thick and slightly bowed. Doors close against layered felt and water-filled seals. Loose objects migrate across shelves. Rain trembles in puddles, and condensation gathers into geometric patterns on vibrating panes. At night, sleepers place ceramic plugs in their ears, though these do little against the vibration transmitted through bone and bedframe.

Farther out, the noise divides into components. There is the steady bellow of the pressure houses, the high electrical whine of substations, the rattle of elevated ore lines, and the vast soft thunder of water descending through hidden spillways. Freight engines move through trenches under the streets. One rarely sees them, but their passage lifts grit from cracks and sends ripples through cups.

The strange thing is how slow everything appears. Cranes turn with solemn deliberation. Trains are long and heavily loaded, creeping for hours along a visible ridge. The wheels of the pumping stations can be watched all afternoon without seeming to move, though by evening their marked spokes have changed position. Even steam escapes in broad, languid columns, spreading beneath the humid air rather than rising.

The city is loud not because it is frantic, but because it is heavy.

Its industry processes what the water brings up: iron salts, pale clays, sulfur, traces of copper, and translucent crystals that form only under pressure. These materials pass through settling courts, kilns, electrolysis halls, and casting houses. Finished goods leave by rail across the western shelf. Waste is not piled. It is dissolved, vitrified, pressed into blocks, or fed into the continually repaired retaining walls. The city has been manufacturing itself from its own residue for so long that certain districts are made largely from the byproducts of older districts.

Brick here is nearly black and rings when struck. Concrete grows a white mineral fur. Copper roofs are green only briefly; the atmosphere turns them a deep blue-gray. Iron is allowed to rust to a stable red shell and then sealed with wax distilled from the reeds.

The streets are unusually empty for a city of this size. Habitation is concentrated inside thick communal blocks, while enormous areas are given over to conduits, inspection terraces, switching yards, evaporation fields, and safety clearances around buried machinery. A person crossing one of the upper plazas may walk ten minutes without passing another. Then a shift gate opens and several hundred workers emerge together, dressed in light-colored coats and vibration masks, moving in quiet groups beneath the roar.

Because ordinary calls cannot be heard, the city communicates visually. Signal lamps burn even during the day. Mechanical arms indicate pressure states from rooftops. Pavement lines change color when underground channels are unsafe. Public clocks have no bells; they release visible plumes of white vapor. Every district has its own pattern of shutters, flags, and rotating mirrors.

There are laws governing resonance. Certain intervals may not be sounded near the pressure houses. Long enclosed corridors are broken by angled walls so that no accidental tone can gather strength. Musical instruments are licensed according to the rooms in which they will be played. A sustained note at the wrong frequency can loosen old mortar, confuse a valve sensor, or make the water in a sealed cistern rise against gravity.

This last phenomenon is accepted without full explanation.

Water in the basin is sensitive to vibration. Under particular combinations of engine cycle and atmospheric pressure, droplets crawl uphill along scored stone. Puddles divide into concentric rings and remain so for hours. In abandoned tunnels, thin streams have been observed clinging to ceilings, moving toward the central works. Engineers chart these events and call them harmonic reversals. Older residents say the plateau is remembering the sea that once covered it.

The city’s maps include waterways that exist only as sound.

Fauna is scarce. The noise drives away most large animals, and the mineral water supports little ordinary aquatic life. Small pale lizards live in expansion joints, emerging during maintenance silences with an almost frantic speed. Moths gather around blue industrial lamps, their wings dusted with metallic particles. A few black marsh birds nest in distant settling fields where the roar is softened by banks of reed and clay. They do not sing. Their courtship consists of opening and closing their wings in precise, visible sequences.

There are also the so-called gauge eels, though no one agrees whether they are animals. They appear occasionally in transparent sections of high-pressure pipe: thin, dark shapes holding perfectly still against currents that should carry them away. When the pumps stop, they vanish. Dissections have never produced a specimen.

Rain comes often but rarely as a storm. It begins as condensation everywhere at once: beads on rails, wetness on skin, a darkening of dust. Then a warm, fine rain settles over the plateau and may continue for days. The city becomes reflective. Roofs merge with reservoirs. Terraces disappear behind veils of spray. Every light doubles itself on the ground.

During these wet periods, the noise grows deeper. Stone channels fill, hidden turbines engage, and the entire basin seems to hum at one enormous pitch. Residents feel the change before instruments record it. Work slows. Freight is secured. People gather in interior courts where suspended lamps sway by fractions of a degree.

Nothing dramatic usually follows. Water is received, processed, and released. The plateau darkens, then dries from the upper shelves downward. Salt flowers appear on walls. Mud contracts around the bases of pylons. The sparse plants unfold for a few days and then close again.

Change here is measured in decades: a drainage stair becoming a chute, a retaining wall bending inward, a factory district migrating gradually upslope as lower chambers mineralize shut. Some buried machines have operated for centuries, maintained without ever being fully stopped. No living person has heard the whole city silent.

There is, however, an hour each year when the pressure houses are brought as close to stillness as the system permits. The turbines continue, and distant water remains audible, but the central pistons rest. The silence that follows is said to be intolerable. People hear blood moving through their bodies. They hear stone grains settling in walls and droplets descending through kilometers of rock. They hear, from far across the open basin, the wingbeats of the marsh birds.

Then the engines begin again, one by one.

A low impact travels beneath the city. Windows tremble. Water rises along the weeping cliffs. Signal arms turn toward their working positions. The plateau resumes its ancient, deafening breath.
