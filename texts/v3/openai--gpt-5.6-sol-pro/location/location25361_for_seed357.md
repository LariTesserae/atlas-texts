The high country is not stone with plants growing on it. There is no exposed stone here, and perhaps no stone beneath it.

The ground is made.

Across a hot plateau, immense root systems have woven themselves into load-bearing terraces: dark cables laid in crossing courses, tightened by growth, sealed with amber resins and packed with centuries of leaf fiber. Where the surface has split, the fractures reveal no soil, only more construction—pale compression layers, hollow vascular conduits, knots large enough to stand inside. The whole region is botanical architecture, though no hand or tool has ever shaped it. Every wall is a stem trained by neighboring stems. Every basin is a cupped leaf reinforced along its ribs. Every bridge is a living graft.

It is difficult to decide whether this is a forest, a city, or a single plant elaborating itself across a mountain.

The plateau extends through several broad valleys and rises again into distant green escarpments. Its surfaces are young and sharply defined. Newly extruded ridges retain the clean edges of folded tissue; fresh terraces stand in glossy tiers, not yet softened by decay. Root buttresses thrust from the plain like the ribs of ships under construction. Some are red and wet, their outer membranes not yet hardened against the sun. Others have already darkened to a near-black green.

Despite the abundance of vegetation, the place feels sparse. Growth does not form a conventional thicket. It organizes itself into widely separated structures with enormous open intervals between them: solitary towers of bundled stalks, low circular reservoirs, arches spanning empty air, and fields of taut living membrane. The open spaces are not barren. They are paved with a thin, continuous skin of mosslike cells, smooth as felt and warm underfoot, but almost featureless for kilometers. Here and there, a vertical leaf rises alone from that green floor, fifty meters high, turning its broad face to the ordinary white daylight.

The sky is close without being dark. Heat gathers beneath large clouds, and moisture hangs in the air as a fine, persistent condensation. Everything beads. Water forms along the undersides of leaves, follows their structural veins, and falls from precisely spaced tips. It clicks against the ground-skin, runs into grooves grown to receive it, and vanishes through valves that open at the touch of sufficient weight.

Much of the plateau’s water never becomes a stream. It moves inside the architecture.

You can hear it underfoot: pressure rushing through buried tubes, deep and rapid, occasionally interrupted by the heavy concussion of a chamber collapsing or filling. Transparent bladders swell along the sides of distant towers. Sunlight passes through them in green-gold blocks. When one reaches capacity, its lower seam parts and releases a white sheet of water over the terraces. The flood lasts less than a minute. Then the seam closes by curling inward, and the emptied bladder folds flat against its supporting ribs.

Rainstorms arrive often and violently. They do not cool the place for long. The rain strikes hot leaves, explodes into vapor, and fills the valleys with luminous mist. Water hammers the broad roofs of the plateaus and drums on hollow trunks. The sound is enormous: not the layered noise of an inhabited forest, but a few vast noises occupying great distances. Rain. The groan of fiber under strain. The cannon-crack of splitting pods. The roar of drainage shafts. The long, low note produced when wind passes through a grove of open stalks.

There are no insects beneath the leaves. No birds circle the reservoirs. No fish move in the suspended pools, and no hidden thing stirs the debris, because there is almost no debris. Dead material is immediately enclosed. Pale feeder threads emerge from the living pavement, wrap each fallen scrap, and draw it down through soft apertures. Silence follows closely behind every disturbance.

The absence of animals gives the flowers an unfamiliar character. They are not signs or invitations. They have no colors meant to be noticed by moving eyes, no fragrance cast outward in search of a visitor. Reproduction occurs by contact, weather, and architectural failure. At intervals, two towers lean together until their crowns interlock. They remain braced against each other for a season, exchanging fluids through hundreds of temporary grafts. Later, a storm pulls them apart. Long strips of their joined tissue tear free and sail downwind like wet cloth. Wherever these strips land, they fasten themselves to the ground-skin and begin laying foundations.

Other structures reproduce catastrophically. Tall seed pylons grow under internal pressure until they burst. Their hulls open along bright seams, flinging flat, heavy seeds over the plateau. The detonation can be heard from valley to valley. The seeds strike hard enough to cut shallow craters in the living surface. Within hours, the crater margins rise around them, knitting new retaining walls.

This is why the country always looks newly made. It does not permit weathering to proceed far. Rain cuts a channel; roots lip its edges and turn it into a conduit. A terrace slumps; the broken layers swell, divide, and return as buttresses. Wind tears a roof membrane; the loose portion twists into a vertical sail, catches more wind, and pulls an entire district into a new alignment. Erosion is not resisted so much as intercepted at its beginning and converted into construction.

Nothing holds still long enough to become picturesque.

On clear intervals between storms, the open plateau shines under a plain blue sky. The light is neither dim nor supernatural. It makes the strangeness more evident. Shadows lie cleanly beneath leaf towers. Water flashes on smooth green planes. Distant rain can be seen traveling across the country as a gray moving wall, followed by rapid changes of form: roofs inflating, basins tipping, stems bowing in sequence.

The heat is thickest in the low folds between terraces. There the air tastes vegetal and mineral despite the lack of exposed mineral—wet resin, bruised chlorophyll, hot cellulose, and the faint bitter vapor released by fresh construction. Condensation runs continuously down the vertical surfaces. Some walls weep clear water; others exude cloudy sap that hardens into translucent braces. New braces are soft enough to sag, and whole galleries sometimes deform before they set, giving certain districts the appearance of architecture glimpsed through moving water.

There are passages into the larger growths, though they were not made for travelers. Openings appear where layers must breathe or release heat. Inside, the air is hotter. Corridors follow branching vascular lines and repeatedly narrow to nothing. Floors pulse at long intervals. Light enters through thin tissue, turning chambers red, yellow, or submerged green. Columns thicken while one watches. A room may become impassable in an afternoon, while a blank wall nearby delaminates and reveals a hall that did not exist that morning.

There are no devices anywhere, no worked metal, no cut stone, no hinges, wheels, lamps, or written marks. Yet the functions of a built world are present in living form. Water is captured and routed. Loads are distributed. Heat is vented. Bridges grow toward opposite banks and splice themselves at the center. Damaged surfaces are removed and replaced. Districts expand around reservoirs. High towers flex to predict storms, their posture changing hours before the first cloud arrives.

It would be easy to imagine a vanished maker, but nothing suggests one. The making is local and continuous. Each piece responds only to pressure, moisture, light, injury, and the chemical messages of adjacent growth. The apparent plan arises over distance: a whole valley terraced for water, an escarpment braced against its own weight, a chain of hollow towers positioned so that the prevailing wind makes them resonate together.

When the wind strengthens, those towers begin to sound.

The lowest note is felt through the woven ground before it is heard. Then other trunks answer, each with a slightly higher tone. Water sloshes in their bases, altering the pitch. Membranes snap overhead. Seed hulls knock against their cradles. Rain approaches. The plateau becomes an instrument several valleys wide, loud enough to erase the sense of emptiness without filling it.

During the most violent storms, construction and destruction become indistinguishable. Towers bend until their crowns sweep the ground. Reservoir leaves overturn, pouring lakes through temporary canyons. Root bridges unzip along sacrificial seams and lash in the flood like severed ropes, though they remain alive. Whole terraces lift as trapped air enters their lower chambers. Some float briefly, vast rafts of interlocked vegetation shifting downslope, before new roots spear outward and anchor them elsewhere.

Afterward, the country has changed.

A valley may have acquired a roof. A plain may now stand on end, its former surface shedding waterfalls. One of the distant green escarpments may have opened into a series of wet, gleaming chambers. The old paths are closed, but long new causeways have appeared across the open spaces, still pale and soft, their surfaces tightening in the heat.

Then the clouds break. Normal sunlight returns. Steam rises from the fresh-made land. Water continues to rush through its unseen vessels, and, in the complete absence of animal voices, the plateau resumes its immense work.
