At the crown of a young mountain, far above the cloud deck, there is a sea with no shore.

It occupies a roughly spherical volume less than a kilometer across. The water does not lie in a basin; it hangs in the open air, held around the summit by a local gravity that points inward from every direction. From outside, the sea resembles a dark blue lens balanced among black pinnacles. Rain falls upward into its underside. Snow approaches from the surrounding sky, touches the water, and becomes a brief silver skin before dissolving.

Within it, “high” and “deep” are difficult words. Down is always toward the mountain’s buried peak, though the direction changes as one swims around it. The summit stands at the center of the sphere like the core of a fruit: a cluster of fresh basalt blades, scarcely weathered, their fractures still angular and blue-black. They look newly broken because they are newly broken. The mountain continues to grow by minute increments. Over decades, pressure forces narrow fins of glass and metal-rich stone out from its central seams. They advance no faster than fingernails, but nothing stops them. Animals caught against these extrusions must move, adapt, or be divided.

There is no soil. There are no grasses, weeds, algae, rooted things, or drifting green films. Every surface is bare mineral or animal.

The central crags are crowded almost to opacity. Shells overlap shells; flexible bodies fill the spaces between rigid ones. The water has the density of a city without containing a single building. Chains of pale, ribbed creatures hang from one basalt point to another. Schools of coin-thin animals turn edge-on to squeeze through crevices. Heavy, many-legged grazers—though there is nothing here to graze—clamber across the rock and rasp iron oxide directly from it. Transparent sacs pulse in clusters, filtering metallic dust, microscopic eggs, shed cells, and the constant fine debris of predation.

Most of the life belongs to no familiar division. There are creatures whose shells are grown as continuous gears. There are colonies organized around rotating mineral cores. There are long, soft-bodied animals that ingest magnetite grains and align them into internal shafts, producing slow torque from the mountain’s field. Their organs do not resemble machines, yet they perform the work of engines: compression, ignition, pumping, separation, transmission.

The loudest are the mill-whales.

They are only six or seven meters long, but in the confined sea they seem enormous. Each carries a set of internal chambers lined with ceramic plates. Water enters through a ring of valves near the head, is compressed by muscular pistons, and leaves through narrow rear nozzles. Their metabolism runs on the oxidation of dissolved metals and sulfur compounds. Every few minutes one fires its chambers in sequence.

The resulting sound is not a call, exactly. It is labor.

**Clang—thrum—thrum—THOOM.**

The whole suspended sea answers. Basalt fins tremble. Shell colonies close their apertures. Clouds of silver larvae scatter, then gradually return. Several whales operating near one another produce the cadence of a distant stamping works: strokes almost synchronized, slipping apart, meeting again after hours. They circle the mountain slowly, following magnetic contours older than their species.

Other animals have adapted to this incessant machinery. Small blue scavengers cling to the whales’ exhaust grooves and feed on precipitated salts. Fan-jawed hunters wait near the outlet jets, where stunned planktonic fauna tumble in the turbulence. Dense balls of eyeless, finger-length creatures gather around warm valve tissue, humming against it with vibrating teeth. Parasites nest in bearing-like joints and are crushed into lubricant when the joints turn.

Nothing here has been manufactured, though nearly everything appears manufactured.

The central mountain releases current through veins of conductive ore. Certain sessile colonies collect that current. Their bodies are stacks of translucent mineral membranes, grown layer by layer, and between the membranes ions move in ordered streams. When enough charge accumulates, blue arcs leap from colony to colony. The flashes are bright but not rare enough to astonish the local animals. They are weather here: branching sparks, a sharp report, the smell and taste of altered salts.

In this way the sea is electrified without wires.

Its factories are organisms, its refineries are digestive tracts, its turbines are cartilage and stone. Production is continuous but patient. A generation of gear-shells may spend fifty years assembling a single toothed ring around a crystal nucleus. When complete, the ring begins to turn, driven by weak convection, and continues for centuries. Its purpose—if purpose is the word—is to grind suspended rock into grains fine enough for the colony’s young to absorb.

Despite the crowding and noise, change is slow. The same animals may remain attached to the same fracture for a human lifetime. Predators take hours to close their traps. A chase can last several days, hunter and prey moving through the packed water by fractions of a body length, each waiting for currents to open a route. Even the mill-whales seem nearly stationary at a distance. Their circuits take months.

The water is cool enough to numb exposed skin, but it does not freeze except at the outer boundary. There the sea meets open mountain air in a wavering, skinless surface. Wind tears droplets away; inward gravity bends them back. They orbit briefly as bright beads and fall into the water elsewhere. During storms, rain strikes the sphere from all sides. The impacts spread across the boundary in overlapping dimples, filling the interior with a continuous low concussion.

Light enters normally, or appears to. Day illuminates the outer waters in clear cobalt. Sunbeams cross the sphere, bend around its central mass, and reveal the innumerable suspended bodies in severe detail: hooks, valves, cilia, glass needles, muscular vanes, embryos coiled in amber shells. Because there are so many creatures, the rays seldom travel far without being interrupted. Shadows overlap until the central crags lie in a restless, mottled dusk.

Yet there is no true night at the core. Electrical colonies flash. Animals release stored daylight from mineral plates in their tissues. Some predators project flat white beams from cavities in their skulls, sweeping the rock as slowly as lighthouse lamps. Others carry rows of steady yellow organs with the impersonal color of furnace windows.

The sea’s outer layer is less crowded, though never empty. Here swim broad animals like transparent kites, their bodies stiffened by struts of grown silica. They sail the curved boundary, half in water and half pressed against the sky. Above the cloud deck, peaks surround them in every direction, and the horizon drops away. Through the clear skin of the suspended sea one can see snowfields thousands of meters below, their apparent position shifting with the inward pull of gravity.

At several points the mountain’s black spires pierce the water’s edge. They project briefly into open air, wet and steaming, then curve back into the sphere. Around these punctures live colonies of armored amphibious things. They have neither lungs nor gills in the usual sense. Their backs open into corrugated metal-colored leaves—not plant leaves, but heat exchangers—which condense mist and extract oxygen. They remain motionless for years, facing the wind, while the water behind them rings with the pounding of whales.

There is birth everywhere, but almost no softness. Eggs are screwed into cracks by spiral-bodied parents. Young hatch with cutting surfaces already hardened. Many species begin as drifting granules indistinguishable from mineral grit; only after passing through several digestive systems do they assemble into animals. Some cannot reproduce without being eaten. Some cannot mature without entering an electrical arc. One tiny species spends its youth as conductive paste inside the joints of larger creatures, then emerges decades later in a burst of needle-legged adults.

The ecological foundation is not sunlight and not vegetation. It is stone under stress.

The mountain’s central fractures release heat, electrons, hydrogen, sulfur, and fresh reactive surfaces. Life intercepts these gradients before they fade. What one organism oxidizes, another reduces. What one precipitates as waste becomes another’s skeleton. The entire sphere is a closed industrial metabolism wrapped around a growing mineral core.

There is little erosion because little has had time to erode. The sharp rocks are softened only by occupation. Every edge wears a sleeve of shells; every crevice is packed with filterers and ambush mouths. Where bare basalt does appear, it is glossy and recent, exposed by a crack that may have opened only a century ago. New fractures draw life toward them with the inevitability of food. Colonies migrate over generations, dismantling themselves at one end and rebuilding at the other.

The governing rule of the place is inwardness.

Water returns inward. Rain returns inward. Bodies sinking from any surface fall toward the central mountain. Even sound seems to return: each blow travels across the sphere, converges, and comes back distorted. Nothing easily escapes. A shed shell may circulate for millennia, becoming armor, abrasive, egg case, digestive stone, and armor again.

A second rule is that function precedes intention. Pistons evolve before anyone needs an engine. Electrical grids spread without planning. Assembly lines arise from feeding behavior. The landscape resembles an industrial complex because industry is simply one of the shapes that hunger takes here.

And beneath the roar, beneath the hydraulic reports and ceramic grinding, the young mountain continues its almost imperceptible ascent. It lifts the entire enclosed sea a few millimeters each century. The animals do not notice. The clouds below slowly recede. Fresh blades of basalt enter the crowded water, bright-edged and uninhabited.

They do not remain empty for long.
