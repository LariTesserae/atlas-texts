The place is called a garden, though nothing in it has ever grown from soil.

It occupies a shallow, oval basin no wider than a small hamlet, enclosed by walls of fitted cream-colored blocks. The walls lean outward by a degree or two, giving the impression that the garden has only just unfolded and has not yet decided whether to remain a room or become a valley. Their upper edges are sharp, unworn, and crowded with hand-cut sockets. Some sockets hold poles, bells, woven screens, or the small clay effigies placed there by the gardeners. Others contain darkness much deeper than the wall.

There is no natural ground. Underfoot lies a pavement of fired tiles, each tile incised before baking with branching channels. The channels resemble leaf veins, river systems, or maps of roads, but they carry no water. In the warmth they exude a clear, viscous oil distilled from pale mineral nodules embedded beneath the basin. It moves reluctantly, creeping perhaps a finger’s width in a day. Where several channels meet, the oil gathers into amber beads and hangs there without spreading.

This is what the garden drinks.

Its vegetation is lush, if vegetation is the right word. Dense thickets rise from ceramic collars mortared into the pavement. Their trunks are braided rope stiffened with resin and packed around cores of porous chalk. Their leaves are thin plates of hammered green stone, hide, mica, and lacquered shell. Many are plainly manufactured: stitch holes remain visible along their edges, and fingerprints have been fired permanently into the clay buds. Yet they unfold toward the light. They shade one another. They shed old leaves and produce new ones from seams nobody can pry apart.

Near the basin’s center, the growth becomes almost impassable. Reedlike rods of blue glass crowd together, jointed and warm to the touch. Copper vines climb them, although no one here knows how to smelt copper anymore. Low mats of felted fiber creep across the tiles, lifting themselves on thousands of tiny wooden pegs. There are fruiting bodies like lidded jars. Once every few years their lids turn aside, revealing clusters of fragrant red teeth. The teeth are collected, ground with stone pestles, and fed to the smallest animals.

Nothing is wet. Even the air is dry enough to tighten leather and split unsealed wood. The mineral oil does not evaporate, and the organisms do not contain water when cut. Their sap is smoke, powder, resin, or a brief spill of warm yellow light. Broken stems often continue growing on both sides of the break.

The garden is low and warm, sheltered from whatever lies beyond the walls. Heat rises through the tilework in broad, gentle patches. During the garden’s “day,” the pavement may become uncomfortable to bare feet, though the air remains pleasant beneath the dense artificial foliage. At “night,” warmth retreats into narrow paths, and animals gather along them in quiet rows.

The light comes from above, apparently. There is a sky over the garden, evenly blue in daylight and charcoal-colored at night, but the walls cast no consistent shadow from it. A lifted hand may cast its shadow east while its fingers cast theirs west. Gardeners determine the hour by watching the flowers rather than the sun. At midday, all cup-shaped blooms turn toward the same empty point about shoulder height among the central thickets. Looking directly at that point reveals nothing. Looking just beside it reveals a distant figure standing there, back turned, busy with an inaudible task.

The figure is never approached. The paths become longer when anyone tries.

Everything in the basin has been made, including the paths, the heat, the fertility, and perhaps the gardeners themselves. There is no exposed bedrock and no dust not produced by abrasion. Where a tile is lifted, another tile lies beneath it, facing downward. Beneath that is a narrow interval of hot darkness full of slowly moving cords. No tool has reached a bottom. Digging is forbidden now, less from reverence than from the practical observation that each removed layer slightly increases the number of walls surrounding the garden.

Construction here is accomplished with simple means. The inhabitants shape clay by hand, cut fiber with obsidian flakes, bore stone with bow drills, and bind structures with gut, resin, and wooden pins. They can repair almost anything visible. A cracked irrigation vein is patched with waxed cloth. A broken branch is splinted. A dislodged wall block is levered back into place by a dozen people with poles.

They cannot build the garden’s deeper mechanisms. They cannot reproduce the copper vines or the self-opening hinges inside certain flowers. Their most sophisticated devices are treadle lathes, kilns, counterweighted lifts, and small looms whose patterns are passed down as songs. Yet, from time to time, a craftsperson makes an ordinary basket and discovers on completion that its interior is larger than the garden.

Such baskets are covered and stored in the western arcade.

The western arcade is a row of twelve stone recesses. There were originally seven. No one remembers the additional five being constructed. Each contains shelves packed with tools, shed antlers, dormant bulbs, eggs, masks, lengths of cord, and sealed jars of mineral oil. The sixth recess is always cool. The tenth smells strongly of rain, although rain has no local meaning except as a word in old songs. In the twelfth, objects left overnight acquire a second shadow. If the objects are then removed, one shadow remains behind and eventually becomes a small, shy animal.

Animals are abundant, though seldom loud. Leaf-moths with wings of stitched membrane cling to the undersides of stone foliage. Six-legged lizards, plated in glazed scales, graze on chalk trunks and leave delicate spirals of ceramic droppings. Warm-bodied beetles bore into wooden vines, making such regular holes that the gardeners use them as measuring instruments. Flocks of pale, long-legged things move through the understory with their heads lowered. Their bodies resemble folded paper lanterns, and their feet make no sound on the tile.

There are also heavier creatures: squat browsers with basket-woven ribs, black tongues, and faces hidden behind naturally growing masks. They browse on lacquer, stripping it from leaves in curling ribbons. When alarmed, they do not run. They become extremely far away while remaining the same apparent size. A patient observer can watch one walk back into nearness over the course of several days.

Predation occurs quietly. A carnivorous trellis may close around a moth without more than a wooden click. Thread-snakes descend from the canopy, unwind a sleeping beetle, and take its place so exactly that the beetle’s companions do not notice. The garden’s largest predator is believed to inhabit gaps: the space under a door, the slit between stacked tiles, the pause between two knocks. Its tracks are occasionally found crossing open pavement, but only when no gap exists nearby.

Because sound carries strangely, the inhabitants speak little. A loud noise is liable to return altered. A shout may come back as a hammer blow, a birdcall, or a spoken sentence in the voice of someone not yet born. Bells are used only at planting and funerals, and each bell is muffled immediately after a single strike. Ordinary work proceeds amid scrapes, dry rustling, insect ticks, the tap of stone tools, and the soft tension-noise of growing fiber. The overall quiet is not emptiness. It is the quiet of a crowded room in which every presence is listening.

The garden changes slowly. Its oil advances through the pavement’s veins. Thickets tighten by fractions. Walls gain recesses. Paths develop forks, usually overnight but sometimes across generations. Since the masonry is still sharp and the tiles show almost no wear, the place seems newly completed; yet genealogies extend back hundreds of years. The contradiction is accepted. Age here does not accumulate on surfaces. It gathers elsewhere—possibly in the western baskets, possibly in the dark sockets along the walls.

There are seasons, but they are architectural rather than climatic.

In the Season of Narrowing, passages between the thickets contract until inhabitants must turn sideways to pass. Animals become slender. Furniture is dismantled and reassembled lengthwise.

In the Season of Facets, smooth things acquire fresh edges. Fruit becomes angular, voices become brittle, and shadows divide into planes. This is considered a young season, favorable for making tools and dangerous for childbirth.

In the Season of the Open Joint, every connection loosens slightly: knots, hinges, bones, kinships, promises. Gardeners spend this season walking from structure to structure, tightening what they wish to preserve.

The rarest is the Season of Soil. For several days the seams between the tiles fill with a dark granular substance smelling of heat and old rooms. Seeds dropped into it produce ordinary green shoots—soft, wet-looking, and impossible. The shoots never survive. By the fourth day they flatten into painted images on the pavement, and the dark grains climb the walls and vanish over the top.

No one has seen what is outside.

Ladders placed against the enclosing wall always prove one rung too short, no matter how many rungs are added. Birds can cross the wall, but those that return are subtly unfinished: missing a color, a habit, a left side. During the quietest hours, one may hear faint scraping beyond the masonry, as if builders are laying another garden against this one. Sometimes a wall socket releases a breath of hot, mineral-scented air. Sometimes a hand reaches through, places a newly made block on the parapet, and withdraws before anyone can look up.

The people have one central rule: anything that begins growing must be given a name before sunset.

Unnamed growth does not stop at its own boundaries. It may spread from vine to wall, from wall to distance, from distance to memory. Once, according to the tile-keepers, a pale tendril emerged from a crack beside the central path and went unnoticed for an afternoon. By dusk it had grown through every depiction of the garden, including maps, embroidery, dreams, and spoken descriptions. Removing it required burning three archives and forbidding an entire adjective.

Even now, some elders pause when describing the color of the inner leaves. They know there was once a precise word for it.

They know, too, that the word is still growing somewhere.
