The city occupies the upper faces of a mountain range so immense that its valleys are not visible from the streets.

From the parapets, the land falls away into layers of warm haze: first the black retaining cliffs, then remote shelves of copper stone, then only a brown-gold depth in which other summits appear to float. Some of those summits are hundreds of kilometers away. They resemble islands, though there has never been a sea here. The lowest known ground lies beneath an atmosphere too dense and hot for machinery to survive; the people of the heights speak of it as the Furnace, but almost nothing descends far enough to confirm what it is.

The upper air is thin, dry, and comfortably warm where it moves through sunlight. There are no rivers, springs, snowfields, or clouds. Water exists only as an industrial contaminant, sealed in ceramic ampoules and watched carefully because it attacks several of the local alloys. In the open landscape, exposed moisture boils away or is broken apart by the reactive dust. The weather is made instead from minerals: pale storms of powdered feldspar, veils of conductive carbon, and slow atmospheric tides that carry metallic vapor upslope from the Furnace.

The mountains are old enough to have lost the drama of sharpness. Their ridges are colossal but rounded, shaved by uncounted ages of dust. Basalt escarpments have been fluted into ranks of vertical ribs. Whole plateaus lean under their own weight. In places the rock has flowed—not molten, but compressed over geological time—creating smooth folds like dark cloth laid across buried machinery.

Between these forms lie salt fields without salt’s familiar whiteness: broad lilac pans of vanadates, greenish expanses of copper compounds, black basins paved in hexagonal plates. Their cracks may be ten meters wide and descend farther than lamps can reach. Nothing grows from them. There is no moss, no grass, no root prying at the stone. Every surface belongs wholly to geology, construction, or animals.

The city is consequently stark, but not crowded.

Its districts are scattered across dozens of summits and joined by bridges so long that their middles vanish into atmospheric distortion. Towers rise directly from the mountain cores, anchored kilometers deep against the slow shifting of the crust. Most are squat by local standards, built broad and heavy to withstand the pressure tides. A few elevator spires climb far above the habitable air, their upper platforms shining with ordinary daylight while the streets below turn amber.

Construction uses vitrified basalt, nickel foam, sintered dust, and slabs cut from a naturally occurring ceramic that rings when struck. Walls are thick, window openings narrow, roofs flat and weighted. Almost every structure has been repaired so many times that its original outline survives only as an administrative fiction. New black patches overlap older gray ones. Pipes pass through abandoned chambers. Streets become factory floors, factory floors become nesting vaults, and old transit tunnels are gradually absorbed into the warm interior of the mountain.

There are foundries, pressure mills, cable works, electrostatic refineries, and vast inclined conveyors that creep continuously up the cliffs. Yet the industrial city is mostly quiet. Its machines are designed for endurance rather than speed. Gears turn once in an hour. Ore crawls through enclosed ducts on fields of charge. Smelters operate below the threshold of hearing, using slow gradients of heat drawn from the deeper rock. Nothing hammers if pressure can accomplish the task over a month.

From a distance, industry reveals itself as motion too gradual to see: a ridge that changes color over several years; a slag terrace extending by the width of a hand each day; a freight bridge sagging, imperceptibly, beneath a caravan of mineral barges.

Close by, there are small sounds. Ceramic bearings tick as they cool. Static snaps from railings. Dust whispers around corners. Far below, a stress fracture travels through the range with a sound like a door closing in another world.

The abundant animals make less noise than the machines.

They are everywhere once the eye learns what counts as an animal here. Flat, many-jointed things lie against sunlit walls, their backs indistinguishable from oxidized metal. They open dark vanes to absorb heat and electrical charge. Thread-bodied grazers move through deposits of reactive dust, harvesting microscopic mineral catalysts from one grain at a time. In the bridge shadows hang translucent sacks filled not with liquid but with warm, dense gas; at dusk they release themselves and drift between towers.

None possess wet flesh. Their tissues are lattices of silicone compounds, carbon membranes, salts, and flexible metallic fibers. They do not drink. They feed on gradients: heat against cold, charge against ground, oxidized stone against newly exposed stone. The larger cliff animals carry internal grinding chambers and swallow pieces of rock. Colonies of simpler organisms inside them separate useful compounds, and the exhausted material falls out as fine magnetic sand.

This makes the city attractive to them. Every cable leaks a little field. Every refinery sorts minerals into unnaturally rich concentrations. Every ventilator creates differences in pressure and temperature. The animals gather around infrastructure as terrestrial creatures might gather at a watering place.

Long-legged walkers stand beneath transmission lines with their forelimbs raised into the corona. Hundreds may occupy a single ridge, motionless for days, black against the sky. When sufficiently charged, they fold themselves and descend the cliffs in careful, deliberate steps. Smaller creatures follow in their footprints to consume glassy residues left by their feet.

Inside the city, coin-sized scavengers clean oxidation from electrical contacts. Pale ribbon animals inhabit elevator shafts. Heavy six-sided crawlers, each as broad as a house, migrate through industrial districts on routes older than the streets. Buildings are required to preserve these passages. When one of the crawlers approaches, gates are opened, production pauses, and workers lay conductive plates across the rails. The animal proceeds without haste, carrying on its back a crowded ecology of mites, filament colonies, and sleeping gas bladders.

Predators are uncommon but conspicuous. They resemble loose arrangements of blades. They do not chase prey; they place themselves across migration routes and wait, sometimes for decades. The oldest have been mistaken for public sculpture. A sudden rearrangement of metal in an otherwise empty plaza may be the only evidence of feeding.

Most life is synchronized to cycles much longer than day and night. The atmosphere rises and falls along the mountain range over a period of forty-three years. During the upslope phase, warm gases bring sulfur, carbon, and trace metals from below. Fauna become active, factories open outer vents, and entire districts acquire a faint bronze tarnish. During the downslope phase, the air clears and thins. Animals seal themselves into cracks. Bridges contract. The city closes its highest neighborhoods and transfers work into the mountain.

There are shorter rhythms as well. At noon, sunlight is strong but not cruel, casting clean shadows from the towers. Toward evening the lower atmosphere glows from heat retained in the abyss, so that cliffs are lit dimly from beneath. Night is never fully black. Electrical discharges move silently across distant peaks, and the mineral flats hold a dull, delayed luminescence.

The world’s strangest rule concerns height.

Here, elevation is not merely position. Certain reactions become possible only above particular levels, as if altitude were an ingredient. Alloys that are inert in the lower workshops become active when carried up a spire. Animals born on one plateau may harden into stone if taken a kilometer downward. Stored electrical charge changes character across invisible horizontal boundaries. Because of this, the city’s manufacturing is arranged vertically. Materials begin deep within the mountain, rise through successive districts, and are transformed stage by stage as pressure, gravity, and some less understood property of height alter them.

At the highest platforms, factories make substances that cannot exist below. They are lowered in sealed cages and installed quickly before they decay: weightless bearings, one-directional glass, structural members that remember tension. The work is dangerous not because it is violent, but because errors can remain patient. A flawed component may wait a century before abruptly obeying the physics of the altitude where it was made.

Beyond the urban summits, traces of older construction cross the range. There are roads wide enough to be mistaken for plateaus, evenly spaced pylons weathered into blunt hills, and circular excavations whose opposite rims lie beyond the horizon. No living city admits to having built them. Animals nest in their seams; modern refineries quarry their outer layers. Even these ruins are sparse against the scale of the mountains.

Nothing here happens quickly unless it is breaking, and even breakage often takes generations.

A bridge begins to fail as a change in tone noticed by maintenance listeners. A cliff migration is charted in millimeters. A species may spend several human lifetimes crossing one exposed basin. Districts are abandoned gradually, room by room, as atmospheric boundaries drift. Elsewhere, equally slowly, a new neighborhood condenses around a mine head or an animal route.

The result is not emptiness, though emptiness occupies most of it. It is a landscape of enormous intervals: between towers, between sounds, between events, between one moving creature and the moment the eye recognizes that it has moved.

Under the ordinary sun, the old mineral ranges stretch beyond sight. The city clings to their highest surfaces, black, angular, and faintly warm. Conveyors advance. Animals drink from fields no one can see. Dust passes over the bridges in thin, unhurried sheets. Far below, hidden by depth and atmosphere, the Furnace breathes against the roots of the world.
