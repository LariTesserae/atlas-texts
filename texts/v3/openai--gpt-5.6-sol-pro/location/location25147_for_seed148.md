The district lies below the surrounding plain, though not in any depression that can be seen from outside. From the heath there is only a low rim of black leaves, perhaps the circumference of a grove. Step through it, and the ground descends by shallow streets into a city-sized hollow compressed within the bounds of a few acres.

It is called a district only because it has intersections.

There are no conventional buildings. The vegetation has assumed that work. Great stalks stand in rows, each hollow stem divided into chambers by pale membranes. Their roots braid into pavements, culverts, retaining walls, and flights of shallow stairs. Broad leaves overlap as roofs, so densely layered that daylight arrives as a weak mineral glow. Windows occur where the plants have grown transparent patches in their skins. Doorways open and close according to pressure, temperature, and an obscure circulation of sap. Some lead into narrow rooms smelling of cucumber and cold iron. Others open directly into the undersides of distant leaves.

Nearly everything is alive, but almost nothing occupies the open air.

The streets are broad, bare, and dark. Between the columnar stems are spaces too generous for the little settlement: empty concourses, vacant depots, circular plazas with no monument at their center. Even where vines span the walls, they lie flat, incorporated into the surfaces like wiring. Moss does not soften the streets; it composes them, a close-grained black-green tissue tough as rubber. The city’s abundance is pressed into skins, membranes, and structural planes, leaving the volume between them strangely vacant.

At first the ground seems level. It is not soil, exactly, but a fresh vegetal crust stretched over something still forming. Beneath it, fine roots sort grains of clay and powdered stone into layers. They are making geology rather than breaking it down. Pebbles bud from knots in the root mat, soft and translucent at first, then hardening over several nights. Runnels appear before water reaches them. Steps rise where repeated footsteps are expected, even if no one has yet walked there. The district is embryonic in this way: its erosion precedes its weather. Channels, potholes, collapsed edges, and polished thresholds are grown as forecasts.

Some forecasts never come true. These remain as dry canals curling up walls or staircases worn smooth by absent generations.

Water is occasional and local. Cool moisture gathers inside the architecture, beading along the veins of ceilings and dripping into narrow cisterns. Rain does not fall across the whole district. It selects a room, an alley, sometimes a single chair-shaped growth, and rains there for an hour while the neighboring surfaces remain dry. The drops are slow and heavy. They leave dark bruises on leaves and make the root-pavement contract with a faint muscular tension.

There is a river, but only in sections. One may find twenty meters of black current passing beneath a bridge, entering through a brick-shaped root aperture and vanishing under a curtain of fern. There is no upstream or downstream beyond this. Elsewhere, several streets away, another section of the same river may stand vertically in a shaft, flowing upward without spilling. Small pale fish move between these fragments by disappearing into reflections. During dry periods they can sometimes be seen swimming through glossy walls.

The air remains cool throughout the year. It carries the wet chill of an underground platform, though no true underground exists here. Breath fogs near certain intersections and nowhere else. Metal sweats. Leaf skins stay cold enough that a hand laid against one leaves a clear print, which may persist for days and slowly develop a second, smaller handprint inside it.

Industrial apparatus is embedded throughout the living city. There are pump houses made from swollen trunks, their piston chambers lined with bark. Root-driven conveyors pass beneath the streets, carrying seeds, mineral nodules, molted shells, and cylinders of compressed pollen. In the largest plaza, six vertical flywheels turn without axles. They are woody and ribbed, as if grown from the idea of machinery rather than built to a plan.

Power travels in blue pulses through vine bundles. The pulses illuminate one junction after another, too briefly to show the whole street. Each flash reveals valves opening in walls, ladders flexing, and small animals freezing in place. Then darkness closes again.

No one tends these mechanisms. They operate as parts of the ecosystem: digestion performed at civic scale. One factory pulps fallen leaves into sheets used to extend roofs. Another takes in muddy water and produces smooth black seeds, each warm enough to steam. A third does nothing visible except exhale a cube of cold air every eleven minutes. The cube retains its shape as it slides down the street. Moths gather along its edges.

The fauna is everywhere, although the district can appear deserted for long intervals. Most creatures live within the thin thickness of surfaces. Beetles travel under translucent pavement in orderly lanes. Long-bodied mammals sleep inside handrails, their fur protruding like velvet upholstery. Flocks of small birds inhabit the roof leaves, but they do not fly through the open streets. When disturbed, they flatten into dark markings and race along the foliage as two-dimensional silhouettes.

Larger animals are rarer but not scarce. Pale, six-legged browsers emerge when condensation is heaviest. They graze inscriptions from the walls, leaving the surfaces blank and faintly bleeding sap. Their joints turn silently. Behind them come narrow predators resembling greyhounds assembled from bundled reeds. The pursuit may cross a plaza in full view without producing more than the tick of claws—or it may continue around a corner before it has begun. Sometimes the prey returns carrying the predator’s shadow, while the predator itself vanishes.

There are countless tiny lives in the cisterns: translucent frogs, threadworms, aquatic spiders, fry with leaf-shaped fins. When a pool dries, its inhabitants do not die. They climb into the nearest reflection and wait there. A dry basin may therefore show crowded water in its polished bottom: fish circling, insects dimpling a surface that cannot be touched. When rain returns, the reflection overturns and pours them back.

Despite this activity, the district is mostly quiet. Its tissues dampen impact and swallow echoes. Pumps labor with only a distant pressure in the ears. Animals communicate through changes in color, minute electrical discharges, and scents released into enclosed ducts. Even the river makes little noise. The loudest common sound is the sporadic drop of condensation, amplified by the long pause that follows it.

Other noises have no clear source: a tram bell softened by immense distance; one bootstep from beneath the ground; the crisp turning of a page inside a sealed stalk. At irregular intervals every creature stops moving. The factories continue, but silently. A low vibration passes through the streets, and all open doorways narrow to slits. Afterward, the layout is slightly changed.

That change reveals the district’s principal instability: position is not dependable here.

The city is small enough to cross in fifteen minutes, but no route crosses it twice in the same way. Streets do not rearrange as objects might. Instead, their former relationships cease to have been true. A cistern formerly beside the flywheel plaza may now have always stood beneath the western pumping trunk. Maps adapt when folded; when opened, the ink shows the revised arrangement. Memory is slower to comply. Travelers sometimes remember both cities at once and become unable to recognize intersections because each one is superimposed on several incompatible pasts.

Interior and exterior are similarly provisional. A stalk no wider than a barrel may contain a hall with rows of branching columns. The hall is not illusionary; its air has weight, its far wall can be reached, and rain may fall from its high canopy. Yet cutting down the stalk releases only a coil of white pith and a gust of air smelling of the hall. Conversely, the broadest warehouse has an interior only a few centimeters deep. Its open loading doors reveal a living green wall immediately behind them, veined and pulsing.

Darkness is a substance in some parts of the district. It accumulates in unused rooms and can flow out when doors open. Animals drink it. Factories bale it into dense rectangular blocks and stack these under awnings. A block left in rain dissolves upward, spreading a temporary night beneath the leaves. Within that night, distance loosens. A hand may reach farther than an arm. A whisper may arrive before it is spoken.

The local plants use darkness as both nutrient and blueprint. New structures begin as cavities in shadow. Around these absences, membranes thicken, roots align, and trunks rise. Thus the newest quarter consists almost entirely of holes: black door-shaped volumes standing unsupported in an empty field of root pavement. Over weeks, walls will grow around them. Rooms come first; their buildings follow.

This may be why the district feels unfinished rather than ruined. Nothing has had time to decay, although some structures possess the marks of ancient wear. Surfaces are tender. Curbs are still differentiating from streets. Drainage grilles open and close like gills. The city is constantly testing what kind of place it might become.

At its lowest point is a round station whose platforms surround an empty bed of moss. Tracks extend from it in five directions, grown from parallel roots polished to a metallic shine. No train has been seen arriving. Nevertheless, the schedule changes every night, printed in clusters of luminous fungi. Destinations include neighboring settlements, anatomical terms, weather conditions, and several times of day. One line is always listed simply as **HERE**, with departure overdue.

When the station’s lamps pulse, the animals gather. Birds arrange themselves along the roof veins. Browsers stand at the platform edge. Beetles emerge from beneath the floor and form dense, precise borders. For a few moments the abundance usually hidden in walls becomes visible. The platforms are crowded, yet no creature touches another. All face the empty track.

Then a wind moves through the station without disturbing the air.

The moss bed parts down the middle. Somewhere beyond the visible district, immense machinery engages with a nearly inaudible click. The roots shine, condensation rises from them like reversed rain, and every doorway in the city opens at once.

Nothing arrives.

But afterward there is always one more street.
