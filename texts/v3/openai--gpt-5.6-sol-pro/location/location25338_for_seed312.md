At first it appears to be a mountain range under a thin, colorless sky: ridges upon ridges, pale stone, snow caught in the seams. Only after some time does the eye begin to separate natural slopes from walls.

The settlement covers nearly the whole high basin.

It does not spread as a single city with a center. It occupies the country the way lichen occupies rock, thickening wherever shelter, water, and level ground coincide. Villages have joined villages; hill forts have accumulated lower wards; seasonal camps have hardened into streets. Between them run causeways, stair roads, retaining walls, animal tracks, irrigation channels, burial terraces, and long lines of cairns. Seen from above, the inhabited basin is less like a city than a densely worked material—every ledge fitted, every hollow assigned a use.

The land itself is young. The mountains have not yet been softened into rounded forms. Their flanks rise abruptly from the plateau, black and gray where the wind strips them bare, rust-colored where mineral seams reach the surface. Broken pinnacles stand above the passes. Recent rockfalls lie at their feet with no soil yet gathered among the blocks. Scree slopes still preserve the pale scars from which they fell. In places the earth has split into narrow ravines whose walls remain sharp enough to show each layer of shale and volcanic stone.

The altitude governs everything. The air is thin and almost always cold. Even in summer, shade keeps a hard edge of frost until midday. Breath smokes at dawn. Water left in shallow bowls skins over during the night, and snow may fall in any month, though it seldom stays long on sun-facing walls.

There is not much water, but the country is rarely entirely dry. Moisture arrives in fragments. Clouds snag on the highest summits and pass over the basin as brief sleet or fine rain. Snowmelt descends in narrow, quick streams, vanishing into gravel fans or being captured by stone-lined channels. Condensation beads beneath slate roofs and inside cold storage pits. In the morning, ropes and wool awnings are dark with dew. Every pocket where water persists—a spring under an overhang, a shaded cistern, a pond formed behind a landslide—is known, named, and guarded by custom.

The largest river is not large. It winds through the basin floor in several braided channels, gray with powdered rock. Its banks shift after each thaw. People have built low weirs from wicker and unshaped stone, knowing they will be destroyed and rebuilt. No one trusts the river enough to build directly against it. The densest wards sit on gravel terraces above the floodplain, descending toward the water in steps of gardens, tanning yards, washing stones, and animal pens.

Vegetation is sparse in the open country. Most slopes carry only wiry grass, low thorn, cushion plants, and dark mats of moss pressed close to the ground. Dwarf juniper grows where boulders break the wind. Along watercourses, thin willow stands make silver-green lines visible from many miles away. Trees tall enough to provide beams are rare. Timber is valuable and reused until it becomes too short for anything but pegs, handles, or fuel.

Yet the city has its own concentrated greenness. Sheltered courtyards hold beds of barley, bitter greens, onions, and medicinal herbs. South-facing terraces are edged with manure and dark household soil carried up basket by basket. Some walls are built double, with earth packed between them to hold warmth. Low stone enclosures trap drifting snow so that it melts slowly into planted ground. The result is not lushness but careful survival: each patch of green has been engineered by generations of hands.

Most construction is stone. The mountains provide it in exhausting abundance.

Lower walls are made from great fitted blocks, dry-laid and slightly tilted inward. Upper stories use smaller rubble set in mud, with roofs of slate over bent poles, brush, felt, and packed clay. In the wealthier districts, houses rise four or five levels along the slope, though from the uphill side they may appear as low compounds. One family’s roof is another family’s lane. Stairways pass beneath overhanging rooms. Footbridges cross alleys too narrow for loaded animals to turn around.

There are few straight streets. The oldest routes follow animal paths and contours; later buildings crowd against them without erasing their shape. Covered passages wind through the denser quarters, dim even at noon. Openings are low to preserve heat. Doorways have thick wool curtains behind wooden planks. Smoke escapes through small roof vents or simply searches its way out through the slate.

The city is dense without being loud. Cold and altitude discourage wasted motion. People speak close together. Felt, wool, earth, and snow absorb sound. There is no continuous clamor of machinery, no iron traffic, no turning wheels on paved avenues. Goods move on backs, sledges, pack animals, and narrow travois. In winter, runners glide quietly over frozen lanes. In summer, the streets fill with the soft scuff of hide shoes and the click of small hooves.

From a distance, the dominant sound is wind.

Closer in, one hears individual things: a hand hammer shaping a copper rivet; a stone mason tapping for flaws; grain grinding between querns; water ticking into a cistern; the creak of rawhide lashings; dogs giving brief warnings from roofs. At certain hours the enclosed livestock districts stir with snorting, stamping, and the dry rustle of fodder. Bells are scarce because metal is scarce. Animals are marked instead with carved wooden clappers, shell pieces, dyed cords, or distinctive cuts in the hair.

Fauna is everywhere, though much of it is small or domesticated. Hardy sheep and long-haired goats occupy every usable margin of the settlement. Compact mountain cattle pull sledges and sleep in ground-level chambers, warming the rooms above them. Dogs guard grain stores and guide flocks through streets that may be crowded with people at dawn and empty by noon. Pigeons nest in wall holes. Ravens watch slaughter yards from parapets. Small finches descend on threshing floors, and pale mountain hares enter abandoned terraces after snowfall.

Beyond the walls, herds of wild sheep move along high shelves. Ibex pick their way over cliffs that seem vertical from below. Marmots whistle during the brief warm season. Foxes patrol the outer refuse grounds, while wolves remain farther out, following migratory herds through the passes. Snow leopards are seldom seen, but their tracks appear occasionally on rooftops at the edge of the upper wards, where the city meets bare mountain without any clear boundary.

The abundance of animals is not a sign of rich land. It is the result of movement. The basin lies where several migration routes and human roads converge. Herds pass through. Birds descend to the river terraces. Livestock are driven in from summer pastures and crowded into winter quarters. For part of the year the settlement contains far more living bodies than its fields could ever support. Dried grass arrives in tightly bound loads from distant valleys. Salt, hides, bone, wool, dung fuel, and grain move in the opposite direction. The city survives because it gathers a vast, thin landscape into a dense knot.

Its technology is simple, but its organization is not.

Stone is split with hammerstones, antler wedges, fire, and water. Copper and soft bronze are known, though expensive; metal is reserved for knives, awls, needles, small chisels, and repairs to older tools. Most vessels are clay, leather, basketry, horn, or carved stone. Rope, felt, and woven wool perform tasks that elsewhere might require timber or iron. Buildings rise through communal labor, using ramps, sledges, levers, and patience.

Water rights are measured by time and shadow. A household may open a channel when sunlight reaches a particular notch in a wall. Pasture boundaries are marked by cairns and recited landmarks rather than surveyed lines. Roofs are private in winter, shared thoroughfares in summer, and disputed during repairs. No one may block a meltwater gutter, even on their own property. A fallen retaining wall must be rebuilt first by those above it, because their houses and soil created the pressure.

Fire is governed as strictly as water. Fuel is too precious and buildings too close together for careless hearths. Most cooking is done in communal ovens sunk into protected courtyards. Dung cakes, thorn bundles, and scraps of old wood are counted through the cold months. A fire that escapes in the lower wards can climb uphill through roofs and awnings, so watch platforms are positioned where sentries can see smoke against the stone.

There are plazas, but few are grand. The largest are practical spaces: market grounds, flock-counting yards, places to spread grain, snow, wool, or bodies for burial preparation. Temples and meeting halls are distinguished less by height than by the precision of their masonry. Their walls are made from stone selected for color and fitted without mud. Many have open courts where the sky, rather than an image, occupies the central place.

The dead are carried outward and upward. The rocky ground is difficult to dig, so burial chambers are built into scree slopes or under stone mounds beyond the inhabited terraces. Over centuries these mounds have formed another district, a silent duplicate of the city made of low doors and cairns. Paths continue among them toward the upper pastures. Shepherds shelter there during storms, leaving offerings of wool or food at entrances whose names have been forgotten.

Weather moves slowly across the immense basin. A storm can be seen gathering on the western rim half a day before it arrives. Its shadow travels over mountain faces, districts, fields, and river channels, revealing the scale of the place by degrees. People secure roof stones and drive animals inward. Then there may be ten minutes of hard rain, a whitening of hail, and hours of dripping afterward. Water runs down every stair. Courtyards become shallow mirrors. The city smells briefly of wet dust, wool, smoke, dung, and opened earth.

Afterward, sunlight returns without warmth. Snow peaks shine above dark roofs. Steam rises from animals packed into pens. Children set small bark or reed boats in the gutters, following them downhill until the water disappears into a covered channel.

Change here is usually gradual. Streets narrow one rebuilt wall at a time. Terraces climb slopes over generations. A spring weakens; a neighborhood slowly empties; another thickens around a newly cut channel. Rockfall may erase a road in an instant, but the response takes years: first a footpath around the debris, then steps, then retaining walls, shrines, stalls, and houses. Eventually the detour becomes an old street and no one remembers the straight way beneath the stones.

The mountains continue rising by measures too small to witness. Frost prises them apart. Rivers carry their powder away. Against those immense processes, the city seems temporary. Against a human lifetime, it seems older and more durable than the land.

At dusk, the separate settlements become visible again. Cooking smoke rises from thousands of roof vents, each plume slight, together forming a low blue layer across the basin. Paths fade. Animals settle. Lamps are few and weak, fueled by fat, so darkness enters rapidly into the alleys while the upper peaks remain lit.

For a short time the entire inhabited country lies between night below and sun above: a vast accumulation of stone rooms, winter pens, stairs, walls, cisterns, burial mounds, and narrow fields, all held together by gravity, obligation, and the careful distribution of water.

Then the light leaves the peaks, and the basin becomes quiet enough to hear ice forming along the river’s edge.
