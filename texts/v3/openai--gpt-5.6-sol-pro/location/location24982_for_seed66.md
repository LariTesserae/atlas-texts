The city occupies the crown of an immense plateau, though “crown” suggests a peak sharper than this. The land is broad and nearly level, a high country extending beyond ordinary sight, its horizon softened by warm vapor. Only at the margins does it reveal its altitude: there the ground breaks into terraced cliffs, descending through cloud in steps of black stone, rust-colored slag, and pale mineral deposits. Water spills from hundreds of seams along those cliffs. None of the falls is especially powerful, but together they make the plateau appear to be slowly weeping.

Rain comes often and without drama. It gathers overhead in low gray sheets, falls warm for an hour or a day, then withdraws into luminous haze. The city never dries completely. Pavements remain dark at the joints; stairwells sweat; droplets form beneath pipes even when no water is running through them. Every depression holds a shallow pool with an oily mineral sheen. Beneath the streets, the water table lies so near the surface that old foundations stand in flooded chambers, and maintenance crews travel through them by narrow barges.

There is no plant life. No grass softens the plazas, no moss works into masonry, no weeds colonize the gutters. At first this gives the city a severe, unfinished appearance, as if it were constructed yesterday on sterilized rock. A longer look reveals the opposite. Everything is ancient in its position. Corners have been rounded by centuries of rain and passage. Steps sag in the middle. Iron railings have been polished smooth where bodies continually press against them. Brickwork is veined with white efflorescence, and whole districts lean almost imperceptibly toward subsiding cisterns.

The city is made from what the plateau provides: dense basalt, porous gray tuff, copper, iron, fired clay, and a translucent stone quarried from deep mineral beds. The oldest buildings are low and massively walled, with narrow doorways and roofs designed to bear warm rain. Newer industrial structures rise above them—gantries, pressure towers, refinery stacks, lift cages, bridges carrying insulated conduits. Their silhouettes repeat across the plateau until distance and weather merge them into a single serrated band.

It is less a city surrounded by wilderness than a continental machine with inhabited districts embedded inside it. Streets widen into sorting yards. Sorting yards narrow into canals. Canals enter covered reservoirs, from which water is pumped uphill into cooling towers and reaction houses. Rail lines thread through markets and apartment blocks without clear boundaries between civic and industrial space. A person might cross a laundry bridge, descend through a turbine station, pass under a rookery vault, and emerge into a public square without ever knowing where one function ended and another began.

The machinery is powerful but rarely fast. Great flywheels turn at the pace of clocks. Bucket chains ascend from flooded shafts one dripping compartment at a time. Ore presses close with patient, irresistible force, pause, and open again. Some pumping engines have completed the same cycle every few minutes for generations. The city’s industry depends less on speed than on continuity. Nothing is allowed to stop. Workshops are built beside workshops abandoned three centuries earlier, and both may still contain moving parts, the older mechanisms driven indirectly by vibration, runoff, or shafts whose original purpose has been forgotten.

The plateau’s warmth comes partly from its latitude and partly from the rock beneath it. Deep fractures release heat into the buried waters. The air at this elevation should be cold, but it is instead mild and heavy, with a faint metallic taste. Steam rises from street grilles. In winter, if the city has a winter, the rain becomes merely cooler. Frost is known only on the tallest aerial masts, and even there it lasts until morning.

The overwhelming fact of the place is its animal life.

Every ledge is occupied. Every cable bears weight. Every sheltered recess contains sleeping, nesting, molting, feeding, or waiting bodies. The absence of vegetation has not made the plateau barren; it has removed the visual obstruction between observer and animal. Fauna are everywhere in full view.

Broad-backed grazers move through the drainage districts in dense, slow herds, scraping mineral films from wet stone with rows of blunt mouthparts. They are knee-high, slate-colored, and almost impossible to distinguish individually. Rain wakes the films they feed on: thin colonies of bacteria and other simple organisms that draw energy from iron, sulfur, heat, and dissolved compounds welling up from below. Within hours of a shower, walls acquire faint red, violet, or cream stains. By the next day the grazers have cleaned them back to bare rock.

Above them hang pale filter animals, soft-bodied and many-limbed, attached to bridge undersides and pipe racks. They open ribbed fans into the moist air, collecting spores, microbes, shed cells, industrial aerosols, and the minute drifting young of other species. When disturbed, an entire avenue’s population folds shut in sequence, producing a rolling clap that travels for kilometers.

The canals are packed with armored swimmers. Some are no larger than coins; others are long enough to block a lock gate. They feed on the microbial blooms of the warm reservoirs, on one another, and on waste from the city’s processing houses. Their backs break the water like moving cobblestones. During rain, they leave the canals and push across flooded streets, obliging trams to slow while thousands pass beneath their carriages.

Tower animals occupy the vertical city. Hook-footed, narrow-bodied creatures climb smokestacks and masonry faces, crowding around warm exhaust vents. Their calls are low and resonant, less like birdsong than air moving through bottles. Colonies tune themselves to neighboring machinery. Near the western compression works they call in a three-beat pulse matching the main pistons; around the old pumping quarter they sustain a wavering drone. Young animals raised in silent chambers often fail to develop normal calls. Here, mechanical rhythm is part of ecology.

Nothing moves with urgency unless attacked. Predators are abundant, but even predation tends to be deliberate. Long-jawed ambush animals wait in gutters for days, their hides taking on the color of the masonry. Heavy six-legged hunters follow the grazing herds at walking pace, selecting the injured by patient pressure rather than pursuit. In the roof districts, translucent gliders ride columns of warm air, circling so slowly that they can be mistaken for stains in the sky.

The city’s people have not conquered this abundance. They negotiate with it architecturally.

Doorways include low side arches for herd passage. Reservoir grilles are spaced to admit small swimmers while excluding larger ones. Warm public halls have nesting shelves built into their upper walls, because attempts to remove the tower animals only drive them into ventilation ducts. Freight schedules pause during the great canal crossings. Buildings intended to last are faced with sacrificial stone, replaced every few decades after the mineral grazers wear it thin.

Some fauna are useful. Filter colonies purify factory air. Armored canal animals keep intake tunnels clear. Vast, nearly immobile creatures live in the deepest settling basins, ingesting contaminated silt and binding heavy metals into layers of shell. Others are nuisances or dangers, but the distinction is never stable. A species may be protected in one district and culled in the next.

The sounds accumulate until they become a climate of their own. Water ticks from thousands of eaves. Pumps thud beneath the streets. Rails sing under slow freight. Animals scrape, drone, clap, bellow, and rattle their shells against stone. Far-off factories contribute continuous tones too low to locate. The city is loud, yet seldom sharp. Its noise is broad and enduring, built from countless modest sources. Silence occurs only inside certain deep cisterns, and those chambers are considered unsettling.

From the upper bridges, the plateau appears almost motionless. Warm rain drifts across roofs. Herds fill entire boulevards, advancing so slowly that their movement is visible only by comparison with statues or lamp posts. Chimneys exhale steady white columns. Floodwater slides along channels worn into the city over many centuries. Beyond the dense center, construction continues in rings: quarry districts, cooling fields, reservoirs, worker blocks, animal corridors, then more factories fading into haze.

Change here is measured in polished thresholds, shifted migrations, deepened channels, and the gradual tilting of towers. Districts do not collapse suddenly; they settle. Canals do not burst their banks; they spend decades choosing a new course. Species enter the city over generations and become architectural facts. Machines outlive their builders and are adapted by successors who no longer understand the original diagrams.

The governing rule of the plateau is persistence through accommodation. Water finds seams. Animals find warmth. Buildings accept pressure and are rebuilt around what cannot be displaced. Industry proceeds not by clearing the living world away, but by passing through it, under it, and beside it.

At dusk the light remains ordinary—gray-gold under the departing rain, neither magical nor dim—but it catches on millions of wet shells, polished backs, windows, rails, and standing pools. For a short while the whole vast city reflects itself in fragments. Then the haze closes, the lamps come on in long industrial rows, and the patient noise continues.
