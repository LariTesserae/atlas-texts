The town occupies a shallow basin no wider than a long walk. From the surrounding flats it looks less built than pressed into the land: a low arrangement of green roofs, chimneys, trellised streets, and open courtyards, all contained within an old oval embankment.

There is no exposed soil inside the basin. The ground is a continuous, centuries-thick mat of living vegetable matter—root, cork, pith, compressed leaf, and pale fungal tissue—firm enough to carry carts, though it yields slightly underfoot. Streets have been worn into this substrate rather than paved. Their surfaces are dark and fibrous, polished by generations of wheels. In the busiest places, the roots have responded by growing dense, parallel grain along the direction of travel. The town’s map can therefore be read in the wood beneath it.

Vegetation fills nearly everything, but does not produce the impression of a jungle. It has been trained into distinct masses with generous distances between them. Great ribbed stalks rise at intersections like columns. Houses are spaced around open yards of moss and low fern. Long green walls bend around vacant squares. Much of the basin is occupied by broad, quiet intervals where only groundcover grows, interrupted by a cistern, a solitary tower, or a bank of cooling leaves.

The buildings are partly constructed and partly cultivated. Their oldest walls began as woven saplings packed with pulp and mineral ash. Over time they rooted, thickened, and took on the blunt, softened forms of things repeatedly weathered and repaired. Corners have rounded. Doorways lean. Rain gutters have become woody channels lined with silver moss. Some facades bear the remains of brick or iron cladding, but roots pass through the joints and hold the masonry together. Removing the plants would not reveal the architecture; it would cause the architecture to collapse.

The industrial quarter lies along the basin’s lowest curve, where intermittent water collects. Its factories are broad, low structures with saw-toothed roofs made of translucent leaf-skin stretched over iron frames. In clear weather the roofs are matte green. After rain they become almost transparent, and the silhouettes of pistons, flywheels, suspended belts, and workers can be seen moving inside.

The machinery runs slowly. There are no roaring furnaces and few fast engines. Power comes from pressure stored in the vegetation itself. During wet periods, specialized trunks swell and lift weighted beams. Their motion winds drums, compresses air, and raises counterweights. During dry periods the accumulated force is released through gear trains. A week of rain may drive the mills for a month. Factories produce wire, ceramic fittings, cutting edges, glass tubing, pumps, and the narrow steel staples used to persuade young buildings into shape.

Every mechanism is designed around patience. A press may descend only twice an hour, but with enormous force. A tram crosses the town on a root-rail that rises beneath it in a traveling muscular wave. Pumps breathe rather than chatter. Elevators are drawn upward by tightening vines and settle back when the vines relax. The whole town is active, but its activity takes place below the threshold of obvious commotion.

Most of what one hears is incidental: condensation ticking from a pipe onto leaves; a belt giving a single soft slap; wind worrying the dry valves of a chimney flower. Beneath these is a low, periodic strain as roots tighten under the streets. It resembles the sound of a wooden ship under pressure. When several factories release their stored weight at once, the basin gives a quiet, collective sigh.

Water is never abundant. Rain comes in brief, cool showers, often too fine to see from a distance. It darkens every surface, gathers in curled leaves, and runs down the chimneys into underground bladders. By the following afternoon, most open water has disappeared. What remains is held in pockets: cistern courts, hollow roots, moss wells, and the shaded seams between buildings.

The town has evolved around this rhythm. Roofs do not merely shed rain; they sort it. The first dirty runoff is directed to flushing channels and dye works. Cleaner water passes into kitchens and nurseries. The last droplets, filtered through hanging root beards, go to the pressure groves that power the machinery. It is considered a serious offense to seal a surface against absorption. Stone and metal must be perforated, jointed, or sloped toward something alive.

Beyond the embankment, the land is cooler and barer: low gravel flats patterned by old flood channels. Their shapes are settled and elegant, worn into branching curves by water that now arrives only a few times each year. The basin itself may be the remnant of one such channel. Its margins are scalloped, its walls undercut but stable, and every exposed industrial pipe follows some ancient drainage contour. Nothing here is sharp unless it has recently been made.

Erosion is treated less as damage than as authorship. Steps are allowed to hollow under feet until they reach a comfortable depth. Channels are patched only when water begins to forget its assigned route. A building judged too rigid is sometimes scored deliberately so that frost, root pressure, and rain can improve it. Surveyors measure not whether structures move, but whether they move at the accepted rate.

In winter the basin rarely freezes solid. Cold settles close to the ground, whitening the leaf roofs with condensation and slowing the engines. On certain mornings every filament of the town is silvered. Steam rises from compost vaults and passes through narrow streets in waist-high sheets. The vegetation remains awake beneath the surface, exchanging heat through the root mat. Standing still, one can feel faint warmth coming through one’s soles.

Animal life is scarce. There are a few pale moths in the mill roofs, narrow snails in the water channels, and small black birds that appear after rain to pick metallic seeds from the tram lines. No large wild creatures live in the basin. The plant city does not exclude them violently; it simply leaves little ecological work for them to do. Pollination is performed by trembling wires and pressure bells. Dead matter is carried downward by fungal cords. Seeds move on the tram wheels, in workers’ clothing, or through tubes that exhale them into prepared walls.

The rarity of animals makes each arrival conspicuous. A fox entering through a culvert can stop work across an entire district. Windows open. People gather silently on balconies while the animal crosses the green squares, alarmed by the attention. The town keeps records of such visits in a ledger called the Bestiary, though several decades may pass before a page is filled.

People live mostly in the upper chambers of the grown houses, above the dampest part of the root mat. Their rooms are cool, high-ceilinged, and spare. Cupboards are recessed into living walls. Pipes emerge like stems. Floors are layers of bark tile that can be lifted and composted when worn. At night, modest electric lamps shine behind ordinary glass, giving the town an unexpectedly familiar appearance: square amber windows, shaded alleys, the occasional white arc light over a loading platform.

From within, however, the town never feels inert. Walls thicken by fractions. Street levels rise and must be pared down. A doorway may narrow over several years. Addresses change when two buildings graft together and become legally one organism. Ownership is therefore temporary and conditional. One may possess a room, a machine, or a harvest of roof membrane, but not the plant body supporting them. That body belongs to the basin as a whole.

The principal civic institution is the Office of Strain. Its engineers monitor tensions running through roots, frames, pipes, bridges, and load-bearing trees. They decide where growth may continue and where it must be checked with cold iron collars. Their maps are revised daily, because the town’s forces migrate with moisture. During a dry month, the eastern streets tighten and the western factories sink slightly. After heavy rain, courtyards swell, old cracks close, and certain doors cannot be opened until noon.

This gives the settlement its mild but undeniable strangeness: it has an urban plan, yet no final form. It is weathered and long established, but also continuously manufacturing itself. Construction and decay are not opposites here. A collapsed wall becomes a nursery bed; a mature nursery is cut into workshops; workshops eventually soften into cisterns. The city advances without expanding, consuming its own interior arrangements and producing new ones.

Toward evening, condensation gathers first on the ironwork. Drops form along railings and transmission rods, bright in the level light. Workers close the leaf shutters of the mills. Pressure engines complete their final strokes. Somewhere under the central square, a stored weight descends through a shaft, turning dynamos for the night.

The lamps come on one by one across the green basin. There are not many of them, and much of the town remains dark—open space, wet roofs, old growth, quiet machinery waiting under tension. Seen from the embankment, it resembles a small constellation caught in a bed of leaves.

Then the roots shift, almost imperceptibly, and the streets settle into tomorrow’s shape.
