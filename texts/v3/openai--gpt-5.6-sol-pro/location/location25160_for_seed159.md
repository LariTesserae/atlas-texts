### The New Underplain

The cavern is too young to have become a cave.

It lies below a plateau of black salt, but nothing here has the settled shape of an underground world. There are no rounded chambers, no patient stalactites, no smooth channels polished by ages of drainage. The stone has only recently separated. Great planes of basalt lean away from one another like slabs in a shattered kiln, their faces raw, angular, and warm. Pale seams of gypsum remain stretched between them, tearing slowly. Cracks open with the sound of splitting timber. Elsewhere, cracks close, crushing whatever has entered them and squeezing ribbons of hot mud onto the cavern floor.

The open space extends for leagues. From the higher shelves one can see across it as across a crowded valley: ridges, pits, low mineral terraces, moving herds, vapor, and distant walls obscured by amber haze. Yet the roof is always present. It hangs between fifty and several hundred meters overhead, descending in places as colossal wedges that nearly touch the ground. The impression is not of confinement so much as a second, inverted terrain suspended above the first.

The light comes from that ceiling.

Thin mineral membranes cover much of it—translucent sheets grown where heat, pressure, and salt meet. They hold a steady cream-colored radiance, close to daylight under cloud. When the stone shifts, sections brighten from pearl to white. Others bruise into violet shadow. The illumination is broad and ordinary enough for clear sight, but it has no visible source, casts several faint shadows around every object, and never travels across the sky. There is no morning here. There are only gradual regional brightenings, as if weather were passing through the rock.

This light nourishes nothing green.

There are no trees, reeds, mosses, or hidden root systems. Bare mineral continues uninterrupted from floor to roof: rust-red clay, white salt, sulfurous yellow crusts, glassy black scarps. The few soft coatings on the rocks are not vegetation but bacterial films, transparent and nearly invisible except where they trap bubbles. Everything larger moves.

The animals are overwhelming.

At first they resemble debris in motion. Pebble-backed crawlers stream over the slopes in millions, each no bigger than a thumb joint, scraping fresh stone with ring-shaped mouths. They pass in bands so thick that the ground seems to darken and flow. Their shells are wet, gray, and faceted, reproducing the mineral patterns of the surfaces they consume. Behind them the rock is only slightly altered: less sharp, faintly scored, touched by the first suggestion of soil that will never become soil.

Larger creatures follow.

Six-legged grazers, low and broad as tables, shovel the crawler swarms into throat pouches. Blind white animals with inflated bodies drift through the warm air on hydrogen bladders, trailing nets of hooked silk. Flocks of thin, copper-winged things burst from fissures whenever the walls tremble. They do not fly gracefully. They ricochet between ledges in metallic clouds, each impact making a small bell note. The largest predators move on two dozen jointed limbs, arching over the crowds like walking bridges. Their hides are mottled with scars where prey has tried to tunnel through them from inside.

There is little empty ground. Eggs lie in drifts along the warmer cracks. Molted shells clog gullies. Animals shelter beneath animals. Some species live permanently on the backs of larger ones, hunting among the plates or drinking condensation gathered in shallow pits of skin. Even the air is occupied by spores, shed scales, floating larvae, and translucent thread-creatures that twist wherever convection carries them.

The basis of this abundance is stone, heat, and chemical difference. The smallest mouths digest fresh mineral exposed by the cavern’s continual breaking. Others cluster around iron-rich seeps, sulfur vents, salt boundaries, and the electrically active seams beneath the luminous ceiling. Life here does not wait for leaves to capture light. It bites the world directly. When one mineral face is exhausted, the swarms abandon it, and the stone remains pale and lifeless until another fracture exposes fresh material.

Water arrives irregularly.

Most of the time the atmosphere is merely damp: warm enough that every cool surface sweats, and saturated with the smell of wet clay, hot shell, salt, and a faint bitterness like struck flint. Condensation gathers beneath the ceiling membranes. Drops enlarge for hours, sometimes days, before falling individually through the bright air. Each impact produces immediate commotion. Small animals converge before the water can soak away.

Then, without warning, entire ceiling districts release at once.

Rain crashes down in localized columns, heavy and straight, while neighboring ground remains dry. Basins fill within minutes. New streams choose routes across surfaces that have never held water before. Because no channel is established, the floods wander, divide, vanish into cracks, and burst from lower faces carrying milky sediment. Pools may survive for a week or disappear before the rain ends. Creatures breed in them with frantic speed. Clear water soon clouds with eggs, blood, shells, and living knots of larvae. When a basin drains, thousands of soft-bodied things migrate overland, producing a wet rustle like soaked fabric being dragged over gravel.

Nothing here has learned stability because stability is not one of the rules of the place.

The cavern expands in pulses. A far wall may split open and reveal another illuminated valley already packed with life. A ridge may rise overnight as mineral foam hardens beneath it. Pressure vents erupt without fire, throwing warm brine and flakes of mica into the air. Mud islands inflate, carry nesting colonies upward, then rupture. Whole migration routes are erased. Predators wait near trembling ground because earthquakes expose burrows and stun the shell-backed herds.

The turbulence is constant but seldom uniform. One district is drowning while another is drying to salt. One ceiling is collapsing while another lifts, widening the air beneath it. Across the Underplain, pale dust fountains mark landslides; spiraling animal clouds mark fresh light; sudden dark patches mark feeding convergences. Seen from a distance, the landscape appears to boil in slow motion.

Its sounds gather into a tremendous, layered roar.

Water drums on shell fields. Wings ring against stone. Herds clatter over gypsum plates. Bladder-creatures sigh and boom as they regulate their buoyancy. Burrowing colonies rasp inside walls. There are predatory whistles, mating knocks, alarm rattles, and the soft but pervasive grinding of innumerable mouths. Beneath it all runs the deep geology: fractures like thunder behind a door, pressure groans, abrupt concussions traveling through the floor.

Silence occurs only immediately after a major collapse. Then every animal nearby stills for several breaths. Dust rises through the steady light. Loose stones continue to tick and fall. The first sound to return is usually feeding.

Scattered through this profusion are signs of deliberate making.

On stable shelves stand low shelters built of fitted shell, clay, and slabs of translucent salt. They occur singly or in clusters of three or four, never forming a true settlement. No timber exists, so roofs are made from the rib plates of enormous animals, weighted with stone and sealed using rendered fat. Doorways are narrow and bent to prevent charging creatures from entering at speed. Around some shelters, sharpened bone stakes turn the migratory swarms aside, though the stakes must constantly be reset as the ground shifts.

Paths are marked with cairns of contrasting mineral: black on white ground, white on red. Simple bridges span active cracks, made from bundled carapaces tied with tendon. There are chipped basalt scrapers, shell bowls set beneath dependable drips, hammerstones, woven sinew nets, and deep pits lined with greasy clay. Metal appears only rarely, as crude beads of native copper hammered cold and hung near entrances, where their vibration warns of approaching tremors.

The builders are difficult to distinguish from the other fauna until they stand upright. They are compact, plated beings with four grasping forelimbs and fanlike sensory organs along the jaw. They travel in small bands because large gatherings attract predators and because no patch of firm ground remains firm for long. Their shelters are camps, not homes. Their craft is devoted less to possession than to rapid departure: collapsible traps, hide bundles, portable drip-catchers, maps scratched on shell and revised after every quake.

They do not try to conquer the Underplain. They read it.

A brightening ceiling means a pressure change. Copper-wings flying low announce rain. A sudden absence of crawlers means that the exposed minerals have soured or that something beneath the surface is breathing. Warm vapor from a dry fissure may promise water by the next light cycle, or an eruption of caustic brine. Every sign has several meanings, and certainty is considered a dangerous form of inattention.

There are places even they avoid.

The largest is a central depression called, by the pattern of warning scratches around it, the Mouth Without Hunger. It is several kilometers across and filled not with water but with loose, warm spheres of mineral glass. The spheres churn continually, rising along the edges and sinking near the center. Animals enter the field to lay eggs between them. Some are swallowed. Others emerge months later altered—larger, transparent, or bearing limbs their species did not previously possess. During strong tremors the entire depression sings, each glass sphere contributing a tone until the basin becomes a single unbearable chord.

Beyond it stand the Bone Weirs: curved walls assembled from the remains of gigantic bridge-predators. Their maker is unknown. They predate the present shelters but cannot be very old, because nothing in the cavern is old. The weirs guide periodic floods into shallow stepped pools. In the dry intervals, colonies shelter inside the hollow bones. During rain, the structures fill with water and awaken dormant needle-shaped swimmers, which pour from every joint in glittering torrents.

Perhaps the weirs were built for them rather than against them.

The farther one travels, the less finished the world becomes. Stone softens into hot mineral paste. Ridges are still swelling from the floor. Ceiling light shines through walls thin enough to reveal silhouettes moving in chambers not yet opened. One can hear claws scratching on the other side. New ecologies wait there in compressed darkness, feeding, breeding, and crowding against a barrier that may split at any moment.

Thus the Underplain does not feel ancient, despite its depth. It feels imminent.

Every pool is temporary, every species provisional, every wall a door under pressure. The first lines of erosion are being written beneath the feet of the swarms, then crossed out by uplift, flood, collapse, and appetite. Nothing has acquired the grace of long weathering. Edges remain sharp. Rivers remain undecided. Life itself seems newly released and excessive, filling space faster than space can be made.

Under the changeless, many-shadowed light, the warm cavern continues opening.

And whatever room it creates is immediately occupied.
