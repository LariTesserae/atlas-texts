The land lies only a little above the black water table. It has been worn so long that almost nothing here can still be called a hill. The horizon is a succession of shallow shelves, drowned basins, and low mineral backs polished down to the curvature of sleeping animals. Rain has no distance to fall before becoming flood. Water stands in every depression and moves beneath every surface, squeezing through pores in the stone, sweating from seams, condensing in the air as a fine, cold suspension.

There are no plants. No moss softens the rock, no reeds mark the channels, no buried roots hold the margins together. The ground is naked and tremendously old: blue-gray plates of compressed mineral, iron-black mud, chalky membranes, and broad fields of glass that have lost their shine. Erosion has passed beyond ruin into completion. Edges are rare. Pebbles have become beads, beads have become paste, and the paste has settled into laminated beds so perfectly level that they sometimes reflect the dim sky from beneath several centimeters of water.

Yet the plain is crowded.

Across the shelves stand dense assemblies of columnar bodies, packed flange to flange. From a distance they resemble the remains of an industrial district—cooling towers, refinery pipes, pressure vessels, banks of turbines—but none were built. There are no foundations, fasteners, welds, or tool marks. Each structure has grown by accretion from the mineral floor, layer after microscopic layer, around a central pulse of heatless electrical activity. Their chimneys are the spiraled shells of colonial animals. Their pipes are calcified feeding organs. Their piston strokes are contractions of muscle too slow and strong to appear biological.

The largest colonies cover entire basins. They take in floodwater through thousands of slotted mouths, separate its salts, metals, suspended spores, and minute animals, then expel it through raised vents. The expulsion occurs in coordinated shifts. A whole valley draws inward with a descending moan; pressure builds under the plain; then towers miles apart release at once, throwing columns of freezing mist into the dark. The mist freezes without becoming ice. It hangs as brittle haze until sound breaks it.

Nothing has room to stand apart. Smaller fauna occupy every available surface. Coin-shaped grazers move over the stone in overlapping sheets, scraping invisible films of mineral-eating microbes. Cable-thin hunters thread among them, knotting themselves around dozens at a time. Bell-bodied drifters crowd the flooded channels so densely that the water appears granular. They have translucent shells filled with dark fluid and strike one another constantly, producing the ceaseless clatter of loose bearings in a steel drum.

Some creatures are recognizable only by what interrupts them. A herd of long, jointed walkers crosses the flats, but their bodies are missing from every third angle. Seen from the north they are tall frameworks of ivory struts carrying sacks of luminous brine. Seen from the east there is only a succession of footprints forming in wet silt. Seen in reflection, they are enormous and buried upside down beneath the water. Their calls arrive before they make them: low mechanical blasts that roll across the plain and cause the smaller animals to clamp themselves shut.

Predators hunt by altering local rules.

In the shallow pools, pale radial things wait under the mineral skin. They do not lunge. Instead, when prey passes overhead, they briefly make downward the nearest direction. Water, mud, pebbles, and animals fall sideways into their mouths. The disturbance lasts only a second, but it leaves crescent scars across the flats and sends sheets of water climbing the low stone backs. Elsewhere, heavy six-chambered creatures prey on vibration. They open their plates and remove one sound from the landscape. Into the resulting silence rushes everything that had been making that sound: calling animals, cracking ice, thunder, even the resonance of distant vents. The hunter closes, and the missing noise returns as a single deafening impact inside its body.

The plain is never quiet. Water hammers through subterranean cavities. Colonies pump and exhaust. Shells knock together. Pressure faults groan beneath the mud. Far off, something turns with the regularity of a vast buried engine, though nothing beneath the flats is actually rotating. The rhythm is created by adjacent moments exchanging places: the same concussion occurring first in the west, then in the past, then underfoot. It gives the impression of machinery continuing its shift across geological ages.

There are cycles, though they are not governed wholly by day.

The sky remains low and dim, a lid of charcoal cloud through which a muted disc sometimes appears. Its light is weak and directionless. Shadows pool beneath objects but often detach during heavy condensation, drifting over the flats like stains. When enough shadows gather in one basin, the temperature drops further. Water skins over with black ice. The ice is never empty: tiny spiral animals are already embedded inside it, swimming through the solid as if it were liquid.

At irregular intervals comes the Compression.

The horizon draws inward without visibly moving. Distances shorten. Features normally separated by kilometers stand packed together for several hours: vent colonies shoulder against grazing fields, distant channels pour through nearby crevices, and migrations collide in walls of churning shell and limb. The land does not buckle; it simply contains more place than it did before. Sound becomes unbearable during these events. Calls overlap with their echoes before they are made. Waterfall, thunder, animal alarm, and pumping pulse condense into a single industrial roar.

No creature seems surprised. Many depend on the Compression. Filter colonies open only when remote currents pass abruptly through their bodies. Predators feed across collapsed distances. Small armored scavengers cling to seams in reality and harvest the pale residue left when two locations separate again. When the plain relaxes, entire populations may be stranded far from where they began, and new ecological boundaries establish themselves before the water has stopped shivering.

The water itself is old enough to have habits. It avoids certain hollows despite their depth and gathers on subtle rises. It remembers channels that erosion erased millions of years ago and continues to flow through them, passing invisibly across dry stone before reappearing in basins with no inlet. If held still—and many animals possess cup-shaped organs for this purpose—it shows not the sky but an earlier version of the plain: sharper ridges, deeper seas, unfamiliar constellations. Drinking from different layers of reflection changes a creature’s age. Most fauna sip carefully. Some predators force prey to drink backward until they become eggs.

There is little decay. Dead organisms mineralize almost immediately in the cold, wet air. Soft tissue clouds into metallic silt; shells fuse with the floor; electrical patterns linger and migrate into nearby colonies. Thus the landscape is densely inhabited not only by the living but by accumulated functions of the dead. A pumping tower may carry the instincts of ten thousand grazers. A stretch of mud may recoil from approaching hunters. Stormwater may briefly remember having wings.

In the broadest basin, where the ground descends perhaps a dozen meters below the surrounding flats, the packed life becomes almost continuous. Towers, shells, limbs, mouths, and resonating plates form a moving surface from shore to shore. There is no open water, only water forced through bodies. Mist rises from the mass. Deep within it, pressure waves travel in organized sequences, switching whole regions on and off. Rows of vents flare with cold blue corona. Vast siphons contract. Armored herds turn in coordinated arcs. It resembles a factory operating at planetary scale, but there is no product, no overseer, and no distinction between machine, worker, raw material, and waste.

The process is the ecosystem itself.

Everything takes in the plain and returns it fractionally altered. Salt becomes shell. Shell becomes grinding surface. Grinding becomes current. Current becomes call. Call becomes predator. Predator becomes mineral bed. Over immense spans, the bed is worn smooth and raised again inside the bodies of the colonies.

And beneath all of it, very close to the surface, water presses upward.

It wells through hairline cracks. It beads on the underside of overhangs. It fills footprints before the feet are lifted. In places it rises in perfectly cylindrical columns, standing unsupported in the air while animals swim vertically through them. In other places it condenses as heavy drops several meters across, each enclosing a distorted section of the horizon. These drops roll slowly over the flats, gathering creatures and loose stone, until they touch one of the natural towers and are drawn inside with a boom that can be heard beyond the shortened world.

Nothing here has been constructed. Nothing here is unfinished. The machinery has no inventor because invention would imply a time before it began, and the oldest stones contain impressions of the same valves, chambers, and articulated forms now crowding the marsh. The plain has always been ancient. Its erosion is not a history but a condition. Its industries are not imposed upon the landscape.

They are what the landscape does instead of growing green.
