The city lies almost level with the sea, spread across an old delta where the ground is neither firm land nor open marsh. Everything rests on pilings, rubble beds, and generations of poured concrete. Beyond the last warehouses, tidal mud extends toward a horizon obscured by warm haze. Inland, the same flatness continues through drainage basins, freight yards, settling ponds, and neighborhoods built only a step above the water table.

There is no green.

No grass grows between the paving slabs. No weeds soften the railway embankments, and no trees stand along the avenues. Salt, heat, contaminated soil, and continual scouring have removed nearly all visible plant life. Bare mineral surfaces remain: yellow-gray silt, black mud, rusted steel, pale concrete, clinker, slag, brick, and broad plates of asphalt polished by rain. In shaded drains there are dull bacterial films, brown or colorless, but from any distance the place appears entirely without vegetation.

Rain comes frequently, though rarely with coolness. It falls from a low ceiling of cloud in heavy, nearly vertical curtains, strikes hot roofs, and immediately begins to steam. The streets are designed to shed it. Water runs along deep gutters into brick-lined canals, through culverts beneath factories, and across immense tilted aprons of concrete. Where the drainage has subsided, shallow pools remain for days. Their surfaces carry an iridescent skin of fuel and animal fat. Bubbles rise from drowned refuse. Elsewhere, walls and bridge abutments weep continuously as groundwater presses through old seams.

Between storms, the heat closes over everything again. Moisture condenses on the undersides of pipes and on the shaded faces of tanks. Handrails are wet at dawn and too hot to touch by afternoon. The atmosphere smells of salt, wet limestone, iron, hot rubber, fish meal, diesel, and the faint sulfurous breath of mud exposed by the falling tide.

The city is large enough to have districts, but it feels empty. Long roads pass between windowless industrial compounds. Rail lines divide and recombine across vacant switching grounds. Warehouses stand hundreds of meters apart, each surrounded by loading yards wider than town squares. Even the residential quarters are sparse: rows of concrete housing blocks separated by storm channels and bare courtyards. Their lower stories are stained dark to the height of old floods.

Most human movement is enclosed. Workers arrive in buses before sunrise and disappear through gatehouses into processing halls, pumping stations, cold-storage buildings, and machine shops. Vehicles are few but immense—tankers, refrigerated lorries, dredges, articulated haulers—so one engine can be heard approaching for minutes before it appears. There are no crowds in the open and little casual street life. Shade is scarce, distances are punishing, and the ground belongs to other animals.

They are everywhere.

Gulls cover the roofs so densely that whole buildings seem white until a disturbance lifts the flock. Terns occupy the crane booms. Cormorants stand along warm discharge canals with their wings open, using the heated air from vents to dry themselves. Under the loading docks, feral pigs lie in wet hollows they have pushed into the mud. Dogs travel the levees in loose, quiet packs. Rats inhabit the stacked pallets, cable conduits, grain elevators, fish warehouses, and hollow concrete seawalls. At low tide, crabs emerge by the tens of thousands, spreading across the mud and into the lowest streets. Their shells click faintly over broken glass.

The insects are more numerous still. Flies collect around rendering plants and drainage mouths, forming dark congregations on walls. Cockroaches fill service tunnels. Mosquitoes breed in every warm cistern not sealed with oil or mesh. Moths sleep beneath lamps by day, layered over one another like scales. Above them, bats crowd bridge chambers and abandoned upper floors. At dusk they pour out in long streams, turning the already dim sky grainy with motion.

There are few loud moments. Heat suppresses activity through much of the day, and the animals have adapted to living near machinery without wasting alarm calls. The city’s ordinary sound is low and dispersed: the drip of condensate, the intermittent ticking of cooling metal, the soft movement of claws in gutters, pumps working somewhere below ground. From far off comes the steady hum of refrigeration plants or the single deep impact of cargo being set down on a quay.

Then, briefly, one district erupts. A fish truck tips its load and thousands of birds descend. Dogs bark under a conveyor. Pigs squeal behind a wall. A ship sounds its horn in the haze. The disturbance travels outward and fades, leaving the city quiet again except for wings settling on roofs.

The industrial system sustains much of this animal abundance. Boats bring catches from offshore waters. Processing plants produce blood, offal, shells, grease, and warm nutrient-rich wastewater. Grain spills from silos. Refrigerated stores fail at their edges. Slaughterhouses, markets, kitchens, storm drains, and refuse compactors leak enough food to support several overlapping ecosystems. Very little is wasted, though little is cleanly consumed. What the gulls miss, the rats take; what the rats leave, insects reduce; what enters the canals is eaten by crabs and fish; dead fish return to birds and dogs.

Predators follow the concentrations. Falcons nest on transmission towers and hunt pigeons between silos. Large monitor lizards patrol drainage channels. Snakes occupy dry cable galleries above the flood line. Stray cats remain close to the housing blocks, where smaller prey is abundant. In the outer mud basins, wading birds form shifting lines at the edge of the tide, each species taking food from a slightly different depth.

The city has been here long enough for its decay to look settled rather than catastrophic. Rain has rounded the edges of brickwork. Salt has opened concrete in branching cracks, exposing corroded reinforcement. Steel stairs have worn thinner under feet and weather. Canal walls bow toward the water but have done so for decades. Old flood defenses stand behind newer ones, producing parallel ridges across the plain. Entire former streets have become drainage channels, their curbs still visible beneath brown water.

Nothing appears freshly ruined. Even abandoned structures have entered the working order of the place. Roofless mills serve as nesting cliffs. Empty tanks become bat chambers. Collapsed piers break the tide and gather shellfish. A disused viaduct carries pipes instead of trains. The city continually converts its own obsolete parts rather than removing them.

Its governing rules are simple and practical. Water always seeks the lowest neglected place. Salt enters everything. Heat favors the shaded, the buried, and the nocturnal. Food appears in industrial pulses rather than seasons: a ship unloading, a processing line opening, a market closing, a drain flushing after rain. Animal populations move in response. Whole flocks change districts within minutes; rat colonies advance warehouse by warehouse; crabs retreat exactly as the tide returns.

At evening, the cloud ceiling lowers and takes on the orange-gray color of reflected industrial light. Lamps come on along the quays, but they illuminate only small circles: wet road, concrete barrier, drifting insects, the eyes of dogs beyond a fence. Most of the city remains dim. Tanks and gantries become broad black forms. The flat water mirrors a few red obstruction lights and then breaks them apart under the first drops of the next storm.

Before the rain arrives, the animals begin moving all at once. Birds leave exposed roofs. Rats climb from lower drains. Crabs turn toward deeper channels. Bats vanish into bridge joints. The streets, already empty of people, seem briefly to flow with bodies.

Then the downpour strikes the hot city, washing feathers, dung, dust, oil, and scraps toward the sea. Steam rises from the paving. Pumps start beneath the ground. For an hour the industrial plain becomes a network of running water, and afterward, when the rain has passed, every surface glistens in the dimness and the animals return to feed along the new edges.
