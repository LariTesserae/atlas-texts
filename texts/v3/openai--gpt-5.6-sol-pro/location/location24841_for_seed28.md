The city occupies the inside of a stone no larger, from the outside, than a low hill.

Nothing marks it except a slit in the southern face and a faint vibration underfoot. The hill lies half-buried in a warm salt flat, its rounded back polished by centuries of abrasive wind. Travelers might take it for a boulder and pass. If they kneel beside the slit, however, bright air breathes against their faces, smelling of hot minerals, crushed leaves, and old smoke. The sound coming through is too deep to be heard cleanly. It shakes the teeth.

Beyond the slit, the stone is impossibly hollow.

The interior spreads farther than its shell should permit, though never with the openness of a true horizon. Its boundaries are always implied: a curving ceiling behind the glare, distant walls masked by pale foliage, the downward bowl of the inhabited floor. It feels at once like a city and like a room remembered at colossal scale.

The land is low throughout. There are no mountains, only shallow mineral swells, terraces, ramps, and broad depressions worn smooth by traffic. Warmth accumulates near the floor. The stone underfoot is cream-colored and faintly elastic, striped with red, black, and amber seams. In places it has been rubbed glassy by generations of bare feet and dragged sledges. Elsewhere it has weathered into soft geometric fluting: ribs, hexagonal pits, shallow cups. There is no flowing water, no damp soil, no mud, no dew. Dust remains dust.

Yet the city is green.

Its vegetation does not drink. The tall plants anchor themselves in cracks filled with powdered salt and ash, spreading thin silver roots over the rock like writing. Their leaves are broad, warm, and leathery, held upright to catch light rather than rain. During the brightest hours they open pores along their edges and draw in the mineral-laden air. Crystals gather inside their tissues; by evening the leaves are heavy with them. At night the plants flex, cracking the crystals into finer compounds with a succession of tiny snaps.

Most of the greenery grows in deliberate bands. Curtains of copper vine hang between buildings without touching the ground. Groves of white-barked fan trees occupy plazas. Low red mosses upholster stair risers, roof ledges, abandoned thresholds, and the shaded undersides of bridges. There are orchards of bladder-fruit: hollow green globes filled with cool gas and edible chalky pulp. When ripe, they loosen from their stems and hover among the branches until gathered with hooked poles.

The lushness is vertical rather than deep. Beneath every garden lies bare mineral. If a trunk is cut open, there is no wet sap—only hot resin, fragrant vapor, and fibers as dry as paper.

Light enters from nowhere obvious. The upper stone burns with a steady white-gold radiance, strongest around a network of hairline fractures. These cracks may lead outside, though no one has ever reached their far ends. Mirrors of polished mica stand on towers and roof corners, throwing the brilliance down into streets, workshops, and cultivated hollows. The result is a city without true shadow at midday. Even covered alleys glow from reflected light. Colors bleach at their edges. Faces appear carved.

Construction is everywhere, but occupancy is sparse.

The city’s builders have divided the hollow into districts with walls, colonnades, planted causeways, and immense ceremonial stairs. Buildings climb over one another in layers: pale brick houses set on older roofs, arcades buried to their capitals, towers stitched together with rawhide bridges. There are kilns, grain courts, ropewalks, amphitheaters, signal platforms, wind chambers, and long empty markets furnished with hundreds of stone stalls.

Almost all of it is vacant.

A household may possess an entire street. A dozen families tend a district built for thousands. Greenery claims courtyards but does not conceal the emptiness; it frames it. Through an avenue of fan trees, one sees door after door standing open onto polished rooms. Cooking stones are cold. Sleeping alcoves hold drifts of aromatic husks. Public squares preserve lane markings and queue posts for crowds that no longer come.

The inhabitants build constantly despite this. They quarry soft blocks from the lower bowl with antler picks and ceramic wedges. They shape them using sand, cord, fire, and patience. Metal is rare, restricted to small copper blades, needles, and ringing plaques. Wheels exist, but the streets are too stepped and broken for carts, so goods move on sledges, shoulder poles, or overhead ropeways. Every repair follows old plans. Every generation adds balconies, buttresses, staircases, and walls to buildings no one needs.

They say an unfinished city attracts the Outside.

The noise is the city’s weather.

Air moves through hidden passages in the surrounding stone. At sunrise it begins as a low pressure in the floor, a pulse felt in the knees. By midmorning the hollow is roaring. Shafts beneath the streets draw breath downward; towers release it through split ceramic mouths. Roof vanes clatter. Tensioned ropes hum over the avenues. Broad leaves rattle like sheets of hammered metal. Empty windows turn whole façades into flutes.

Conversation becomes impossible in the open.

Citizens speak by hand, by facial signs, and by placing their foreheads against resonant walls. In houses, they use carved speaking bowls: one person presses their mouth to one side while another rests an ear against the opposite rim. Announcements are not shouted but drummed into the pavement, traveling as coded vibrations through the city’s foundations.

The loudest places are the wind courts. These are circular plazas lined with stone fins, where colliding currents create a continuous concussive bellow. The sound is strong enough to lift dust in patterned rings. Priests enter wearing wax packed into their ears and heavy jaw-bindings to keep their teeth from cracking. They read the shifting dust figures as forecasts, laws, and accusations.

No one agrees whether the city was designed to withstand the roar or to produce it.

In the afternoon the sound rises beyond hearing. It becomes a physical condition: pressure across the ribs, ripples in bowls of loose seed, trembling in the roots of the trees. Towers bend slightly. Bridges draw taut. Doors must be latched with woven straps. The city seems to be sounding a note too low and too large for the people inside it.

Then, toward evening, the currents change direction.

The mirrors dim from white to amber. Warm gas rises from the orchards. The great roar fractures into separate voices—moans in the western ducts, a chattering from the roof fields, hollow booming under the old market—and finally falls to a pervasive hum. This is when people emerge. Gardeners scrape crystal residue from leaves. Builders inspect cracked mortar. Gatherers pursue floating fruit with long poles. Children run through abandoned houses, trailing ribbons to map subtle currents.

Animal life is scarce. A few flat, six-legged grazers live beneath the red moss, so thin they can slip between fitted stones. They feed on shed leaf scales and produce hard black pellets used as pigment. Pale kite-creatures sometimes appear high in the radiant vault, coasting for weeks without landing. They possess no mouths that anyone has seen. Their translucent bodies swell in the morning currents and shrink after dusk.

More rarely, something large moves inside the walls.

Its passage is marked by a traveling silence. Pipes stop singing as it approaches. Leaves turn edge-on. Dust settles. For several breaths an entire district becomes perfectly quiet, a quiet so complete that inhabitants wake from sleep and clutch one another in alarm. Then comes a distant grinding, the warmth returns, and the city resumes its endless note.

The people avoid those districts for a season afterward. They seal certain doors with red fiber and build new streets around them.

There is no weather in the usual sense, but the hollow has cycles. Every nine days, salt blossoms from the ground in delicate branching structures. Every thirty-three, the bright ceiling dims and the plants fold shut, leaving the city in copper twilight. At irregular intervals, a “dry rain” falls: buoyant flakes of transparent mineral descending slowly from the vault. They shatter on roofs and are swept into kilns to make lenses.

Fire is carefully rationed. With no water to quench it, each district maintains smothering blankets of mineral felt, sealed sand bins, and streets designed as firebreaks. Cooking is done in sun boxes and buried heat chambers. Kilns stand in isolated courts surrounded by bare stone. Open flame is used chiefly in funerals.

The dead are not buried. There is no soil deep enough, and the ground resists excavation. Bodies are dried among fragrant leaves, wrapped in copper vine, and placed in the uppermost empty rooms. Their names are cut into shutters. When the great air currents eventually tear those shutters free, the city considers the names released.

Near the center of the hollow lies the lowest place: a shallow basin paved with millions of fitted tiles. No building stands there. No plant roots in it. The tiles are worn but never dusty, because air continuously escapes between them with the force of breath through clenched teeth. The noise is unbearable even by the standards of the city.

At the basin’s center is a small round opening.

It is covered by a stone grille greened with age. Through it comes the brightest light, the hottest air, and the foundational tone that causes every wall and bridge to tremble. Builders regularly add weight around the grille—rings of brick, carved slabs, abandoned millstones—but the tiles beneath remain level. Nothing shifts. Nothing cracks.

Sometimes, during the dim cycle, the roar below stops.

In those moments the vast hidden city, its forests, empty avenues, towers, ropeways, sealed rooms, and countless vibrating instruments all fall still. People stand wherever they are and wait. They can hear the scrape of their own skin against clothing. They can hear the tiny crystal-fed plants snapping their evening meal. They can hear, very far beneath the grille, a slow and deliberate inhalation.

Then the note begins again, and the city continues building.
