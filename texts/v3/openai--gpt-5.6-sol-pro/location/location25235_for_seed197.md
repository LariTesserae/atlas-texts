At first it appears to be a clearing.

It lies in a shallow depression no more than a few hundred paces across, in warm low country where rain comes abruptly and leaves just as quickly. The surrounding ground rises only enough to conceal the horizon. There are no mountains, no cliffs, no monumental distances. The place is bounded by a ring of pale trunks and by the feeling—stronger than any visible barrier—that beyond a certain line the same world does not continue.

The clearing is almost empty. This emptiness is deceptive.

There is scarcely any bare earth. What looks like soil is a single, continuous rind of living material: tough, matte-green, and faintly elastic under pressure. It puckers around depressions and stretches smoothly over old roots. In shaded places it pales to the color of unripe fruit; where the sun reaches it, it thickens into overlapping scales. Fallen leaves do not remain fallen. They settle, soften, and rejoin the floor, their veins linking to the larger web beneath. Nothing vegetal here is quite detached from anything else.

The whole place may be one plant, or a dense agreement among plants that has become indistinguishable from one organism.

Yet above this living pavement there is tremendous open volume. The few trunks are spaced far apart, each one occupying the place where a tower, gatehouse, or public building might stand in a human settlement. Their crowns do not form a continuous canopy. Instead, they spread into broad shelves and arches, outlining streets of open air. Long branches cross the clearing at different heights, joining trunk to trunk in deliberate-looking spans. Some have hollowed into galleries. Some descend in smooth ramps before rooting again. Others divide into ranks of ribs, making roofless chambers full of ordinary daylight.

It is urban without having been made.

There are avenues, but no paving stones. There are courtyards, but their walls breathe. There are colonnades grown from the repeated downward rooting of branches. Knotted intersections swell wherever several flows of sap meet, and these swollen hubs have accumulated balconies, hollows, gutters, alcoves, and narrow green shafts. The structures are old enough to have lost any suggestion of novelty. Wind, rain, browsing mouths, fungal action, and the slow redistribution of weight have weathered them into settled forms. Worn channels run along every sloping surface. Scars have rounded over. Cracks have become windows. Collapsed limbs have been digested back into buttresses.

No hand designed the settlement. No tool has ever struck it. Its architecture is the residue of growth responding to growth.

The central space is the emptiest part: a broad, shallow bowl occupied only by a few pools. During dry intervals these shrink to black saucers set into the green floor. Their edges are upholstered in bladderwort, tiny ferns, and translucent moss. Condensation gathers under the branch bridges at night, travels along the vein-ridges, and falls from carefully recurved tips. Each drop lands in one of the pools with such regularity that the pools seem to be positioned beneath dripping eaves.

When rain comes, the city changes all at once.

The floor loosens and sinks by a finger’s breadth. Seams open between its scales. Water passes through the streets in silver sheets, filling the central bowl and turning every root-ramp into an island. Cups high in the crowns overflow into channels; channels pour into larger channels; the pale trunks darken from the ground upward. For an hour, sometimes for a day, the clearing becomes riverine. Currents circle through the hollows and emerge elsewhere, carrying pollen, larvae, seeds, and small white fish that cannot be found during dry weather.

Then the water is gone.

It does not drain toward any visible low point. It simply passes into the living substrate, and the pools resume their separate shapes. Wet marks remain halfway up trunks even when the flood was never that deep.

The warmth is constant but not oppressive. Surfaces hold the day’s heat after sunset. Moisture collects in the skin folds of the ground and under the roofs of leaves. The air smells of green sap, bruised citrus, wet bark, animal musk, and occasionally the mineral scent of rain on stone—although there is almost no exposed stone anywhere. A person might search for it and find, beneath the vegetal floor, not rock but another layer of leaves, and beneath that a web of roots, and beneath that a second daylight sky.

This is one of the place’s ordinary impossibilities.

The roots do not descend consistently. A hollow beneath one tree may contain darkness, cool air, and soil. A neighboring hollow opens upward into blue distance. Seeds dropped into certain cracks fall back from the crown minutes later. Water poured into one pool may rain from the underside of a branch across the clearing. The settlement is small enough to cross quickly, but a complete circuit of its outer avenue always includes one more turning than can be counted from the center.

No path is infinite. None is reliably finite.

The fauna treats these conditions as weather.

It is everywhere, though rarely still enough to contribute much visible density. Bright, flat-bodied lizards sprint across the living pavement and disappear into seams that close behind them. Beetles crowd the inner bark in metallic layers, stripping old tissue and polishing the new. Small antelope-like browsers, no higher than a knee, move along branch roads overhead. Their split hooves grip the bark even when they cross the underside of an arch. They descend only to drink, lowering their heads to pools while their reflections remain standing upright.

Flocks of narrow-winged birds inhabit the open air between the structures. They nest in pendant seedpods and hunt the clouds of gnats that rise whenever the floor exhales. Some birds have calls; others have openings in their chests through which the wind plays. Their migrations are local and ceaseless: crown to pool, pool to arcade, arcade to crown. Green snakes trail them through the branches, passing from tree to tree without crossing the gaps between. A snake reaches from one limb, vanishes for the length of the open span, and appears headfirst from the next.

During rain, the temporary waterways fill with soft-shelled frogs and translucent crustaceans. During drought, these animals survive inside unopened flowers. Predators pry the blossoms apart to reach them, pollinating the flowers as they feed.

The city is nearly empty in the architectural sense—few great supports, broad courts, generous intervals of air—but biologically it is full beyond measure. Life is not arranged as clutter. It is packed into surfaces, vessels, hollows, skins, and moments. A smooth trunk may contain a million mouths without showing one. A vacant shaft of sunlight may become, at the turn of a breeze, a vertical river of insects.

And the sound is continuous, enormous, almost physically solid.

The first layer is the insects: a high metallic pressure without pause, made by wings, rasping legs, vibrating leaves, and chambers in the trunks that amplify tiny bodies into civic machinery. Beneath it is the booming of the hollow root system. Sap moves through the old wood in pulses, each pulse striking resonant membranes deep below. The trunks answer one another across the clearing. One gives a long bass note; another replies with three shorter blows; a whole colonnade begins to throb until the green floor trembles.

Birds scream over this. Frogs hammer from the pools. Seedpods rupture like stones splitting in a fire. Branches under tension creak and detonate. Rain, when it arrives, is deafening beyond description: every leaf becomes a drum, every hollow a bell, every gutter a roaring throat. Even in clear weather there is never silence. The settlement’s emptiness acts as a resonating chamber, preserving sounds after their causes have stopped.

Some echoes arrive before the noise.

A pod may be heard bursting several breaths before it opens. Thunder rolls through the trunks under a cloudless sky, followed later by a storm that seems to be remembering the sound rather than producing it. An animal startled by a distant crack will flee, and only then will its own footfall make the crack that startled it.

The inhabitants have adapted. Birds begin songs at their final note and work backward toward the first. Predators wait where prey has already screamed. Flowers close in anticipation of being touched.

There are districts, though their borders migrate. In one quarter, the branches grow in parallel tiers crowded with edible cups of nectar. In another, the floor rises into low domes, each with a warm chamber inside where animals sleep communally. Along the outer ring stand the oldest trunks, vast but widely spaced, their surfaces worn into deep vertical fluting. They lean outward, as if holding the boundary open.

Between two of them is a gate.

It has no door and leads nowhere unusual at first glance: merely into the scrub beyond the depression, where the plants become separate individuals and the land resumes a more ordinary damp warmth. But animals leaving through the gate cast their shadows back into the clearing for several days. The shadows wander independently among the pools, drinking, sleeping, and sometimes being hunted. If a shadow is killed before its animal returns, the animal outside gradually becomes transparent.

For this reason most creatures do not leave.

Near the center, one pool never reflects the sky. It reflects the city as it was long ago—or as it will be after another thousand seasons of growth. Its image shows the same open court, but the architecture differs: fallen arches restored as saplings, living towers shown as cavities, present animals absent and unfamiliar animals drinking in their place. When rain strikes the pool, each ring reveals a different age. The rings widen, overlap, and erase one another until the surface shows only green light.

Nothing here appears to rule the settlement, but there are rules.

No dead plant may remain separate from the floor by morning.

Any branch forming a complete circle becomes an entrance.

Water must always be found in more places than it is seen.

A creature may cross an empty space only while something else is crossing it in the opposite direction. Birds satisfy this rule for most animals; insects satisfy it for the birds.

A sound repeated exactly returns as an animal.

The last rule explains some of the stranger fauna. In the deepest hours before dawn, when condensation falls steadily and the great trunks pulse at their slowest rate, repeated noises gather bodies. Three identical drips may become a pale, hopping thing. A rhythm beaten by rain may detach from a root and scuttle away on many legs. The city’s deafening chorus is therefore also reproductive. Noise breeds motion; motion breeds more noise.

At sunrise, light enters plainly. There is no spectral gloom, no permanent mist, no theatrical color. The sky is blue when clear and gray when raining. Leaves are green, bark is cream and brown, pools are dark. This normal light makes the impossibilities sharper. Nothing is hidden by atmosphere. One can watch a root bend into the distance and return from overhead. One can see a flock enter a trunk’s shadow and emerge as falling seeds. One can stand in the open central court, surrounded by abundant life and immense sound, and see almost nothing occupying the air.

The place continues in balance, if balance can include constant revision. Growth lifts the streets; browsing lowers them. Rain hollows chambers; roots roof them over. Predators thin the choruses; the choruses generate new bodies. Floodwater rearranges the pools, but by the following dry spell they have returned to their remembered positions. The old architecture survives not by resisting change but by digesting it.

Nothing is completed. Nothing is truly ruined.

The clearing only keeps becoming the city it has apparently always been.
