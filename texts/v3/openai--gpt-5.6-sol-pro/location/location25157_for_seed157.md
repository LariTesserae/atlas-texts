High on the mountain, where the slope has weathered down to old ribs of stone, there is a hollow no larger than a village green. It is enclosed on every side by steep, inward-curving walls. Nothing has been built there. The enclosure is entirely geological: a shallow bowl worn into dark basalt, its rim crowded with leaning trees and its floor sunk beneath centuries of moss, roots, water, and accumulated life.

The hollow receives almost no sky.

Cloud lies permanently across the mountain, pressing against the rim and spilling through the branches as a cold, granular mist. Above it, daylight may pass from morning to evening, but down in the bowl the change is too slight to distinguish. The place remains in a deep blue-black dimness. Wet surfaces show themselves by reflection: the vitreous shine of a leaf, a silver line on a snail shell, a bead of water held under a fern tip. Everything else merges into soft, crowded shadow.

The stone walls are extensively eroded, though the erosion seems complete rather than ongoing. Water has shaped them into flutes, pockets, rounded shelves, and narrow hollows resembling eye sockets. Their surfaces are softened by liverwort and black lichen. In places the rock weeps steadily, but no droplet is ever seen to gather or fall. Moisture simply occupies the stone’s face, as if rock and water have reached a permanent agreement.

There is a pool at the lowest point, shallow and almost perfectly circular. It is full to its mossed lip but never overflows. No stream enters it. No channel leaves. The water is clear only for a finger’s depth; below that it becomes brown-black, crowded with minute suspended bodies and the interwoven filaments of aquatic plants. Its surface is without ripples. Even when mist collects above it, the water does not seem to receive anything.

Vegetation fills every available interval.

Low mountain trees grow from cracks in the walls, their trunks bent inward by the shape of the hollow. Their bark is black with saturation and furred by pale epiphytes. Branches meet and overlap until they make a low, tangled roof. Beneath them stand dense tiers of fern, horsetail, broad wet leaves, moss columns, fungal fans, and fine-rooted plants without obvious stems. There is no bare soil. Where rock is not covered by moss, it is covered by lichen; where moss thins, roots lie over it; where roots divide, translucent fungi occupy the seams.

The plants do not visibly grow. No bud opens, no leaf drops, no stem turns toward light. Yet none appear dead or stunted. Each is held in the exact fullness of its life: fronds unrolling but never quite unrolled, flowers poised at the point before their petals loosen, seed heads heavy but unshed. Decay is present in the form of dark wood and leaf mold, but it too has settled into permanence. Fallen trunks are soft, hollow, and intricately inhabited without ever collapsing further.

The density of animals is at first impossible to perceive because almost all of them are still.

Every branch carries weight. Small slate-colored birds sit shoulder to shoulder beneath the leaves, their feet sunk into moss, their eyes open and black. Moths plate the trunks like bark scales. Frogs occupy the wet pockets in the stone walls, some no larger than fingernails, others broad and folded like damp hands. Pale salamanders lie in the shallow margins of the pool. Beetles fill the undersides of fallen wood. Snails crowd the stems so closely that their shells touch. Higher in the canopy, long-limbed mountain primates cling among the crossing boughs, wrapped in dense gray fur jeweled with condensation.

Nothing hunts. Nothing flees.

Predators are present: narrow cats crouch between root buttresses; small owls wait in cavities; water snakes lie threaded through submerged moss. Their prey rests within reach. The relationship between them is not abolished, exactly, but suspended. Teeth, claws, venom, camouflage, and alarm coloration remain as properties whose use has become unnecessary. Hunger has reached the same equilibrium as water. Every body is nourished without feeding and alive without consuming what surrounds it.

The most unusual inhabitants are the primates in the roof of branches. They possess crude tools, though none have manufactured a shelter or altered the hollow. Each holds a naturally broken stone, a sharpened sliver of bone, or a length of twig stripped of bark. The objects are selected rather than made. Hands curl around them with obvious familiarity. One animal holds a stone above the shell of a nut; another has inserted a twig into a rotten log; a third presses a bone point against a knot of fiber. They remain at the instant before action.

No blow descends.

No tool enters wood.

No mark is made.

Thus the place contains technology as intention but no artifact as consequence. The hollow is untouched not because no creature has imagined changing it, but because change never proceeds beyond readiness.

The silence is not absolute. It is densely composed of tiny, continuous sounds: moisture whispering within moss; the minute rasp of breathing through thousands of throats; the tension of bark under roots; a soft intestinal clicking from the pool. Once these sounds are noticed, they seem to come from every direction. Yet there are no calls, no wingbeats, no splashes, no sudden cracks. The multitude makes less noise than an empty forest in wind.

There is no wind.

There may once have been weather here. The rounded stone and deep channels prove a history of rain, frost, runoff, and patient abrasion. The hollow is not young or preserved before erosion; it is ancient and thoroughly finished. Every edge has been worn to the shape it was seeking. Every loose fragment has found its final niche. Water stands at its chosen level. Roots occupy precisely the volume the rock allows. Animal populations have filled the remaining space down to the smallest crevice. Even the cloud overhead neither thickens nor clears.

To enter the hollow is to become another fixed presence within it.

A foot pressed into the moss leaves no lasting hollow, but neither does the moss spring back; rather, the impression seems always to have been part of its surface. Breath becomes mist without dispersing. A hand laid against the rock feels cool water and, beneath it, an immense steadiness. Direction grows uncertain because every path is blocked equally by foliage, bodies, roots, and stone. There is room to stand, perhaps room to kneel, but never an open route through.

The animals look toward a newcomer without turning their heads. Their eyes are already arranged to meet every possible point of arrival.

Nothing here forbids movement. There is no force, wall, spell, or visible law holding the hollow still. Movement simply remains forever about to occur. Water is about to drip. Ferns are about to uncurl. A moth is about to lift from bark. A cat’s forepaw is about to touch the ground. Stone is about to fall from the saturated wall. The hand in the canopy is about to bring its tool down.

The whole small landscape rests in that interval: not frozen, not dead, but complete enough that the next moment has nowhere to enter.
