Beneath a low plateau lies a single hollow known, if anything knows names here, as the White Bell.

It is not a deep place. The land above is only a thick roof of porous limestone, roots, and pale soil, yet the chamber feels wholly removed from the surface. It is roughly circular and no larger than a grove, though its domed ceiling gives it the proportions of a small cathedral. Most of that volume is empty. A few stone ribs lean inward from the walls; otherwise there is open air, an uncluttered floor, and great intervals between the things that grow.

The brightness is the first impossibility.

Light fills the chamber without entering as a beam. The limestone roof is threaded with translucent crystal and the hollow casts a diffuse white radiance from every surface, strongest around noon but never clearly directional. Shadows are faint, gathered immediately beneath stones and animals. Even at night the ceiling retains a diluted afterglow, as if daylight takes many hours to finish passing through it. During rare cloudless afternoons, the entire dome becomes painfully brilliant. The floor gleams like bone under shallow water.

It is warm below, warmer than the plateau during the night and cooler than it during the day. The temperature changes slowly. Air rests in the hollow, soft and mineral-smelling, with a trace of wet clay and the bitter odor of crushed fern. Condensation forms where the pale walls meet darker veins of stone. Droplets enlarge for hours before falling, one at a time.

There is no central pool. Water survives in pockets.

Centuries of dripping have made basins in the floor: thumb-sized cups, shallow saucers, and several smooth bowls broad enough for a person to lie across. Most are dry at any given time. A few hold clear water over rust-colored sediment. After rain on the surface, moisture descends through the roof in widely separated threads, and the bowls fill in an irregular sequence. One may overflow while another, an arm’s length away, remains dusty. Narrow runnels connect some of them, but these channels are old and settled, polished to gentle curves. Nothing here looks freshly broken. Every edge has been rounded, every fall of stone weathered into belonging.

The lowest part of the floor is a plain of pale carbonate crust. It is almost level, but not quite: an ancient, frozen ripple runs across it, recording some long-vanished flow. Bare stone occupies most of the chamber. Vegetation appears as isolated decisions rather than a continuous cover.

A grey-green fern grows from one wall seam, its fronds thick and sparsely divided. Mats of velvet-dark moss circle the dependable water cups but stop abruptly beyond the reach of splash. There are transparent filaments in the wettest cracks, neither root nor fungus, which curl inward when touched and slowly reopen over the course of a day. High on the dome, where moisture rarely lingers, small red rosettes cling directly to the stone. They have no obvious leaves. In bright hours they flatten against the ceiling, exposing their crimson centers; in darkness they close into hard beads.

Nothing grows tall. There is too little soil and no need to compete for light.

The chamber nevertheless contains an abundance of animals, though at first it seems empty. Life here is distributed into the smallest possible presences. Pale gnats hang motionless in the upper air, each separated from the next by several body lengths. Mites graze the margins of the moss. Glassy snails move along condensation paths, leaving trails that catch the chamber’s radiance. Their shells are so thin that the pulse of the animal inside can be seen.

The water bowls support whole temporary worlds. When a basin fills, dormant comma-shaped creatures awaken from its sediment. They swim, feed, mate, and leave eggs before the water disappears again. Tiny flat fish inhabit the three deepest hollows; when one pool begins to fail, they press themselves into wet cracks and remain there, breathing through the skin, until the next seep. Insects with long, careful legs visit each basin in turn. Their footfalls do not break the surface tension.

Larger animals are fewer but conspicuous against the open floor. Warm-bodied lizards, white except for blue markings around the eyes, bask in the chamber’s brightest center. A long-eared cave rat sometimes crosses from one wall to another, running with a pebble or snail shell held between its forepaws. These rats possess a simple, almost ceremonial cleverness. They use shards of naturally flaked stone to pry snails from crevices and hard seed pods to carry water, but they build nothing and keep nothing. A tool is chosen, used, and dropped where it ceases to be useful. No nest, cache, path, or midden lasts long enough to resemble construction. Their traces vanish into the chamber’s natural scatter.

At the roof’s highest point hangs the White Bell’s strangest inhabitant: a loose congregation of translucent, batlike animals with no wings.

They suspend themselves on threads of mucus strung between mineral teeth. Their broad membranes open to the radiance, gathering heat and whatever nourishment the light permits. Once every few days one detaches and drifts slowly downward, steering by flexing its body. It may take half an hour to reach the floor. There it folds itself around a water cup, drinks through its skin, and climbs back by gripping irregularities in the wall. Their movement is so unhurried that they are often mistaken for hanging films of moisture.

Despite the number of animals, the chamber is mostly quiet. There is no wind to stir the sparse growth. The constant sound is almost below hearing: an intermittent tick of condensation, followed by a small reverberation in the dome. A lizard’s claws make a dry whisper. Gnats produce a thin collective note only when the light changes suddenly. The cave rats communicate by touching whiskers and tapping stones once or twice, never in long sequences.

After heavy surface rain, the White Bell briefly becomes louder. Water speaks in several places at once. Runnels silver over; dry basins fill; snails emerge from sealed cracks. The sound lasts perhaps a day. Then the flow reduces to dripping, the dripping to condensation, and quiet reasserts itself.

Change here occurs by accumulation rather than event. A basin deepens by the thickness of a hair over many seasons. A mineral rib grows downward while the floor beneath it rises to meet it. The chamber is not actively collapsing or expanding; it has reached an old balance between dissolution and deposition. Water removes stone from the roof, carries it through the air, and restores it to the floor. The hollow slowly remakes itself from top to bottom without becoming larger.

Its governing rule seems to be that nothing may be wasted, but nothing may remain gathered.

Water collects, then disperses. Animals congregate, then separate. Tools are taken up, then abandoned. The light is everywhere but has no source within reach. Life is plentiful, yet space dominates every view. Even death is quickly divided: mites, translucent worms, and mineral-forming films reduce a body until its salts become a pale outline in the crust. Rain eventually blurs that outline away.

There are no entrances at floor level. Access, if it can be called access, is by narrow fissures in the roof and a winding animal passage scarcely wider than a shoulder. Nothing in the White Bell has been placed, carved, stacked, or claimed. Its symmetry is accidental; its smoothness is patient. From the chamber’s center, surrounded by brilliance and almost nothing else, it is possible to feel not enclosed underground but suspended inside a small, warm vacancy—an empty room the earth formed for creatures too slight and too numerous to need a world any larger.
