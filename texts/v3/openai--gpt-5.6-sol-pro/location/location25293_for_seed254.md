### The Lower Nursery

The district lies beneath the old ground level, not deep enough to be a mine and not open enough to be a valley. It occupies a brick-lined excavation sunk below the surrounding city: several connected courtyards, service galleries, cistern rooms, and one long factory hall whose roof has partly become a garden. From above, little can be seen except condensation on the railings and a green darkness between buildings. Stairs descend through increasingly warm air.

At the bottom, there is almost no bare ground.

Roots have lifted the paving into low ridges. Liverwort fills the cracks, moss covers the mortar, and thin grasses stand in the gutters where warm water runs continuously. Walls support layered curtains of ivy, climbing fig, fern, pepper vine, and broad-leaved shade plants. Their stems have thickened over decades into cables and lattices. In places they bear enough weight to function as architecture: old walkways hang from bundled roots, and balconies are held level by trunks that began as ornamental saplings in brick planters.

The settlement was built densely even before the vegetation took possession. Workshops, pump houses, narrow residences, boiler rooms, and storage vaults crowd one another around passages scarcely wide enough for a cart. Pipes cross overhead in stacked ranks. Conduits emerge from walls, turn corners, vanish beneath root mats, and reappear farther on with their metal darkened by moisture. Every surface has acquired another surface over it: brick beneath lime, lime beneath algae, algae beneath roots, roots beneath new masonry laid to keep a passage open.

Nothing here looks recently ruined. The district has not collapsed so much as settled into a long compromise.

Water enters from the surrounding soil and from the city above. Rain takes hours to reach the lower chambers. It filters through road beds, cellar walls, and old drainage aggregate, emerging as beads on ceilings or as clear threads down weeping brick. After a storm the walls shine. Cisterns rise by a hand’s breadth. The shallow channels along the streets deepen and spread over the worn paving, making broad films that reflect the few lamps.

Most of the water is captured. Brass grates lead to settling tanks crowded with reeds. Pumps lift it through iron mains to roof beds and vertical planting galleries, after which it descends again through troughs, irrigation ropes, and porous ceramic pipes. There is no single waterfall or river, only water everywhere in small amounts: dripping, sweating, seeping, condensing, draining. The air tastes of wet stone, warm iron, tannin, and green stems cut open.

The heat comes from below and from the machinery. Old steam lines pass through the district on their way to other wards. Motors drive ventilation fans, circulation pumps, grinders, and presses. Some machines are enclosed in tiled rooms; others occupy the streets, sheltered beneath corrugated canopies overgrown with vine. Their belts turn slowly but continuously. Flywheels revolve behind wire guards furred with moss. Pressure gauges tremble at familiar marks. Where a pipe has been replaced, the new steel appears startlingly bright among the green-black iron and softened brick.

The noise is immense.

It is not the sharp noise of rapid work, but a permanent industrial weather. Pumps strike in deep, staggered beats. Fans push air through ducts with the sound of distant traffic. Water rattles in vertical pipes. Condensate traps knock. Loose sheet metal drones. Every few minutes a valve discharges steam into a cooling chamber, and the whole lower ward gives a long breath.

Plant leaves modify the sound without quieting it. Broad leaves flap near vents. Hanging roots quiver against pipes. Dense foliage absorbs the higher echoes, leaving the district full of low frequencies that are felt through handrails and paving. Conversation, if there were anyone to speak, would require shouting.

There is no animal life. No insects move among the flowers, no worms turn the saturated beds, no rats inhabit the warm ducts, and no birds descend from the city rim. Water surfaces remain unbroken except by drips. The absence is maintained rather than mysterious. Fine screens seal the ventilation shafts. Incoming water passes through sand and heat exchangers. Seed stock is cleaned, soil is steam-treated, and organic refuse is fermented in closed tanks. The district’s plants reproduce by runners, cuttings, spores carried on managed air currents, and mechanical pollination. Brushes mounted on slow rails pass along flowering rows at set intervals. Fans shake pollen from one tier toward another.

Because there are no grazers, the vegetation’s chief limits are space, light, and maintenance. Leaves press against leaves. Vines climb vines when no wall remains available. Fallen matter does not disappear quickly into an active soil fauna; it accumulates in damp layers until workers—or the automated rakes that continue in their absence—draw it toward the compost digesters. Fungi and bacteria soften what the machines miss. Old leaf beds become dark, fibrous ground over brick.

Light enters reluctantly. At noon, narrow shafts descend from glass blocks set into streets far overhead, showing suspended mist and the outlines of upper catwalks. Most illumination comes from industrial lamps housed in wire cages. Their glow is amber, greened by the foliage, and often hidden behind leaves. Reflective panels once directed light into cultivation bays, but oxidation has dulled them. The brightest places are not streets but work zones: a propagation room washed in pale electric light, the mouth of a maintenance tunnel, the enamel face of an instrument cabinet.

Beyond these pools, the district is layered in dimness. Depth is measured by overlapping silhouettes—pipe, stem, railing, frond, gantry, trunk—until the passage seems solid.

Its central space is the former loading hall, a room roughly the size of a small cathedral. Rail tracks remain beneath the vegetation, running toward doors that have not opened in years. Along the tracks stand rectangular planting beds made from freight bins. Trees rise from them to the trusses, their crowns flattened beneath the roof. Some branches have grown through broken clerestory windows into narrow cavities above, then returned through neighboring panes, giving the ceiling a stitched appearance.

The hall’s crane still moves. Very slowly, at scheduled intervals, it travels from one end to the other carrying irrigation hoses and racks of cuttings. Its wheels squeal on the elevated rails. Leaves part before the suspended load and close behind it. The full crossing takes nearly an hour.

Nothing changes quickly here. Water levels rise and fall by centimeters. Roots widen around columns. Mineral deposits thicken beneath leaks, building pale lips and shelves over many years. Brick corners round under constant damp. A blocked drain may redirect a street’s runoff over the course of a season, creating a new reed bed where a workshop entrance used to be. Machines wear down gradually, and when one stops, its silence is more conspicuous than any alarm.

The district has rules imposed by its confinement. Space must be reused. Water must descend before it can be lifted. Heat gathers in the lowest galleries. Anything left still becomes a support for growth. Anything that vibrates remains comparatively bare. Thus the active pipes form dark, clean lines through the foliage, while abandoned ones swell into green columns. Fan housings are clear at their centers and thickly fringed at their edges. Walkways survive where footsteps or maintenance brushes disturb them; elsewhere, plants close the route.

At the far end of the lower street, the paving sinks into a marshy court. Old foundations create shallow rectangular pools, each edged with sedge and glossy creeping plants. The water is warm enough to mist in cooler hours. Brick chimneys rise from the pools, capped and obsolete, their sides wrapped in roots. Beneath the surface, submerged stairs descend to sealed basement doors.

The court has no breeze of its own. Air arrives through the ventilation system in slow, dependable pressure changes. When the main fans turn to their night cycle, every hanging leaf in the district leans a few degrees in the same direction. Steam thins. Condensation begins to collect more heavily on the metalwork. The lamps dim but do not go out.

Then the Lower Nursery seems less like a garden occupying a city than a city made from the habits of plants: tiered, crowded, opportunistic, sustained by circulation. Its masonry provides only the oldest pattern. Roots have drawn newer streets. Water chooses the active boundaries. Machinery keeps the climate from resolving into flood or stagnation.

It is warm, wet, deafening, and almost motionless—a complete little ward beneath the surface, packed to its limits, enduring through the patient cooperation of brick, iron, water, and leaf.
