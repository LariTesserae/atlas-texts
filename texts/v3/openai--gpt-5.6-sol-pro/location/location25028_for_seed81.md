The city stands high enough that the plains below have disappeared beneath weather.

It occupies a broad saddle between two worn volcanic ridges, neither summit nor valley but an elevated shelf of black stone extending for many kilometers. From its outer embankments, one sees only the nearest slopes and, beyond them, a dim copper haze. On clear intervals—brief openings lasting seconds—the horizon reveals itself as a darker line impossibly far below. Then vapor closes again, and the city seems suspended in a warm, gray void.

There is water everywhere, though almost none of it lies still.

It beads on ironwork, gathers under ceramic eaves, and sweats from the fitted joints of walls. The plateau’s stone is porous and saturated; pressure forces hot water through hairline cracks so that roads weep along their entire length. Gutters run continuously. Stairwells descend into ankle-deep films. From bridge undersides, condensation falls in slow, heavy drops, each one briefly silver in the low light.

Yet the air is nearly furnace-hot. Rain arrives already warm, striking roofs with the temperature of blood, and the larger cisterns steam. Anything exposed alternately drenches and dries, drenches and dries, acquiring layered skins of mineral residue: white carbonate, green-black copper salts, yellow sulfur, and a translucent glaze resembling old bone. The whole city looks repeatedly cast from its own runoff.

Nothing grows here.

There is no moss in the channels, no weed between paving blocks, no lichen softening the retaining walls. The empty soil beds are filled with crushed glass to prevent dust from blowing. Courtyards contain arrangements of stone fins and cooling pipes where trees may once have stood, though no surviving inscription calls them trees. Even decay is inorganic. Metal scales away; concrete opens into aggregate; glass becomes cloudy and then slumps at the edges under decades of heat.

The city is immense but sparsely occupied by objects. Its buildings are widely separated—long industrial halls, reservoir towers, black apartment stacks, exchange stations, silent foundries—each surrounded by expanses of wet pavement. Streets are broad enough for machines that no longer pass. Elevated conduits cross overhead in bundles, linking structures across gaps of several hundred meters. Many carry hot water. Others contain cables that vibrate faintly despite there being no visible source of power.

The architecture has been made for circulation rather than habitation.

Doors are few. Windows are narrow and placed high. Every district is connected by ramps, rails, pressure mains, drainage courses, and enclosed bridges. The oldest structures appear to have been carved directly from the plateau: low chambers with rounded corners, their surfaces polished by centuries of flowing water. Later construction rises above them in corroded steel and dark vitrified brick. Still later additions are lighter—frameworks of pale ceramic, heat-shields, suspended ducts—yet even these are weathered enough to seem native to the place.

There are no crowds. Usually there is no one at all.

Across a plaza, one may see a solitary maintenance carriage moving along a recessed track. It is a low six-wheeled thing with no cabin, carrying coils of pipe or sealed drums. It stops beneath a dripping gantry, waits, then continues. In the factory quarter, cranes rotate by a few degrees every hour, keeping their joints from seizing. Pumps wake in sequence beneath the streets. Valves open. Water changes direction. Somewhere underground, enormous pistons make one slow stroke and rest.

These processes give the city its life without making it noisy.

Most of the time the dominant sound is water close at hand: ticking from ledges, whispering through runnels, boiling softly behind walls. Distant machinery is felt before it is heard, a pressure in the jaw or a tremor transmitted through the soles. When a pumping cycle begins, the sound crosses the city as a series of muted knocks. A minute later, roof vents exhale white steam. Then everything subsides.

Thunder occurs below the plateau.

It rises through the cloud layer as a deep, smothered concussion, as though another city were moving heavy furniture beneath the world.

The dimness is constant. Dense atmospheric vapor absorbs the sun and spreads it into a rust-colored glow without direction. Shadows are weak except near industrial lamps, which burn in isolated amber cages over intersections and hatchways. The lamps do not illuminate far. Their light is caught by condensation and made solid-looking, each halo ending in a luminous wall of mist.

At certain hours the vapor itself glows faintly blue around energized cables. Droplets align along invisible electrical gradients, forming trembling sheets between pylons. These sheets are not barriers exactly, but objects passing through them emerge carrying a charge: rivets spark, water leaps sideways, and exposed metal begins to hum. The city’s oldest routes bend carefully around such regions. Newer streets pass straight through, marked by ceramic posts and warning grooves cut into the pavement.

Weather does not merely fall here; it circulates through the construction.

Warm cloud enters the western intake towers, condenses on nested metal vanes, and is routed underground. Deep in the plateau, the water meets hotter rock and returns as pressurized steam. Steam drives turbines, turbines drive pumps, and pumps draw more cloud into the city. What began as infrastructure has become a local climate. The city makes the rain that powers the city.

No one now knows whether the cycle was designed to sustain the settlement or whether the settlement was built to stabilize a natural cycle already present. The distinction has eroded. Pipes mineralize into stone. Stone fractures into channels. Channels are lined with metal. A map of the district resembles a diagram of an organ, while a geological cross-section resembles a machine.

The few animals are difficult to recognize as such.

Near the warmest drains, flat translucent things adhere to the undersides of grates. They are the size of a hand, nearly colorless, and consist mostly of folded membranes around a dark mineral kernel. They move only when water chemistry changes, peeling themselves loose and gliding upstream with slow contractions. Whether they feed, reproduce, or merely precipitate is uncertain.

Higher on the reservoir towers live pale, many-jointed scavengers with shells like fired porcelain. One may glimpse a single individual crossing a parapet after rain. It taps the wall with filament-thin legs, collecting droplets enriched with metal salts. At the slightest vibration it folds into a seamless bead and rolls into a drainage slot. Their abandoned shells accumulate in sheltered corners, indistinguishable from broken insulators.

Once or twice a season, something enormous passes through the cloud below. Its presence is known from the temporary failure of condensation: gutters cease, windows clear, and the city’s hanging droplets rise from surfaces and drift outward. A broad darkness moves under the ledges. No complete shape has ever been seen. Afterward, warm rain falls upward for several minutes, vanishing into the underside of the clouds.

The plateau itself is old and settled. Its cliffs have rounded back from the weather; its ravines are deep but no longer collapsing. Erosion here has reached an accommodation with construction. Flood channels follow streets. Foundations divert seepage into ancient faults. Buildings on the eastern edge have lost their lowest floors to the slow retreat of the escarpment, yet rather than fall, they are progressively bridged inland by new braces. From a distance they resemble empty cages leaning over the vapor.

Within the central district lies the Dry Basin, the only place without visible water. It is a circular depression almost a kilometer across, paved in black glass and surrounded by stepped administrative walls. The air above it shivers with heat. Rain evaporates several meters before reaching the ground.

At the basin’s center stands a small structure with no entrances: a cube of dull metal, weathered but uncorroded. Every conduit in the city ultimately approaches it. Some terminate at the basin rim. Some descend beneath the glass. Some emerge on the far side carrying nothing measurable.

When the city enters its longest pumping cycle, the cube becomes colder.

Condensation forms across it, first as a sheen, then as streams. The water runs upward over its faces and collects along the top edge. There it hangs, a dark, trembling volume, until the cycle ends. Then it lifts intact into the dim air and disperses as cloud.

This is the closest thing the empty city has to a ceremony.

All across the plateau, valves close. Carriages stop. Electrical sheets slacken between pylons. The rare porcelain creatures fold themselves away. For perhaps half a minute, even the subterranean engines are silent.

Only the upward water moves.

Then the pumps strike again, deep beneath the stone. Warm rain resumes. Steam pours from the towers, and the city returns to its patient work: collecting itself from the atmosphere, sending itself through pipes, and depositing itself in fresh mineral layers over the old.
