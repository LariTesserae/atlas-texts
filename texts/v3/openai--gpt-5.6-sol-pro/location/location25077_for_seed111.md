### The Cleft Above Weather

Near the summit of a young mountain, higher than the ordinary paths of clouds, the stone splits open.

The cleft is not large in extent. A person could walk around its rim in less than an hour if the rim permitted walking, though almost nowhere does. It is a cramped, cathedral-shaped wound: a narrow basin pressed between converging walls of black volcanic glass and pale, newly exposed rock. Pinnacles crowd its interior. Ridges rise like rows of blades, near enough in places that an animal can brace itself between them without touching the ground. The whole place seems packed beyond its capacity—with stone, wings, breath, echoes.

Its depth is difficult to judge. Darkness fills it long before the bottom should be reached.

The mountain is geologically young. Nothing has been rounded. Broken faces retain the sheen of the instant they split; ledges end in lips as sharp as chipped pottery. Frost has begun to work on the outermost edges, but it has not yet softened them. Here and there the walls display smooth, swollen folds where once-molten rock cooled while still creeping upward. Elsewhere they are shattered into thin plates that ring when struck.

There is no soil in the ordinary sense. Dust gathers only in seams sheltered from wings. The few level surfaces are made of fallen feathers, shed skins, powdered stone, and old droppings slowly welded by damp into a gray organic crust.

The cleft stands above most rain. Moisture comes intermittently and sideways. Clouds climb the mountain, catch on its summit horns, and are pressed through the opening as cold fog. For a few hours the interior sweats. Condensation pearls on every wall and runs down the glass in hair-thin threads. It gathers in small, perfectly black pockets beneath overhangs—basins no wider than a hand, but sometimes many arm-lengths deep where water has found a crack.

Then the cloud passes. The rivulets stop almost at once.

Water here is less a landscape than an event.

No stream crosses the cleft, though during rare storms every blade of rock sheds a silver skin. The water falls only a short distance before vanishing into fissures or being intercepted by the animals. Thousands drink directly from the walls. Some spread pleated membranes to collect droplets. Others sleep with their mouths open beneath reliable condensation points. When the stone dries, these creatures fold themselves shut and wait.

It is always cool and often near freezing, but the cold is rarely clean. The air is crowded with warm bodies. In the deepest chambers, the combined respiration of the colonies raises the temperature by several degrees. The walls there feel clammy rather than icy, and a faint animal vapor rises from them. Step away from the colonies and the cold returns abruptly.

Light enters reluctantly. The rim is frequently capped by cloud, and the narrowness of the opening reduces the sky to a crooked gray seam. Even at midday, most of the cleft remains in blue-black dusk. Wet surfaces give brief glints. Eyes answer from recesses in green, red, and dull pearl.

At night, there is almost no distinction between stone and open space.

Vegetation survives mainly as stains. A charcoal lichen grows on faces where fog strikes most often. A few mosses occupy the wettest cracks, each patch small enough to cover with a palm. During the brief thaw season, translucent stalks emerge from the organic crust. They have no true leaves. Their pale cups face downward, gathering warmth from the packed bodies below rather than light from above. They flower for three or four days and are stripped bare almost immediately.

Yet the cleft is full of life to an almost oppressive degree.

The air belongs to veilwings: dark, long-bodied animals with two broad flying membranes and several smaller steering fins. They do not wheel freely, for there is no room. They rise and sink through the stone blades in slow, vertical columns, folding one side of the body to pass narrow gaps. Tens of thousands cling to the walls in overlapping layers. Their claws fit into the fresh fractures as if both animal and mountain were made to the same pattern.

Veilwings feed not on the sparse plants but on what the mountain combs from the upper atmosphere. At dusk—though dusk here is only a further thickening of darkness—cold winds carry swarms of nearly invisible aeroplankton against the summit. The veilwings open their mouths and throat-fans. For hours, the cleft becomes a living net.

Below them crawl the hookbacks, squat six-limbed scavengers armored in slate-colored plates. They move through the colony without provoking alarm, pruning parasites and carrying away eggs that have failed. Their front limbs end in blunt gripping pads, but they commonly hold slivers of naturally fractured glass between them. With these they open leathery egg cases and scrape frozen tissue from cracks.

They do not shape the glass. They test one flake after another, keeping those with the right edge and dropping them when they dull. A particularly useful shard may pass from hookback to hookback for years, migrating through the cleft without ever belonging to any one creature. This is the nearest the place comes to craft. Nothing is built. Nothing is stored in an enduring cache. The stone supplies the tools already finished, and the users leave no order behind them.

Higher on the pinnacles live pale, ropelike grazers that feed on lichen and mineral film. They anchor their hind ends in crevices and extend several body lengths into open air. When alarmed, whole clusters retract at once, making the cliff seem to inhale.

In the water pockets are small eyeless swimmers whose bodies remain liquid-flexible even when the surface freezes. They survive long dry intervals as hard knots sealed into damp cracks. The arrival of fog wakes them. For a few hours the pools cloud with frantic reproduction, feeding, and predation; then the surface stills, and the inhabitants return to dormancy. Their lives are rapid, but the rhythm governing them is patient. A pool may wait half a year for its next wet hour.

The largest animals are seldom seen in full. They occupy the lowest dark, where the cleft narrows into a maze of warm fissures. Their presence is known by slow displacements among the roosting veilwings and by a deep, soft knocking transmitted through the rock. Occasionally a long head rises between two pinnacles, blind and furred with sensory filaments. It selects a crowded ledge, closes its mouth over several animals, and sinks again without haste.

Nothing flees far. There is nowhere far to go.

The overwhelming quantity of fauna makes the cleft loud, though no single sound dominates for long. Wings snap and rustle like heavy cloth. Claws tick continuously on glass. Hookbacks knock flakes together while choosing an edge. Water drips into hidden pools, each drop repeated by dozens of close echoes. The ropelike grazers produce a dry communal hiss. Beneath all of it is the colony’s respiration: a vast, uneven exhalation that rises and falls over several minutes.

At intervals, every veilwing goes silent.

The silence passes through the cleft from top to bottom, not instantaneously but as a descending front. Wings fold. Mouths close. Even the scavengers freeze with their tools held up. In that quiet, one can hear the mountain itself: minute frost fractures, pressure settling through new stone, and the distant submerged knocking from below.

Then one animal calls, and thousands answer.

The sound is not musical. It is a dense, metallic braying, amplified until the narrow basin seems to have become the throat of something enormous. Loose flakes tremble from the walls. Condensation falls as brief rain. The chorus may continue for an hour, exhausting itself gradually rather than ending at once.

Despite all this movement, the place changes slowly. The colony has occupied the cleft for longer than the oldest living animal, but perhaps not much longer than the cleft has existed. Its cycles are measured in cloud seasons, frost lines, accumulated crust, and the rare collapse of a pinnacle. A fallen slab may alter flight paths for generations. A newly opened crack becomes a pool, then a nursery, then a feeding territory, though no boundary marks these stages.

Erosion has only begun its argument with the mountain. The summit is still sharper than weather. The inhabitants do not tame it; they fit themselves into its fresh violence.

There are rules here, though none are written or deliberately enforced. Every surface is temporary. Every wet place belongs first to thirst. Height offers no openness, only another layer of occupation. Tools may be carried but not kept. The dead descend, feeding each level in turn, while life born in the lowest pools eventually rises on wings toward the narrow strip of sky.

From outside, the summit appears barren.

Only at the rim does the warm breath of the cleft reach the face. Only then does the darkness below stir, revealing that nearly every ledge is alive.
