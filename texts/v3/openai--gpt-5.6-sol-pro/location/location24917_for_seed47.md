At first it resembles a high desert: pale ground, cool sun, long shadows, nothing moving.

The surface is a hard mineral pavement the color of old bone, interrupted by seams of violet glass and low drifts of metallic dust. It has been exposed so long that there are no fresh fractures. Every stone has lost its corners. Ridges have been pared into smooth backs; impact scars have softened into shallow bowls; even crystals lie dulled beneath microscopic pitting. The land has not weathered through rain or rivers. Water is almost absent here, surviving only as hydroxyl locked inside certain black minerals. Erosion is accomplished by radiation, charged dust, thermal strain, and the infinitesimal abrasion of ages.

Then the horizon changes.

What appeared to be a distant plateau passes slowly in front of the sun. Its underside is visible: thousands of vertical kilometers of dark, tapered stone, descending into brightnessless air. Beyond it, another piece of country rotates almost imperceptibly, carrying its own plains and mountain stumps. Between them is not a valley but open space.

This landscape is part of a dispersed world.

Its crust no longer forms a sphere. It exists as an immense storm of continental fragments, shelves, needles, and mineral sheets circling a dim gravitational center. Some fragments are broad enough to have horizons and local weather. Others are no larger than houses. Their distribution is extraordinarily sparse. From a given surface, neighboring lands may seem close because of their size, but the clear gaps between them are hundreds or thousands of kilometers deep. The farthest pieces appear as narrow strokes of ocher and silver against the stars.

Elevation has become difficult to define. There is no sea level and no continuous ground. “Down” belongs to whichever slab one stands on, and even that is not entirely reliable. The largest fragments retain a gentle pull. Smaller stones borrow gravity from passing masses or from mechanisms hidden within them. A traveler can walk across a plain, climb a low ridge, and discover that the ridge is the worn lip of a cliff extending farther downward than the eye can follow. On certain margins, loose grains roll toward the edge, pause, and then stream upward into the sky.

The place is ancient beyond the usefulness of ruins. Its surviving landforms have reached their oldest stable shapes: low domes, blunt blades, broad abrasion terraces, pits with mathematically smooth walls. Nothing resembles a young mountain. Yet the whole region is violently dynamic.

The turbulence is chiefly silent.

Continents drift through one another’s gravitational wakes. Their orientations change. Invisible fields tighten and release. Entire gravel plains rise at once, every pebble lifting a finger’s width before settling in a new arrangement. Dust crosses the void in braided rivers hundreds of kilometers long, moving without wind. Where two streams of charged particles intersect, they twist into luminous knots and then unravel. Slabs the size of provinces flex like thin metal, although they are composed of stone. On the largest fragments, the ground can tilt several degrees over the course of an afternoon. Shadows alter direction without the sun moving. Horizons climb, split, and withdraw.

There are collisions, but few are abrupt. The old surfaces seem governed by a rule against sharp contact. Approaching masses begin to shear apart into flakes, or exchange momentum through forces that have no visible carrier. One continent may pass through the loose outer layers of another in a slow storm of interpenetrating dust. For several days the sky fills with boulders. Then the two lands separate, each missing a little of itself and wearing a little of the other.

The thin atmosphere clings locally to the larger fragments. It is cold and dry, mostly argon, nitrogen, and mineral vapor, too tenuous to carry ordinary sound far. Footsteps are felt through the soles before they are heard. A falling stone makes one small tick. Dust striking a visor resembles faint static. During the passage of a neighboring continent, there may be a deep pressure in the ribs, but no roar. Even the largest upheavals occur with the solemn quiet of objects moving behind glass.

In sunlight the landscape is plainly visible, without perpetual gloom or theatrical coloration. The star is a normal white disk. Exposed rock appears beige, gray, rust-red, or black. Shadows are clean and cool. Only the emptiness makes the light uncanny: it illuminates surfaces separated by distances that the eye persistently underestimates. At night, reflected light from other fragments may create several dim “moons,” each showing cliffs, plains, and perhaps a single standing tower.

Life occupies narrow exceptions.

In protected cracks grows a sparse black mat that is neither quite lichen nor plant. Its threads penetrate silicate grains and extract charge from temperature differences. During daylight it lies flat, dull as soot. At dusk it raises transparent vanes no thicker than soap film, gathering static from the passing dust. Colonies may live for tens of thousands of years and spread only a few meters. Their dead layers become glossy dark stains that are among the few organic marks on the stone.

Elsewhere, isolated stalks stand in fields where certain metals concentrate. They are low, jointed things with ceramic skins and branching crowns. They do not seek moisture. Their roots follow electrical gradients, and their crowns open only when another landmass approaches. For an hour or a century, depending on the encounter, they release glittering spores into the gravitational current. Most vanish into the void. A very few reach another surface.

Animals are rarer still. The most commonly reported are pale, many-limbed crawlers living beneath warm stones near active seams. They fold themselves almost flat and may remain dormant for generations. At the margins there are gliding organisms shaped like strips of dark cloth. They launch only when the local downward pull weakens, sailing across gaps that may be wider than countries. They have no wings in the ordinary sense; their tissues adjust to electric and gravitational gradients. Most crossings seem impossible. Empty skins found on distant slabs prove that some succeed.

Scattered through the dispersed crust are structures.

They are infrequent enough that a traveler may cross a continental plate without seeing one. Some resemble narrow towers, seamless and slightly inclined, their surfaces reflecting the sky while refusing to reflect the observer. Others are rings set horizontally into ancient stone, from a few meters to hundreds of kilometers across. Dust avoids them. Cracks approaching their edges curve away. The oldest mineral mats grow toward them but never touch.

No roads connect these artifacts. There are only faint alignments: rows of shallow sockets, straight discolorations visible from altitude, occasional chambers opened by erosion and found to contain nothing but altered gravity. A building may consist of a doorway standing alone on a plain. Passing through it does not lead elsewhere in space; instead, the local horizon rotates, and for a few minutes a person can walk along what had been a vertical face.

The mechanisms appear to regulate the storm without calming it. They prevent catastrophic collapse while allowing constant rearrangement. Perhaps they preserve the fragments. Perhaps they are breaking them apart with exquisite patience. A continent approaching the central void accelerates, deforms, and emerges elsewhere in the swarm with its ancient surface intact but its internal strata reordered. Mountain roots appear above ground. Buried ruins become exposed. Surface dust is sealed into deep veins. The world continually destroys its geography while conserving its age.

This is one of its central laws: nothing becomes young again.

Every new surface emerges already abraded. Freshly split rock dulls within moments, not through rapid weathering but as if its unspent sharpness has been transferred elsewhere. New craters slump into old profiles. Constructed objects, if left unattended, retain their function but acquire the visual exhaustion of geological time. Edges round. Colors settle. Materials enter the landscape’s final vocabulary of smoothness, pallor, and silence.

Another law concerns distance. Empty space is not consistent here. Two points visible across a gap may be days apart in the morning and unreachable by evening. Paths depend on the arrangement of the larger slabs, the operation of the scattered towers, and the slow breathing of the central field. Navigation is therefore done by sequence rather than direction: cross when the black ridge eclipses the western ring; wait until loose dust rises; follow the shadow that belongs to no nearby object.

At intervals, the entire dispersed world enters a high storm-state. The causes are not apparent. The gravitational center flickers or shifts. Every fragment begins to migrate. The sky fills with rotating countries. Plains bend into shallow bowls. Cliffs shed sheets of stone that sail outward like leaves. Dust braids tighten into radiant cords. The sparse plants close themselves inside mineral shells, and the gliding animals vanish from the margins.

There is almost no sound.

One may stand beneath an ordinary white sun and watch a mountain thousands of kilometers high turn end over end above a plain. Pebbles hover around the ankles. A distant tower repeats itself along the horizon, appearing in twenty places at once. The ground changes its idea of downward. Far below—or overhead—a continent opens along an ancient seam and unfolds into a thin, shining fan.

Then the phase passes. The great fragments drift apart. Dust settles in new hollows. Gravity becomes local again. The horizon stabilizes around an altered set of neighbors.

The land looks as though nothing has happened. Its stones are still rounded, its plains still vacant, its rare black growths scarcely larger than before. Every surface carries the calm of something that reached its final form ages ago.

Only the sky remains crowded with the slow departure of worlds.
