## The White Undergreen

The first impression is of a sky where no sky should be.

Far below the sealed surface, beneath strata of ice and dark stone, an immense hollow opens—so broad that its far wall lies beyond the curve of sight. A cold white radiance fills it from end to end. There is no central sun. Light comes from the landscape itself: from pale membranes stretched across the ceiling, from veins in the ground, from suspended seed-bodies glowing like distant moons. Shadows exist, but they are thin and bluish, and rarely point in the same direction.

The hollow is mostly empty air.

Its floor may be thirty kilometers below its roof; in some regions the gap is much greater. Through that space rise only occasional columns, root-trunks of impossible height, narrow enough to seem like threads at a distance. They connect floor to ceiling, or nearly do. Some terminate in branching crowns that hold nothing up. Others have snapped recently, leaving bright splintered ends and slowly falling cables of vine.

Yet every solid thing here is vegetation.

There is no exposed stone. What looks like a cliff is a cut face through compressed wood, layered in cream, green, violet, and black. What looks like shale is stacked leaf-skin. The sharp scree at the foot of a slope consists of brittle seed scales and splinters of frozen pith. Even the translucent ridges underfoot are old sap, hardened into amber-clear plates and repeatedly cracked by the cold.

The terrain is young—not because the hollow itself is new, but because its surfaces never remain old. Growth continually overturns them. A plain swells overnight into a pleated ridge as buried roots drink from some hidden pocket. A cliff unfolds into fans. Needle forests erupt, flower once, then collapse into rafts of pale fiber. The land is always recently broken, recently healed, recently made strange. Edges remain sharp. Fresh wounds shine almost painfully in the omnipresent light.

There is little ordinary weather. Moisture is too scarce and too unevenly distributed for that. Most of the air is dry enough to pull water from exposed skin, and cold enough that breath falls as powder. But water travels through the living substrate in vast concealed pulses. When one of these pulses reaches the ceiling membranes, condensation blooms across hundreds of square kilometers.

Then rain begins.

It may fall only in one narrow shaft, a silver column descending through otherwise empty brightness. Elsewhere it gathers in suspended leaf-basins, each basin kilometers wide but only a handspan deep. Some water never reaches the floor. It freezes in descent, becoming a slow glitter of needles, or is caught by hairlike vines that uncoil into the rain and drink it away.

The permanent pools are few. They occupy hollows between folded roots or the sealed cups of enormous flowers. Their water is dark, intensely clear, and covered with flexible skins of duckweed-like tissue. Beneath those skins, roots braid and unbraid in patient currents. No fish move there. Occasionally something touches the underside from below, raising a traveling bulge, but it seldom breaks through.

Despite the cold, the vegetation is not dormant. Its chemistry is built around freezing. Sap moves in briny threads. Leaves grow as thin mineralized blades that harvest the cavern’s cold light. Frost itself is cultivated: feathery organs reach into damp air, gather ice, then fold inward and dissolve it with stored salts. In dry districts, whole plains are covered in white collecting hairs. They resemble snow until they turn, almost together, toward the passage of a condensation cloud.

The great emptiness makes each living form feel isolated, though all are joined beneath the surface. A single green-black stalk may stand fifty kilometers from its nearest neighbor, yet both are lobes of the same buried organism. What appears to be a meadow may be one leaf. What appears to be a mountain range may be the ridged back of a tuber extending farther down than anyone has measured.

Change comes violently, but often without much sound.

A growth-front can cross a plain in an hour: the floor bulges, splits into geometric seams, and sends up ranks of glassy shoots. They climb, branch, shed luminous dust, and wither before the last rank has emerged. Elsewhere, old root vaults lose pressure and collapse inward, swallowing valleys into soft black cavities. Ceiling membranes periodically release their tension. Vast folds snap loose and descend through the hollow like slow sails, trailing frozen pollen and luminous shreds.

The air is thin, and fibrous surfaces swallow echoes. Even immense events are strangely muted. A falling trunk produces first a distant dry tearing, then a long interval, then a low pressure felt in the ribs. Rain hisses only where it strikes frozen leaves. Growing tissue makes faint clicks, like cooling pottery. In the broadest reaches there is almost nothing to hear but one’s own clothing and the intermittent fall of ice grains.

There are winds, though they do not behave like surface winds. Respiration drives them. When a continent-sized leaf-bed opens its pores, air streams toward it from every direction, cold and steady. When the pores close, the current reverses. During mass flowering, heat from rapid growth creates local storms: pollen spirals climb thousands of meters, collide with descending frost, and become pale lightning-filled vortices. These storms are spectacularly bright and nearly silent. Their charge crawls along vines in blue-white threads rather than leaping through open air.

Fauna exists, but sparingly. Most motion belongs to plants.

Small six-legged grazers live inside warm knots of rotting pith, emerging only during sap surges. They are colorless and nearly transparent; green fluid can be seen moving through them after they feed. Long-bodied gliders sometimes cross the open gulf, riding respiratory winds between widely separated crowns. Their wings are stiff fans of borrowed leaf tissue, cultivated on their backs from spores. They may travel for months without landing.

Larger animals are known mostly by signs: a cleanly severed root cable; parallel impressions in a pollen bank; an emptied water-cup with its rim crushed inward. Nothing here supports large numbers of hungry bodies. A creature that cannot sleep for years, migrate enormous distances, or feed directly on light does not last.

Scattered through the hollow are the works of people, though no settlement is large enough to alter the view.

Their shelters hang beneath root arches or cling to the high side of trunks, safely above sudden growth-fronts. They are woven from living strips rather than cut lumber. Walls continue to thicken after construction and must be pruned from within. Doors are narrow wounds trained not to heal. Roofs curl closed when frost falls and open during rare rain.

Metal is uncommon. Tools are made from thorn, hardened sap, ice-glass, and the black cutting edges of ancient seed husks. A fresh husk can be sharper than flint but becomes soft if warmed. Fire is possible only in sealed claylike pods made from pulped bark, and fuel is too valuable to burn casually. Light needs no tending; even enclosed rooms glow faintly through their walls.

Paths are marked with braided cords and shallow cuts, because the landscape cannot be trusted to preserve a road. Bridges are grown: a shoot is aimed across a fissure, tensioned with fiber, and fed until it reaches the opposite side. Towers are little more than platforms on the tallest stalks, used to watch the weather of distant organs. From them, observers track rain shafts, pollen storms, collapsing leaf-fields, and the slow movement of the ceiling.

The oldest structures are not the largest. They are small rings of dead wood driven into places where the ground has remained still. Some surround nothing. Others contain beads, broken thorn blades, strips of faded membrane, or a cup kept deliberately dry. Their purpose is uncertain. In a world where all material tends to sprout, swell, close over, or be reabsorbed, preserving an empty space may itself be sacred.

There are rules one learns quickly.

Never camp on a surface without veins; it may be the lid of a closed flower.

Never follow warmth unless water is visible. Warmth usually means fermentation, pressure, or rapid growth.

When the light dims, climb. Darkness is not night here. It means that some vast organ has turned its inner face away, and the air beneath it will soon move.

Do not cut a root that trembles in rhythm with your pulse. The plant is already sensing you.

And if rain begins to fall upward—droplets lifting from a pool toward the ceiling—leave the basin immediately. A new connection has opened somewhere in the buried vascular world. The ground will fold toward it.

From a high platform, the White Undergreen appears almost peaceful. The luminous ceiling has the smooth brightness of overcast noon. Root columns vanish into haze. Rain hangs in isolated silver curtains. Far off, a field of needle-leaves turns in a coordinated wave, each blade catching the light, until an area the size of a country flashes white.

Then a seam opens across the plain.

The floor peels back in petals. A forest rises from beneath it, already full-grown, its crowns spreading into the empty air. For a few moments the cavern seems crowded. Then the tallest trunks split, release clouds of shining seed, and begin to fall.

By the time the sound arrives, the next landscape is already forming.
