The water has no visible edge.

It occupies an ancient basin so broad that currents turn within it like weather systems. Cold fronts exist underwater: mile-deep bodies of denser water advancing slowly beneath comparatively mild upper layers, carrying veils of silt and the faint mineral taste of distant masonry. There are tides, but they are weak and complicated, divided by innumerable walls below. A rise of a handspan may take days to pass from one district of the basin to another.

Nearly everything lies under water. The surface, where it can be reached, is a dark sheet beneath permanent cloud and rain. There is no clear division between lake and sky there. Rain strikes the surface continuously, though usually without violence, and mist hangs low enough to make the horizon seem close. Below, however, the flooded world extends for hundreds of leagues.

It is a low world, built outward rather than upward. Its highest surviving structures are only broad-backed ridges under the water; its deepest places are trenches between foundations. There are no mountains, cliffs, reefs, or natural beds. Every surface is made.

The bottom consists of fitted slabs.

Some are stone, hauled long ago from sources no longer exposed. Others are fired clay, dark glassy brick, compressed chalk, shell-lime without shells, or timber made almost mineral by cold immersion. The slabs range from the size of a palm to platforms large enough to carry forests. Beneath them are further courses: paving over vaults, vaults over drainage chambers, chambers over older paving. A probing pole may descend through cracks for many lengths without touching anything that was not shaped, placed, packed, or joined by hands.

There is no raw earth here. What passes for soil is the accumulated decay of gardens, mats of microorganisms, powdered mortar, softened wood, and fine sediment worn from the works themselves.

The construction is immense but not sophisticated in its parts. No single joint requires more than rope, lever, wedge, mallet, fire, patience, and many workers. Blocks were fitted by abrasion. Timbers were pegged rather than nailed. Arches were raised on woven frames. Water channels were sealed with clay, pitch, fiber, and crushed mineral. The basin did not begin as one plan. It became total through repetition. One generation lined a shore; another built farther into the shallows; another roofed channels and planted the roofs. Over ages, every remaining patch was faced, terraced, bridged, dredged, or paved. The scale came from duration rather than machinery.

Now the workmanship has been softened almost beyond recognition.

Edges have rounded. Steps have become shallow ripples. Inscriptions survive only as long, smooth depressions where water moves differently. Roads can be distinguished from walls mainly by width. Great doors lie fused to their thresholds under blankets of green growth. Towers collapsed so long ago that their stones have been reworked by currents into low oval mounds, each still betraying its manufacture through repeated sizes and regular seams.

Erosion has reached a kind of old equilibrium. Nothing breaks quickly. The cold limits rot; the mild currents lack the force to tear whole structures apart. Instead, water works grain by grain. Mortar clouds and settles nearby. A wall loses material from its exposed face and gains nearly the same material in its sheltered joints. The built world is gradually redistributing itself without ceasing to be built.

Vegetation covers much of it.

In the upper water, broad dark fronds grow from sockets, gutters, roof seams, and the perforations of drowned screens. They are olive-black rather than green, adapted to the weak gray light passing through cloud, rain, and turbid water. Some spread flat just beneath the surface, collecting what little daylight exists. Others form hanging forests along the sides of terraces, descending until light is too poor even for them.

Farther down, the growth changes. Pale bacterial films coat masonry in layers as thin as breath on glass. Rust-colored threads gather where water seeps through iron-rich stone. White and yellow colonies surround slow mineral leaks from buried cisterns and old kilns. These deep growths do not depend on sunlight. They live on chemical differences maintained by water passing through the many buried strata of the basin.

There are no animals.

No fish move between the stems. No crustaceans pick at the paving. No worms turn the sediment, no birds cross the surface, and no insects hatch in the rain-dark air. The vegetation consequently grows in peculiar completeness. Fronds remain uneaten until age and current pull them loose. Dead material does not vanish into mouths; it sinks slowly, folds into corners, and becomes dense felt. Seedlike spores travel only by water. Pollination, where needed, is performed by contact between drifting strands or by the release of fine reproductive dust into seasonal currents.

Without grazers, many gardens choke themselves. A terrace may support a dense canopy for centuries, then darken its own lower layers until the holdfasts fail. The entire mass lifts away in one slow sheet and drifts into a neighboring court. Such events are among the largest movements in the landscape.

The drowned structures create distinct provinces.

There are plains of paving where rows of low curbs run beyond sight. In these places the water is open and bitterly cold, and vegetation survives chiefly in the cracks. There are roof-fields, each roof sagging gently between supports, densely clothed in ribbonweed. There are labyrinths of chambers whose ceilings have partly fallen, leaving long parallel galleries. There are vast circular reservoirs inside the greater basin, their walls now submerged, where water remains exceptionally still and clear.

In some districts, upright posts stand in close ranks. They may once have carried floors above the old waterline. Now they support layered canopies of vegetation, making submerged woods with no true trees. Elsewhere are terraces of shallow trays, tens of thousands together, perhaps former cultivation beds. Their partitions remain visible under black silt. The trays collect pale mats that rise and settle with tiny pressure changes.

The densest regions are almost impassable. Walls, screens, poles, ropes hardened by mineral deposits, fallen beams, and curtains of growth occupy every depth. There may be only narrow channels between them, and even these turn back upon themselves. Yet the density is orderly. Repeated dimensions recur everywhere: the span of a forearm, the length of a body, the distance a rope could be drawn taut, the comfortable reach of a worker kneeling in water. Human measure is embedded in a landscape now too large to comprehend.

Light diminishes quickly. Near the surface there is a colorless dusk. Ten or twenty lengths down, forms become silhouettes. Deeper still, touch and current matter more than sight. Occasionally a seam of very old luminous bacteria gives off a subdued blue-green haze, not enough to illuminate a chamber, only enough to reveal the outline of the surface it inhabits. Most of the drowned world remains completely dark.

The silence is not absolute.

Rain produces a remote, continuous hiss overhead. In submerged halls it arrives as a soft pressure-noise, felt as much as heard. Water moving through a narrow sluice gives a low breath. A loose slab may knock once against its bed and then remain still for years. Vegetation brushes vegetation with a dry-seeming rasp transmitted through the water. At great intervals, some distant roof settles. The sound travels through connected masonry as a long sequence of dull taps, each fainter than the last.

There is little suddenness. Storms at the surface take days to influence the lower chambers. Cold seasonal water descends along the outer retaining walls and spreads across the paving in slow invisible floods. Silt clouds may remain suspended for months. A broken beam takes generations to be buried. Whole districts alter, but on a scale too patient for observation: channels close, gardens migrate, mortar becomes sediment, and sediment hardens again inside joints.

The world’s principal rule is that water must pass through what has been made.

It cannot flow in natural valleys, so it follows corridors, spillways, staircases, ducts, courtyards, collapsed roofs, and the hairline separations between fitted blocks. Old construction continues to govern every current. A wall laid in a forgotten age may determine where cold water gathers today, where a dark garden thrives, and where mineral films spread a thousand years from now.

Another rule is that nothing returns to wilderness. There is no wilderness underneath. When a structure fails, it falls onto another structure. When vegetation makes soil, that soil rests in a basin, tray, room, gutter, or paved depression. When currents expose a buried layer, they reveal worked surfaces below: another floor, another roof, another arrangement of patient hand-laid material.

The place is therefore both ruin and habitat, ocean and artifact. It has been submerged so long that drowning is no longer an event in its character. The water belongs here now. The gardens belong here. The cold, the dimness, and the near-silence have worn away any sense of catastrophe.

Across the vast low basin, rain continues to fall onto water. Far beneath it, black fronds lean together over roads no longer traveled, and the oldest walls quietly decide the direction of the sea.
