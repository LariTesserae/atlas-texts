At the summit of a long, bare escarpment lies a hollow called the **Rattle Garden**.

It is not large. From one rim, a person can distinguish the opposing wall through the dimness: a broken crescent of ocher stone perhaps three hundred paces away. Yet the hollow feels larger because its floor is uneven and much of its interior is hidden behind pillars, suspended growths, and curtains of blowing dust. Paths double back through clefts only wide enough for a shoulder. A central chimney rises nearly to the height of the rim, dividing the place into chambers like the interior of a ruined cathedral.

There is no water here. None beneath the ground, none stored in stems, none falling from the sky. The rock has been dry farther back than memory. Powdered salts lie in the cracks, and anything damp brought into the hollow is stripped of its moisture within moments. Skin tightens. Wood creaks. Clay vessels develop pale seams.

Nevertheless, the Garden is lush.

Its vegetation is not green. Tall, widely spaced trunks of black fiber stand across the slopes, each rooted in a collar of splintered shale. They resemble trees only from a distance. Up close, their bark is woven from millions of hollow threads, and their branches divide into thin resonant vanes of copper, amber, and bone-white. These vanes absorb warmth, mineral dust, and reactive gases exhaled by vents in the mountain. The plants live by trapping the wind.

Their crowns are enormous. A dozen trunks can roof an entire section of the hollow, though there is ample empty ground between them. Overhead, their flat leaves overlap in dark fans, leaving the floor in a warm, reddish dusk. Each leaf is hard and dry, more like mica or horn than flesh. It grows at one edge while being continually abraded at the other. Fragments snap off and tumble through the Garden in glittering flocks.

Lower growths occupy the sheltered faces of stone. There are velvet-black mats that feed on static charge, brittle fans that open whenever a shadow crosses them, and coils of pale membrane packed inside cracks. During calm intervals these coils seem dead. When the air begins to move, they unroll across the rock and expose thousands of tiny hooks, catching airborne grains rich in sulfur and iron.

Nothing here droops. Nothing wilts. Life chips, frays, tarnishes, delaminates, and sheds.

The hollow is warm even in darkness. Heat rises steadily from fissures under the central chimney, carrying the odor of hot flint, old spice, singed hair, and metal struck against metal. The warmth never becomes oppressive, for the summit wind is relentless. It enters through three notches in the rim and collides over the hollow. The opposing currents fold into one another, split around the chimney, plunge to the floor, and surge upward again. Dust rises in columns, collapses, and is immediately lifted elsewhere.

This is the source of the Garden’s great noise.

Every living surface has evolved to sound. The high leaves clap and chatter. Hollow trunks boom in different pitches according to their age. Hooked coils hiss against the rock. Seed cases spin on their cords, producing shrill, continuous whistles. Loose mineral plates skip over the ground with the clatter of thrown pottery. At times the entire hollow becomes a single instrument: a low pulse from the trunks, a crackling middle register from the crowns, and above it the piercing cries of animals.

The fauna are abundant but rarely large. Empty space belongs to the plants; motion belongs to the animals.

Six-legged runners race between patches of shadow, their feet ending in soft pads that adhere briefly to stone. They are the color of ash until startled, when plates along their backs turn reflective and scatter fragments of the dim sky. Long-bodied grazers hang upside down beneath the leaves, scraping thin layers from them with rotating mouthparts. They do not eat the plants so much as polish them, feeding on reactive crystals that accumulate overnight.

There are swarms of thumb-sized sail creatures whose bodies are almost weightless. They cannot choose where they fly. Instead they alter the shape of their translucent fins and allow the turbulent currents to sort them. Entire populations may be swept from one side of the hollow to the other in seconds, striking branches with a sound like handfuls of dry seeds. Predators follow: narrow, many-jointed things that launch themselves from trunk to trunk, opening nets of barbed filament in midair.

The loudest animals live inside the plants. They are pale, eyeless borers that chew chambers into the hollow fibers. Colonies communicate by striking their heads against the interior walls. One trunk may contain tens of thousands of them. Their rhythms pass from tree to tree through touching branches, and just before a major gust the whole Garden begins to knock.

That warning is useful, because the place is never stable for long.

A wind event can remake the hollow in an afternoon. Branches interlock, snap, and become lodged elsewhere. Whole crowns tear free and walk awkwardly across the basin, propped on hooked limbs. Dust buries one path and excavates another. Static-fed mats detach from the walls and roll into black, twitching balls. Animals pour after them. Newly exposed vents ignite in brief blue flashes, while older vents choke on debris and fall silent.

What appears to be destruction is often migration. Many of the largest plants have no permanent roots. Their trunks wedge themselves into suitable fractures and remain there for years, perhaps decades, growing broad crowns. When the fracture widens or the wind reaches a particular pitch, the root collar shatters by design. The trunk is carried a short distance, shedding branches, until it catches in another cleft. There it begins again. The Garden’s arrangement changes constantly, but its overall character has remained much the same for ages.

The stone bears this history. Walls are polished at shoulder height by centuries of passing crowns. Grooves curve around the central chimney where uprooted trunks have circled it again and again. Pits in the floor are packed with finely ground leaf fragments, insect shells, salt, and black fibers. The deposits do not decay. They compress into a light, tough material that can be cut in slabs.

People have done so.

Small shelters stand in the lee of the western wall, scattered far enough apart that one collapsing roof will not strike another. They are low, windowless structures built from stacked shale and slabs of compressed garden debris. Their doorways face away from the prevailing wind and are closed with curtains of braided fiber weighted by stone teeth. No mortar binds the walls. Mortar requires liquid, and liquid has no place here. Instead, every stone is notched, wedged, and tied.

The inhabitants use flint blades, bone punches, simple copper hooks, and ceramic hammers baked directly over the hot fissures. Their finest craft is knotting. Cord is wealth, architecture, clothing, memory, and law. Knotted lines brace roofs, tether tools, mark safe routes, record gust cycles, and keep children from being lifted into the crowns. A family’s history may be worn as a belt whose patterns can be read by touch in the dimness.

There is no central village. The Garden will not permit one. Houses gathered too closely alter the wind and create destructive eddies. Thus the shelters remain sparse: three under one ledge, another alone beyond the chimney, two more half buried near the southern notch. Between them stand small frames strung with tuned fibers. These are weather instruments, alarms, and perhaps shrines. When the wind shifts, they sing before anything else does.

Fire is possible but dangerous. Most fuel burns too quickly, and sparks travel far. Cooking is done in sealed stone boxes placed beside thermal cracks. Food consists of roasted borers, eggs scraped from the undersides of leaves, mineral-fed growth mats, and the dense inner tissue of young wind plants. Nothing is boiled. Nothing is washed. Dust is removed with brushes, vibration, or handfuls of abrasive powder.

The people cover their mouths with layered masks, not because the air is poisonous, but because the Garden is fertile. Seeds are everywhere. They are finer than flour and equipped with microscopic teeth. In open air they remain dormant, but inside a warm lung they attempt to unfold.

Death here is followed by swift enclosure. A body left exposed would become a nursery by morning.

Above the hollow, the sky is usually obscured by high sheets of mineral haze. Daylight arrives as a dull copper glow with no visible sun. At intervals the haze thins, and a hard white beam crosses the Garden. Every reflective animal flashes at once. Leaves rotate toward it. The black charge-mats contract. For a few breaths the entire place becomes dazzling, sharply edged, almost motionless.

Then the beam passes. Warm dusk returns. The winds meet over the chimney. Trunks begin to drum, seed cases scream on their cords, shelters strain against their knots, and the living roof rearranges itself once more.
