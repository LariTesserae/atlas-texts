The high country lies under two kilometers of black water.

It is high not because it approaches the air—there may be no air left anywhere—but because the surrounding seafloor falls away from it on every side. The place is an immense drowned tableland, a pale elevation suspended above trenches too deep for the weak descending light. Along its margins, whole districts have slumped outward into darkness. From those edges the water rises in cold vertical rivers, strikes the plateau, and breaks into ceaseless weather.

Nothing here is natural substrate. There is no bedrock under the silt, no continental bone. If one could descend through the floor, one would find vault below vault: pressure-crushed galleries, sealed courtyards, blind stairways, chambers nested inside larger chambers. The plateau is entirely made.

Yet there are no machines.

No cables cross the floors, no lamps burn, no doors remember how to open. Tool marks are absent. The substance of the place is a secretion-stone, dense and faintly translucent, laid down in overlapping mineral sheets as shells are laid down. Some extinct population may have grown the world rather than engineered it. Perhaps there was never a distinction. Pillars fork like ribs because branching carried weight; domes resemble skulls because curves endured pressure. What appear at first to be streets may simply be the spaces left between neighboring structures as each enlarged itself over centuries.

Age has erased purpose more thoroughly than ruin could. Corners have been softened into saddles. Walls have been planed down to low, whale-backed ridges. The oldest plazas are shallow bowls paved with millions of fitted scales, each scale worn smooth enough to reflect a dull smear of blue. Tower bases remain, but the towers themselves have been dispersed as sand through distant basins. In sheltered interiors, reliefs survive: repeating cups, spirals, sockets, and branching channels. They might be ornament, language, circulation system, or the fossil geometry of the builders’ bodies.

The plateau extends farther than sight. Nearby forms loom with deceptive clarity and then vanish in suspended mineral dust. Beyond them are dim arcades, then open causeways, then clusters of hollow mounds like abandoned neighborhoods. The greatest gaps are broad fields of nearly level pavement where only an occasional tilted fin breaks the current. Distance is measured by the fading of surfaces rather than by horizon. There is always another pale structure emerging behind the last one, though it may take hours to reach.

The water is bitterly cold. It does not merely surround the plateau; it moves across it with violent, complicated intent.

Tidal masses collide over the high ground. Upwellings from the trenches punch through collapsed rotundas. Dense sheets of brine pour down stairways and gather in chambers before spilling through cracks. At narrow passages, currents become muscular enough to strip loose animals from the walls. Pressure waves pass across entire districts, setting arches trembling. Fine grit races over the pavements in low silver storms, engraving the same ancient surfaces still further.

Thus the place is both settled into its final shape and never still. Its architecture changes by subtraction. A balustrade thins for ten thousand years, then vanishes in a single turbulent hour. A sealed chamber fails; the water enters with a concussion, and a cloud of untouched sediment blooms from within. An arcade stands through ages of cold, then folds quietly as a joint gives way. Ruin here is not an event but the climate.

There is little sound most of the time. The water muffles and enlarges silence. One hears the soft, continuous abrasion of grit, like breath passing over teeth. There are occasional porcelain clicks from feeding animals, distant knocks transmitted through the constructed floor, and low groans when pressure moves through the buried vaults. During the strongest currents, the whole plateau acquires a nearly inaudible tone. Each hollow structure answers at its own pitch. The drowned districts hum together for a few minutes, an immense chord felt more in cartilage than heard.

There is no vegetation. No fronds wave from the parapets, no moss stains the stone, no rooted colony softens the open squares. Even the familiar mats and films of simpler life are rare. The surfaces remain naked—white, gray, blue-black, or rust-colored where unusual salts have entered old fractures.

Animal life, however, is everywhere.

It is abundant without making the landscape feel crowded. Most of it drifts in the water as sparse constellations: clear-bodied swimmers with rows of pale cilia, chains of beadlike grazers, disc-shaped hunters that flex once and glide for many meters. Clouds of pin-sized shells gather in the lee of walls, vanish when the current turns, and reform elsewhere. Long animals as thin as wire pass through holes in the pavement. Their bodies carry no light; they are visible only when they cross the faint blue above.

Larger creatures keep great distances from one another. Six-limbed crawlers move over the domes, testing the surface with delicate hooked feet. They scrape mineral residue, eggs, and the discarded casings of smaller fauna from the old secretion-stone. Their shells closely match the architecture, so that an apparently fixed gargoyle may detach itself and descend a wall.

In the deeper courtyards, broad white animals hover head-down over shafts. They feed on whatever the vertical currents deliver. Each has a ring of soft jaws and a mantle armored with loose plates scavenged from the floor. When alarmed, it releases the plates all at once. The false armor clatters downward while the animal contracts into a ribbon and disappears.

Predators are seldom seen whole. A moving shadow crosses three buildings. A row of pressure-sensitive pits closes along the street. Small creatures flatten themselves against the paving. Then something long and colorless passes overhead, too high and dim to resolve, bending the current around its body. Its wake arrives several seconds later and throws loose shells into the air.

Life gathers around turbulence rather than shelter. Current junctions are feeding grounds. Breached chambers become nurseries. Fresh collapses expose sealed pockets of concentrated salts, and animals may travel across half the plateau to reach them. For a season—or for what passes as a season in the lightless water—a new ruin will teem. Then the pocket is exhausted, the current shifts, and the district empties again.

The surviving fauna also modifies the structures, though none uses tools. Crawlers seal their eggs into old sockets. Tube-bodied animals dissolve narrow passages through walls and line them with hard secretions. Colonial shell-creatures join broken slabs with living hinges, only to abandon them when they have reproduced. Over generations these acts can brace a collapsing dome or divert a stream of water into a new avenue. No creature comprehends the resulting plan. Nevertheless, construction continues: instinctive, incidental, and inseparable from feeding and birth.

There are rules to this place.

Nothing rests permanently where it falls. The currents sort every loose thing by shape. Flat fragments migrate to the eastern basins; spheres collect in the blind courts; needles lodge in archways and form trembling barriers. Bodies, shells, and architectural debris enter the same circulation.

Openings inhale and exhale. A shaft that draws water downward today may erupt tomorrow. Animals recognize the change before it happens and leave in sudden, disciplined streams. The cause lies far below, in flooded volumes too deep and interconnected to map.

Height is safety only from burial, never from current. Hollows offer refuge only until they become traps.

Light does not belong to living things. The dim illumination comes from far above, sifted through immense depths, and from the material of the plateau itself. Certain old walls retain a weak blue afterglow when struck or fractured. During collapses, fresh interiors shine for a few hours. Animals converge on these cold gleams, though the light provides neither warmth nor food. Perhaps it resembles some vanished signal. Perhaps the attraction is older than the animals now obeying it.

At the outer rim, the made ground ends without transition. Pavement narrows to a lip polished by ages of overflow. Beyond it is the trench, a vertical absence in which the descending walls soon vanish. Fragments torn from the plateau fall there continually. Some can be watched for a long time, turning slowly through the dimness, before darkness takes them.

From below, other fragments sometimes rise.

They are smooth, hollow forms buoyed by trapped fluids: pieces of deeper architecture broken free from buried levels. They ascend through the trench like pale moons. When they reach the plateau’s current, they are seized and carried sideways, striking towers and archways with tremendous but distant impacts. Fauna follow in their wakes, feeding on whatever the ancient interiors release.

No one can tell whether the plateau was once a city, an organism, a reef without plants, or the continuous shell of a world-sized animal. These interpretations may all be artifacts of scale. A doorway seen nearby becomes a pore from above. A boulevard becomes the seam between two plates. A buried district may be one chamber among thousands in a structure that was never meant to contain inhabitants.

The place offers no intact answer. It has been worn beyond testimony.

Still, under the pressure and the cold, among the drifting animals and singing vaults, it does not feel dead. Its life is not renewal but rearrangement. Water dismantles it; animals stitch parts of it together; gravity draws it into the trenches; deep currents return pieces from below. The entire high plateau is passing through itself, slowly in stone and violently in water, holding its ancient form only as a whirlpool holds a shape.
