The land is called the **Last Pavement**, though no one has found an edge.

It lies low beneath a white, oversized sky: a continent of constructed ground so ancient that construction and geology have become indistinguishable. There is no soil. There was never soil here. Underfoot are interlocking plates of pale ceramic, vitrified ash, black diamond foam, and alloys whose names survive only as spectral signatures. The plates have settled over immense spans of time, tilting by fractions of a degree, cracking, resealing, and wearing down until the whole world resembles a salt flat made by an intelligence rather than a vanished sea.

From a distance it appears almost empty. Up close, there is scarcely a handspan that is not occupied.

The surface is packed with minute structures: heat vanes, capped conduits, hexagonal pores, sensor hairs, flush-set lenses, nests of articulated pins. Most rise no higher than an ankle. Trillions of these devices fill the plain in bands and provinces, giving the ground the fine texture of fabric. They lean together according to prevailing thermal gradients, so entire regions seem brushed in one direction. Between them run narrow seams of dark metal, branching and rebranching like dried river systems. They carry no liquid. They conduct charge, strain, encoded light, and slow packets of manufactured matter.

There are larger forms, but even these have been eroded toward flatness. Towers have collapsed into long ribs. Domes have slumped until they are only circular rises a few meters high. Ancient transit works cross the horizon as parallel ridges, too broad and too straight to be mistaken for nature, yet so worn that their original purpose is impossible to infer. Some ridges continue for thousands of kilometers without a visible deviation. Others end in smooth, bright scars where the pavement has rewritten itself and removed whatever came before.

The air is warm and extraordinarily dry. Exposed moisture cannot persist; the ground recognizes loose water as contamination and divides it almost at once, drawing hydrogen into deep reservoirs and releasing oxygen through catalytic vents. Frost, rain, mist, and dew are absent. The sky holds fine mineral dust and a sparse suspension of reflective motes. At noon these motes align, turning the atmosphere into a luminous veil. Shadows grow sharp-edged but strangely faint, as though brightness comes not only from above but from every direction at once.

The sun is severe without being scorching. Heat spreads through the pavement and is carried laterally by buried systems, so afternoon warmth can arrive before dawn in one district and linger for days in another. There are no conventional seasons. Instead, there are **thermal migrations**: continental fronts of redistributed heat that move slowly over the plain. Ahead of one, the ceramic ground ticks as it expands. Behind it, microscopic shutters close, reflective fields darken, and stored radiance is passed into the depths.

This is the weather of the Last Pavement.

Other weather occurs too, though little of it moves the air. At irregular intervals a charge front crosses the landscape. Millions of surface pins rise together, no more than a finger’s width, and the horizon takes on a faint violet cast. Static crawls across exposed objects without sparks. Dust lifts in sheets a few centimeters above the ground and streams silently along invisible paths. From high above, such a front would look like a vast stain passing over the continent, changing its texture but not its shape.

There are also repair tides.

They begin as a darkening along the seams. Grains no larger than pollen emerge from hidden vents and spread over the pavement in dense, coordinated films. They dismantle damaged mechanisms, fill cracks, sort windblown minerals, and rebuild whatever pattern the district still remembers. A repair tide may take a century to cross a single plaza. It may pause for decades around a fragment it cannot identify. Sometimes two tides meet bearing incompatible instructions. Then a low, intricate border forms between them: ridges, pits, tiny walls, repeated geometries laid down and erased in patient argument. These conflicts can extend across hundreds of kilometers. They are among the most active things in the world, though to a short-lived observer they look like discolorations on stone.

Nothing here is truly natural substrate. Dig beneath a plate and there are more plates: load-distribution webs, evacuated chambers, computation lattices, dormant manufactories, and braided structural members descending farther than instruments can reliably sound. The world may be a shell around an older planet, or it may have been assembled without one. Gravity varies by barely perceptible amounts from province to province, suggesting that mass is moved below. Deep tremors are uncommon and gentle. They feel less like earthquakes than enormous machinery changing its mind.

The pavement’s age is visible everywhere. Not as ruin alone, but as refinement by repetition. Sharp corners have been rounded by dust, thermal flexing, and repair systems that no longer understand ornament. Inscriptions have become shallow grooves, then chains of dimples, then only variations in conductivity. Machinery once made from thousands of distinct parts has been replaced and simplified until it performs its function as a single smooth block. The oldest systems have settled into forms almost too plain to notice: a slope that always remains cool, a line that dust will not cross, a patch of ground on which falling objects come to rest upright.

These are the local laws, maintained rather than given.

In one region, sound is absorbed so completely that footsteps are felt only through the legs. In another, loose fragments drift several centimeters west each day regardless of wind. There are fields where metal fatigues instantly and ceramic never breaks; corridors where distance contracts slightly for machines carrying approved signals; circular precincts that remain at noon brightness through the night. The effects are seldom dramatic. The builders favored continuity, and after deep time the surviving miracles are the ones that require almost no spectacle.

Life exists, though sparsely and with difficulty.

The nearest thing to vegetation grows along warm seams. These **wire thickets** are low, black, and brittle-looking, composed of carbon filaments surrounding pale silicon cores. They do not use water. Their chemistry runs on temperature differences and airborne mineral ions. During bright periods they open fans thinner than foil, each fan patterned with a maze of silver veins. At the approach of a charge front, they fold flat and feed excess current into clusters of seedlike capacitors.

They grow very slowly. A thicket several meters across may be older than a mountain on another world. Its dead portions do not decay; they are gradually harvested by repair tides or worn into conductive dust. Their distribution is sparse enough that one can travel for days without seeing any, then encounter a seam lined for hundreds of kilometers with black fans all turned toward the same invisible gradient.

Other growths resemble stains, crusts, or misplaced architecture. Amber photovoltaic lichens coat the sheltered sides of old ribs. Pale nodules gather around exhaust pores and split when internal pressure becomes high, scattering geometric flakes. In deep recesses, mats of catalytic cells metabolize trace gases released from below. None are lush. There is no green, no shade broad enough to rest beneath, no smell of sap or rot. The living colors are black, copper, pearl, and a bruised violet visible only at certain angles.

Animals are rare enough to become events.

Small six-legged gleaners sometimes cross the plain at dusk. Their bodies are thin frameworks of ceramic bone with reflective membranes stretched between them. They feed by scraping charged dust from sensor fields, pausing whenever the ground beneath them transmits a diagnostic pulse. They make no audible call. Their communication is a sequence of taps so faint that the pavement itself carries it farther than air could.

Higher in the bright sky, solitary sail-creatures ride thermal boundaries. They may remain aloft for years, descending only to lay hard, magnetized eggs in the hollows of ruined towers. Their shadows pass slowly and are often mistaken for faults in the atmospheric veil. Nothing is known to hunt them regularly. Their greatest danger is being classified as debris.

There are rumors of larger creatures in the service depths: blind forms adapted to vacuum ducts, feeding on obsolete insulation and abandoned computation. Now and then a sealed hatch is found open, its edges polished from the inside. Tracks lead out—broad, shallow impressions in the dust—and end where the pavement has cleaned itself.

For all its activity, the Last Pavement is nearly silent. There is too little wind for a continuous voice, and moving components are designed without friction. One hears isolated things: the dry click of a cooling plate; a faint settling murmur beneath the ground; the soft hiss of dust passing over glass; a distant tone when sunlight strikes some exposed cavity at the correct angle. Once every few hours, perhaps, a deep pulse passes through the plain. It is below hearing at first, then becomes a pressure in the chest, then is gone.

At night—where night is permitted—the brightness does not vanish. The sky dims to silver. Lines beneath the pavement illuminate in muted colors, tracing networks too immense to understand. Some flare near the horizon and continue over the curvature of the world. From a high place, if any high place could be found, they might resemble the circuitry of a single device.

But the landscape offers almost no height. Its old prominences have been dismantled, collapsed, or planed down by long maintenance. The horizon is remote and level. A person standing on the plain feels exposed not because there is nothing nearby, but because everything nearby is low, dense, and watchful. Millions of lenses occupy the ground. Most are blind. Some track the sun. A few turn toward movement.

No central intelligence is evident. The world does not speak or issue commands. Its systems continue through inherited rules, local negotiations, and procedures whose purposes have worn away. It repairs a road no traveler uses. It maintains a temperature for an organism extinct for geological ages. It opens enormous buried doors on a schedule tied to a moon that no longer exists. It rejects water, preserves levelness, redistributes heat, and keeps the sky bright.

The Last Pavement is not dead. Nor is it exactly functioning.

It has passed beyond both conditions into persistence: a made world weathered down to its most stable instructions, vast enough that errors become climates and maintenance becomes ecology. Across its shining lowlands, repair tides creep, wire thickets unfold, heat migrates, dust obeys buried fields, and rare animals step carefully between the mechanisms.

Almost nothing happens quickly.

Almost nothing ever stops.
