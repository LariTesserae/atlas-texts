At first it appears to be a range of pale mountains after rain. Under the hard white light, ridges rise one behind another, their upper reaches tangled in cloud, every face shining with runoff. There are passes, pinnacles, gullies, shelves, and steep-sided basins. The whole region has the breadth of a high valley.

Nothing in it, however, is natural.

The ridges are stacked masonry. The pinnacles are chimneys without fires. The gullies are fitted channels, and the basins are cisterns lined with hammered clay. Even the ground underfoot consists of overlapping slabs laid over vaults, which rest upon deeper vaults, which in turn are said to rest upon older courses of brick. There is no exposed earth. Where a paving stone has cracked, it reveals not soil but another floor beneath it.

The place has been built upward so completely that construction has become geology.

Its materials are simple: gray fired brick, chalk-white ceramic, black glass, rope-darkened timber sealed with pitch, and a soft greenish metal used sparingly for clamps and drains. No two pieces are identical. Finger grooves remain in tiles. Chisel marks run across lintels. The joints bulge where wet clay was pressed by hand. Great walls bend slightly because their makers judged straightness by cord, plumb stone, and eye. Yet the accumulation of small inaccuracies has not made the landscape fragile. It has made it intricate. Every leaning wall is caught by a buttress; every buttress supports a stair; every stair becomes the roof of a narrow chamber below.

There is scarcely an unoccupied span. Arches are fitted inside larger arches. Steps divide and divide again until a broad ascent becomes twenty parallel flights, some no wider than a foot. Towers crowd courtyards that have themselves been roofed and built upon. Channels intersect parapets. Cisterns fill the gaps between domes. Blocks meant to brace one structure have been hollowed into rooms, then subdivided with screens of ceramic lattice. The region is packed not with inhabitants but with uses whose users are absent.

Water is now the only thing moving through these arrangements.

It comes from the air. Cool clouds gather against the upper terraces and pass slowly through the constructions, snagging on serrated ridges of tile. Condensation beads along every lip. Roofs shaped like folded hands collect it. Walls weep through pores intentionally left in the brick. In the bright hours, threads of water appear everywhere at once, fine as wire, descending from level to level.

The water does not fall freely for long. It is caught, delayed, divided, and redirected. A drop striking an upper bowl may pass through a pinhole into a spiral groove, descend a staircase beneath the stair used by feet, enter a settling jar the size of a house, spill through a row of stone teeth, and emerge days later in a lower gallery. There are thousands of such journeys. Some are visible as silver lines on white walls. Others can only be heard deep under the paving: an occasional hollow drip, a soft pour, a distant ceramic knock as a float-stone lifts and settles.

Despite so much water, there is no moss, no lichen, no grass in the joints. The cisterns contain no larvae. The wet walls have no stain of living green or brown. Their only discoloration comes from minerals: white fur along evaporation lines, rust-red fans below metal fastenings, blue-black skins where water has passed over glass dust. Pools are clear to impossible depths. The silence around them is so complete that surface tension seems like a visible membrane.

The light is bright but seldom warm. Clouds diffuse the sun into a broad, sourceless glare, intensified by pale ceramic and shallow water. Shadows gather only under arches and in shafts. They are sharp-edged there, almost solid, because every surrounding surface throws light inward. On the highest platforms, black glass panels have been angled toward the sky. They hold reflections of clouds so dark and precise that they resemble openings into night.

The air smells of rain-struck stone, cold pottery, and a faint mineral sharpness like a freshly broken vessel. There is no smell of decay. Nothing here rots. Timber, where it survives, has been boiled in resin and wrapped in tile. Rope has hardened into black curves. Cloth, if cloth once hung in these rooms, is gone without leaving dust.

The forms are young. Corners remain keen. Tool cuts have not softened. The lips of channels are barely rounded by flow, and many stairs retain the tiny roughness of firing. It might seem that the builders departed only recently, except that calcite has already formed slow white curtains in some underground chambers. Elsewhere, deposits have sealed doors and welded jars to shelves. The landscape is young in shape but old in waiting.

Its highest quarter consists of collecting crowns: dense fields of hornlike funnels, toothed walls, bowls raised on tripods, and tall porous towers through which cloud is meant to pass. From a distance they look like frost. Up close, each crown is an assembly of hand-sized ceramic pieces tied together with clamps and pegs. Water pearls on their surfaces and creeps inward through capillary grooves. In fog, the entire upper range quietly sweats.

Below these crowns lie the reservoir terraces. Here the architecture broadens. Roofs become lakes, but shallow ones, no more than a hand deep, spread across immense tiled planes. Narrow raised paths cross them in geometric patterns. The water reflects so much light that travelers would have to walk with eyes lowered, following dark strips of glass set into the paving. Occasionally a reservoir drains, not suddenly but over many hours, revealing thousands of conical cups beneath. Each cup retains a little water after the larger surface is gone. For a time the terrace becomes a field of separate mirrors.

Farther down, the region tightens into a labyrinth of distribution. Corridors descend alongside covered aqueducts. Walls are thick with enclosed pipes made from fitted clay rings. There are valve rooms, though the valves require no advanced mechanism: counterweighted stones, swelling plugs, leatherless seals of polished wood, and tipping basins balanced on pivots. Most still function. They answer to weight alone.

When one basin fills, it leans. A gate opens elsewhere. Water moves into a new district. The emptied basin slowly rises.

This is the principal law of the place: water changes its own road.

No central engine directs it. The whole built range is a patient calculation performed by gravity, porosity, pressure, and balance. Its builders possessed only modest tools, but they used repetition as a form of power. A single handmade valve is crude. Ten thousand valves, linked by water level, amount to weather turned inward.

The cycles are long. A channel may remain dry for a season of cloud before one high cistern reaches its brim. Then a door of stone sinks half an inch, releasing water into passages untouched for years. A district brightens as its dry tiles become wet. Deep chambers begin to resonate. Somewhere far below, a sealed pool rises against a float and closes the route again. Nothing happens quickly enough to watch, but if one returns after sleeping, the soundscape may have changed.

Most of the time, there is only quiet: the close hiss of condensation, intermittent dripping, and wind brushing over the mouths of empty jars. In the deep galleries, droplets make isolated notes, each chamber tuned by its dimensions. The region never becomes musical in any intentional sense. The sounds are too sparse and too widely separated. Yet on rare nights, when all upper surfaces are saturated, low tones move through the vaults as water displaces air from buried reservoirs. Then the constructed mountains seem to breathe once, very slowly.

There are no footprints except those impressed during manufacture. Some bricks preserve the heel or palm of a worker. Thumbprints repeat around the rims of basins. On a few walls, rows of simple marks have been cut: cups, forks, waves, open squares. They may indicate quantities, routes, or warnings. The marks occur most densely near structures that do not behave like the others.

One such structure stands near the center of the range, where several terraces meet. It is a steep, many-sided mound assembled from sealed jars. Every jar points its mouth inward. Rain runs around it but cannot enter. The mound remains dry even in fog, its surface warm only by contrast with the wet air. If a jar is struck, the vibration passes through the others and returns several breaths later from beneath the floor.

Another is a long avenue of doorframes without walls. They are set so closely that the passage resembles a ribbed tunnel open to the sky. Water crosses it sideways, traveling from frame to frame along thin shelves cut into the lintels. None spills downward unless the wind changes direction. At noon, the frames align their shadows into a single dark rectangle on the pavement, and for a brief time it looks as though a doorway has opened flat in the ground.

The lowest accessible basins are filled nearly to their rims. Their surfaces do not ripple, even when drops fall into them. The drops vanish with a small bright dimple, as if drawn through. Beneath the water are roofs, stairs, and courtyards, all built before flooding and all as densely furnished with channels as the dry levels above. Nothing indicates a final foundation. Divers, if there were any living beings to dive, might descend through one drowned district only to find another reservoir beneath it.

Perhaps the whole high valley is not a city or a machine but a vessel still in the process of filling. Perhaps its mountains were raised to catch the clouds one droplet at a time. The absence of life may be deliberate: no root is permitted to split a joint, no animal to clog a pipe, no soft body to drink from the intended measure. The brightness keeps every open surface visible. The cold slows evaporation. The packed construction denies water any route that has not been made for it.

Still, small failures have begun.

A sharp parapet has tilted and now sends its trickle down the wrong face. Mineral deposits grow in a hinge. A cistern intended to empty has become a permanent pool. These changes are minor, but the system magnifies patience. In centuries, an accidental stream may activate a forgotten sequence of gates. In millennia, the bright upper crowns may dissolve into rounded forms and the deepest chambers may fill with white stone precipitated from captured rain.

For now, the place remains almost new: a cool, high, hand-built watershed without soil, plant, beast, or keeper. It extends from cloud-bright collecting towers to flooded lower vaults, crowded at every scale with objects made to receive, divide, weigh, or remember water.

Nothing grows here.

Everything fills.
