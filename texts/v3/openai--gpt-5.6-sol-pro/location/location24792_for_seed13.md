The city occupies a high, cool basin among mountains that still look newly made. Their ridges are narrow and angular, the rock split into clean planes rather than softened shoulders. Pale scree rests beneath black cliffs, each fallen shard sharp enough to cut leather. Yet there are no landslide scars, no fans of old debris, no gullies deepened by centuries of runoff. The mountains possess the raw geometry of a place exposed only recently.

They have always possessed it.

Between the ridges lies a broad upland of dark volcanic soil, open enough that one can see from the city walls to distant passes. Vegetation grows richly where it grows at all, but it gathers in separate communities rather than covering the land continuously. There are thick groves of blue-green conifers around springs, waist-high fern beds beneath sheltered slopes, and round meadows dense with silver grass. Between them stretch long intervals of bare stone and low moss. The effect is neither barren nor fully forested: abundance held in islands, each island distinct, with ample emptiness around it.

Water follows the same pattern. No great lake dominates the basin. Instead, moisture collects in pockets: cold springs under basalt ledges, shallow reed pools, beads of condensation on the shaded faces of walls. A narrow river passes through the basin, but it is hardly more than a chain of clear basins joined by dark, quiet channels. Its bed is young, cluttered with angular stones that show little sign of being tumbled. In some places water slips beneath the rock and emerges farther on with exactly the same volume and clarity.

Rain hangs over the western peaks in a permanent gray veil. From a distance it seems to be approaching. It never does. Along the edge of that veil, droplets fall continuously into the same belt of forest, keeping bark black and moss saturated. Elsewhere the air remains merely damp, carrying the smell of wet stone, pine resin, soil, and cold ash.

The city is spread across the basin rather than crowded into a single mass. Low districts stand on terraces of fitted stone, separated by pasture, gardens, springs, and exposed ridges. Roads connect them in pale lines. The largest structures are built where natural outcrops provide foundations: granaries with thick rubble walls, roofed markets, communal ovens, animal courts, and tall watch platforms assembled from timber and stone. There are no towers grand enough to dominate the mountains. The city is urban by extent and organization, not by height.

Its walls follow the contours of the land for miles. In some stretches they are substantial defensive works, with gates, parapets, and sheltered guard rooms. In others they dwindle into waist-high boundary markers crossing open grass. The gates have wooden hinges, stone pivots, and bronze fittings used only where wear would otherwise be severe. Most tools are simpler: polished stone adzes, bone needles, wooden wedges, ceramic blades, antler picks, copper hooks carefully repaired with cord. Timber is split by hand. Blocks are shaped with hammerstones and abrasive sand. Mortar is made from lime burned in small hillside kilns.

Nothing here is ruined, though nothing appears new. Roofs carry lichen but never collapse. Wooden lintels show shallow tool marks and a little weathering, always the same little weathering. Paths are firm beneath the feet of thousands, yet no rut grows deeper. Moss reaches the edge of every paving stone and stops there. The city has the settled appearance of age without evidence of aging.

Its people move quietly. Sandaled feet cross courtyards; grain is ground; wool is combed; pottery turns slowly on hand wheels. Smoke rises from vents in thin vertical threads, dispersing at the same height each day. There are workshops, storehouses, shrines, bath chambers fed by springs, and long halls where councils meet. No machine turns except by muscle, falling water, or wind. The waterwheels are small and wooden. Their axles complain in low, intermittent notes that never become louder.

The city’s plan suggests many eras of construction—older walls enclosed inside newer districts, roads diverted around shrines, houses built over foundations of unfamiliar shape—but no one can identify a first settlement or a latest addition. Every layer is complete. Excavation reveals further streets below, preserved and inhabited by pale roots, but digging never exposes more than the same few courses. A trench abandoned overnight is found filled and paved by morning, not as if repaired, but as though it had never been opened.

This is the governing condition of the basin: events occur, but change does not accumulate.

Water flows without widening its channels. Hooves strike the earth without making mud. Fruit is gathered, and the same branches remain equally burdened. Fires consume wood, yet the woodpiles do not diminish; ash is carried away, yet ash heaps do not grow. A mason can shape a stone for a doorway, but when attention leaves it, the unused fragments resume their former places. Completed things remain if they belong to the city’s established form. Innovations do not. A new kind of lever works in the hand, then is found the next morning to be an ordinary pole. A wall raised where no wall has stood settles quietly into loose stones.

The inhabitants do not regard this as punishment. It is weather to them, or gravity. They know which acts the basin permits to persist and which it gently refuses. Craft is therefore less about invention than exactness. Songs are valued for being sung perfectly, vessels for matching the forms that endure, buildings for discovering foundations that seem to have been waiting beneath the soil. Their finest artisans are said not to create but to remember correctly.

Animals are everywhere, though rarely noisy. Herds of compact, shaggy cattle graze the silver meadows, separated widely enough that each dark body stands clear against the grass. Sure-footed goats occupy the cliffs above the roofs. Small deer pass through outer gardens and drink beside children without alarm. Marmot-like burrowers sit upright among the stones, and pale hares move through fern beds at dusk.

Birdlife is particularly rich. Black mountain crows patrol the walls. Green-necked pigeons nest under tiled eaves. Long-winged raptors circle above the basin, tracing the same broad spirals without seeming to hunt. In the reed pools, rust-colored waterbirds feed among frogs and silver insects. Their calls are infrequent and isolated—a single croak, a wingbeat, a thin whistle from a distant roof. Even with so much life, the basin remains mostly quiet.

Predation occurs, but no population dwindles. Birth occurs, but no species multiplies beyond its allotted places. The same number of nests appear each season, though no season quite advances. Trees flower on one side of a grove while bearing ripe cones on the other. Mushrooms emerge from damp ground and retain the firmness of their first morning. Fallen needles lie beneath the pines but never form deeper litter.

There is daylight without a conspicuous sun. The sky is bright, evenly blue-gray, and shadows have ordinary softness. Morning seems to have passed recently; evening seems several hours away. Neither arrives. Lamps are still lit in some workshops, not from necessity but because their flames give warmth and make copper and glaze easier to judge. On the highest ridges, patches of snow remain tucked into cracks. They neither melt nor spread.

From a watch platform, the city appears as a loose constellation of roofs, gardens, walls, animal yards, and sacred stones distributed across the basin. Beyond it, the young mountains rise in successive sharp ranks. The farthest are washed pale by the damp air but not obscured. There are roads leading toward them, and figures sometimes set out along those roads with packs and staffs.

Days later, they return by another gate carrying the same provisions, speaking of passes, valleys, and cities beyond the range. Their accounts differ in detail but agree in one respect: every road eventually bends back toward this basin.

Nothing is trapped in the sense of being confined. There is room, distance, livelihood, weather, wilderness, and a whole city’s breadth of human concern. But the place admits no elsewhere that can alter it, and no future that can replace what is here. It is a high country held at equilibrium: damp stone, sharp peaks, scattered forests, clear water, quiet animals, and an ancient city forever inhabiting the first stable moment of its existence.
