### The Warm Works

The place lies beneath a city, though not very far beneath it. In some rooms the paving overhead is only the depth of a man’s height above the sweating ceiling. Carts, buses, and footsteps pass as blunt vibrations through the concrete. Dust loosens from seams with each heavy vehicle, falls through the humid air, and vanishes into the mud below.

The Warm Works were excavated recently, by underground standards. Their walls have not yet acquired the rounded, settled shapes of old tunnels. Drill scars remain visible in the claystone: parallel grooves, socket holes, abrupt shelves where excavators stopped at the end of a shift. Concrete has been sprayed over some surfaces but not others. Rebar projects from unfinished partitions in neat rusting ranks. Survey marks—numbers, arrows, colored lines—are still legible under films of condensation.

It is less a cavern than a small subterranean town forced into a cluster of adjoining chambers. There are pump halls, workshops, transformer closets, storage vaults, stairwells, animal-control stations, and narrow lanes between them. Raised walkways serve as streets. Pipe bridges cross overhead. Corrugated sheds stand beneath concrete arches as if someone built an industrial hamlet indoors and then forgot to leave room for sky.

Beyond the last sheds, the Works end abruptly at raw excavation faces. No distant country opens there. A wall of damp ocher earth simply waits in the dimness, ribbed by machinery, still holding the shapes of roots severed during digging.

Heat is the first condition. It gathers from the buried city, from electrical equipment, from steam mains, from engines that never fully cool. The temperature varies little between day and night. Metal handrails are warm. Concrete radiates stored heat. Air clings to the skin and makes every breath feel used.

Water is everywhere without quite becoming a flood. It beads on pipes and hangs under cable trays. It seeps from joints in the stone, darkening the raw walls in vertical fans. Every few seconds, somewhere, a drop strikes sheet metal with a clear report. Shallow gutters run beside the walkways, carrying gray water toward grated sumps. The lowest floors are slick beneath a skin of mud, oil, and mineral foam.

After heavy rain above, the seepage strengthens. Threads of water emerge from places that were dry the week before. At first they follow no channels. They spread across fresh concrete, test bolt holes, gather around discarded washers, and deposit pale rims of lime. The landscape’s erosion is only beginning. Tiny gullies form in uncompacted clay. Soft corners slump by a finger’s width. Rust traces new watersheds down steel panels. Nothing has yet had time to become graceful.

Light comes from spaced industrial fixtures: amber cages over doors, green indicators on pump cabinets, long fluorescent tubes with dead sections at their ends. Large portions of the Works remain in half-darkness. Machinery appears in fragments—an enamel housing, a spinning coupling behind mesh, a wet wheel, a sequence of numbered valves—while the rest recedes into brown shadow. Reflections in the gutters make the place seem deeper than it is.

Almost nothing green grows here. Near a ventilation grille, where dust and water have collected together, there are a few wan ferns. Algae stains the warmest drains in black-green lines. Thin seedlings sometimes push up through cracks below sidewalk-level vents, fed by light from the city above, but they become pale and collapse before producing more than two leaves. Fungal films thrive better than plants: soft white fans behind timber braces, orange freckles on damp insulation, translucent threads in old sacks.

Animal life, by contrast, is excessive.

The ceilings belong to bats. They occupy expansion gaps, abandoned ducts, cable recesses, and the rough pockets left between stone and concrete lining. In the largest pump hall they hang in dense pleated masses, thousands of small bodies packed over the machinery. Their droppings gather on unused platforms and must be shoveled into bins. At evening—felt here only as a change in airflow—they stream upward through ventilation shafts toward the surface. Those that remain shift and quarrel continuously, producing a dry, layered chitter that fills the upper darkness.

The wet floors support other populations. Cockroaches crowd the warm pipe trenches. Cave crickets spring from walls when a door opens. Moths rest on electrical cabinets with their wings powdered gray. Mosquitoes breed in neglected buckets and inaccessible recesses. Rat paths shine along the edges of rooms. The rats themselves are rarely alone: five or six may be seen drinking from a leaking flange, and many more move behind the wall panels with a steady noise like paper being folded.

In the sumps, pale fish and surface minnows live together, washed in through drains or deliberately introduced to consume larvae. Small frogs have colonized the quiet settling tanks. They call from beneath gratings, their throats amplifying in the pipework until a single frog sounds as large as a dog. Eels occasionally appear after storms, stranded in the shallow channels, working patiently toward deeper water.

The animals are not an invasion in any temporary sense. They are the majority population. Human construction has produced heat, shelter, ledges, water, and an endless drift of food. The Works were designed as infrastructure for the city above, but on most surfaces their real function is habitat. Every box is a nest site. Every conduit is a migration route. Every drain is a creek.

The noise never resolves into silence. Pumps turn with a low, bodily throb. Ventilation fans beat at the air. Relays snap. Water taps metal. Bats rasp overhead. Frogs call from the sumps; rats scream briefly in the ducts; insects hum around the lights. When a maintenance cart passes, its wheels hammer across the joints of the raised road and the whole chamber answers. Yet the place changes slowly. The loudness is mostly repetition, not event.

There are rules here, learned through maintenance rather than written down.

Nothing may rest directly on the floor, because sooner or later water will find it. Doors must be latched, or rats will nose them open. Fans cannot be stopped for long: heat and animal smell accumulate with surprising speed. Before starting any large motor, workers strike its housing with a wrench to scatter roosting creatures. Grates are lifted from the far side, never while standing over them. Lamps are replaced in pairs because insects overwhelm a lone new bulb. Unused rooms are surrendered almost immediately.

Human occupation persists in narrow clearings. A workshop bench stands under bright task lights, its tools arranged above the reach of floodwater. A tea stall operates beside the shift office, enclosed in fine mesh. Boots hang upside down from pegs. Lunch tins lock with wire clips. On an elevated platform, three concrete houses face one another across a passage barely wide enough for two people to pass. Their windows look not onto a street but onto pipes, cable bundles, and the moving black veil of bats beyond.

Air from the surface enters through large louvered shafts. It brings brief evidence of weather: cooler breath during a storm, the smell of wet asphalt, diesel fumes, flower pollen, smoke from cooking fires. Rain above becomes pressure below. The gutters rise by centimeters. Pumps alter pitch. New damp patches blossom on the walls. Then, over days, the water subsides.

The boundary of the place is always apparent. At one end stands the sealed bulkhead leading to older tunnels. At the other is the raw face where expansion is planned. Painted outlines show future doorways on solid earth. Drainpipes stop with capped ends, waiting for chambers that do not yet exist. A short rail line runs directly into clay.

So the Warm Works remain unfinished in two directions: the city is still building them, and water is only beginning to reshape what the city has built. Between those patient processes, the animals have already arrived in full number. They occupy the interval before completion. In the dim heat, amid engines and wet stone, they make the new underground seem ancient.
