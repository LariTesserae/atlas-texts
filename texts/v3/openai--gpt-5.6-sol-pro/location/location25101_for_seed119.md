The place is a shallow, roofless basin, no larger than a village common, though its boundaries are difficult to judge in the dimness. Low walls surround it in a series of irregular rings. None rises much above a person’s shoulder. Beyond the outermost ring there is only gray vapor, luminous enough to mark the wall’s edge and opaque enough to conceal whatever supports the horizon.

Nothing here is unmade.

The ground resembles weathered bedrock at first: broad shelves, rounded gutters, shallow depressions polished by standing water. A closer look reveals joints everywhere. Each shelf is composed of fitted slabs of fired clay and dull green stone-glass, shaped by hand and pressed together without mortar. Finger grooves remain along their undersides. The gutters were cut with abrasive cords. Even the pebbles gathered in their bends are pellets of kiln-fired earth, each pinched into an imperfect oval. Where erosion has opened the pavement, there is no soil beneath it, only older pavements—darker courses, crushed tiles, woven reed mats turned black and hard, and then another layer of carefully laid slabs.

Rain has been falling here for so long that it has become one of the builders.

It does not often fall in drops. Moisture gathers in the low air and beads on every edge. The walls sweat. Stone-glass lips develop clear, trembling fringes; then, all at once, they shed their water into the channels below. From holes in the surrounding masonry come thin, continuous streams. They cross the basin through an elaborate network of grooves, disappear under bridges no wider than a hand, rise through ceramic grates, and collect in several knee-deep pools.

The water is cold, tea-colored, and nearly transparent. Its color comes not from leaves—there are few plants—but from tannin baked into ancient wooden conduits. When the pools overflow, water spreads in a shallow skin across the floor, revealing worn patterns incised into the tiles: spirals, branching lines, crude figures with many legs. For an hour the entire basin gleams. Then hidden drains begin swallowing, and the water retreats with a long chorus of sucking and gulping noises.

The basin is almost empty of objects. In its center stands a single hut-sized structure made from stacked ceramic ribs. It has no door, only narrow slots. At the eastern wall there is a leaning frame of lashed poles, gray with age but periodically repaired. Three stone stools occupy one dry ledge. Otherwise there is open floor, open water, and large distances between the few things that interrupt them. The emptiness makes the place seem larger than it is. A person crossing it remains visible from almost anywhere, isolated against wet paving.

Vegetation survives only in concessions made for it. Pale moss follows certain joints but refuses neighboring ones. A few clumps of black rush grow from baskets sunk into the pools, their roots unable to penetrate the constructed layers beneath. In wall niches, translucent liverworts spread like patches of damp skin. There are no trees and scarcely any grass. A wiry vine emerges from a pipe near the north wall, crosses two arm-lengths of masonry, and returns into another pipe without ever touching soil. During the coldest part of the cycle it flowers: five colorless bells, each filled with a drop of warm water.

The animals are everywhere.

They occupy the emptiness without filling it.

Small blue-backed crabs patrol the gutters, knocking ceramic pellets together with their claws. Long-legged water mice run from drain to drain, rarely venturing across dry tile. Thumb-sized frogs cling inside the sweating wall mouths, inflated throats turning the masonry into a bank of resonating chambers. Flat white worms browse the submerged carvings. Clouds of gnats hang over the pools, each cloud maintaining the shape of a narrow column even when the air moves.

Above them wheel dark birds with blunt, almost square wings. They nest out of sight inside the central ribbed structure, entering through its slots at speed. Their calls are short metallic cracks. Every few minutes the whole flock erupts, circles the basin once, and pours back into the structure as though tipped from a bowl.

Most of the noise, however, comes from below.

Water hammers through hidden pipes. Air trapped in the old conduits booms underfoot. Loose valves chatter in unseen sockets. Frogs answer the impacts; birds answer the frogs; crabs rattle pellets in territorial displays. When a drain opens, the basin gives a low note that can be felt through the soles before it is heard. Different parts of the pavement sound at different depths. The western shelves ring thinly, like plates. The center responds with a buried, cavernous pulse.

Silence occurs only after the largest overflow. Then every drain closes together. The birds settle, the frogs flatten themselves into their holes, and even the insects descend to the water. For perhaps twenty breaths the basin holds perfectly still. Condensation hangs from the walls without falling.

This is when the hunters emerge.

They are narrow, jointed things the length of a forearm, built—or born—from wood, shell, and fired clay. Their central bodies are living: a soft gray animal much like a slug. Around that body they carry six replaceable legs assembled from pegs and knotted sinew. No two sets match. Some legs are carved bone, some are bent twigs hardened in smoke, and some are fragments of old tools. They move carefully across the wet floor, stopping to tighten their own bindings with small mouthparts. They prey on frogs and water mice, but they also gather broken pieces of one another. A hunter that loses a leg may follow the channels until it finds a suitable replacement polished smooth by decades of flow.

Whether these creatures were fashioned once and then learned to reproduce their craft, or whether the basin itself still makes them, is not clear.

There are signs of hands everywhere, but no people. Repairs continue nonetheless. A cracked gutter found in the evening may be sealed by morning with fresh clay. Reed baskets around the rushes are replaced before they collapse. The leaning frame at the eastern wall acquires new lashings, rough and competent, made from vine fiber and animal gut. Tools sometimes appear on the stone stools: a flint scraper, a wooden mallet, a needle made from bird bone. They are simple implements, damp from use. If moved, they return within a few water cycles, though seldom to the same stool.

Nothing more sophisticated than a lever, a siphon, a fired pot, or a tied cord can be found here. Yet the whole environment is fabricated. Its complexity comes not from precise machinery but from repetition: thousands of hand-shaped pieces, millions of small adjustments, generations of channels laid over channels. The basin does not run like a device. It persists like a craft tradition.

Its erosion is mature. Water has rounded all sharp corners, but nothing seems close to failure. Wear has become part of the design. Channels deepen until they intercept lower channels; then the flow changes course. Slabs subside into catchments. Cracked tiles become shelter for larvae. Fallen wall fragments are rolled by floodwater until they become the ceramic pebbles gathered by crabs. Destruction here is slow manufacture.

Even the weather follows the architecture. Cool vapor enters through low gaps in the outer wall. It condenses on upright surfaces, drains inward, and is warmed slightly beneath the central structure by the packed bodies of nesting birds. The warmed air rises through the ceramic ribs, drawing more mist across the basin. Thus animals, water, and masonry make their own weak climate: a continuous cold inhalation around the walls and a damp exhalation at the center.

There is a rule to crossing the place. It becomes obvious without being taught: do not step over running water.

The channels are too narrow to present a real obstacle, but every creature respects them. Mice stop at their edges. Crabs remain within a single branch of the network. Hunters travel only where dry tile connects to dry tile. Birds cross above, but their shadows bend strangely when they pass over a stream, following the water rather than the bird.

If someone steps across, the sound below changes. The deep hammering falters. Nearby drains close. Frogs turn in their holes to face the trespasser. Water begins gathering on the higher side of the crossed channel, although the floor appears level. It continues to rise until the foot is withdrawn or until a new path is walked around the channel’s source.

No punishment follows. The basin simply insists that its separations are real.

In the dim gray hours there are hundreds of such boundaries, enclosing territories no larger than sleeping mats, courtyards, bowls, or cupped hands. In flood they vanish beneath a single reflective sheet. Then all the basin’s animals are briefly thrown together: crabs climbing walls, mice riding floating reed bundles, frogs swimming over places they could not cross on foot. The jointed hunters fold their handmade legs and drift like debris. Birds skim the surface, striking at insects.

The flood is loudest at its end. Water pours into every opening at once. The central structure roars through its slots. The outer walls weep in steady curtains. Ceramic stones tumble through the channels with the clatter of distant applause.

Afterward the basin returns to its spare arrangement: a few pools, a few stools, one ribbed house, a crooked frame, sparse black rushes, and open wet pavement. Mist closes over the boundary walls. Animals resume their divided lives. Somewhere underneath, unseen hands—or handlike mouths, or the pressure of water working with old instructions—begin setting another loosened tile back into place.
