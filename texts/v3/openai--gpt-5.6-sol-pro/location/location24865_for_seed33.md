The city occupies the uppermost reaches of a mountain range, though *occupies* is misleading. There is no clear boundary between city and mountain. Streets emerge from strata, cliffs arrange themselves into facades, and whole districts consist of rooms exposed by the slow peeling-back of stone. Nothing appears built by tools. There are no cut marks, no fittings, no mechanisms, no inscriptions. Nevertheless, the place has avenues, plazas, stairs, courtyards, arcades, retaining walls, towers, and neighborhoods divided with almost administrative precision.

It stands at an elevation where the surrounding world has fallen out of sight.

Below the outer parapets is not a visible slope but a dim, granular depth. On rare clear intervals, ridges can be discerned far beneath, receding like the teeth of a buried animal. Usually the lower country is hidden by a motionless gray opacity—not cloud, because there is no moisture here, but a suspension of microscopic mineral plates. They lie edge-on to gravity and face-on to light, so the depths brighten or vanish depending on where one stands.

The air is cold without being wintry. It does not bite; it steadily removes warmth. Metal, if there were any, would be painful to touch. Stone holds the chill of astronomical night. There is no frost, no snow, no dew, and no trace that liquid water has ever passed through the city. Channels occur everywhere—gutters, culverts, steep runnels down the stairways—but they carry only shadow. At certain hours darkness gathers in them as if poured, flows downhill, and spills silently over the precipices.

The oldest districts are made from pale saltstone that is not salt. It breaks into dry, translucent wafers, each carrying a faint interior glow. Newer-seeming quarters are black glass, dull pumice, chalky columns, and a dense violet mineral whose surface has been softened by ages of exposure. Doorways have rounded. Steps sag in their centers. Towers lean toward plazas with the settled posture of things that have been leaning for millennia.

Erosion is everywhere, but no wind can be felt.

Edges recede by minute degrees. A cornice becomes powder without falling. A wall grows thin enough for the view behind it to show through, then thickens again several streets away. Dust rarely accumulates. It crosses the city in long, low processions, sliding around corners and ascending stairs in perfect silence. Sometimes it arranges itself into the shape of a collapsed building before dispersing, including fragments of rooms that have never existed nearby.

The city is vast enough to contain distinct elevations within its already impossible height. The lower terraces cling to opposing cliff faces, connected by broad bridges of natural stone. Above them rise needle districts, where narrow towers are spaced hundreds of paces apart and joined by streets too thin to walk comfortably. Higher still, the mountain divides into a field of vertical slabs. Between them hang plazas with no visible support. A traveler can spend days crossing one district and still see the same empty tower on the horizon, always three streets away.

There are signs of civic organization without signs of civic life.

At regular intervals, hexagonal squares open among the buildings. Each contains a shallow basin, perfectly dry, with a raised central plinth. The plinths hold nothing. Colonnades surround the squares, their pillars varying in number according to a rule that can almost be understood: seven, eleven, thirteen, then a number the eye cannot count. Alleys feed into major avenues. Avenues converge at gates. Gates divide one kind of stone from another. Beyond some gates, gravity changes direction.

No machinery enforces this. There are no hinges, cables, lamps, lenses, engines, wheels, or remnants of forgotten technology. The city’s infrastructure is a property of shape itself. A stair is not an object placed upon a slope; it is the behavior the slope adopts when approached. An arch is where two distances agree to meet. Walls bear loads because the concept of weight becomes obedient near them.

The dim light has no clear source. The sky is a charcoal vault crossed by broad, darker bands. At times a remote pearly radiance spreads from beneath the horizon, throwing the towers’ shadows upward. At other times the interior surfaces of buildings become faintly luminous while the open streets darken. There is never a true day. Color persists only in protected crevices: bruised purple, old ivory, sulfurous gray, and the deep red of stone that seems warmer than it is.

Nothing grows.

There is no moss in the joints, no lichen on the shaded walls, no seeds waiting beneath the dust. Even decay has a mineral patience. What resembles fungus sometimes appears in sealed chambers: branching white structures, soft-looking from a distance. They prove on contact to be networks of cracks filled with powdered crystal. Over several hours these networks rearrange themselves, withdrawing from one wall and flowering across another. Whether they are alive depends on how long one watches and what one is willing to call hunger.

Fauna are rare, and sightings seldom agree.

The most common are the lintel animals: thin, jointless creatures the color of unlit stone. They lie stretched over doorways, indistinguishable from architectural moldings until a person passes below. Then they contract by a finger’s width. They have no visible eyes or mouths. Their movement produces no sound, and no one has seen one away from a threshold.

Farther out, among the unsupported plazas, small flocks of angular bodies drift through the air. They do not beat wings. Each animal resembles a folded sheet of black mineral with a pale seam along one edge. They turn by disappearing from one orientation and appearing in another. They feed, perhaps, on the suspended dust, leaving temporary corridors of absolute clarity behind them.

Something larger inhabits the abyss beyond the eastern walls. Its passage is known only because distant portions of the city are briefly concealed, one after another, as though an enormous transparent body were moving between them. On those occasions the dry channels fill with upward-flowing darkness, and every lintel animal shortens at once.

Despite the silence, the city is never still.

Whole districts exchange places during the dimmest hours. Towers lengthen by adding rooms to their middles. Courtyards close, becoming roofed halls; elsewhere ceilings separate into paving stones beneath an unfamiliar sky. Bridges migrate slowly along the cliff face, leaving polished scars. The motion is continuous but cannot be directly witnessed. Look away from a parapet and it may return with another avenue attached. Watch it without blinking, and the distant mountain moves instead.

The quiet makes these changes more disturbing. Stone falls without impact. Dust moves without hiss. The rare animals have no cries. Even footsteps are subdued, as though sound loses confidence here. A clap produces only the physical sting in the hands. Speech travels a few paces, then becomes something visible: faint distortions that lodge in corners and remain there. Old conversations collect in abandoned rooms as trembling bands in the air. Passing through one may fill the mouth with words no one remembers saying.

The city has rules, though they are local and temporary.

A doorway crossed from the east cannot be crossed back from the west.

Stairs with an even number of steps lead upward; count them twice and they may lead inward.

A room containing two shadows is larger than its exterior. A room containing no shadows is outside the city, regardless of the walls around it.

Distance increases when named.

Nothing falls forever, but not everything falls downward.

These are not enchantments imposed upon the landscape. They are its weather.

The active forces here are pressure, absence, orientation, and time. They sweep through districts in cycles. During a pressure season, empty rooms become difficult to enter, though the air remains thin; their emptiness has gained mass. In an absence season, sections of masonry vanish, not by crumbling but by having apparently never been present. Streets continue across the gaps. Later the vanished structures return badly aligned, with doors opening onto the backs of walls.

There are also elevation tides. Without warning, the entire city feels several thousand meters higher. Breath shortens. The sky draws close and develops hairline fractures. Distant peaks sink. Hours later the altitude recedes, though no stone has moved. These tides expose different layers of reality in the same way a retreating sea might expose reefs—if a sea had ever existed here.

Yet the place is not chaotic. It has endured too long for chaos. Its instabilities are worn smooth by repetition. The impossible bridges have weathered footpaths along their centers. The migrating towers fit into ancient sockets wherever they stop. Even the breaks in causality have rounded edges.

From the highest terrace, reached by a stair that spirals around an absent column, the city can be seen extending across the mountaintops. Its districts are sparse, divided by immense tracts of bare tilted stone. Towers stand miles apart like punctuation marks. Bridges reach into vacancies. Squares glow faintly in the dusk. Beyond them rise further peaks, each carrying fragments of the same urban order, until height and distance become indistinguishable.

There is no smoke, no river, no garden, no lamp, no engine, no crowd.

Still, the city seems occupied—not by inhabitants, but by its own ongoing act of arrangement. It is forever settling into a final form and forever discovering that the final form lies one terrace higher.
