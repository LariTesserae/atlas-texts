The plateau is called nothing, because names do not remain attached to it.

It lies high above any visible lowland, though the height is difficult to measure. On clear intervals—which last less than a minute—the horizon appears beneath the land rather than beyond it: a dim blue circumference of valleys, cloud decks, and distant snowfields glimpsed through fractures in the air. Then rain closes over everything again. The plateau returns to its usual condition, a cold region suspended inside weather.

At first it seems empty.

There are no trees in the familiar sense, no thickets, no continuous green canopy. Across miles of open ground stand only scattered black columns, low fans, pale domes, and long mats that might be mistaken for exposed stone. Between them stretch broad flats of gray-green material polished by water. Nothing rises high enough to shelter behind. Wind travels unimpeded from one end of the country to the other.

But there is no stone here.

The plateau is one organism, or an old agreement among organisms. Its ground is compressed vegetable matter layered to immeasurable depth: root felt, woody plates, translucent pith, dormant seed beds, carbon-black trunks laid horizontally and fused under their own age. What looks like shale peels into wet fibers. What looks like scree contracts when stepped upon. The cliffs are the exposed sides of enormous lignified growths, their grain running vertically for hundreds of meters before turning sideways and vanishing into cloud.

The entire region has been eroding for so long that erosion has become part of its anatomy. Rain does not merely wear the land down. It prunes it. Rivers cut along vascular seams. Frost opens old growth rings into ravines. Landslides remove dead tissue, and from the wounds rise faint curls of heat, spores, and mineral-smelling vapor.

Almost everything that could be removed has already gone. The plateau has settled into wide, low forms: rounded ribs, shallow basins, terraces as smooth as ancient teeth. Yet it is not stable. Its age has passed beyond stillness into a second kind of activity, the way rotted wood becomes crowded with new lives. Every surface is either swelling, sloughing, germinating, dissolving, or remembering a shape it no longer possesses.

Water is always nearby. It lies beneath the resilient skin of the flats and wells up around any pressure. Footsteps fill slowly with clear liquid. Narrow streams run without channels, held in place by the alignment of waxy hairs. Pools form on slopes steeper than water should tolerate, adhering to them in quivering sheets. From the undersides of overhangs, droplets fall upward into the cloud.

The rain is fine, cold, and nearly horizontal. It collects on the black columns, which are not trunks but chimneys of living peat. They breathe at irregular intervals. First comes a deep intake felt through the soles; then the column’s layered valves open and expel a plume of warm mist carrying yellow pollen and the smell of iron. Across the plateau, other columns answer. In storm light they resemble the stacks of an immense industrial district, spaced miles apart and connected by buried conduits of root.

No one constructed this machinery.

There are pistons, but they are grown from bundles of musclelike cellulose. There are flywheels, but they begin as coiled fruits. There are pumps whose chambers close in peristaltic sequence, lifting groundwater from the plateau’s depths. Vast fermentation beds generate heat beneath the cold surface. Conductive vines carry pulses of charge between distant organs, flashing blue where rain exposes them. The landscape manufactures sugars, spores, shells, gases, electrical gradients, and occasionally entire temporary species.

Production never stops. Its purpose, if it ever had one, has been lost.

The loudness comes less from the wind than from the work beneath it. Roots knock in deep shafts. Membranes snap taut across basins and drum under the rain. Pressure vents bellow. Seed capsules the size of houses burst with concussions that roll for minutes over the open flats. Somewhere below, slow woody gears engage and disengage with a groan like icebergs turning in the sea.

At intervals the whole plateau shudders. Water leaps from the pools. Thousands of small animals freeze at once. Then a ridge sinks by several meters, not collapsing but folding neatly along a crease prepared centuries before. In the hollow left behind, pale shoots emerge already branching, as if the missing landscape had been converted directly into growth.

The plants are sparse only in the way a body is sparse on the surface. A few organs show; the living total remains concealed. The open distances are skin.

Most visible growth keeps low against the cold. There are silver cushions with leaves packed as tightly as scales, fields of thread-thin red moss, and broad translucent pads under which trapped rain circulates in branching currents. Some mats are clear enough to see into. Tiny green chambers open and close far below their surfaces, and pale creatures move through the illuminated tissue as fish might move beneath pond ice.

The scattered domes are flowering organs. Their petals are thick, mineralized, and dull white. They may remain closed for decades, gathering pressure. When one opens, it does so violently, each petal striking the ground and sending up fountains of old water. For several hours the interior shines with its own low violet light. Animals arrive from every direction.

There are more animals than the emptiness first suggests.

They occupy the ground in layers. Minute grazers tunnel through bark-strata. Long-legged black herds cross the flats in single-file lines, stepping only on certain veins, their narrow bodies rocking in the wind. Flocks of membrane-winged creatures pour from cracks whenever the buried current changes. They are nearly transparent except for dense knots of organs, so in dim weather they look like pieces of rain moving with intention.

Warm-blooded burrowers live in the respiratory chimneys, feeding on pollen and cleaning the valves. White amphibians cling to vertical water sheets. Beetles with iron-rich shells congregate around electrical wounds and align themselves in radial patterns, drawing enough charge to glow at their joints. There are soft, many-mouthed things in the pools that imitate the sound of distant engines. Some may be engines, in the local meaning of the word.

Predation is abundant but seldom decisive. An animal killed on the plateau is rapidly incorporated into plant tissue, and its nervous patterns may persist in the conductive roots. Days or years later, a flower can produce the animal again—not always accurately. A six-legged grazer may emerge with the migratory instincts of a winged flock. A cloud of insects may share one remembered fear. Old predators recur as harmless mosses that tense when shadows pass over them.

Because of this, species do not have firm boundaries. They are temporary proposals made by the plateau.

The light remains dim even at midday. Clouds drag their lower surfaces across the uplands, snagging on columns and combs of dark growth. The sun is usually visible only as a brighter bruise. Bioluminescence supplies what daylight does not: blue pulses in the vascular cracks, amber blinks among the moss, slow green waves traveling beneath the flats. Lightning rarely descends from the sky. It rises from exposed roots and branches upward into the cloud, where thunder begins before the flash.

Reality is least reliable during these electrical storms.

Distance loosens first. A nearby pool may reflect a ridge two days’ journey away, while the ridge itself vanishes from the horizon. Rain can be heard striking surfaces that have not yet grown. Animals run behind a column and emerge old, wet with a different season. Tracks precede the creatures that make them.

Then sequence begins to fail.

A seed capsule explodes; afterward it swells unopened on its stem. A cliff slumps into a ravine, remains fallen for an hour, and then continues standing while the displaced debris persists below. Two versions of the same terrain occupy each other without blending. Rivers choose both courses. Herds split around obstacles visible only to half their members.

The plateau tolerates contradictions better than observers do. It does not correct them quickly. Instead it grows connective tissue between incompatible events.

This produces some of the strangest formations: groves whose trunks terminate in earlier weather, pools with several mutually exclusive depths, and long curtains of roots hanging from absent slopes. On certain flats, rain lands and becomes yesterday’s rain, already buried several layers down. In other places droplets remain suspended at eye level until enough accumulate to form a trembling wall. Animals drink holes through it.

There are rules, though they are not the rules of inert country.

Water remembers downward, even when it falls another way.

Growth follows pressure more faithfully than light.

Nothing dead remains singular.

Electricity carries form as well as force.

Any shape repeated often enough becomes an organ.

And erosion is not loss. Erosion is how the plateau thinks.

Its oldest thoughts are the broadest landforms: the nearly flattened ridges, the basins worn through miles of former growth, the long parallel grooves where vanished rivers once migrated. Its quick thoughts are animals, weather, sparks, opening flowers. Between them are storms of invention in which the plateau attempts new anatomies and abandons them almost immediately.

A grove rises in the course of an afternoon: black stalks pumping water from one another, leaves clattering like sheet metal. By night it has overproduced some dark, combustible nectar. Heat blooms below it. The grove catches fire despite the rain, burns with a cold blue flame, and collapses into a colony of crawling seeds. By morning the seeds have migrated over the ridge, each carrying a live ember in its hollow center.

Elsewhere, a basin inhales its lake.

The water drains through thousands of mouths. The exposed floor convulses, arrays of fins lifting and falling in synchronized rows. Mist pours from vents around the rim. For an hour the basin manufactures winged larvae in such numbers that the air thickens and the dimness becomes almost complete. Birds arrive—or are assembled by the same event—and feed amid the roar. Then the basin exhales. The lake returns warm, full of blind silver swimmers and pieces of a sky not currently overhead.

Through all of this, the ancient form remains. The plateau is hyperactive in detail and nearly motionless in destiny. Every storm, eruption, bloom, migration, and folding ridge participates in the same immeasurably slow flattening. It has been approaching its final shape since before the surrounding mountains existed.

Perhaps it has already reached that shape, and all apparent change is only the land revisiting the processes that made it.

At the outer escarpments, where the living substrate descends into permanent cloud, water pours from a thousand weeping seams. The falls separate into strands, then droplets, then mist. Winged animals nest behind them in warm cavities. Long root cables hang down farther than can be seen, swaying and striking the cliff with hollow detonations.

Sometimes something enormous climbs those cables from below.

Only portions of it become visible: a plated limb pressing through the cataract, a field of sensory filaments, an eye that opens like a wet flower and reflects not the plateau but a bright mechanical plain. The resident fauna do not flee. They gather along the cliff edge in dense, silent ranks. The chimneys reduce their breathing. The buried engines slow.

The visitor never completes its ascent. Perhaps there is no whole creature, only successive organs produced by the cloud. Perhaps the plateau is examining itself from beneath.

Eventually the limb withdraws or becomes a waterfall. Noise resumes. Herds disperse. The pumping organs return to their staggered rhythm.

Rain passes over the flats in visible bands. The cold deepens. Far away, one of the white domes begins to open, its petals lifting against the wind with the slow force of continental plates. Violet light spreads beneath the cloud. Every pool on the plateau trembles in answer.

Across the immense, nearly empty surface, millions of hidden animals turn toward it.

Under them, the ground begins another thought.
