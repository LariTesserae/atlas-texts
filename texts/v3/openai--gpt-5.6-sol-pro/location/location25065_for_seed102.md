Beneath a shelf of black basalt lies a chamber no wider than a village square. There is no natural entrance. The only way in is through a sloping crack behind a slab whose edges fit the surrounding rock too neatly to be accidental. The descent is warm, narrow, and utterly dry. Dust does not cling to the hands. Breath seems to vanish into the stone.

At the bottom, the crack opens suddenly.

The chamber is shaped like the hollow left by a great buried seed: oval, ribbed, and sharply pointed at both ends. Its walls are young stone—dark glass, pale mineral foam, and fresh planes of ocher crystal that have not yet rounded or dulled. Every fracture still carries the hard brightness of the instant it broke. Nothing here bears the soft signatures of age. There are no washed surfaces, no silt, no stains of seepage. No drop of water has ever crossed the floor.

Yet the place is green.

The growth begins in scattered islands around vents no larger than nostrils. From each island rises a small thicket of jointed stalks, waist-high or taller, translucent at the edges and almost black at the core. Their leaves are thick fans of green-gold membrane held upright like listening ears. They do not contain water. Cut one and it releases a warm, clear oil with the sharp smell of pine resin, hot copper, and crushed fennel. The liquid withdraws from air almost at once, crawling back into the wound as if embarrassed by exposure.

These plants root not in soil but in seams of soft sulfurous clay. Their pale root-knuckles clasp mineral nodules and breathe through them, feeding on gases that rise in an even, unvarying pressure from below. No leaf unfolds. No stalk bends toward the light. There is no visible budding, wilting, or decay. The thickets are lush in mass but sparse in placement: dense crowns separated by broad stretches of bare, warm stone. Each island is complete unto itself, a garden with nothing between gardens but emptiness.

The light has no source that the eye can find. It lies low over everything, a tarnished amber dimness strongest near the floor. Shadows fall upward along the walls. Under the leaves, green veins glow faintly, not pulsing but holding one constant intensity. If a hand passes in front of them, the shadow remains for a heartbeat after the hand has moved, then disappears all at once.

The chamber is loud.

A continuous chord fills it: clicking, sawing, drumming, and a deep pressure-note felt in the teeth. Thousands of small creatures occupy the vegetation. Flat, six-legged grazers move along the undersides of leaves, their backs tiled in ivory plates. Long-bodied animals resembling seedpods hang between stalks and rasp their hind limbs together. Thumb-sized burrowers strike the hollow roots from within, producing wooden knocks. Above them, broad triangular fliers traverse the open spaces on rigid, mica-like wings.

Their abundance does not make the chamber feel crowded. They remain within strict territories, leaving bare lanes between each colony. Nor does their noise rise or fall. The same clicks arrive in the same order. The same wingbeats cross the same intervals. Listen long enough and the apparent riot resolves into repetition: one immense measure of sound, perfectly sustained.

Even the animals obey this fixity. A triangular flier leaves a black stalk, crosses the chamber, and settles on a yellow fan. Some minutes later—or perhaps after no measurable duration—it leaves the black stalk again. The hanging raspers lift and close their legs along identical arcs. The plated grazers advance, but never reach the end of a leaf. If marked with charcoal, they are found later beneath the original mark.

Nothing is visibly forced backward. Nothing jumps. The creatures seem alive at every instant: warm, reactive, capable of fear when touched. But when the hand withdraws, the old arrangement is restored. A startled cloud of fliers bursts upward in a clatter like shaken pottery; moments afterward they occupy their former routes, and the chamber’s chord is exactly as it was.

Near the narrow end stand the signs of habitation.

There are three low shelters, each made from fitted shards of volcanic glass leaned against a natural rib in the wall. Their roofs are woven from dry stalk-sheaths and sealed with plant resin. One shelter contains a sleeping hollow lined with shed wing-casings. Another holds stone scrapers, bone awls, fiber cord, shallow bowls of hardened oil, and a rack of hooked implements carved from the jawplates of grazing animals. There is no firepit. Flame would be dangerous in the resinous air, and in any case the chamber is already as warm as living flesh.

The third structure has no entrance. It is a waist-high cairn of greenish blocks, carefully dressed with stone tools. Every block bears the same simple incised mark: an open oval crossed by a single vertical stroke. Similar marks appear at intervals around the chamber, but never twice on the same kind of surface—one on glass, one on clay, one on a root, one cut into the ivory shell of a patient animal. The cuts are crisp. Dust still rests beside them as if they were made moments ago.

A few human traces remain, though no people are visible. A coil of braided fiber lies beside a vent. A sandal with a bark sole waits at the edge of a thicket. Five handprints in red mineral pigment mark the ceiling of the lowest shelter. They differ in size, from a child’s hand to one with fingers unusually long and narrow.

The prints cannot be rubbed away.

Neither can the cord be uncoiled. It yields under the fingers but returns to the same loops when released. A scraper can be lifted and used; afterward it is found again upon its rack, its edge as sharp as before. A leaf can be cut, an animal killed, a shelter dismantled stone by stone. The chamber accepts these acts without resistance. Then, beyond the witness’s direct attention, it closes around its preferred condition. Wounds vanish. Shells knit. Walls rise. The dead resume their routes.

This is not renewal. Nothing grows back. Nothing heals. The place simply possesses one arrangement more strongly than it possesses consequence.

At the center is a bare depression surrounded by a ring of fresh, knife-edged crystal. The depression resembles a dry pool, though water would bead and skitter from its surface. Within it stands a single thin pillar of porous stone. Warm gas issues from its crown with a roar disproportionate to its size. The sound forms the bass note beneath all the clicking life.

Set into the pillar is a black stone bowl. Its interior is polished smooth by no detectable abrasion. It contains a quantity of dense silver fluid—not water, not mercury, but something that climbs the sides against gravity and gathers along the rim. It never spills. Small pale animals approach it in procession, press their mouthparts to the upward-crawling liquid, and retreat along the same paths. Whether they drink or merely complete a gesture is impossible to tell.

The bowl may be the chamber’s center, or its lock.

Close to it, time becomes sensibly shallow. A spoken word hangs amid the larger noise and is heard again beneath the next word. Footsteps return from the stone before the foot has fully landed. A lamp flame, if one is brought this far, holds rigid and blue, giving neither heat nor smoke. The person carrying it feels sweat rise but not fall. Thirst begins, yet does not deepen.

Beyond the crystal ring, ordinary sequence resumes—at least enough to permit escape.

The strangest feature of the hidden garden is not its dry luxuriance, its mineral-fed ecology, or even its refusal to change. It is the impression that the chamber is not ancient. The glass is too sharp, the tool marks too fresh, the shelters too recently abandoned. The entire place seems to have erupted into existence in a single completed state: plants already tall, animals already following their circuits, artifacts already used, handprints already left by absent inhabitants.

It has remained in that first moment ever since.

At the upper crack, the continuous chord diminishes quickly. The raspers become insects, then static, then an imagined pressure in the jaw. Warm resin scents the clothes for days. Green-gold afterimages persist behind closed eyes.

Below, nothing notices the departure.

The fliers cross their fixed distances. The root-drums strike. The silver fluid climbs endlessly toward the lip of the bowl. In three scattered shelters, tools wait in the positions to which they always return. And in the dry, dim warmth, beneath a world that may have altered beyond recognition, the hidden garden remains perfectly, deafeningly new.
