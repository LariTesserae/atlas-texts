The land is a floor of fired clay.

No soil lies beneath it, at least none that anyone has reached. Where the pavement has cracked, the fissures disclose another course of bricks, and beneath that a layer of fitted black bowls turned mouth-down, and beneath those—judging by the sound when struck—hollow chambers. Wells sunk for repair have descended through forty different constructions without finding bedrock. Each level appears handmade. Finger furrows remain in the oldest tiles. Some bear the impressions of woven mats; others the blunt signatures of palms, knuckles, shells, and tools made from sharpened ceramic.

The plain extends farther than a shout can travel, low and nearly level, yet so crowded that it cannot be seen as empty. Ribs of masonry rise waist-high from the pavement. Kilns lean against cisterns. Roofless corridors converge on circular yards packed with stone tripods, drainage jars, wind screens, drying racks, and stacks of unused tile. Towers occur here and there, but none are tall. They are squat, massive drums built to resist pressures that come not from wind but from below.

Everything has been made. Even the horizon is constructed: a dark, irregular embankment of arches fitted one inside another, the outer wall of some district too large to cross in a day. There are no mountains, only heaps of discarded architecture. There are no caves, only rooms whose entrances have been sealed. What looks at first like a boulder is usually an unsuccessful vessel, a fused mass from a collapsed kiln, or a knot where generations of walls have been built around one another until their purposes disappeared.

The coolness is persistent. It is the coolness of cellars and wet pottery, not of snow. The air clings to surfaces. Moisture gathers on glazed ceilings, iron-dark pipes, and the undersides of suspended slabs, then falls in heavy drops. Rain comes occasionally, without warning and often without visible clouds. It descends through the darkness in narrow local curtains, soaking one courtyard while a neighboring passage remains dry. The water has a mineral taste, faintly bitter and faintly smoky.

Nothing grows where it falls.

There is no moss in the joints, no weed in the cracks, no lichen softening the kiln walls. Damp tiles remain bare. Pools are edged not with reeds but with crusts of pale salts and delicate mineral fans. Along some cisterns, translucent films spread over the water like sheets of oil. They contract when touched and re-form in angular patches, perhaps chemistry, perhaps a kind of life too simple or too alien to be called vegetation. The people of the plain scrape these films into cups and use them to seal hairline fractures.

Water is never abundant enough to dominate the place, but it is always arriving somewhere. Condensation beads in the cold galleries. Sluices cough out brief brown torrents. Buried reservoirs overflow and send shallow streams wandering through streets that were not built to receive them. The streams seldom keep their courses for long. They find newly opened seams, drain into unseen rooms, or are intercepted by crews carrying clay gutters on their shoulders.

Thus the first erosion is beginning.

The constructed world is young in its weathering, though it may be unimaginably old in years. Edges remain sharp. Tool marks have not yet been erased. Channels cut by runoff are shallow, tentative things, often only a finger’s breadth deep. The water seems to be learning how to damage the plain. It tests mortar, worries at unfired plugs, collects in the slight hollows left by knees and thumbs. Here a brick face has begun to powder. There a drip has bored the first dark pit into a stair. Elsewhere, one night’s flood has accomplished what centuries of condensation could not, lifting a whole pavement like a lid.

Every such failure exposes more construction beneath.

There is no true daylight. Above the plain hangs a ceiling of black slats, chains, ducts, and overlapping canopies, some high enough to vanish into soot. Whether this roof covers the entire region is unknown. Narrow openings occasionally admit a dim gray radiance, but it falls in columns and seldom reaches the lower lanes. Most illumination comes from shallow oil cups, charcoal braziers, and the dull red mouths of kilns. Wet glaze catches these points of light and repeats them in walls and puddles, so that a traveler may mistake a reflection beneath their feet for a distant fire.

The darkness is crowded with sound.

Hammers strike forming blocks in uneven rhythms. Stone pestles grind grog and salt. Wooden cranes groan as crews haul slabs from lower chambers. Water wheels turn in hidden culverts, their paddles knocking against housings with the slow insistence of enormous teeth. From under the pavement come booms, scrapes, and resonant collapses. Sometimes the ground hums for several minutes, setting jars trembling on their shelves. People stop speaking while it lasts, listening for changes in pitch.

No one has engines. No one has fine machines. The sophistication of the place lies in repetition, leverage, memory, and hands. Doors weighing many tons are moved with rollers, ropes, wedges, and patient crowds. Pressure is measured by columns of colored water. Distances are kept in knotted cords. Maps are scratched onto plates and fired, because paper and cloth decay too quickly in the damp. Metal exists only in small, treasured pieces: needles, knife edges, hooks hammered thin and repaired until scarcely any original material remains. Most tools are stone, wood hardened over coals, shell-like ceramic, or fragments salvaged from older constructions.

Yet the whole plain is artificial. Its immensity does not come from advanced machinery but from uncounted labor. Every hand adds a tile, seals a seam, replaces a gutter, or builds a new wall against a problem no one fully understands.

The inhabitants do not speak of building the world. They speak of keeping it built.

Their settlements are difficult to distinguish from workshops. Sleeping recesses occupy the warm backs of kilns. Markets form on broad drain covers, where jars, lamp fat, rope, fired wedges, bone pegs, and cups of condensed water are exchanged. Communal halls are low vaults crowded with supports added at different dates. Some columns are structural; others are memorials shaped exactly like structural columns. Removing the wrong one may offend the dead or bring down the roof, and often no one knows which danger is greater.

Open spaces exist, but openness here means a court large enough for sound to return as an echo. The greatest is the Basin of Lids, a shallow expanse several miles wide paved with circular ceramic plates. Each plate seals a shaft. When pressure rises below, the lids lift imperceptibly and exhale cool vapor. Thousands may breathe at once. The basin then fills with a dense ground fog smelling of wet ash, and the lids chatter in their sockets like teeth.

Crossing during an exhalation is forbidden. Not because the vapor is always poisonous—it usually is not—but because shapes move through it.

Animals are rare enough to become events. Small white crustacean things sometimes emerge from drains after rain, walking sideways on too many jointed legs. They have no visible eyes and carry droplets on their backs in neat rows. Children follow them until they reach another opening and vanish. In the cistern districts live flat, black swimmers that resemble scraps of fired leather. They surface only when bells are sounded underwater.

Larger creatures are known chiefly by damage. Something drags itself through the deep utility passages, polishing the walls with its flanks and cracking drain grates from below. It has never been seen whole. Once every few years, a long pale limb or feeler appears through a broken pavement, explores the air with slow deliberation, and withdraws. The citizens mark the place with concentric brick rings and divert traffic around it. Eventually the rings become a shrine, then a market, then the foundation of another kiln.

There are also birds, or things given that name. They nest nowhere and are seen only during rainfall: narrow silhouettes crossing the roof openings, wingbeats hidden by the roar of water. They sometimes drop smooth blue stones onto the plain. Since no blue material is manufactured anywhere nearby, the stones are kept in dark cupboards and brought out for births, structural failures, and the choosing of new routes.

The landscape never settles. A district may wake to find its gutters flowing backward. A sealed door may begin sweating warm water. Floors rise by the thickness of a hand, driven upward by pressure in buried rooms. Crews arrive with mallets and sounding rods, tapping every surface and listening to the replies. A clear note means emptiness. A dull note means water, rubble, or a second wall close behind. A note that changes while being heard means everyone must leave.

Construction follows failure so quickly that the two are almost the same process. A collapsed corridor becomes aggregate. A flooded chamber becomes a cistern. A tilted tower becomes an anchor for ropes. Nothing is discarded beyond use; it is merely incorporated at another angle. This is why the plain grows denser without growing much taller. It folds inward, packs itself, fills voids with smaller voids. In some quarters, doors open onto walls built centuries later. Stairs rise three steps and end beneath floors. Windows admit views of bricks close enough to touch.

Far from the inhabited kilns, the pavement becomes cleaner and more unsettling. The structures there appear unfinished. Rows of waist-high walls define rooms that have never been roofed. Vast fields of damp clay tiles wait in ordered stacks, though no makers guard them. Shallow channels have been scored but not excavated. Foundation lines curve across miles and converge on centers where nothing stands.

On certain nights, after rain, tapping begins in these empty districts.

It starts beneath the stacked tiles: a soft, methodical knocking, as though hands below are testing the thickness of the world. Other knocks answer from cisterns, buried galleries, and the hollow bowls under the pavement. Soon the entire plain participates. Lids tremble. Kiln mouths pulse red. Water jumps in cups. The people add their own hammers to the noise—not to drown it out, but to keep its rhythm from changing.

They believe that as long as the blows remain irregular, the world is only being repaired.

A steady rhythm would mean it is nearly finished.
