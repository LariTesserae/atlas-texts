At the summit of a mountain too steep to possess ordinary slopes, there is a hollow.

It is not a broad plateau. From a distance the peak appears complete: a black-green tooth rising above the lower clouds, its upper crags sharpened by weather. Only from directly overhead—or by climbing the single broken chimney on its eastern face—does the hidden basin become visible. It lies inside the crown of the mountain like the print of a giant thumb, no more than a few hundred paces across. Its enclosing walls rise in uneven tiers, giving it the proportions of a roofless cathedral.

Within those boundaries, however, there is an astonishing amount of sky.

The center of the hollow is almost empty. A shallow tarn once filled it, but the water found cracks long ago and drained into the mountain. What remains is a floor of pale, compacted silt interrupted by broad slabs of stone. Rainwater spreads over it in a silver skin and vanishes within hours. A few channels, fixed by centuries of runoff, cross the floor like the fine lines of an open palm. There are no trees in the middle, no standing stones, no tall growth—only open air descending to bare earth.

All the life has gathered at the margins.

Water seeps continuously from the western wall. It does not emerge as a single spring but condenses upon the cold rock, gathering beneath mats of moss until the entire cliff seems to perspire. Dark threads run down its face. Droplets swell on fern tips, strike leaves below, and join narrow runnels braided through the rubble. After rain, the wall becomes a hundred small waterfalls. In drier intervals it still weeps, fed by cloud vapor caught against the peak.

The basin is high enough to stand above most weather yet low enough inside its rim to trap what passes. Clouds arrive from below. They climb the outer mountain, pour over the lowest notch, and unravel across the hollow as long white streamers. For a few minutes the place is reduced to wet stone, near grass, and the silhouettes of birds. Then the mist thins, disclosing distant ranges far beneath the rim. The whole basin seems suspended, not on a summit but in the open atmosphere between earth and weather.

The coolness is persistent rather than brutal. Frost forms in shaded cracks, though the exposed ground often remains soft. Even at midday, the stones retain the chill of deep water. The light is plain and ample when the clouds permit it: neither enchanted nor dim, but sharpened by altitude. Colors stand cleanly apart. Moss is green-black, new sedge yellow-green, wet rock blue-gray, and the tiny flowers along the northern ledges an almost painful red. Shadows move quickly as scraps of cloud cross the sun.

The mountain’s age shows everywhere. Nothing here looks newly shattered. The cliffs have rounded seams; their fractures have been softened by lichen, water, and repeated freezing. Fallen blocks sit settled into the earth, half absorbed by roots. Even the channels in the basin floor seem to have negotiated permanent routes. Erosion continues, but without violence most days. Grain by grain, the hollow is being deepened. Grain by grain, its floor is leaving through the stone.

That hidden drainage governs the place. Water falling in the basin rarely escapes over the rim. It enters holes among the central slabs and can be heard traveling beneath them. On wet days the whole summit murmurs internally. Far down the southern face, the water emerges as a cold spring, though it may have spent years in the mountain before returning to light.

Vegetation forms a crowded green ring around the empty center. Dwarf birches grow no higher than a person’s knee, their trunks thick, twisted, and silver with age. They lie along the ground rather than standing against the wind. Between them are dense cushions of moss, saxifrage, cloudberry, mountain sorrel, and low ferns with translucent stems. Tough grasses occupy every seam in the rock. On sheltered shelves, broad leaves overlap so completely that the soil cannot be seen.

The plants are lush but compressed. Height is punished here. Anything that rises above the rim of its neighbors is stripped, chilled, or broken. Life succeeds by clinging close, sharing warmth, and growing sideways. Some of the oldest shrubs cover several paces yet stand no taller than a boot.

There are few large presences, but animals are everywhere.

Black mountain swifts occupy holes in the eastern wall. Their nests are felted from grass, feathers, and moss, held together with mud gathered from the tarn bed. They leave in sudden companies, filling the hollow with cutting cries and the hiss of wings. Small gray rodents travel through tunnels beneath the vegetation, occasionally making the ground tremble in a narrow moving line. Beetles shine in the runoff. Pale worms emerge after rain and retreat before the sun dries the silt.

A colony of cliff goats visits through the northern notch. They are slight, long-legged animals with wide hooves and coats stained green at the knees from kneeling in moss. They do not remain in the basin for long. They cross its open center nervously, each hoof sounding against the slabs, then feed along the wet wall. Their arrival brings the mountain’s hunters: narrow-bodied eagles that circle beyond the rim, almost motionless against the abyss.

Most of the place’s volume is vacant air, and most of its life is small, concealed, or moving. This makes every appearance abrupt. A basin that seems empty at first glance can erupt in wings. The moss can part around a dozen hidden bodies. A silent ledge can suddenly hold three goats and an eagle’s shadow.

The summit is loud enough to make speech tiring.

Wind strikes the outer cliffs with a deep continuous pressure, then enters the hollow through cracks and notches as distinct voices. One passage whistles. Another produces a low, fluting moan. The narrow eastern chimney pulses like someone blowing across the mouth of a jar. Water ticks, splashes, trickles, and gurgles underfoot. Swifts scream in their thousands during feeding flights. Loose sedges thrash. Stones occasionally knock together below the drainage holes, shifted by water in darkness.

After storms, the sounds overlap until the small basin seems to contain something much larger than itself. Thunder rolls around the enclosing rock. Water drums on leaves. Runoff begins in all the old channels at once. The central floor becomes a shallow mirror, trembling under rain. Frogs—tiny, cold-adapted creatures that spend dry periods buried in mud—surface and call from every temporary pool. Their voices are surprisingly resonant, like wooden cups struck with reeds.

By the next clear morning, most of the water has gone.

Human marks are scarce, though unmistakable. Near the south wall stands a low shelter built from fitted slabs without mortar. It is too small to stand in and deep enough for two people to lie side by side. Its roof consists of three long stones covered in turf. The doorway faces away from the prevailing wind. Inside, the walls are blackened by old fires, and a shallow niche holds fragments of charcoal, a chipped stone blade, and curls of dried bark.

No path reaches the shelter cleanly. The route has broken and re-formed many times. A few footholds have been cut into the eastern chimney; some are worn smooth, while others have split away. At the basin’s lowest notch, a line of upright sticks supports braided grass cords. These are replaced whenever climbers come, perhaps once in several seasons. Bundles of feathers tied to the cord indicate wind direction and warn of sudden cloud pouring over the rim.

There is also a stone basin beneath the weeping wall, carefully pecked into a natural ledge. Water gathers in it drop by drop. The overflow runs through a groove into a second, smaller cup. The larger basin is for drinking. The smaller one is never touched. It contains beads, bird bones, bright pebbles, and tiny figures carved from horn. The oldest objects are furred with mineral deposits and have become partly stone themselves.

Those who climb here do not build higher than the dwarf birches. Whether this is custom or practical wisdom is unclear. Old stakes driven upright have all been torn out by wind or lightning. Low things endure. Tall things are taken.

The basin has another minor peculiarity. Sounds made near its center return not as echoes but as altered fragments from different walls. A clap may come back as three stone taps. A shouted word may be answered by a whistle from the eastern chimney. The effect is caused, perhaps, by the hollows and passages inside the peak, but animals respond to it. Swifts change direction when the basin repeats a sharp noise. Goats freeze when underground water produces a cadence resembling hoofbeats.

Because of this, visitors speak sparingly. They tap the ground before crossing it, listening for the depth and tone of what lies beneath. Some slabs ring solidly. Others answer with a hollow boom, suggesting chambers below the tarn bed. No one has opened them. The floor has reached an old equilibrium: thin stone over void, held by weight, roots, and habit. Disturbing one part might alter the hidden drainage of the entire summit.

The day proceeds in quick transformations. Clear light brings insects out onto the rocks. Insects bring swifts. Swifts bring eagles. Heat lifts mist from the wet wall, while new cloud climbs the mountain’s exterior. Goats enter, feed, and vanish. Wind shifts the flowers so that whole ledges change color. Water appears and disappears. The open center alternates between dust, mirror, feeding ground, and cloud-filled absence.

At dusk, the air drains rapidly of warmth. Birds funnel back into the eastern wall. Rodents emerge beneath the flattened birches. The last sunlight catches the far ranges while the basin itself turns blue. Water continues moving in the dark, louder now that the wings are still.

Then the frogs begin, if there has been rain.

Their calls rise from shallow hollows across the apparently empty floor, each animal hidden in a spoonful of water. The notes join the wind in the chimney and the buried streams below. For a place so small, so remote, and so sparsely furnished, the summit never truly becomes quiet. It is an empty chamber full of processes: weather passing through, stone dissolving, water descending, roots tightening, wings arriving, and the whole worn mountain breathing through its cracks.
