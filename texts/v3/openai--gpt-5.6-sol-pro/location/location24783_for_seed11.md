The country is called, by the few people who cross it, **the High Roar**.

It is not a range of young mountains. There are no sharp summits striving upward, no raw scree or bright fractures. These heights are what remain after mountains have endured almost everything that can happen to stone. Their peaks have been planed into long, shallow tables and their flanks hollowed through. Seen from a distance, the land resembles a continent-sized ruin: immense arches, freestanding walls, tilted shelves, and pillars so weathered that their surfaces have become soft-looking, almost muscular.

The lowest valleys are still far above the surrounding world. The highest plateaus rise into the middle weather, where clouds drag their bellies over the ground.

Between these masses lie astonishing intervals of emptiness. A person may look across forty kilometers of warm, lucid air and see only three things: a green escarpment, a column of rain, and a solitary shelter clinging beneath an overhang. Distance is difficult to judge. The old erosion has stripped away intermediate scales. There are either tiny details—a bead of water trembling on a leaf, a stone blade, an insect casing—or features too large to understand from one vantage.

The stone is predominantly dark ocher, lavender-gray, and old black. Its exposed faces are deeply varnished, polished by ages of mineral-bearing mist. Under ledges it turns pale and friable, marked with horizontal lines like the grain of ancient wood. There is almost no loose rubble. Whatever could fall has fallen. Whatever could wash away has washed away. The remaining forms appear permanent, but they are not peaceful.

Air moves violently through them.

Wind enters the hollow mountains by apertures on their western faces and is forced through chambers, shafts, and narrowing stone throats. The whole region becomes an instrument. Some mesas produce a continuous bass pressure felt in the teeth and sternum. Needle-like pillars scream. Deep pits cough at irregular intervals, expelling warm fog and dead leaves. Long arches give off tones so low that they cannot be heard directly, only sensed as a trembling in water, skin, and the roots of plants.

The sound is nearly constant and often deafening.

There are places where conversation is impossible even when the sky is clear. Travelers communicate by touch or by broad gestures. Most shelters have no doors facing the wind, and their interiors are lined with woven moss, bark, and bundles of dry pith to deaden vibration. People sleep with plugs of wax and fiber in their ears. Children born here learn to read lips before they speak.

Yet the High Roar is not especially wet.

Rain comes hard and briefly. Warm storms form with startling speed along the upper rims, boil over the plateaus, and collapse in one valley while another remains sunlit. For minutes at a time, waterfalls appear everywhere: silver ropes spilling from ledges, sheets flung upward by crosswinds, thin streams traveling sideways between separate cliffs. Then the rain passes. Within an hour many of the falls have vanished, leaving only dark tracks and airborne moisture.

Water persists in hidden forms. Condensation gathers every night on the windward vegetation. Certain caverns sweat continuously. Shallow basins in the plateau stone hold brown rainwater under mats of floating fern. In the deepest shaded clefts, narrow rivers run without ever seeing the sky. Their presence is revealed by cool breath issuing from cracks and by the remote, rapid knocking of stones below.

The vegetation is abundant but does not fill the space. It adheres to the land in dense, sharply bounded provinces.

Every damp face carries green. Ferns descend in layered skirts from the overhangs. Thick-leaved vines bind themselves to columns and hang hundreds of meters into the open air, their lowest ends twisting freely. Plateaus support low forests of pale, wind-combed trees whose branches all grow toward the lee side, making each grove look like a crowd fleeing the same event. In protected bowls, broad leaves overlap so completely that rain may take an hour to reach the soil.

Then the greenery stops.

A cliff edge may divide waist-high jungle from bare stone as precisely as a cut seam. Beyond it is only open air. Vast shelves remain empty because the wind strips seedlings from them. Other surfaces are salted by mist rising from mineral springs. The result is not a continuous wilderness but an archipelago of lushness separated by immense, resonant voids.

Many plants have adapted to turbulence rather than resisting it. Rope grass grows in long braided cables that tighten under strain. Cup trees fold their leaves before storms and collect water afterward. Black-veined cushion plants lie flat against the rock, their roots penetrating hairline fractures. There are hanging orchards whose fruits grow beneath leaves on tough cords; when ripe, they beat against the cliff in the wind until their shells break and cast seeds into the updraft.

Some seeds do not fall. They rise.

The warm vertical currents carry them between plateaus, sometimes for days. During the seeding season the air contains slow-moving constellations of fluff, translucent vanes, and spinning pods. From afar these migrations resemble smoke traveling against the wind.

Animal life is scarce, or at least rarely visible. The country offers much plant matter but little secure ground. Small gray browsers live in the inner forests and flatten themselves beneath roots when the stone begins to resonate. Long-winged birds cross the gaps singly, never in flocks. They seldom flap; instead they enter one current, vanish below a cliff, and reappear impossibly high on another.

There are also the bell crawlers, though most travelers know them only by their traces. They are hand-sized, many-legged creatures with thin mineral shells. They cluster in the throats of the stone vents. When pressure changes, thousands shift position at once, altering the pitch of the mountain. Whether they do this deliberately is unknown. The local people say that abrupt changes in the Roar are the crawlers warning one another of weather several valleys away.

Larger animals are almost absent. Occasionally an enormous shadow passes through the cloud deck, too diffuse to identify. Once or twice in a lifetime, a settlement may find broad feeding marks on the treetops after a night storm, as if something had grazed the forest from above.

Human presence is slight.

The inhabited structures stand far apart, usually one to a valley or one to an entire plateau. They are low buildings of fitted stone, split timber, bark cloth, and braided grass. Their builders use wedges, adzes, fire-hardened drills, bone needles, stone weights, and a small amount of soft hammered copper traded from the lower country. They do not attempt large bridges. Anything rigid eventually tears itself apart in the wind.

Instead, paths are made from replaceable elements: knotted vine rails, ladders that can be pulled up before storms, lines of handholds cut into sheltered rock, and swinging baskets drawn across short chasms. A route may exist one season and be dismantled the next. Cairns are useless here, so travelers mark safe passages by inserting tuned reeds into cracks. Each route produces a recognizable chord when the wind is right.

The oldest human remains are not buildings but hollows. Shallow sleeping niches have been polished into certain cave walls by generations of bodies. Fire pits contain ash compacted into black stone. Lines of red handprints occur under ledges, but nearly all have blurred at the fingers, as though the painted hands were moving when they touched the wall.

No settlement claims the land beneath it. Ownership is considered absurd in a place where paths migrate, rain chooses different basins each year, and even the voice of a mountain can change overnight. People claim only intervals: the sheltered hour before dusk, the three dry weeks when a vine bridge can be crossed, the silence following a pressure collapse.

Silence is rare and feared.

Sometimes, without warning, the whole country stops sounding. One mountain falls quiet, then the next, until the usual roar drains from the valleys. Leaves sag. Hanging vines cease twisting. Water in the basins becomes perfectly smooth. Even insects disappear into bark.

These silences may last several breaths or an entire afternoon.

Then the air reorganizes.

Clouds are drawn downward into pits. Warm winds emerge from caves that ordinarily inhale. Water climbs cliff faces as spray. Loose vegetation, seed husks, and abandoned tools lift and stream toward unseen centers. The mountains begin sounding again in a new sequence—first a distant pulse, then overlapping moans, then the full unbearable chorus.

Afterward, the landscape is recognizably the same but locally transformed. A forest may have been combed flat. A waterfall may now drain into the sky-facing mouth of a cavern. A familiar arch may sing in a different register. New pools appear in old footprints; old pools are found empty but lined with fresh green shoots.

This is the central contradiction of the High Roar: the stone has reached an almost final form, but everything touching it remains in furious motion. The foundations are older than memory, pared down to what cannot easily be removed. Upon them, weather, water, plants, paths, and sound continually assemble and disassemble temporary worlds.

At sunset the turbulence often slackens. Warm light reaches laterally through the immense gaps and stains the green faces gold. Rain hangs in isolated curtains miles away. The oldest stone glows faintly violet. For a brief time, each mesa and pillar stands distinct in the open air, and the scale of the country becomes almost comprehensible.

Then the night winds enter the hollows.

One by one, the mountains begin to speak.
