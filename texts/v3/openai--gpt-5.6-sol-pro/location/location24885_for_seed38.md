At first it seems to be a forest on a high plateau. Then the distances refuse that simple description.

There is no visible ground. What passes beneath the feet is an accumulation of living and half-living vegetable matter many thousands of years deep: braided roots, compressed leaf-felt, pale fungal cords, old trunks laid horizontally and absorbed into newer growth. The uppermost layer gives slightly under weight. Water wells into each footprint without forming a puddle, then is drawn back through hair-fine roots. In places the surface is taut and resonant, stretched across hollows large enough to contain valleys. Elsewhere it has folded into terraces, ridges, and long shallow basins. The entire plateau is, in some sense, one plant-built landform.

It occupies an elevation where mountains elsewhere would be cold, but the air here remains warm. Heat rises from the enormous depth of decay beneath the living surface. The plateau exhales through vents in the rootwork, releasing breath scented with wet bark, green sap, minerals, and the faint sweetness of fermentation. These currents meet the cooler upper air and condense. Mist hangs under the canopy without drifting far. Leaves gather droplets even when no rain falls, and every edge weeps.

The canopy is not overhead in any consistent way. It rises and descends with the plant-land, sometimes closing into a low green ceiling, sometimes opening around columns of fused trunks that climb out of sight. Along broad ridges, the crowns spread laterally for leagues, knitting with neighboring crowns until they become a second, elevated terrain. Pools collect on this upper country. Reeds and small forests grow there, rooted in soil made from the fallen leaves of the trees supporting them. Some pools overflow in thin, constant curtains. The water falls through several levels of foliage, breaking into mist before it reaches the lower world.

Thus there are rivers, but few run over stone. They travel inside roots, along channels between layers of bark, or through dark galleries under woven mats of vegetation. A stream may be visible for a hundred paces—tea-colored, warm, carrying yellow pollen—then vanish into a seam in the living ground. Days later it emerges from the side of a distant escarpment as hundreds of separate threads. Nothing rushes. Even the waterfalls descend softly, divided by mosses and hanging roots into ropes of droplets.

Stone appears only rarely. Where it does, it is extremely old and almost without edges. Low domes of dark rock protrude from the green substrate like the backs of buried animals. Their surfaces have been hollowed into bowls by ages of rain. Moss fills the bowls; bladder-leaves float in them; tiny translucent creatures graze on the films at their rims. The rock itself sweats continuously. Mineral-rich water passes through it so slowly that the stone seems less eroded than persuaded, grain by grain, into softer forms.

The plateau has no young cliffs. Its walls have sagged, slumped, rooted, and been overgrown until height expresses itself in depth rather than sharpness. Ravines are immense but rounded. From one side, the opposite wall may be hidden by its own weather. Warm cloud occupies the middle distances. Great slopes emerge through it in muted bands—black-green, gray-green, the deep red of mature leaf undersides—then disappear again.

Almost every open space is temporary. A fallen crown may create a clearing several miles long, but vines begin crossing it before the upper leaves have yellowed. They extend from both sides, growing on suspended fibers no thicker than hair. Epiphytes collect on these bridges. Ferns follow. Within decades, the clearing is roofed; within centuries, it has become another layer of ground. What seems like an empty gulf is often merely a floor that has not yet finished forming.

The oldest plants are difficult to distinguish from geography. They do not have single trunks. A ridge may be one organism whose original stem died so long ago that even its decay has been recycled. It persists as a migrating boundary of shoots, roots, and self-grafted descendants. It advances perhaps the length of a hand in a human lifetime, leaving mature forest behind it and growing mature forest ahead. Its flowers open only along one remote contour at a time. Their blossoming travels across the plateau over centuries, a faint belt of ivory moving through the dimness.

Light arrives strained through many layers. Noon is green dusk. Under the thickest regions, sight depends on pale leaf undersides, luminous molds, and flowers shaped to retain and redistribute the little light they receive. Broad leaves turn slowly throughout the day, passing a softened gleam downward from one tier to the next. When clouds close over the plateau, the lower spaces darken almost to night, though the warmth hardly changes.

There are animals everywhere, but the place remains mostly quiet.

Small bodies move in the leaf-felt: six-legged grazers with soft shells, blind burrowers that swim through wet mulch, narrow amphibians coiled inside unopened flowers. Long-limbed browsers travel the middle canopy without breaking its surface, their weight spread across many branches. They are glimpsed as a sequence of gentle depressions moving through the foliage. Moths rest in overlapping colonies on trunks, indistinguishable from lichen until one section of bark lifts and rearranges itself.

The larger animals are slow and seldom seen whole. Dark, broad-backed creatures graze the floating marshes of the upper canopy. Entire gardens grow in the grooves of their hides, and seed-eating birds follow them from one feeding ground to another. In the humid ravines, pale gliders pass between root curtains, stretching membranes from limb to limb. They do not flap. The dense air and constant updrafts carry them for hours.

Nothing calls often. Sound does not travel cleanly through so much leaf and wet fiber. It is absorbed, softened, and divided. Nearby there may be the ticking of condensed water, the careful tear of a mouth through a leaf, a wing opening once. From far below comes an occasional wooden groan as a buried trunk gives way under centuries of accumulated weight. The sound can last several minutes, descending in pitch until it becomes something felt through the feet. Afterward, a portion of the plateau may settle by a finger’s breadth.

Rain has several forms. There is ordinary rain from the sky, warm and fine. There is canopy rain, released hours or days afterward when leaves tilt under gathered water. There is upward rain at dawn, when vapor from the deep compost rises and condenses against cooler growth. And there are local rains generated by flowering: vast inflorescences heat the air around them, lifting moisture until small clouds form beneath the upper crowns and drift no farther than the neighboring valley.

Storms are rare and exhausting rather than violent. The plateau has made its own climate too stable for abrupt changes. Pressure gathers over weeks. Leaves gradually reverse their surfaces; animals move down into the root chambers; the dimness thickens. Then rain persists for a month. Water rises through the plant-mat and spreads in broad, shallow sheets. The land becomes a marsh without losing its forest. When the rain ends, the water takes years to disappear completely.

Scattered through this immensity are structures, though it is uncertain whether anything intended them.

Some are chambers formed where trees grew around open air and later hardened. Their walls are smooth wood, dark as soaked earth, with ribbed arches and narrow openings that resemble doors. Roots have made steps where repeated animal passage once abraded a slope. Elsewhere, rings of upright trunks surround rain pools in arrangements too regular to feel accidental, but there are no cut surfaces, no joinery, no metal, no worked stone. If builders existed, they persuaded plants rather than shaping them with tools—or perhaps the builders were themselves plants, and architecture is only one of their methods of flowering.

One such place stands along the rim of a mist-filled basin. It consists of seven hollow towers, each grown from interlocked trunks. Their upper portions have collapsed into hanging gardens. Inside, the air is cooler. Water descends the walls in thin films, and small animals have worn passages through the fibrous floors. At the center of each tower is a vertical shaft descending into darkness. Warm breath rises from below with a pulse so slow it might be seasonal.

No object remains that can be carried away. There are only patterns: spirals in the grain, alcoves at similar heights, rows of seedpods hardened into black plaques. These patterns recur hundreds of miles apart. Whether they are ruins, organs, or habits of growth is never resolved.

Distance is deceptive here. Because the terrain is made of organisms, routes close and open over long intervals. A bridge of roots may thicken for a thousand years and then become impassable under thorny flowering shoots. A valley may be roofed over, becoming a cavern. A marsh may slowly lift as its supporting branches grow, until it hangs above the clouds and drains into a watershed that did not exist before. Maps would have to measure botanical time.

The world obeys accumulation rather than replacement. Nothing disappears quickly. Fallen things are received layer by layer, softened, inhabited, and built upon. Death becomes structure before it becomes soil. Soil becomes root. Root becomes wall, reservoir, road, canopy, and eventually the support for another soil.

Even erosion participates in growth. Water wears channels, and plants line them. A slope collapses, and its torn roots graft themselves where they land. Rock dissolves into mineral seepage, feeding the organisms that conceal it. The plateau has reached an ancient equilibrium in which wearing down and growing upward are nearly the same process.

At the widest view, from one of the rare crowns that rises above the cloud layer, the landscape extends beyond curvature and haze. It is not a forest surrounded by another biome. It is the prevailing surface of the world: a warm, elevated continent of intergrown life, holding its own rivers, weather, chambers, and buried ages. Here and there, immense depressions mark places where whole provinces sank into the decomposing depths. New canopies span them. Cloud fills them. Their boundaries have already begun to round away.

Nothing seems urgent. Species bloom at intervals longer than lives. Rivers change direction by the growth of roots. Hills breathe and subside. The scattered towers lean by fractions too small to witness. Yet the place is not still. It is active everywhere, continuously and almost silently, with the patience of something that has outlived catastrophe and no longer distinguishes between landscape and body.

In the dim warmth beneath the leaves, water continues to gather.

A drop forms on the tip of a fern high above. It swells for an hour, reflecting a green world upside down, then falls through one layer, and another, and another. It may never reach the bottom.
