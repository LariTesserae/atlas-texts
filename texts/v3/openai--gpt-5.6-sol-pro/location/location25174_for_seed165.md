At this altitude the world does not narrow to a summit. It opens.

Beyond the last ordinary slope lies a broad, shallow country suspended among peaks: a tableland of dark stone, perhaps thirty valleys wide, though distance behaves poorly here. Its horizons are higher than its center. Mountains rise on every side in long, weathered walls, their teeth worn down not by recent violence but by an erosion so old that each fracture seems deliberate. There are no fresh scars, no tumbling screes, no unstable cornices. Loose stones have already found their hollows. Frost has entered every seam it will ever enter. The terrain has settled into a final arrangement.

Nothing moves.

The cold is not sharp. It is deep, uniform, and without weather—a cold soaked into the rock until stone and air seem made from the same material. Low clouds occupy the plateau rather than passing over it. They lie in trenches, fill saddles, and gather beneath overhangs in dense gray folds. The light comes through them without direction. There is no visible sun and almost no shadow, only gradations between slate, ash, tarnished silver, and the brown-black of wet mineral faces.

Water is present in small, persistent ways. It beads on the undersides of cliffs. It films the basalt in the lowest depressions. Narrow runnels descend from nowhere, cross a few meters of level ground, and disappear beneath stones without sinking or freezing. Along the valley floors lie isolated pools as smooth as sealed glass. They are never large enough to become lakes, and no stream visibly feeds them. Their margins show centuries of mineral rings: white calcite, greenish iron salts, thin bands the color of old bone.

Rain hangs in parts of the air.

It does not fall. Under certain ledges, thousands of droplets remain suspended in vertical cords, each drop fat and perfectly still. Walking past one of these rain curtains—if walking here is possible—produces no disturbance. The droplets neither strike skin nor give way. They continue downward through whatever crosses them, as though body and rain briefly belong to different versions of the place. Elsewhere condensation crawls uphill along rock faces, but so slowly and so consistently that it cannot be called movement. It is simply the shape moisture has there.

Vegetation survives in separate, guarded patches. In the seams of south-facing stone grows a black lichen with a faint blue edge. It resembles scorch marks until touched, when its surface contracts like an eyelid. Low mats of rust-colored moss surround some pools, each patch no wider than a sleeping animal. There are also pale stalks rooted in naked gravel. They bear no leaves and branch underground rather than above it. Their hollow stems point toward different empty regions of sky, and wind—or what sounds like wind—passes through them with the tone of distant flutes.

The loudness of the plateau has no visible cause.

A continuous roar fills it. At first it might be taken for a cataract hidden behind the mountains, or for a storm moving through some adjacent valley. Yet it does not swell or fade. It has no direction. Kneeling with an ear against the ground reveals the same roar in the stone; covering the ears reveals it inside the skull. Within it are lower structures: a grinding like glaciers under impossible pressure, a long metallic bellow, the crack of an avalanche that never reaches its collapse. These sounds repeat without sequence. They do not occur one after another. They are all happening permanently.

Near the pools the roar becomes almost articulate. Great vowel-like forms stand in the air. They are too broad to be voices, but the body hears them as speech and instinctively waits for an ending that never comes.

Despite the noise, the atmosphere feels empty. Peaks that appear a few hours away may take months to approach, while a ridge on the far horizon can sometimes be reached in twenty steps. Open gravel plains contain almost nothing: one boulder, one damp hollow, one patch of lichen, each separated from the next by expanses out of proportion to their visible dimensions. Space accumulates between objects. Two stones lying a handspan apart may have a colder, dimmer distance between them than exists across an entire valley.

The few built forms make the emptiness stranger.

Scattered along the plateau are low stone enclosures, rarely more than three walls and a roof slab. No mortar joins them. No stone shows a tool mark. Their blocks fit because they seem to have weathered into one another, each surface carrying the inverse of its neighbor. Some resemble shelters, though their entrances are too narrow for any known body. Others are open to the sky and contain a single pool or a bed of dry, white gravel. They might be ruins if they showed any evidence of having once been whole. Instead they seem complete in their incompleteness.

On exposed ridges stand columns made from flat stones balanced edge upon edge. They should fall in any wind, but there is no wind, only its deafening sound. Frost rims one side of every column. The opposite side is wet. The distinction never changes.

No road connects these structures. There are only faint lines pressed into the ground, too shallow for paths and too regular for drainage channels. They begin at cliff faces, cross open country, and end in midair above ravines. When viewed from one direction they look worn by repeated passage. From the other, they are raised seams rather than depressions.

Animal life is difficult to confirm. Occasionally a small white shape can be seen far across the plateau, standing beside a pool. It might be a hornless goat, a bird with folded wings, or a piece of exposed quartz. It never changes posture while watched. Looking away does not make it vanish, but looking back places it at another pool, sometimes much closer. No tracks lead between these appearances.

Under the overhangs are nests of coarse gray fiber. They contain translucent shells, each with a dark speck suspended at its center. Nothing hatches. Nothing decays. Once in a great while, a shadow passes across the clouds from below. The roar deepens at the same moment, though “moment” is uncertain here.

There are no days. The dimness neither brightens nor declines. Ice does not advance. Pools do not evaporate. The rain does not finish falling. Even erosion has ceased to be a process and become an anatomy: channels are permanently channel-shaped, cliffs permanently worn, stones permanently rounded by waters that are no longer present. The land looks ancient without continuing to age.

Travel reveals the plateau’s governing impossibility. Nothing changes, including the traveler.

Footprints appear in damp ground but do not accumulate. The first remains the only one, recurring beneath each step. Breath clouds the air and hangs there, yet the plateau never fills with breath. Hunger begins but does not deepen. Wounds neither close nor worsen. A dropped stone remains beside the hand and is also visible where it lands. One can walk away from a shelter for hours and still hear the tiny drip inside it beneath the universal roar, as clear as if kneeling at the threshold.

There are places where the landscape folds through itself. A ravine may show, in its depths, the underside of the plateau: inverted pools clinging to a dark ceiling, rain rising motionlessly toward them, stone shelters hanging roof-first over an immeasurable gray atmosphere. Across some gaps, the opposite cliff displays the observer’s side of the valley, complete with a distant figure at the edge. The figure always stands still. It cannot be approached because it begins approaching too, though neither figure appears to move.

The highest point is visible from almost everywhere: a broad, blunt summit beyond the northern wall. It is capped not with snow but with a level sheet of dark water. The water rises vertically from its rim like a tower without sides, extending upward into cloud. Its surface lies both horizontal and upright depending on where it is seen from. The plateau’s endless roar may come from that impossible reservoir, but the water itself is utterly calm.

Sparse black growth circles the summit in concentric bands. Among those bands are many small stone rooms, more numerous than elsewhere but still widely separated. Their doorways all face inward toward the water. From a distance they resemble an abandoned settlement. Up close, each room proves to be the same room approached from a different direction.

Inside is a dry floor, a wet wall, and a shallow niche containing one rounded stone.

The stone is warm.

It is perhaps the only warm thing in the entire high country, though its warmth gives off no heat and melts no frost. Holding it does not remove it from the niche. Breaking the wall does not damage the room. Leaving and returning changes nothing.

The plateau admits events, but not consequences.

And so it remains: cold, damp in isolated pockets, nearly barren, thunderously loud, and perfectly at rest. Its few lives do not breed or die. Its ruins were never built and do not collapse. Its waters neither arrive nor depart. Above everything, cloud presses low against the weathered heights, preserving a dim country that has somehow reached the end state of every process without passing through time to get there.
