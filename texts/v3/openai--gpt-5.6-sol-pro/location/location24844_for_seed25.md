### The Low Roaring Plain

The land lies only slightly above the reach of buried water. It is not a marsh, though it often behaves like one, and not a desert, though most of its surface is dry enough to powder beneath weight. From a distance it appears nearly level: a broad, dark basin extending to a horizon that is seldom visible. Up close, the plain is divided into countless shallow forms—runneled stone, collapsed hollows, salt-rimmed pans, narrow gutters, and long shelves of black sediment polished by generations of passage.

Nothing grows.

There are no grasses between the stones, no moss on the damp faces, no roots holding the banks. Even the concept of bare ground is difficult here, because the ground is almost never bare. It is occupied instead by animals in such numbers that geology reveals itself only intermittently: a slab glimpsed when a mass migration parts, a cliff exposed for a few moments when resting bodies lift and turn, a wet channel flashing under thousands of crossing limbs.

The basin is cool and held in darkness. Above it hangs a permanent ceiling of dense, low cloud, thick with mineral dust and the condensed breath of the plain. Day arrives only as a dilution of black into charcoal. At rare intervals the clouds pale to iron-gray, and every wet shell and exposed tooth catches the weak light. There is no direct sun. Night is less a change in illumination than a change in which creatures make the illumination themselves.

Moisture enters irregularly. Fine rain sometimes descends for hours without forming visible drops; it collects on hides, whiskers, spines, and the undersides of wings, then runs from body to body until it reaches the stone. At other times a hard, brief shower beats the basin, filling its old erosion channels in minutes. The water travels only a short distance before vanishing into porous beds or settling into low pans. These pools may remain for days, black and perfectly reflective at their centers, cloudy with salts around their edges. Some are warm from deep seepage, but most are cold enough to numb exposed flesh.

The plain has been shaped by water despite possessing little of it. Every surface bears the patience of intermittent flow. Limestone has been scalloped into overlapping bowls. Softer beds have worn into smooth trenches packed with shed armor, bone grit, and pale coils of discarded membrane. Basalt ridges stand low and rounded, their edges rubbed down not only by rain but by contact: flanks, claws, bellies, and dragging tails have polished them over ages. The oldest migration routes are engraved into the basin as geological features, broad grooves several body-lengths deep.

The life here does not rise from the ground toward the sky. It lies upon the ground in layers.

Nearest the stone are the pressers: flat, many-limbed animals with bodies like wet seals of cartilage. They adhere to mineral faces and rasp away translucent films too thin to be called vegetation. The films are made by simple colonies living on chemical seepage—colorless skins that spread after rain, feed on dissolved iron and sulfur, and are consumed almost as quickly as they form. From above, the pressers appear to be part of the rock until they contract. Then whole acres seem to shudder.

Among them move needle-legged pickers, blind and jointed, extracting mites and soft eggs from crevices. Dense streams of fist-sized shellbacks push through in opposing directions, climbing over one another rather than deviating from their inherited paths. Larger grazers follow, though there is nothing recognizable for them to graze. They scoop mouthfuls of wet sediment and living bodies alike, sorting neither. Their broad throats filter nutrients while the indigestible mineral slurry pours from slits along their sides.

Predators have no need to stalk. There is nowhere empty enough to hide in, and prey is never absent. They hunt by creating moments of order.

A long-bodied splitter may throw itself sideways through a moving herd, causing everything nearby to surge in the same direction. It waits beneath the resulting pressure, jaws open, until smaller animals are forced into its mouth by the crowd. Circular hunters coil around patches of ground and tighten gradually, turning a thousand separate creatures into a single compressed meal. Tiny predators hunt even larger hosts, descending in veils and stripping the soft material from around joints before being shaken loose.

Above this crawling density is another, more turbulent layer. The dark air is full of wings.

Some are narrow and translucent, beating too quickly to see. Others are great pleated sails held aloft by slow muscular ripples. They do not have feathers. Many have no stable front or back: they turn their ring-shaped bodies in flight and feed through whatever opening faces the wind. Their migrations cross one another at different heights, forming moving ceilings beneath the clouds. When they descend to drink condensation from the backs of terrestrial animals, the plain becomes almost solid with life from bedrock to cloud.

The sound is continuous and enormous.

It is not one roar but thousands of kinds of noise forced together: shell plates knocking, wing membranes cracking, feeding rasps, pressure whistles, mating booms carried through the ground, and the wet percussion of bodies colliding in darkness. Burrowing animals drum against hollow strata. Airborne swarms produce pitches so high they become a sensation in the teeth. The largest migrators call at intervals with notes too low to hear cleanly; pools wrinkle, loose stones creep downhill, and the chest tightens before the call itself seems to arrive.

There is no silence between these sounds. Even after sudden rain, when the winged masses fold themselves against the ground and the great herds become still, the basin continues humming with digestion, circulation, scraping mouthparts, and millions of bodies breathing. Silence would signal not peace but a disturbance vast enough to frighten the entire plain at once.

Because sight is of limited use, much of the ecology is conducted through vibration and heat. Creatures read the landscape through their feet, belly plates, whisker fans, and fluid-filled horns. A distant stampede can be detected hours before it arrives, although determining its direction is difficult; the basin’s buried cavities reflect tremors until they seem to come from everywhere. Warmth is similarly deceptive. A tightly packed colony may resemble a geothermal vent. Predators sometimes imitate the thermal pattern of crowded resting grounds, drawing cold animals close during the deepest part of the night.

Light belongs almost entirely to the fauna. It appears in brief, functional signs rather than steady radiance. Blue pinpricks mark the joints of low scavengers. Pale bands travel down the bodies of airborne rings as they coordinate turns. When a large animal is wounded, organisms in its blood flash white on contact with air, illuminating the feeding mass gathered around it. During certain migrations, the plain becomes visible in fragments: ribs, eye membranes, open throats, and wet channels revealed by living pulses, then lost again.

No individual seems to command the landscape, yet the abundance is not chaotic. The plain follows old cycles.

Rain wakes the mineral films. The films draw the pressers from deep cracks. The pressers attract pickers and filter-feeders. Their movement dislodges eggs laid during the dry interval. Hatchings feed the flying swarms. The swarms attract descending hunters, whose cast-off tissue enriches the pans. As the pools shrink, the animals crowd inward, breed, feed, and consume the water itself in the making of new bodies. Eventually only damp salt remains, bearing the impressions of countless mouths.

Death does not clear space for long. A fallen giant is covered before its final movements stop. Scavengers enter through breathing apertures; smaller species enter through the scavengers. Within hours, the carcass becomes a warm, moving mound. Within days, only mineral braces and hard mouthparts remain. These are gnawed, carried, swallowed, and passed from body to body until they join the pale sediment in the low channels.

Here and there, the density briefly breaks.

There are sinkholes with edges too smooth for gripping, descending into cold cavities where underground rivers can be heard but not seen. Animals crowd around these openings yet avoid falling in, guided by some shared vibration. There are broad plates of glassy volcanic stone that shed water instantly and offer nothing edible. During migrations, these become dark islands amid the flow, though flying creatures rest upon them in trembling multitudes. There are also the Mirror Pans, shallow depressions lined with a metallic mineral that poisons the simple films. Their water remains untouched. Nothing drinks there. Even the winged animals divert around them, leaving rare circles of open sky reflected in perfect black.

The strangest places are the breathing fields.

Across several low tracts, the ground rises and falls by a finger’s width over the course of an hour. The motion is geological: trapped gases moving through layered mudstone. But the fauna treats it as if the land itself were an animal. Vast crowds synchronize their bodies to the swelling earth, flattening when it rises and lifting when it settles. The resulting waves move outward for kilometers. Seen under the weak flashes of airborne life, the whole plain appears to inhale.

There are no paths made by intention, no walls, tools, shelters, or signs that any mind has tried to separate itself from weather and hunger. Yet the basin is full of routes older than many rock faces, nurseries defended across generations, communal rhythms, and agreements enacted without language. Certain narrow crossings are left open for the passage of egg-bearing giants. Certain pools are approached in alternating tides by rival swarms. Predators stop feeding when the deep ground-call passes beneath them. Whether these are instincts, customs, or merely the stable shapes of survival cannot be determined from the landscape itself.

At the farthest visible limits, low escarpments enclose the basin. They are not high, but in this level country they resemble the walls of the world. Rainwater cuts down their faces in silver threads, and pale cave mouths open along their bases. Every few days, one of those mouths darkens further as a new migration pours out.

Then the roar thickens.

The animals already on the plain shift by fractions. Flyers climb into the cloud. Burrowers withdraw below the polished stone. Pools tremble in their basins. Across the old, damp, lightless country, millions of bodies make room for millions more—not by opening an empty space, for there is none, but by changing the shape of the whole.
