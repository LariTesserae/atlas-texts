The high country has been worn almost level.

Not smooth—never smooth—but reduced to a vast, intricate remainder: low shelves of red stone, fields of blunt pillars, shallow basins, and long seams where softer mineral has vanished from between harder bones of rock. Mountains must once have stood here. Their roots now lie open beneath a warm, pale sky, extending from horizon to horizon in terraces scarcely higher than a person. Nothing sharp has survived. Every edge is rounded, every exposed face darkened by age, every pebble nested firmly into the hollow it has occupied for centuries.

From a distance the plateau appears sparsely vegetated and almost empty. There are no forests, no grasslands, no green slopes. A few gray fans of thorn grow where dust collects against stone. Waxy red succulents occupy cracks in groups of three or four. Black lichens spread at the pace of stains. Along the lowest terraces, pale reeds stand widely separated, each stalk surrounded by bare ground.

But the emptiness is deceptive.

Nearly every surface is inhabited.

The plateau’s animals are so numerous that the landscape’s colors belong partly to them. What looks like a field of dun gravel is a congregation of small, hard-backed creatures pressed side by side, their shells patterned exactly like the stones among which they rest. The rounded knobs on distant ledges are larger animals folded into themselves. Pale ribbons in the ravines are thousands of sleeping bodies arranged nose to tail. Even the dark stippling on sunlit rock resolves, at close range, into minute six-legged grazers, each feeding on a separate island of lichen.

They make almost no sound.

The country is warm, and haste wastes water. Most creatures remain still through the day, exposing armored backs to the sun and keeping softer limbs in the damp shadow beneath. Movement comes as an occasional rearrangement: one plated animal uncurls; a hundred nearby bodies make room without visible alarm; the gap closes behind it. A flock of translucent-winged insects rises from a basin, turns once in the light, and settles again ten paces away. The total effect is not silence exactly, but restraint—a continuous faint rasp beneath hearing, the brush of shell on shell, the dry click of a closing joint.

Wind is louder than life here, and the wind is usually weak.

Moisture gathers without ever making the plateau lush. During the night, air rising from lower, hotter lands cools over the stone. Condensation beads along the shaded faces of pillars and under the rims of ledges. Before dawn, the rocks sweat. Droplets creep through grooves polished by uncounted generations of tongues and feeding parts, collecting in holes no wider than an eye.

These tiny reservoirs govern everything.

Around each one lies a compressed ecology of drinkers, hunters, parasites, egg-layers, cleaners, and creatures that live only in the fur or gill folds of others. A basin holding a cup of water may be ringed by ten thousand animals, yet seen from above it resembles only a dark border around a glint. They wait in patient ranks. The smallest drink first from the thin film climbing the stone by capillary action. Larger mouths arrive later, when the sun has warmed the pool and driven some of it into the air. There is little struggle. The order appears older than instinct.

Rain comes occasionally, usually as broad curtains visible for hours before they arrive. It does not fall hard. Large warm drops strike the plateau one by one, darkening the rock in separate circles until the circles join. Shallow channels fill and run for a few minutes. Water passes through old courses incapable of deepening further, their beds polished to a dull luster. Then it disappears into pits, porous shelves, and the bodies of waiting animals.

After rain, the plateau changes color. Dormant membranes open in the stone. Threads of green emerge, flower, and collapse within days. Soft-bodied grazers uncurl from cavities that seemed too small to contain them. Flat creatures as wide as mats slide over wet rock, transparent enough that the mineral grain remains visible through them. The air acquires a mild smell of iron, yeast, and warm shells.

There are rivers, though only in the loosest sense. They occupy long, shallow depressions crossing the tableland, and for most of the year they are chains of separate pools. Each pool is crowded from bottom to surface. Blind swimmers churn slowly beneath rafts of eggs. Long-necked waders stand motionless among them, their narrow bodies indistinguishable from dead reeds. Colonies of breathing sacs cling under overhangs, inflating when shade cools the water and collapsing as noon approaches. No clear patch remains unused.

Where a pool dries entirely, its inhabitants do not leave. They contract. They seal themselves in mud or under one another’s shells, becoming a dense pavement of dormant life. Some remain that way for decades. Their waiting bodies preserve the shape of vanished water.

Scattered across the plateau are structures that resemble ruins, although no tool-making people have lived here. Low walls curve around the deeper basins. Arches bridge narrow cuts. On exposed shelves stand hollow towers with many small chambers, all softened by weather until they might be remnants of an ancient settlement.

They are biological constructions.

Generations of mud-carrying animals raise the walls grain by grain, guided by gradients of heat and moisture rather than plans. The towers are communal nurseries built from saliva, shed shell, clay, and windblown mineral. Some have been abandoned for so long that their builders have changed into other forms, yet later creatures still occupy the chambers. Rain has reduced many towers to knuckled foundations. Others persist because living colonies continually repair them, though the repairs are almost imperceptible: a dark mouth placing one grain, withdrawing, and not returning for an hour.

There are also roads, or what appear to be roads. They run between the water pockets in pale, braided lines, crossing open stone without regard for slope. They are migration paths worn not by feet alone but by the passage of armored bellies over immense spans of time. Some are broader than rivers. A traveler standing in one may see no animal move and still know the road is in use, because every stone upon it has the same polish and all the resting bodies face in one direction.

Migration here is measured in lifetimes. An individual may begin moving toward a distant basin and die before reaching it. Its young emerge already aligned along the route. In this way the population travels, while almost none of its members travels far.

The deeper clefts contain the strangest communities. Warm air descends into them during the day and cools against buried rock. Their walls remain wet, sometimes draped with condensation even when the upper plateau is dry. Vegetation is still sparse—ropes of rootless moss, isolated fans of colorless tissue—but animal life fills the available space. Shells overlap in curtains. Soft colonies pulse in cracks. Pale, eyeless bodies hang from ceilings by filaments and drink droplets before they fall. Predators resemble empty openings in the rock until something brushes their lips.

No chorus rises from these multitudes. Sound attracts attention and spends energy, and in the long quiet of the plateau many lineages have lost it. Courtship is conducted through changes of color too slow to notice, through warmth passed from body to body, through mineral scents laid on old routes. Alarm travels as pressure: one creature tightens, touching its neighbor, and a wave of stillness spreads across a valley. From a height it can be seen as a dulling of the ground.

The largest animals are the easiest to mistake for terrain. They settle in shallow hollows and gather dust until succulents root in the seams of their armor. Their backs form domes among the eroded stone. They may not rise for years. When one does, usually after an exceptional rain, the event is nearly geological. Soil slides from its flanks. Sleeping colonies wake in the sudden light beneath it. The animal extends many short legs and crosses the plateau over several weeks, leaving behind an oval of unweathered rock.

Nothing here seems young, even when newly born. The hatchlings emerge wrinkled, plated, and deliberate. Flowers open already faded. Water arrives carrying the taste of old mineral. Death does not clear space quickly; dry bodies become shelters, then nurseries, then pieces of the ground. Shell accumulates in cracks and is cemented by dew. Over ages it becomes a pale stone unlike the darker bedrock, and erosion begins again upon this newer, living-made layer.

The landscape is crowded almost beyond measure, but its crowding is folded inward. Life occupies undersides, hairline fractures, sealed chambers, the films between grains, the temporary dark under a wing. The open view remains broad and austere: warm terraces under an ordinary sun, sparse thorns, distant rain, low biological towers weathering among the roots of vanished mountains.

Only by staying still does the true density become apparent.

A ledge breathes. A gravel field slowly turns toward shade. The margin of a pool blinks in a thousand places. Far off, what seemed to be a long bar of stone loosens at one end and advances the width of a hand.

By evening, the plateau appears unchanged.

It has changed everywhere.
