The city lies at the bottom of a warm inland sea, in a basin so low and old that all rivers, salts, and fallen things eventually find their way to it.

There is no shore within sight. Above, the water rises as a single black depth, nearly opaque with suspended silt and drifting life. Daylight does not reach the streets. Even at noon the upper sea is only a pressure felt in the body—a remote warmth, a faint downward radiance too weak to give objects color. Here, light belongs to animals.

The city was not built by hands. It has no tools, engines, worked metal, fired brick, glass, hinges, or wheels. Nevertheless, it is unmistakably a city.

Its towers are made from the accumulated chambers of colonial creatures. For thousands of years, soft-bodied masons have secreted pale mineral around themselves, died, and been built over by their descendants. Their branching tenements crowd the basin floor in districts: blunt domes where the currents are strongest, thin spires in sheltered water, vaulted reefs along the old channels. Some structures are hollow from foundation to crown, ventilated by the slow movement of the sea. Others contain labyrinths of thumb-wide cells, brood cavities, feeding courts, and deep communal chambers warm with fermentation.

Roads run between them. They are not paved, but repeatedly traveled: broad grooves pressed into the sediment by generations of heavy migratory animals. At intersections, the mud has been compacted until it is nearly stone. Low arches span these routes where neighboring colonies have grown together. Curtains of translucent tissue hang from the arches and taste whatever passes beneath.

Nothing is square. No line remains straight for long. Yet the place has order.

The basin itself is broad and shallow-sided, ringed by long escarpments worn into terraces. Ancient floods carved the first avenues before the sea completely covered the land. Since then, slower erosion has taken over. Warm currents comb the terraces. Burrowing animals loosen the slopes grain by grain. Mineral-rich seeps cement some surfaces while acids from living mats dissolve others. Corners round, cavities deepen, roofs collapse and become courtyards. Every ruin is quickly occupied. The city has reached the kind of balance in which destruction no longer erases it; collapse is simply one of its methods of growth.

The ground is a dark, fine sediment composed of volcanic dust, shell powder, fecal pellets, pollen brought down from distant water, and the softened remains of innumerable bodies. It rises in low clouds at the slightest disturbance. In undisturbed plazas it lies smooth as ash. Elsewhere it is stippled with vents, tracks, feeding pits, and small round mouths.

Vegetation is scarce. A few pallid ribbons grow where chemical springs seep through cracks in the basin. They have no green in them. They stand knee-high from the mud, leafless and soft, absorbing dissolved minerals through their entire surfaces. Elsewhere, red-brown films coat exposed stone, and sparse thickets of black, threadlike fronds cling to the upper towers where descending nutrients collect. These growths are less a forest than a stain.

The city’s abundance is animal.

Small grazers cover the walls so densely that the architecture seems furred. Blind shrimp move through them in coordinated sheets, polishing one district before vanishing into another. Transparent worms extend crowns of filaments into the current and retract together when a large shadow passes. Shell-backed crawlers gather in sheltered stairlike formations, stacked almost upon one another. Schools of narrow fish pour through the larger passages, each body emitting a dim blue point from beneath its jaw. From a distance they resemble windows lighting one by one.

Larger animals use the city according to routes older than any individual. Broad, slow filter-feeders drift along the principal avenues with their mouths open, stirring warm currents through the side streets. Long-limbed hunters hang motionless beneath archways, indistinguishable from collapsed beams until prey approaches. Armored scavengers descend on fresh carcasses and strip them in near silence. Pale, raylike creatures rest in public squares, half buried, breathing through paired openings that pulse like doors.

There are citizens only in the loosest sense. No creature understands the whole. The colonies build because growth is what they do. The migratory herds clear roads because they return along the scents of their ancestors. Cleaner animals establish crowded stations at natural bottlenecks. Predators learn those stations and wait nearby. Brood chambers form around warm vents. Waste drifts downhill into cultivated beds of bacteria, which feed the grazers, which feed almost everything else. Organization arises without law, planning, or speech.

The closest thing to governance is chemistry.

Each district has its own dissolved signature. Invisible plumes mark nurseries, hunting grounds, diseased quarters, spawning chambers, and places recently disturbed. Animals cross these borders and change behavior at once. Some become docile. Some turn back. Some begin to flash. A tower under attack releases a bitter compound, and neighboring colonies contract their openings in sequence, shutting down whole blocks as if doors were being barred.

The water is warm, almost body-warm near the deepest fissures. Heat rises from beneath the basin through a network of old faults. It does not erupt violently; the geology has long since settled into steady exhalation. Clear water wells from cracks, carrying iron, sulfur, and dissolved stone. Around each spring, bacterial veils billow like smoke. The warmth sustains the city, but it also consumes oxygen. Life crowds wherever faint currents replenish it, making the habitable passages dense and the stagnant interiors nearly empty.

Pressure is constant, intimate. The sea occupies every room and body cavity. Nothing falls freely here. A loosened fragment drifts, turns, and settles with ceremonial slowness. A dead animal may remain suspended for hours while scavengers dismantle it from below. Dust does not descend so much as negotiate its way downward.

Sound is rare and close.

There is no surf, no wind, no rain against roofs. The city’s loudest continuous noise is a low vibration transmitted through stone from the warm springs and the distant shifting of the basin. It is more easily felt along a jaw or shell than heard. Near the towers come tiny noises: the sandpaper rasp of grazers; the click of shells closing; brief cracks when mineral branches give way; the wet thump of a large fin displacing water in a narrow street. From somewhere inside the walls, colonies sometimes contract in waves, producing a sequence of soft knocks that travels block after block and fades.

Most predators make no sound. Their approach is announced by absence. The shrimp stop moving. Feeding crowns vanish. The points of blue light go out, and an entire avenue becomes black.

Darkness is not merely a lack here; it is the city’s common medium. Light is costly and therefore meaningful. A green pulse near the ground warns of poison. Blue flickers call a school into alignment. Amber glows mark gravid bodies. Some creatures cast decoy lights into empty passages while they flee unlit in the opposite direction. During mass spawning, towers that have been black for years kindle from within as millions of larvae emerge, each bearing a cold white spark. For several minutes the streets resemble constellations seen through fog. Then mouths open everywhere, and the stars are eaten.

Activity comes in tides, though no surface tide reaches the basin directly. Distant weather presses on the sea. Seasonal inflows alter salinity. The warm vents strengthen and weaken. In response, the city breathes. Curtains of flesh open. Schools arrive. Hunters follow. Whole districts feed, spawn, empty, and go dormant. A current may turn overnight and transform a thriving quarter into a suffocating dead zone. Months later another route opens through a collapsed tower, water returns, and the walls flower with animals again.

Beyond the central structures, the city thins into suburbs of isolated mounds and buried foundations. Travel grooves continue across open mud toward the terraced rim. There, old channels climb into darkness, flanked by eroded pillars that may once have been denser colonies. Farther still are only low ridges, mineral seeps, and scattered animals moving between faint chemical landmarks.

Yet from the rim, looking down with senses adapted to almost no light, the settlement has a shape. Warm plumes rise from its center. Living towers interrupt the slow current. Migrating bodies circulate through its arteries. Brief signals pass from quarter to quarter. Ruins become nurseries; nurseries harden into walls; walls are bored through and inhabited; inhabited chambers fail and sink into the mud.

Nothing here was invented.

Nothing here is empty.

And although no one built the city, the city continues to build itself.
