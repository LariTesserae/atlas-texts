The plain lies low enough that water is never absent, only hidden.

It rests beneath a continuous black canopy, though canopy is too simple a word. There are no recognizable trunks and crowns, no open soil, no separate plants. Vegetation is the terrain itself: a continental depth of braided fibers, pressure-swollen chambers, root lattices, leaf plates, and dark, load-bearing tissues. Where the surface appears firm, it is merely an old green-black rind stretched across buoyant growth. Press a weight upon it and water would rise in perfect beads from microscopic pores—but there are no animals here, and nothing presses.

The land is warm without being hot. Its warmth has the steadiness of deep machinery, held at the same narrow temperature everywhere. Moisture condenses continually on the undersides of leaves and on the high, smooth ridges of living material. Droplets hang there without falling. Pools occupy the shallow places, ink-dark and untroubled, their surfaces crowded by films of tiny interlocked fronds. Beneath them, clear water penetrates for immeasurable distances through vascular canals.

Nothing flows in the ordinary sense. There is no visible river, no rain, no runoff cutting channels toward a sea. Water is lifted, divided, filtered, and returned through the plain by capillary forests. It enters pale root-membranes below, rises through columns of tense liquid, condenses in vast internal caverns, and is drawn down again. The cycle is complete and almost perfectly still. Even evaporation seems less like motion than a property the air has always possessed.

The atmosphere is nearly saturated. It has weight. A faint warm mist occupies every gap between surfaces, but there is no wind to gather or disperse it. Visibility ends not because the fog moves in banks, but because distance itself accumulates moisture and darkness.

There is no sky.

Far overhead, the plant-mass has roofed the world in overlapping sheets. Some are thin enough to show faint internal veins; others are kilometers thick, buoyed by sealed reservoirs of gas. They meet and intergrow until they form a ceiling that extends beyond any possible horizon. Only traces of light penetrate it: a dull red seep, the color of blood seen through closed eyelids, and rarer lines of cold green where buried tissues conduct energy across the roof. These lines do not flicker. They remain fixed, branching over the darkness like diagrams of lightning that occurred once and never ended.

The plain has the scale of a planet, but not the openness of one. It is densely partitioned. Curtains of filament descend from the ceiling and merge with the floor. Towers rise from the marsh, yet they are not constructed: they are extrusions grown by pressure, sharp-edged and newly skinned, ribbed with parallel ducts. Broad horizontal organs bridge the spaces between them. Bulbous vats hang beneath these bridges, full of translucent sap stratified into unmoving layers. In other places, walls of stacked leaf-blades stand nearly touching, making corridors thousands of kilometers long and scarcely wider than a canyon.

Every surface performs some industrial function.

One district fixes dissolved minerals into hard green ceramic. Another cracks water using buried chemical gradients, storing the products in wax-lined bladders. Fields of stiff black laminae absorb the faint geothermal glow that passes upward through the wet substrate. Great vascular trunks carry sugars and salts under pressure. Root bundles separate metals atom by atom and deposit them as conductive threads. Vast nodules, warm and enzyme-rich, assemble complex compounds in repeating sequences. There are refineries without furnaces, transmission lines without cables, pumps without moving parts, factories whose walls and products are the same continuous organism.

Yet nothing here was built.

There are no joints, fasteners, cut surfaces, foundations, ruins, roads, tools, or signs that a hand ever distinguished useful matter from useless matter. The apparent machinery is entirely botanical. What resembles a turbine is a spiral fruit-body held forever at the verge of opening. What resembles a bank of cylinders is a colony of pressure organs sharing one root skin. The long silver rails crossing the marsh are exposed veins, lacquered with conductive resin. They carry minute electrochemical potentials across continental distances, but no current rises or falls. The system holds itself in equilibrium.

Its industry is not production toward an end. It is maintenance elevated to geology.

The land looks young. Its ridges are knife-clean, its folds unsoftened, its vertical skins free of scars. Pale growth-seams mark where immense sheets once divided, leaving edges as crisp as freshly split stems. Needle-shaped shoots pierce the pools and stop at identical heights. There is no accumulated litter. Nothing decays visibly. No old leaves curl underfoot; no fallen timber sinks into mud. Waste is taken back through the same membranes that release it. The entire world appears to have emerged only moments ago, wet and sharp from its enclosing husk.

But that moment does not pass.

Droplets remain pendant on weeping rock-like growths. The growths themselves never lengthen. Chemical pulses stand arrested in luminous veins. A ring of ripening tissue holds one shade between green and black. No blossom opens; no seed falls. The place is neither dead nor dormant. It is fully active at every scale, but all processes balance so exactly that activity produces no change.

This stillness gives the landscape its deepest strangeness. Warmth persists without weather. Moisture circulates without currents. Plants metabolize without growth. Energy is transmitted without loss or gain. The world is young without having a past and industrial without making anything new.

There is no animal life—not even insects, mites, worms, or microscopic grazers. No wings disturb the saturated air. No feet test the floating mats. No mouth has ever bitten a leaf, so the vegetation possesses no thorns for defense, no bitter warning pigments, no flowers designed to be noticed. Its colors are inward colors: bruised violet, chlorophyll black, waxy gray, the dim amber of sealed fluids. Pollination, if the concept applies, occurs through direct fusion of neighboring tissues. Seeds are unnecessary where every surface already touches another living surface.

The silence is nearly absolute.

A listener might eventually perceive sounds, but none arrive as events. There is a minute tensile hum within the conductive veins, too low to locate. Occasionally a membrane settles by less than the thickness of a cell and gives a soft, damp tick. Water under pressure whispers through capillaries, though the whisper is so uniform that it becomes indistinguishable from silence. There are no splashes. The suspended droplets do not fall. There is no rustle because there is no wind, and no creak because nothing bends.

Across unimaginable distances, this same equilibrium continues. Marsh floors rise into vascular plateaus only a few hundred meters high, then descend through terraces of slick, newly divided tissue. Inland seas appear, shallow and roofed, their dark waters crossed by floating meshes that partition them into hexagonal basins. Beyond them stand forests of hollow columns, each wide enough to contain its own climate, though every internal climate is the same warm, wet darkness. Farther still, the ceiling descends until it nearly touches the floor, compressing the world into horizontal pores through which only mist and fine root hairs could pass.

There may be no true ground beneath any of it. The living plain could be thousands of kilometers deep. Its lowest layers may be under such pressure that cellulose becomes stone-like, then metallic, while remaining technically alive. The “planet” may be a single vegetative body wrapped around a warm ocean, with no mineral surface except what its roots have manufactured. Or it may be one tissue among many, filling a cavity too large for its boundaries to matter.

There are indications of purpose everywhere, and none of intention.

Parallel grooves converge on reservoirs. Conductive strands branch according to strict ratios. Identical organs repeat at distances too large to survey. In the darkness, the whole landscape feels governed by a plan, but the plan may be nothing more than the geometry required to remain unchanged. Every chamber compensates for every other chamber. Every absorbed molecule is released elsewhere. Every pressure has an answering pressure.

If part of the plain were cut, perhaps the entire continent would respond. But nothing cuts it.

If a light appeared, perhaps hidden leaves would turn toward it. But no light changes.

If a sound were made, it might travel for years through the taut water-veins, ringing silently through the rooted factories of the dark. But no sound is made.

The place continues: wet, warm, enormous, freshly formed, and older than duration—an untouched industrial wilderness whose only product is its own exact persistence.
