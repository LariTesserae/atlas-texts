### The Condensary

The city occupies a blister beneath the desert, a single vaulted cavity no wider than a few streets laid side by side. It is small enough that every resident knows its boundaries: the hot northern wall, the cistern steps in the west, the sealed excavation face, the low southern galleries where adults must stoop. Yet almost none of the original cavern remains visible. Buildings, bridges, pipes, gardens, ducts, cranes, and retaining frames fill it from floor to ceiling. There are no open squares. Even the air seems allotted.

The cavity is young. It opened only a few generations ago when subterranean heat split a bed of black stone away from the strata above it. The fracture surfaces are still sharp, oily-looking, and crowded with pale mineral whiskers. No old river rounded these walls. No long weathering softened the ledges. Where the city has not covered them, slabs jut like freshly broken glass.

Below the lowest inhabited platforms, the crack continues into heat.

The Condensary is always hot. Iron railings sting the palm. Wax slumps in storage jars. People sleep in woven hammocks because floors retain too much heat, and they wrap themselves not for warmth but to keep sweat from evaporating too quickly. The atmosphere smells of wet masonry, machine oil, bruised leaves, copper salts, and the faint sulfurous breath coming through the lower grilles.

There is little standing water. Moisture arrives instead as a daily occupation of surfaces.

Warm vapor rises from the deep fracture and meets cooler stone near the roof. Condensation beads on cables, bridge undersides, ceramic eaves, leaves, and the ribs of the great cooling vanes. At certain hours the whole city begins to drip. The drops are slow and heavy. They strike stretched cloth, run down chains, and gather in narrow gutters that braid through walls and workshops. Every sill is shaped to catch them. Every stair has a lip. Even statues have channels cut behind their ears and beneath their tongues.

Rain sometimes enters from above, though no one can see the sky. During rare storms, water finds the original fault and descends through the settlement in dozens of temporary threads. The inhabitants call this the High Leakage. Doors are opened to it. Dusty bowls appear on balconies. Children press their faces against cold trickles seeping from the ceiling. For a few hours, turbines turn faster and public cisterns deepen by a handspan. Then the rain passes, and the city returns to collecting itself drop by drop.

Vegetation thrives wherever condensation persists. It is not a wilderness so much as a second, living architecture packed into the first.

Glossy vines sheath support columns. Ferns grow from warm brick joints. Root mats have been trained beneath walkways to filter runoff, hanging in dark curtains over workshops and sleeping alcoves. Broad-leaved plants crowd the upper galleries, where they catch moisture before it can evaporate. Their leaves are almost black, with silver undersides that show when the ventilation changes. Pale moss fills letters on old signs until official notices appear written in green relief.

Every available niche is cultivated. There are medicinal molds in locked cabinets, edible stalks spiraling around drainpipes, peppery ground-cover in the boiler courts, and small fruiting shrubs grafted directly into porous wallstone. The city’s gardens have no soil beds. They inhabit baskets, cracks, suspended troughs, broken machines, and the hollow ceramic bodies of retired pumps.

Some plants have been engineered—or patiently selected—to perform civic functions. The red bladder vine swells when a steam line leaks. Fanleaf folds itself hours before an influx of cooler air. Dense mats of whisper grass are planted around factories because their fibers absorb vibration. As a result, the city’s industry is curiously quiet.

Machines are everywhere, but few move quickly.

Piston pumps rise and fall with the deliberation of breathing animals. Chain lifts carry baskets between levels at walking pace. Cooling drums revolve so slowly that their rotation is noticed only by comparing the rust marks over several days. Workshops use pressure, heat, and patient repetition rather than violent force. Ceramic presses close over the course of an hour. Mineral baths plate copper onto tools overnight. Huge flywheels, half-hidden by vines, store energy from the intermittent falls of water and release it in steady increments.

Nothing here clatters unless it is failing.

The usual sounds are drips, the dry tick of cooling metal, cloth brushing leaves, and the distant exhalation of pressure valves. Voices are kept low, partly by custom and partly because the packed surfaces make louder speech return from too many directions. A shout can seem to come from every wall. The inhabitants find this indecent. Markets conduct business in murmurs and finger signs. Factory shifts are announced by lamps dimming in sequence rather than bells.

Light comes from shielded electric filaments, fungal panels, and narrow blue lamps mounted under bridges. None is bright. Strong illumination encourages evaporation and bleaches useful molds, so public light is directed downward and enclosed behind green or amber glass. The upper foliage dissolves into darkness. Suspended buildings can be identified only by constellations of windows and occasional wet gleams along their gutters.

The city has been built in layers without room for orderly expansion. Houses sit on workshop roofs; workshops hang beneath cisterns; cisterns are braced against old housing blocks. Ladders pass through kitchens. Public passages narrow around private additions, then widen inside abandoned pressure vessels. Addresses include height, pipe color, and nearest living landmark: *Third tier, beside the sleeping wheel, under the forked fig.*

Walls are made from black fracture stone, fired clay, riveted copper, glass slag, compressed fungal fiber, and a pale cement grown by mineral-feeding bacteria. New construction looks raw and angular. Because the cavern itself is recent and the city has expanded quickly, there are few ruins in the ordinary sense. Abandoned places have not had time to decay. They remain sharply intact behind later structures, sealed rooms with tools still arranged on hooks, their entrances simply built over when circulation changed.

The oldest district is only sixty years old, but it lies under so many additions that people speak of it as the Deep Past.

Animals are uncommon. The city has no flocks and almost no vermin. Heat, restricted food, and the cultivated molds discourage most creatures. Tiny glass-winged gnats appear during the High Leakage, live for a few days, and vanish. Blind threadfish sometimes emerge in cistern filters after heavy rain; finding one is considered both contamination and blessing. They are transferred to a covered pool in the western works, where perhaps twenty survive.

A single kind of lizard inhabits the hottest pipes. It is small, white, and nearly motionless. Its skin slowly darkens in the presence of poisonous gases, so workers tolerate it. Many factories keep a designated wall niche for one, though most niches are empty.

The strangest life is harder to classify. Beneath the city, along the hottest edge of the fracture, translucent columns grow from mineral seepage. They resemble roots, glass organs, or buildings seen at a great distance. Over years they develop internal chambers. Warm water moves upward through them without pumps. When connected to the municipal pipes, they improve pressure but alter the taste of the water and sometimes produce condensation in impossible places: inside sealed jars, under sleeping tongues, between the pages of ledgers.

The engineers insist the columns are geological. Gardeners insist they are alive. The city’s laws classify them as infrastructure.

Growth is slow here, but relentless. A bridge shifts half a finger’s width in a decade. A root curtain closes a disused alley. Mineral crust gradually narrows a pipe until it begins to sing. Families inherit valve schedules and pruning obligations. Maps are revised not because streets change suddenly, but because walls thicken, gardens sag, and buildings settle toward the heat.

The Condensary does not feel active, despite being full. Its packed machinery and vegetation seem held in a prolonged, humid pause. Nothing has room to rush. Water gathers before falling. Wheels store force before turning. Plants spend years finding the next crack. The stone itself continues to separate by increments too small to hear.

Everyone knows the cavity is still opening.

Measurements are taken at the sealed eastern face, where a black seam runs from floor to roof. Once each year, surveyors drive silver pins into the rock and stretch fine wire between them. Each year the distance grows.

Not much—sometimes the width of a leaf vein.

But beyond that seam, instruments detect cooler air, mineral moisture, and a hollow return when the stone is tapped. The city may someday gain another chamber. It may take ten years, or a hundred. No one proposes blasting through. Sudden openings are distrusted here. The Condensary was founded by a fracture, but it has survived by making every other change slowly.

So the eastern wall remains bare except for measuring wires and a dense border of moisture-loving plants. Residents visit it in the dim hours between shifts. They put their hands against the young, hot stone and wait.

Occasionally, from somewhere on the other side, a drop of water falls.
