The place occupies a shallow basin no larger than a compact town, though nearly every part of it has been built upon. It lies just above the level of the surrounding marsh. A concrete floodwall marks its boundary: a low, unbroken ring stained black at the waterline and pale with mineral salts above it. Beyond the wall there is only flat reedless water, mud, and a dim horizon dissolved in heat.

Inside is a district of pumps, workshops, storage yards, workers’ blocks, and narrow canals. There are few streets in the ordinary sense. Most routes are poured-concrete ledges running beside water channels, or steel walkways threaded between buildings. Pipes cross overhead in orderly bundles. They turn corners on square braces, dip beneath loading cranes, and enter walls through collars of tarred cloth. Everything is close enough to walk across in half an hour, yet the ducts and partitions make it feel larger—a small place repeatedly divided.

The ground is new. Much of it is hydraulic fill: gray sand, crushed shell, clinker, and dredged clay compacted into angular banks. Where the fill remains exposed, its cuts are sharp and undecayed. Rain has made only shallow grooves. Rusted reinforcement mesh protrudes from a few unfinished embankments, and fresh slabs meet the mud with abrupt, geometric edges. There are no old gullies, rounded stones, or deep soils. Even the natural ground appears recently uncovered, a dark blue-black clay that holds the squared impressions of boots and tires without collapsing.

Water is everywhere without dominating the view. It occupies trenches beneath grates, rectangular settling ponds, flooded cable vaults, roof gutters, and the narrow gaps between foundations. It beads on pipes and condenses on the undersides of catwalks. Walls weep continuously through seams in the concrete. A person resting a hand against almost any shaded surface comes away wet.

The water level never seems to rise or fall. Pumps draw from the canals at exactly the rate that seepage, condensation, and runoff replenish them. Overflow lips carry thin, permanent sheets from one basin to the next. Every drain has a steady throat of water turning inside it, neither swallowing faster nor backing up. The marsh outside presses inward; the machinery presses outward. The town exists at the balanced line between the two.

It is intensely hot. The heat comes from the low latitude, from the wet air, and from the industry itself. Boiler houses and motor rooms radiate through their walls. Buried steam lines warm strips of pavement. Even in the dimmer passages, the air has the close, used quality of a laundry or engine compartment. Sweat does not evaporate. Metal railings feel warm, rainwater is tepid, and the mud beneath the floodwall holds heat like flesh.

Yet the place is not bright. A ceiling of vapor and industrial haze spreads the daylight into a weak, colorless glow. Deep eaves cover most walkways. Steam gathers between the taller sheds, turning their roofs into blurred silhouettes. Electric lamps burn at all hours: amber globes above doors, white tubes in service tunnels, red indicators on control cabinets. Their light shines in wet concrete and black water but travels only a short distance.

Buildings are practical and densely layered, though open yards and water basins leave broad intervals between them. There is a squat turbine hall with ribbed walls, three cylindrical tanks, a row of identical apartment blocks, a machine shop, a cold store, and a pumping station whose intake screens face the outer marsh. No building is truly tall. The largest structures achieve their grandeur through interior volume: high trusses, hanging chains, crane rails, and shadowed roof spaces like industrial naves.

Vegetation survives in scattered, stubborn patches. Wiregrass grows from cracks beyond the reach of foot traffic. A few sedges fringe the quieter settling tanks. Algae make green-black skins on shaded water, and pale lichens mark the upper face of the floodwall. There are three planted trees in the central yard, each confined to a square concrete bed and pruned away from the cables. Their leaves hang dusty and still. No wilderness is reclaiming the district; roots, silt, and shoots are removed as part of routine maintenance.

Animals are far more numerous than plants. Mosquitoes occupy the moist air in fine clouds. Small frogs cling to drain walls and the legs of storage tanks. Geckos gather around lamps, where moths and midges arrive continuously. Rats use the pipe racks as elevated roads. Pigeons crowd the turbine-hall trusses, and dark swifts circle under the vapor ceiling, feeding without rest.

The canals contain finger-length fish, translucent shrimp, snails, and heavy-bodied carp that turn beneath the oily reflections. Crabs inhabit the culverts and emerge onto the warm concrete shelves. None of these creatures seem intrusive. They are part of the operating environment, as established as the valves and grates. Intake screens are cleared of weed, plastic, fish scales, and shells; refuse from the clearing feeds birds and rats; frogs feed on the insects bred in the drain sumps. The ecology loops tightly around the industry.

The sound is immense and almost uniform. Pumps produce the lowest note, a pressure felt through soles and teeth. Fans add a broad metallic roar. Belts tick at their joins. Water falls behind walls. Transformers hum in locked cages, and loose sheets of roofing vibrate with a hard, insectlike buzz. Animal calls fit into the gaps: frogs knocking from the culverts, pigeons clattering under the roofs, gulls crying beyond the wall.

There are no true quiet periods. Machinery does not start up or shut down; it is simply running. Warning lights do not flash so much as maintain their red glow. The workers change, but the work does not appear to. One figure at a gauge is replaced by another. Handcarts travel the same painted routes. The same wet footprints are renewed on the same floors. Maintenance here is less an interruption than a continuous condition.

Nothing visibly ages. Rust forms, but is scraped and painted at the same pace. Silt enters, but dredges remove it. Animals are born and eaten, yet their numbers remain constant. Concrete cracks are sealed before they widen. The town does not expand into the marsh, nor does the marsh reclaim it. Even the haze holds at the same thickness, and the water on the outer wall rests against the same black line.

The chief rule of the place is balance enforced by labor and machinery. Every leak has a drain. Every drain has a pump. Every pump has a motor, every motor a cable, every cable a dry conduit raised just above the wet ground. The system is not pristine, but it is complete. It has the sealed, self-maintaining character of a terrarium assembled from concrete, hot water, animals, and noise.

At the center stands a small open square around a circular inspection well. This is the nearest thing the town has to an empty place. The paving is wet and unadorned. Apartment windows, ducts, and workshop doors face it from all sides. Beneath the iron cover, water can be heard moving through the foundations in a constant, muscular rush.

Above it, three sparse trees hold their leaves in the dim heat. Around them, the town vibrates without advancing, a young landscape kept forever at the instant of its completion.
