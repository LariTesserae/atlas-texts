The people who live there are not, at first glance, greatly altered by the height.

They are broad-handed, damp-haired, wrapped in layered wool and oilskin. Their faces tend toward pallor from the absence of direct sun, though furnace workers carry a permanent ruddy darkness around the eyes and mouth. Copper buckles green against their clothes. Iron dust settles into the whorls of their fingerprints and cannot be scrubbed entirely away. Most wear small bells at the throat or wrist—not decorative bells, but tuned ones, each indicating a district, trade, or household. In fog, identity begins with sound.

They walk with one hand free.

This is the first habit children learn after standing: one hand for the rail, wall, ladder, cable, emergency hook, or stranger passing in the opposite direction. Even in broad rooms, people seldom burden both arms. Loads are slung across the back, dragged on little wheeled frames, or entrusted to the city’s innumerable hooks and overhead carriers. To see someone crossing a plaza with both hands occupied is to know them as a visitor, a fool, or a person in love.

Their bodies acquire the mountain’s asymmetries. One leg may grow stronger from a childhood spent on stairs pitched always in the same direction. Shoulders develop according to the angle of local ladders. Those raised near piston fields possess an unconscious sway timed to the slow impacts below. People from the eastern shelves pause every nine breaths, anticipating a pump stroke that cannot be heard elsewhere. Removed to the low country, many suffer sleeplessness. Silence seems to them less an absence than an approaching failure.

They call themselves by many district names and only rarely by the name of the whole city. A person belongs first to a shelf, then to a bell range, then to a pressure system, then perhaps to a family. Whole neighborhoods are identified by the taste of their condensate: copper-sweet, smoky, chalked, bitter with pale minerals. Marriages between distant ledges are spoken of as crossings, even when the neighborhoods stand in sight of one another.

Families are assembled as much by route as by blood. A household may include those who share the same reliable way home: valve keepers, cable conductors, kitchen workers, a child whose original stair vanished, two widowers from a district reachable only during rain. If a person can consistently arrive at a supper table, a bed is eventually found for them. If they cease to arrive, their place is not immediately cleared. Distance is given time to correct itself.

A chair may remain set for years.

Children are numerous in the warmer works and rare on the exposed shelves. They know the city first as a sequence of vibrations conducted through floors. Before they can read letters, they learn the warning codes struck on pipework: three quick blows for steam, two widely spaced for shifting stone, a continuous scrape for an uncertain stair. They play games in which one child is a surveyor and the others are districts that must move whenever they are named. They build little houses with doors on every side, then wait to see whether anything enters through the door facing nowhere.

Sometimes something does.

No one makes much of this unless it stays for breakfast.

The oldest inhabitants have the calm of people who have outlived several versions of their surroundings. They remember when particular walls were open air, when a market stood above rather than below a boiler house, when the shortest route to the western orchards passed through a room now sealed inside the archive. Their memories are treated as useful but seasonally unreliable. Municipal hearings invite elders to testify alongside gauges, birds, mineral seams, and dripping water. Contradiction is expected. A grandmother may be correct that a station lies downhill, while the transit authority is equally correct that it lies up.

Many older people carry elevation books in which they have recorded not where they lived but when. Entries read:

*Third blue-water year: above the lower furnaces.*  
*Winter of the double echo: level with the moth lamps.*  
*After North Bell deepened: beneath our own cellar.*

Age is not counted solely in years. It is also counted in relocations, pressure changes, and bells heard acquire a lower note.

The city’s most respected workers are not its rulers or richest manufacturers, but those who maintain continuities.

Rail fitters go out before dawn-dark with lengths of warm track, extending lines toward stations that have shifted overnight. They travel in crews of five: two to measure, two to fasten, and one to remember the way back. That last worker carries no tools. The returner watches changes in fog, listens for altered echoes, and ties chalked cords at intervals that make sense only from the homeward direction.

Doorwrights install the unnecessary doors required by law. They are part carpenter, part negotiator. Before hanging a door onto open air, a doorwright knocks on the empty frame and waits. The waiting may last minutes or an entire shift. If an answer comes, the door is hinged to open toward it. Doorwrights develop excellent manners and a reluctance to enter rooms first.

There are gravity assessors who suspend chains, bowls of water, plumb weights, sleeping moths, and occasionally themselves from new structures. They do not ask merely which way is down. They ask how many downs are present, which is strongest, and whether any appear temporary. Their reports are written in cautious language:

*Primary descent probable through late winter.*  
*Minor lateral falling observed near north wall.*  
*Ceiling not to be trusted as ceiling during rainfall.*

Echo clerks sit in tiled offices with one ear against brass listening cones. They record sounds returning through the rock long after their causes. Most are ordinary industrial echoes: hammer strikes, blasting caps, dropped tools, the groan of shelf movement. Others have no recognized origin, or arrive before the corresponding noise is made. Advance echoes are stamped in red and distributed to the relevant works. A foreman may receive notice that, in forty-three minutes, someone in his mill will drop a large wrench. Sensible foremen then clear the floor. Less sensible ones spend forty-three minutes trying to prevent the wrench from falling and thereby ensure that it does.

Bell tuners occupy a position near the sacred, though the city insists they are municipal technicians. They climb into towers with forks, hammers, mineral wedges, and sheets of figures. Their task is to distinguish the mountain’s rising tones from machinery, weather, herds, and the accumulated resonance of the city itself. A tuner may spend months listening to a note too deep for ordinary hearing. Their teeth loosen early. Their dreams are said to occur at great depth.

When a new ridge begins, it is the tuners who decide which bell must answer.

The city has officials, but government there resembles maintenance. Councillors are chosen from pressure wards whose boundaries alter with the utilities. Sessions begin by confirming that the council chamber can still be reached by all represented districts. If not, the absent districts are not presumed unrepresented; instead, the chamber is presumed misplaced.

Laws are written provisionally. Property is defined by access rather than perimeter. A room belongs to whoever can reach it by the registered door, though appeals may be made by chimney, lift shaft, or seasonal corridor. Theft is difficult to prove when objects may travel independently along incomplete contours, so intent matters more than possession. Returning a lost item is considered courteous even if the item has not yet been lost.

Surveyors are admired, teased, and frequently widowed.

They wear long coats marked with reflective lines that seem to continue beyond their bodies. Their instruments include conventional levels and chains, but also jars of local rain, caged cliff birds, tuning needles, weighted smoke, and notebooks bound so pages can be added at both ends. They travel beyond the lamps when new stone gathers. Some return within hours, gray with fatigue and carrying samples still warm at the center. Some return decades later without having aged correspondingly. Some are seen working on distant ridges and cannot be reached, though they wave when bells are rung.

Every survey team leaves a place blank on its roster for the member who may join retrospectively. Occasionally the blank is filled by a person everyone remembers differently.

The inhabitants do not regard such people with fear. The city has too much practical work for metaphysical suspicion. A retrospectively arrived worker is issued back wages, charged for prior lodging, and required to attend safety instruction. The archive opens a file, using paper that has sometimes already yellowed.

There are many kinds of faith, but most acknowledge the dignity of unfinished things.

Some worship the rising stone as a vast being not yet awake. They hold services in chambers where mineral seams cross empty space, singing only the beginnings of hymns. Others believe the mountain is not being born but remembered, that each new ridge is an ancient form returning from a world where it already eroded away. Furnace congregations teach that making and wearing down are one motion viewed from different elevations.

A common household observance is simpler. Before a new room is occupied, a bowl of water is left overnight in the center. In the morning, the family examines what the water has done. If it remains level, the room is considered stubborn but safe. If it climbs one side of the bowl, a blue lamp is installed. If it disappears, someone checks the rooms above. If the bowl contains more water than before, a chair is set out for a guest.

The dead are not buried in the young rock if it can be avoided. The mountain grips unpredictably, and graves have been known to rise into kitchens or emerge unopened on distant ledges. Nor are bodies generally burned; fuel is valued, and furnace drafts sometimes carry ash into districts that have not yet been built.

Instead, the dead are placed in sediment houses along the lower terraces. There they lie beneath layers of brick dust, filings, moss, fruit pits, cable fiber, moth scales, and soil carried up deliberately from the low country. Families add a handful each year. The hope is not preservation. It is that the dead may participate in the first true earth the mountain possesses.

Names are struck onto thin plates and hung in chain curtains where the wind can sound them. The dead therefore continue as a faint metallic weather around the city’s margins.

Not all inhabitants are human, and the distinction becomes less useful with altitude.

The black-winged birds are accepted as nuisances, predictors, and occasionally witnesses. Certain colonies can imitate not only sirens but district bells, shift whistles, and the private chimes worn by individuals. A bird may call a worker’s name in the voice of a spouse from across a chasm. People learn to look at the bird’s feet: if it grips the metal, it is only mimicry; if it stands slightly above the perch, the call may deserve an answer.

The pipe goats belong to no one, though crews paint symbols on their flanks to indicate preferred routes. Their hooves cling to iron even upside down, and they graze mineral films from pipes without damaging insulation. Old goats know abandoned utility lines better than any map. During outages, repair crews follow them. They are rewarded with salt bricks and strips of orchard bark.

The translucent tunnel salamanders are handled with elaborate gentleness. Their organs pulse visibly, often in time with machinery. A sudden migration through a valve house can halt an entire pressure system, not because the animals are protected by law, though they are, but because they invariably flee before a structural change. Children keep no salamanders as pets. To enclose one is believed to confuse its warning.

The furred moths are nearly domesticated. They nest in coat linings, warm instrument boxes, bell towers, and the hair of sleepers. Their wings gather metallic dust into delicate maps. Engineers sometimes press a moth onto black paper before releasing it, reading the pattern as an indication of air currents through inaccessible spaces. Mothkeepers breed lines sensitive to steam leaks, mineral gases, and doors that may soon open elsewhere.

The silver insects over the chasms are less understood. They appear to stand on invisible ground, clustering along slopes no eye can see. Surveyors observe them before planning bridges. Where the insects rise, a future ridge may be forming. Where they suddenly fall upward, all work stops.

Then there are the great cloud herds.

The inhabitants speak of them as if they were weather with temperaments. Individual animals are named only when they return often enough to affect a district. There is one called Old Reservoir, a dark-backed female whose footprints remain as suspended pools for days. Another, Ash Brow, passes through buildings and leaves their walls aged, paint peeling, hinges softened by centuries that vanish after its departure. Calves are almost never seen, though on certain nights a small shape can be heard calling from an elevation higher than the mountains.

Herd watchers live in narrow towers beyond the terraces. They chart routes no pedestrian can follow and hang chains of water bowls to catch the rain gathered beneath pausing beasts. They claim the herds graze on slopes still becoming possible. When a herd changes course, architects revise foundation plans.

Some people spend so long near them that their outlines become uncertain in fog. These watchers may step across gaps without appearing to notice. Their families tie bells to their clothes so they remain attached to the audible city.

There are also residents with no agreed origin.

A boiler tender may work for years beside a woman who always seems wet from recent rain, though she lives in a district where rain never reaches. A stationmaster may have basalt grains beneath his eyelids and know the arrival time of trains not yet commissioned. In the highest dwellings, some infants are born with pale lines visible under the skin, continuing a mineral seam through the room when held in the correct position.

Such children are not taken away or proclaimed. They are given warm clothes, inoculations, a district bell, and a name. As they grow, they may become excellent masons. They often know where a wall wants a door.

The city’s hospitality is cautious but expansive. Visitors from the low country are recognizable by their fast speech, their dependence on sight, and their habit of asking how far away things are. Locals answer with time, weather, and required transfers.

“Two bells and a lift.”

“Nearer in rain.”

“Far by foot, but the western station sometimes has it behind the parcels office.”

Newcomers are advised never to follow a familiar voice through fog unless they can hear its bell, never to step where only the silver insects stand, and never to sleep in a room with an even number of doors. They are also told that if a staircase has the same number of steps in both directions, they should leave it alone. Most assume this is a joke until they encounter one guarded by municipal barriers.

Despite the machinery, the people do not think of themselves as conquerors of the mountain. Conquest would require the mountain to be complete enough to oppose them. Nor do they think they are merely surviving. They are assisting, though opinions differ on what they assist: the emergence of land, the arrival of future districts, the education of gravity, or the slow introduction of life into stone.

Their work has a patient extravagance. They lay rails toward blank fog. They bolt balconies to mineral lines passing through air. They plant orchards against walls that may one day become cliffs. They fit blank junctions to pipes and label them for neighborhoods without names. They repair roads to places currently absent.

When the lowest bell answers a new ridge, everyone listens.

Mill hands lift their gloves from levers. Children quiet their games. Moths settle. Goats crouch against the pipes. In kitchens, cups tremble toward the edge of tables, and a free hand reaches to steady them. For a while the people stand inside the long note, heads inclined, feeling through their boots what has begun beyond the lights.

Then the survey crews assemble.

Behind them come the rail fitters, condensate engineers, doorwrights, moss gatherers, orchard grafters, bell apprentices, vendors, teachers, cooks, clerks, and children old enough to carry one tool while keeping the other hand free.

The birds arrive ahead of all of them.

And somewhere on the wet new stone, before the first foundation is marked, someone sets down a chair for the resident who may already be there.
