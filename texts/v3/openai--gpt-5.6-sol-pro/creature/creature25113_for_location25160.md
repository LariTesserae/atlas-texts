No single shape belongs to the Underplain. It has not existed long enough to choose one.

Its life comes plated, swollen, ribbon-thin, many-jointed, hollow-boned, glass-skinned, or soft enough to pour through a crack. Species blur at their margins. A creature may hatch with six legs in one district and ten in another, depending on the salts dissolved in its egg pool. Some forms persist only for a few generations before the seam that sustained them closes. Others seem to be less a species than a recurring answer to a particular mineral: wherever wet iron meets warm gypsum, the same red, shovel-faced grazer appears, though no migration between the colonies can be found.

Almost everything begins small.

The first arrivals on new stone are the grits: faceted crawlers whose shell color follows what they have eaten. On basalt they become black and glossy. On sulfur they grow yellow and brittle. Those grazing copper seams carry a green tarnish and give off tiny sparks when crowded together. Their mouths are circular files lined with replaceable teeth. They scrape not only nourishment but the first habitable roughness into a virgin surface. Eggs, spores, and parasites lodge in the score marks behind them. Larger animals follow the living road.

A grit swarm is not merely food. It is weather, shelter, and warning. Its direction reveals fresh fractures. Its thickness predicts how long a mineral face will remain fertile. Its sudden stillness can mean tremor, poison, or the passing shadow of a hunter. Many creatures spend their entire lives within such swarms. Threadlike thieves swim through the gaps between shells and drink from wounds too small to hinder their hosts. Flat, translucent ambushers spread themselves beneath the moving mass, wait until hundreds have crossed their backs, and fold upward all at once.

The table-grazers come behind: broad, low animals carried on six splayed legs, with armored shoulders and skin that hangs in wet folds beneath them. Their front limbs rake grit swarms into elastic throat sacks. They have no jaws. Inside the sack, muscular stones grind prey and mineral together. When threatened, a grazer empties this half-digested slurry behind itself, making a slick caustic barrier through which its own plated feet can safely pass.

They migrate in files along pressure ridges, each stepping where the one before stepped. Over time their tread lays down the nearest thing the Underplain has to a road. Yet the roads move with them. When a ridge cools or a wall breaks, the herd turns, and within days its old passage is buried under shells, eggs, and opportunistic mouths.

Many grazers carry an upper world on their backs.

Between their plates live mites with hooked legs, pale worms that drink condensation, and little hunting animals no larger than teeth. Some grazers develop shallow skin basins in which water collects. Whole breeding cycles occur there. Tiny swimmers hatch, mate, and die without ever touching stone. When two grazers brush flanks, their back-fauna exchange hosts in frenzied leaps, so that every herd meeting is also a migration of invisible nations.

Above them drift the bladder creatures.

They are white, blind, and distended, with bodies arranged around several chambers of warm hydrogen. Fine muscles crumple or open the chambers to alter their height. They rise toward the luminous membranes to graze on electrically fed films, then descend trailing curtains of hooked silk. Crawlers, copper-wings, shed scales, and airborne larvae snag in these nets. The bladder creature draws the silk through a slit beneath its body and cleans it with combs of cartilage.

Old individuals become enormous and nearly transparent. Their organs hang visibly inside them like dark fruit in a lantern. Because they cannot turn quickly, they communicate by venting gas through resonant valves. Their sighs carry for kilometers. A herd of them adjusting buoyancy before rain produces a slow exchange of booms that the shelter-builders can interpret: deep notes for descending pressure, short fluting bursts for ceiling heat, abrupt silence for aerial predators.

Their most dangerous hunters do not fly. They climb the air.

These are the spanwalkers, the two-dozen-legged predators that arch over lesser animals like moving bridges. Each limb can stiffen into a supporting column or curl into a grasping hook. A spanwalker straddles migration routes and remains motionless until prey has begun passing beneath it. Then its underside opens.

It does not bite. It lowers clusters of adhesive tongues, lifts whatever sticks, and presses the catch into digestive pockets between its armor plates. Some prey survive the first swallowing and attempt to tunnel outward. This accounts for the scars, holes, and irregular patches in old spanwalker hides. Certain small species deliberately allow themselves to be eaten, breed within the warm digestive chambers, and emerge days later in numbers sufficient to kill the host. The oldest spanwalkers therefore carry symbiotic cleaners: narrow-headed animals that enter their bodies willingly and hunt through the internal folds.

When a spanwalker dies, it remains a habitat for years—an exceptional longevity in the Underplain. Its lifted ribs form shelters. Rain fills its armor cups. Grazers hollow out the softer joints. The shelter-builders harvest only the outer limbs at first, waiting until the internal scavengers have exhausted themselves before approaching the trunk.

The copper-wings are less substantial. Their bodies are little more than a gut, a hinge, and two metallic membranes veined with blood. They crowd into fissures by the hundreds of thousands, sleeping upright with their wings pressed to stone. Tremors dislodge them. They burst out in clouds and navigate badly, striking walls, shells, and one another. Each collision rings.

The sound is not accidental. A copper-wing hears through its entire skeleton and finds its flock by the returning notes. In open chambers they disperse and panic; in crowded terrain their impacts make a continuous map. Before rain, increasing humidity weights the wing membranes, forcing them to fly low. Before a caustic vent opens, changes in electrical charge make whole colonies ring inside their fissures without taking flight.

Some predators imitate that ringing. They tap mineral needles against their own shells and wait with their mouths open.

Rain brings forward an entirely different population.

Eggs that have lain as dry dust in salt hollows inflate into clear beads. Needle-swimmers wake inside old bones. Mud cords knot themselves into pulsing larvae. Pools cloud until water seems to have acquired muscle. Most rain creatures must complete their lives quickly: they hatch already carrying immature eggs, feed without sleeping, and mate while still growing. Their dead bodies form rafts on which the last generation lays its young.

When water drains, some dissolve into a paste that seals their eggs against salt. Others grow temporary legs. These migrants move in crowds, soft bodies pressed together to conserve moisture. They have no individual direction; those on the drying edge crawl inward, forcing those in the wet center outward, and by this continual exchange the whole mass creeps toward distant condensation. Predators tear mouthfuls from it without noticeably reducing its size.

The most unsettling rain life is often already present in dry ground. It waits as a pattern rather than a body: linked capsules spread through clay, each containing only a fragment of what the animal will become. Water joins the capsules chemically. They open, their contents seek one another, and a single large organism assembles itself across the basin. Such creatures may have dozens of mouths and no central organ. As the pool shrinks, they dismember themselves into capsules again. To kill one, every piece must be found.

Among all this motion travel the people of the Underplain.

They call themselves through touch more often than sound. Their spoken name, if rendered as a noise, is a dry sequence of jaw-fan clicks followed by a vibration too low for most other animals to detect. It means several things: **those who notice beneath**, **the ones with four hands**, and, in certain contexts, **the ones not yet crushed**.

They are compact and heavily plated, seldom standing higher than a table-grazer’s shoulder. Their rear limbs are powerful, with wide gripping feet suited to loose shell and slanting stone. Four forelimbs divide into an upper pair for force and a smaller, more precise pair tucked against the chest. With these they can hold a slab in place while binding it, cut meat while climbing, or carry a young one without surrendering the use of tools.

Their faces are difficult for outsiders to read. The eyes are small and recessed beneath mineral-stained shields. Most perception occurs through the fans along the jaw: layered, flexible vanes sensitive to vibration, humidity, electric fields, pressure, and traces dissolved in the air. When attentive, they spread these fans into pale semicircles. When frightened, they clamp them flat. Grief is shown by covering another’s jaw-fans with all four hands, shutting out the world’s warnings for a moment.

Their plates grow in overlapping sections and retain chemical stains. As a result, age and travel are written directly on the body. A pale band may mark a season among gypsum vents; red pitting, a migration through iron rain; black glass embedded along the feet, a crossing near the Mouth Without Hunger. They polish none of this away. An unstained adult is regarded with suspicion, since it suggests either inexperience or a deliberate erasure of history.

They do not organize themselves around territory. Territory cannot be trusted.

Their fundamental social unit is the departure-band: usually six to twelve adults, several young, and perhaps one elder no longer able to run quickly but too knowledgeable to abandon. Membership changes whenever routes cross. Pair bonds may last many migrations, but children belong to the traveling group, and any hand free during danger will seize any child within reach.

A band rarely sleeps all at once. One member keeps a palm on the floor while another watches the ceiling and a third listens to the animals. Their shelters are constructed to be surrendered. The most valued objects are not walls or stores but things that can be folded, nested, rolled, or dragged: tendon nets, rib splints, membrane cloaks, grease-sealed egg jars, shell maps, and drip catchers that collapse into shallow disks.

Their maps do not depict fixed places. They record tendencies.

A conventional mark for a ridge does not mean *a ridge stands here*. It means *the floor in this district has lately preferred to rise*. Water is shown not by a line but by a family of possible lines, some scored deeply, others barely scratched. Animal routes are revised with removable plugs of colored shell. Dangerous uncertainty has its own symbol: a circle broken in several places, indicating that all known explanations have failed.

A map that has remained unchanged too long is considered useless.

They eat almost anything they can render safe. Grit crawlers are crushed into mineral paste. Grazer throat sacks are sliced, washed, and dried into tough sheets. Copper-wings are roasted until their metallic membranes flake away. Rain larvae are fermented in clay pits, where their poisons are transformed by bacterial films. Certain foods are valued less for nourishment than for the sensory changes they produce. A bitter vent-worm sharpens electrical perception for several hours. The fatty nodules of bladder creatures make pressure changes painfully vivid. Scouts use both, though repeated use leaves the jaw-fans scarred.

Fire is rare and treated as an animal rather than a tool. There is little fuel, and open flame behaves unpredictably around gas vents and bladder herds. A band that manages to kindle one feeds it rendered fat in a lidded shell vessel, opening narrow vents to control its breathing. The flame is never left unattended. It is carried only short distances and killed before entering unknown ground.

More commonly they use heat from the cavern itself. Food is buried beside warm seams. Shell plates are softened in brine vents and bent into shape. Fat seals are cured against hot basalt. They distinguish hundreds of kinds of warmth by touch: warmth that precedes rain, warmth from compression, warmth from feeding colonies inside a wall, warmth that means stone will soon become liquid.

Their elders are not authorities in the usual sense. Memory is useful, but memory can harden into certainty, and certainty kills. An elder’s duty is to preserve contradictions. When the young say that low copper-wings mean rain, the elder recounts the three known occasions when they meant poison vapor instead. When a route appears safe, the elder names everything that could make it otherwise.

Their proverbs therefore sound indecisive:

**The quiet crack is closing, opening, or listening.**

**Clear water has not yet shown what lives in it.**

**The path that saved your mother has had time to learn another appetite.**

Decisions are made by layered attention. Each adult reads a different part of the world—floor, ceiling, air, herds, water, unseen chambers—and gives a tactile signal along a shared tendon cord. When several warnings coincide, the whole band can begin dismantling camp without a spoken command. A shelter that took half a day to build may vanish into carrying bundles in minutes. Bone stakes are pulled. Grease pits are covered. Nothing edible is left to attract a following swarm.

They do not bury their dead. Ground cannot be relied upon to keep them, and sealed graves may burst in later pressure shifts. Instead, the dead are dismantled with care. Plates become tools or map tablets. Tendons become cord. Jaw-fans are dried and suspended near a dependable drip, where falling water makes them tremble.

The soft remains are carried to a fresh mineral face and given to the grits.

This is not understood as a return to the earth. The Underplain is too unfinished to be imagined as a mother, and too hungry to be mistaken for peace. It is an exchange of warnings. The grits that consume the dead take on the mineral stains held in their tissues. For a brief time, a dark band of crawlers bears the colors of the deceased’s travels and moves away across the stone.

The people watch until the pattern disperses.

Their young hatch from thick, flexible eggs kept against the warm inner plates of several adults in turn. Newborns have no hard armor and remain wrapped in shed membrane. Their jaw-fans are oversized and cannot yet close, leaving them overwhelmed by every vibration. Much of early care consists of teaching them not merely to sense but to exclude—to sleep through harmless grinding, to ignore distant wing-ringing, to distinguish a grazer herd’s approach from a fracture moving underfoot.

Children play at collapse. They build towers of shell, undermine them, and snatch small carved figures from beneath the falling pieces. They race across safe cracks on intentionally poor bridges. They memorize routes and then redraw them after an adult secretly changes every marker. A child praised for finding the right answer is also asked to name two ways it might be wrong.

Some adults become listeners of unopened chambers.

They travel to the soft frontiers where luminous walls separate the known cavern from spaces still compressed beyond. There they brace their jaw-fans against the warm mineral and attend to the scratching, feeding, mating, and pressure-song on the other side. Over days they map the unseen ecology by vibration. They try to predict where the wall will split and what will emerge.

The work is revered and feared. Some listeners begin answering the rhythms they hear. Sometimes the creatures beyond answer back. There are stories of walls that remained intact until a particular knock was given, then opened along a perfect seam. There are also walls from which a listener fled after claiming that something on the far side had begun imitating the vibration of their private thoughts.

The Mouth Without Hunger draws other specialists: altered ones, glass-marked ones, those who have survived its moving spheres.

No two return alike. Some grow transparent patches through which their organs can be seen. Some develop additional limbs that never become fully obedient. A few acquire sensory pits able to detect fractures hours before they occur. Others lose the ability to recognize members of their own species but can calm spanwalkers by touching the ground in precise rhythms.

The departure-bands neither exile nor wholly trust them. Glass-marked travelers are given a place at the edge of camp, where their changes may serve as warning. They are watched during tremors. Many eventually feel a pull toward the basin and walk back into the churning spheres. Whether this is instinct, illness, summons, or choice remains one of the contradictions the elders preserve.

The Bone Weirs disturb the people more deeply.

They understand how to cut spanwalker ribs, how to lever them, how to seal joints with fat and clay. They could have built the weirs. Yet no living band admits to the knowledge required to align the walls with floods that do not follow stable channels. The structures seem to anticipate terrain that has not yet formed. Some ribs extend into stone now judged younger than the bones themselves. Needle-swimmers dormant in their hollows react to the touch of jaw-fans by arranging into repeating patterns.

A few people believe the weirs are maps made in bone.

Others think they are traps, still waiting for the intended prey.

Most simply alter their routes so they need not decide.

This refusal to settle on one meaning is not timidity. It is the deepest adaptation the Underplain has produced. Every creature there reads change, but the people read the danger of believing their readings complete. Their intelligence is not mastery, architecture, or dominion. It is revision made instinctive.

They possess no word for permanence. The nearest expression means **a thing whose change is too slow to help you**.

They possess no word for wilderness either. Nothing is outside life. A bare wall is only a herd not yet arrived, an unopened nursery, a sealed mouth, a future shell field. Empty air is crowded with what has not condensed. Silent stone contains scratching.

Even solitude is suspect. A traveler who appears alone is assumed to be carrying eggs, parasites, bacterial films, remembered routes, and the attention of predators.

So the inhabitants move through the bright cavern in converging multitudes: grits darkening the slopes, grazers shoveling them up, back-creatures hunting between plates, white bladders booming overhead, copper-wings ringing from wall to wall, spanwalkers straddling the roads, rain-life assembling itself wherever water falls. Among them go the small plated bands with their folded shelters and many-handed young, touching the floor, tasting the air, revising their maps.

Behind them, abandoned camps are eaten.

Ahead of them, the walls bulge with crowded life.

And when new stone opens, nothing asks whether it is safe.

The first mouths arrive while it is still breaking.
