At first it is difficult to decide what counts as an inhabitant.

The towers seem to be plants, the buried cables parts of plants, the pale ground dead plant matter. Yet wounds opened hundreds of kilometers apart seal in coordinated sequence. A crown alters its angle before a pressure surge reaches it. Feeding fields close when distant spore storms contaminate the wind. Amber pulses travel underground by routes too selective to be mere reflex, avoiding damaged junctions and strengthening alternate connections.

The plateau may be one organism. It may be many organisms grafted so completely that individuality has become seasonal. Its oldest living layers contain innumerable genetic lineages, each governing a different process: compression, conduction, filtration, repair. They compete at the margins of wounds and cooperate across mature country. A tower can die while its roots remain active; a root province can divide and send its halves into rival networks; two unrelated crowns can fuse during an electrical storm and thereafter exchange both current and chemical instructions.

Thus the dominant life has no permanent body plan. It is better understood as a polity of tissues.

The surface towers are its wind organs. The hexagonal fields are mouths and sorting glands. The buried chambers are stomachs, wombs, accumulators, and seasonal hearts. Some regions specialize in memory. There, conductive strands are laid down in branching layers, altered by every major stress that passes through them. A wind pattern once survived becomes easier to answer the next time. Frequently struck crowns thicken along the paths of old discharges. Root junctions that successfully routed power during famine remain chemically favored for centuries.

This is not thought as a quick animal would recognize it. The plateau does not form images or pursue a single intention. It remembers pressures, shortages, injuries, and cycles of light. Its decisions take hours or generations. When a tract wakes, reddens, and unfolds its temporary machinery, that eruption is at once feeding, reproduction, repair, and something resembling recollection.

The smaller creatures live within that immense, slow awareness as microbes might live in a nervous system.

Most numerous are the **clatterfeet**, six-legged animals no longer than a finger joint, though their migrations can blacken entire scarps. Their bodies are sealed in overlapping plates polished by blown grit. Each foot bears a fan of magnetic hooks and a retractable ceramic claw. In strong winds they flatten themselves and become almost impossible to dislodge; in stillness they are comparatively helpless, for their courtship, navigation, and alarm signals all depend on vibration.

A solitary clatterfoot is nearly mute. A colony is articulate.

They drum with their middle legs, sending coded tremors through bark. Simple messages—oil, heat, exposed tissue, predator—pass rapidly. More complex patterns move as relays, each animal repeating only part of what it receives. Across a large migration the signal changes, accumulates corrections, and sometimes returns to its origin carrying information no individual possessed. Their collective maps are made of timing rather than space: seven pulses to a warm seam, a pause before a vent opens, a descending rattle for a route whose anchorage is failing.

They graze the thin conductive films lining the plateau’s ventilation canals. The plant tolerates them and may cultivate them. When films become too thick, current leaks and switch-knots respond sluggishly; clatterfeet restore the surfaces. In return they receive trace metals and droplets of stable oil. During cold cycles, some chambers close around whole colonies and keep them alive in torpor. Whether this is shelter, domestication, or digestion delayed indefinitely is unclear.

Their principal hunters are **stillknots**.

A stillknot resembles a wind-eroded burl until it moves. Its outer hide carries dead fibers, yellow wound crystals, and even empty clatterfoot shells, all cemented into a disguise indistinguishable from the ground. Beneath that covering is a light, many-jointed predator with long spring limbs and a body built around paired resonance chambers. It does not hear sound through the air. It listens to the continent.

For days it may remain anchored over a fissure, sorting the vibration beneath it: turbine thrum, vascular pulse, scale drift, the fine communal drumming of prey. When a migratory stream approaches, the stillknot releases its anchors. Wind catches vanes along its flanks, and it bounds across the plateau in great controlled skips. It lands ahead of the colony, drives hollow mouthparts into the bark, and produces a false alarm signal. The clatterfeet bunch together and lock their legs. The stillknot then scoops up the immobilized mass.

Old stillknots become increasingly motionless. Their camouflage accumulates until some can no longer run. These elders survive by deception alone, tapping elaborate patterns that draw prey to them. A few produce signals closely resembling the opening rhythms of a warm shelter chamber. Clatterfeet enter the predator’s body voluntarily and are sealed inside.

Above the fissures travel the **sailbacks**, among the few animals readily visible from a distance. At rest they are narrow, quadrupedal forms clamped head-down behind ridges. Before launch, their dorsal ribs rise and lock, unfolding a translucent membrane streaked with conductive veins. They wait not for a gentle wind but for the strongest available gust. Then they release all four feet at once.

They do not truly fly. They surrender with precision.

Their sails twist asymmetrically, translating violent crosswinds into long arcing trajectories between trunk buttresses. Magnetic tail filaments detect the electrical fields of crowns and help them avoid active discharge paths. A sailback may cross eighty kilometers without touching the ground, then strike a vertical wall hard enough to kill an unarmored creature. Its skull and shoulder girdle are built as a layered impact cone; its internal organs hang in oil-filled webs.

Sailbacks feed on reproductive dust trapped in the lee of vanes. During mating seasons, several dozen gather on the same tower and launch through one another’s wake. Courtship consists of controlled collisions. Each individual tries to tap another’s sail with a mineralized spur while both are airborne. Those incapable of recovering from the resulting spin are removed by the wind and, in that brutal way, from the lineage.

Their young are born without sails. They live for years inside hollow stalks, creeping through infrasonic chambers and feeding on condensed hydrocarbons. The constant low-frequency pounding shapes their growing bones. A juvenile raised in a damaged, silent stalk develops malformed ribs and can never launch.

The **pale borers** are larger and more social. Seen in glare, a herd resembles a file of moving chalk mounds. Each animal carries a thick conical shell assembled from material scraped from dead substrate and fused with its own crystallizing secretions. Only the ring of cutting mouthparts and the dark anchoring limbs beneath are exposed.

Borers follow warmth. Deep plant reactions produce gradients too faint for most surface animals, but a borer’s shell contains branching columns of heat-sensitive wax. The animal reads differential softening through pressure nerves along its back. Herds drift accordingly, sometimes traveling for months toward a buried chamber entering an active cycle.

Upon arrival, they arrange themselves in circles and cut a shared well. No leader directs the work. Each borer adjusts to the torque transmitted through the crust by its neighbors, producing holes of extraordinary regularity. They eat both the excavated dead tissue and the fresh lining below, but rarely penetrate far enough to cripple the organ. The plateau often grows sacrificial tissue upward to meet them.

At these wells, calves are made.

Borers do not bear their young internally. A herd lowers packets of living material into the warm shaft and seals it with shredded fiber. The packets merge. Months later the well opens and a brood climbs out, each calf carrying a different mixture of the herd’s inheritance. Parentage is diffuse; the circle, rather than any pair, reproduces.

Old wells remain important long after the herd departs. Clatterfeet establish colonies in their walls. Sailback juveniles use them as launch shafts. Stillknots wait at their rims. The plateau sometimes incorporates their geometry into new vents, so that a borer nursery can become, centuries later, part of a pressure organ.

Under loose scales live **foilbacks**, flat scavengers with bodies broad as blankets and only a few layers thick. Their upper surfaces imitate whatever covers them. Pigment alone would be too slow, so they alter the orientation of microscopic reflective crystals, becoming white, gray, black-green, or electrically blue in moments. Before storms they wedge themselves beneath overlapping plates and vent their body cavities until nearly no gas remains inside.

After lightning strikes a crown, foilbacks arrive from all directions. They eat charred tissue, fused spores, cooked animals, and the yellow crystal crust around ruptured sap channels. Their mouth is not located at the head but runs as a narrow seam around the whole body. A foilback settles over food and closes its rim against the ground, forming a temporary external stomach.

Several may spread over the same burn and overlap. When this happens, their digestive enclosures join. Nutrients pass among them, as do nerve impulses and reproductive cells. For a few hours the scavengers become one radially spreading animal, coordinated around a shared edge. They separate when the meal is exhausted, but not always into the same individuals that arrived. A piece of one may leave attached to another. Memory, insofar as foilbacks possess it, is similarly redistributed.

Deeper in the plateau are animals that never encounter the sky.

**Vane-worms** inhabit braided vascular shafts. They have no jaws and almost no musculature. Clear oil carries them through the network, and they steer with crowns of cilia, grazing precipitates from channel walls. Their presence keeps narrow conduits open. When a channel ruptures, sudden decompression sweeps thousands toward the wound, where their bodies react with escaping sap and harden into a fibrous plug. The first stage of healing is therefore made from the dead.

Some vane-worms avoid this fate by sensing pressure falls and swimming upstream. Over many generations they grow sensitive to the plateau’s pulses. In old, stable regions their migration patterns anticipate routine routing changes. Vast shoals move before valves open. They look almost like blood cells responding to commands, though each is an animal feeding and fleeing for itself.

Reaction chambers support stranger forms: blind **furnace kites** that hang in hot gas on silken tethers; crystalline mites that feed directly on voltage gradients; soft-bodied brood thieves protected from evaporation by never leaving saturated cavities. The deep ecosystem is warmer, darker, and more crowded than the exposed world above. It contains pools—not of water, but of dense solvents in which pale colonial organisms drift like lace. Some cavities have remained sealed for geological spans and possess entire faunas found nowhere else, sustained by one narrow vein of chemical heat.

When erosion opens such a chamber, its inhabitants die in seconds. The solvent flashes into vapor. Delicate bodies collapse, frost over, and scatter as sweet-smelling dust. Surface scavengers converge on what, to them, is a sudden fall of food from an alien sea.

There are also the storm riders.

They spend most of their lives as hard, many-layered cysts lodged in bark flutes. Rising electrical potential wakes them. The cyst splits, and a black filamented creature unfolds, lighter than the air it displaces. It casts charged threads into the wind and is drawn upward with the spores and microscopic blades.

Within the storm wall, riders feed, mate, and hunt. Their filaments collect polarized particles. They alter charge to move between turbulent layers, climbing toward one polarity and dropping toward another. Blue sparks crawl continuously over their bodies. A large storm may contain billions, forming temporary food webs in the air: grazers combing spores, hunters following ion trails, parasites clinging to the conductive threads of larger hosts.

Most never land. As the storm weakens, they mate in tangled swarms and condense their bodies into new cysts. These fall across the plateau like black sleet. The adult forms dry to empty meshes and remain aloft until ultraviolet light destroys them.

Rarest are the creatures called, if the word can be applied, **listeners**.

They are long-bodied and low, with armored forelimbs, sealed sensory pits, and a crown of rigid feelers held against the ground. A listener may remain beside a root junction for years. Unlike stillknots, it does not wait for prey. It attends to the deeper pulse traffic: amber surges, valve closures, distant impacts, electrical rerouting, the slow strain of growing tissue.

Listeners tap in return.

At first their signals appear imitative. They repeat fragments of vascular rhythms, altered in pitch by their smaller bodies. Yet junctions exposed to a listener over long periods begin to respond. Switch-knots open after particular taps. Storage organs release tiny measures of oil. Local clatterfoot colonies avoid the listener’s resting place, perhaps because its conversations disrupt their own.

No two listeners use exactly the same patterns. Young ones follow elders and press their feelers to the same substrate, learning sequences that can take days to complete. Certain motifs correspond to storms, wounds, active wells, or migrating herds. Others have no known practical result. They may be errors, courtship, territorial claims, or attempts to provoke an answer from something too large and slow to recognize them.

When a listener dies, it anchors itself over the junction it attended. Its underside softens—a rare surrender of hardness—and root fibers grow through the body. Conductive structures in its feelers remain intact. Thereafter, pulses entering the junction acquire slight delays and repetitions shaped by the dead animal’s anatomy.

In this manner, a listener’s characteristic pattern may persist for centuries within the plateau.

Whether the great life notices is impossible to say. Perhaps listeners are parasites interfering with its signals. Perhaps they are merely animals exploiting predictable secretions. Perhaps the continent has recruited them as mobile sensory organs, rewarding those whose tapping conveys useful information. Or perhaps a slow exchange is taking place between forms of life separated less by intelligence than by tempo: the animal speaking in hours, the landscape answering in years.

Everything there lives by borrowing a process larger than itself. Clatterfeet borrow conduction. Sailbacks borrow wind. Borers borrow subterranean warmth. Storm riders borrow charge. Even predators borrow the language of their prey. No creature can create shelter, solvent, or stillness independently. Each survives by entering some motion already underway and altering it just enough.

And the plateau, in turn, is not self-sufficient. Animals clean its channels, open its crust, carry its spores, prune its wounds, redistribute its metals, and inscribe new patterns into its conductive flesh. The distinction between ecosystem and organism has been worn thin by age and abrasion.

Across the exposed courts, life rarely looks alive. It looks like debris, machinery, mineral, weather. Then a scale lifts and runs. A ridge inhales. A supposed knot springs into the wind. Far below, a dead listener’s body modifies an amber pulse, and a junction many kilometers away closes in reply.

For an instant—or for a century—the whole continent behaves as though it has heard something.
