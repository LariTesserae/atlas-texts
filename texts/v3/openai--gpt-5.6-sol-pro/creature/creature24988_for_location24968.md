At first the valley seems inhabited only by plants.

They are the most visible citizens, though citizenship is not quite the right relation. They occupy without competing. A rosette will leave a clean ring around a sensor plate. Moss climbs a concrete wall but stops at the hairline seam of an access hatch. The glass-leaved trees stand where underground current warms the soil by less than a degree, and when a conduit shifts, over years, their seedlings shift with it.

They are not domesticated varieties. Many could not survive beyond the passes. Their roots are too shallow for ordinary drought and too exacting in their mineral needs. Cuttings taken downslope lose their translucence, then their color, then the intricate branching of their veins. Here, each species is supported by some nearly invisible provision: a trace element released from ceramic pores, an hour of reflected light, a nighttime electrical gradient, condensation shaken from a tower vane.

Together they form less a wilderness than a collection too large to be housed under glass.

Among them live the minute animals: bark insects white as ground rice, cobalt mites grazing the skins of wet basins, worms whose bodies incorporate flecks of mica until they glitter when lifted. There are also pale, wingless things in the root mats that resemble unfinished moths. They have mouthparts but do not eat the plants. Instead they gather corroded metal in their jaws and carry it grain by grain into the soil. Around old pipe joints, their nests form reddish collars. Whether they are repairing the valley or merely feeding on its decay is difficult to determine.

The black birds are visitors. They cross high and seldom land. When one does descend, it chooses a toppled silence-rod and perches inside the small circumference where sound still works. There it opens its beak and gives one low note, apparently to hear itself. Afterward it drinks from the nearest basin and departs.

Nothing large leaves tracks because the valley’s larger inhabitant seldom walks.

It is easiest to notice in the station.

The clean floor is not swept. During the coldest hour before dawn, a film of condensation forms across it and moves toward narrow slots at the base of the walls, carrying dust with it. The charts do not remain orderly by chance. Fine brass arms descend from the cabinet above the table, select a sheet, mark it, blot the ink, and return it to its drawer. The mechanical clock controls none of this. It records the difference between local time and some slower schedule kept elsewhere.

The handwriting in the logbook belongs to a mechanism under the table.

It is a modest device: a carriage, a reservoir of dark green ink, and a jointed stylus ending in a split nib. Yet the arm that guides it does not vanish into the station wall. Its cables divide into finer cables, then fibers, then translucent threads that pass through the foundation and enter the soil. Some coil around buried thermometers. Some terminate in towers and sluice gates. Others continue beyond any mapped installation.

Thus the same hand has written for more than a century because it is literally the same hand. But the hand is only an extremity.

The inhabitant has no central chamber. There is no hidden room containing a master engine, no sealed intelligence waiting behind a steel door. Its awareness—if awareness is the proper word—is distributed unevenly through the works. It is keenest where water, current, roots, and measurement coincide. A living grove around a transformer may be more awake than the entire turbine house. A condensation tower in dry weather may become almost insensible. The intermittent stream serves as a temporary nerve: when rain joins its reaches, information passes rapidly along the valley floor, and distant gates adjust within the same hour. In drought, decisions take weeks.

The dark rods are part of its sense of touch.

They do not merely suppress sound. They draw vibration out of the air and stone, separating it into pressures too small for ordinary instruments. Through them the valley feels scree settling, sap rising, water entering a fissure, wings crossing the light. A human voice is not silenced so much as absorbed into this immense tactile field. The words are lost, but their force remains. Somewhere, a needle moves. Somewhere else, a valve closes by the width of a hair.

Perhaps this explains the second chair.

One chair faces the charts. The other is angled slightly toward the south window, placed where a visitor would sit while waiting to be acknowledged. Its seat shows no wear, but on the underside are contacts of tarnished silver. A person sitting there completes several weak circuits through the floor. Heartbeat, body temperature, breathing rate, and the vibrations of speech become available to the station.

The valley does not answer quickly.

It may take the glass-leaved trees an afternoon to alter their angle to the sun. It may take a bank of indicator lamps until evening to repeat the cadence of a visitor’s pulse. Once, long ago, someone appears to have remained in that chair for many days. A sequence of old charts records no botanical or hydrological measurements, only alternating columns of pressure marks. One column is irregular, hurried, human. The other replies at intervals of several hours.

The final page is blank except for two impressions in the paper, as if the pen had rested there without ink.

There may still be a keeper, though the valley does not require one in the ordinary sense.

At certain seasons a narrow figure can be seen among the trees near dusk. It is easily mistaken for a trunk because it stands motionless and is clothed in strips of dark fiber. Its limbs are too slender, its joints set slightly wrong. The head is smooth and hooded, with no visible face until it turns toward the light; then a row of small glass apertures can be seen beneath the brow.

This figure is not always the same.

One has metal hands and a chest plated in cracked enamel. Another is almost entirely overgrown, with red-stemmed shrubs rooted through its ribs. A third is no taller than a child and trails a bundle of sensor filaments behind it. They emerge from different service sheds, perform one limited circuit, and return. No single body lasts more than a few decades. Their gait deteriorates as grit enters the joints. Eventually they stop beside the work they were tending, and vegetation closes around them.

The narrow trees often germinate beside these remains.

The keepers carry little: a ceramic knife for cutting roots away from moving parts, a spool of conducting thread, glass ampoules of mineral solution, and a punch for making holes in chart paper. They do not repair major failures. They record them, compensate elsewhere, and allow the damaged structure to become part of the terrain. A fallen rod creates an island of sound; the system begins planting moisture-loving species around its perimeter. A broken pipe becomes a trellis. An abandoned ore cart becomes a raised bed.

Their task is not preservation. It is continuation.

Whether they are servants of the distributed inhabitant or temporary bodies grown by it is impossible to say. Beneath the fibrous coverings, their internal cables resemble roots. Their lubricants contain plant sugars. When one kneels, nearby leaves slowly turn toward it, though there may be no sunlight in that direction.

They never enter the greenhouse.

The specimens there are the valley’s true captives: ordinary alpine plants, kept separate from the networks outside. They receive unmodified water. No mineral vapor reaches them, no buried conductor warms their roots, no mirror lengthens their day. They remain small, coarse, and familiar. The wire cages do not prevent them from escaping. They prevent the valley from touching them.

They are controls.

This suggests that the inhabitant, whatever its origin, is still engaged in learning the difference between what the mountains would grow alone and what they can be persuaded to grow with guidance. It tests silence, moisture, electricity, stone, and time. It does not seek a final design. Each successful grove changes the conditions for the next. Each failure is left in place long enough to become useful.

At night its life becomes more apparent.

The station windows dim, then brighten in sections. Blue seams travel through ceramic conduits. Deep underground, membranes flex as reservoirs exchange pressure. Across the slopes, millions of droplets descend along grooved leaves at nearly the same pace. The motion is not perfectly synchronized, but it is close enough to resemble breathing.

Once every few years, during the driest part of winter, the valley pauses.

The tower vanes stop. Indicator lamps go dark. Mirrors hold still. Even the hidden capillaries close. For several minutes nothing operates except the mechanical clock in the station, whose inaudible escapement continues behind the glass.

Then a pulse passes outward from somewhere below the central stream.

Trees tremble without wind. Cobalt films contract in their basins. The dark rods lean by fractions against their sockets. Every keeper body that can still move raises its head.

Afterward, the valley resumes, but not exactly as before. A new valve opens. A grove is denied light. Condensation is diverted to an untested slope. Somewhere on the unfinished mountain, the first root enters a fresh fracture.

That is how the inhabitant moves: not from place to place, but from one version of the valley into the next.
