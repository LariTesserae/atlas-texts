Nothing on the plateau lives by holding very tightly to itself.

The first inhabitants are almost too small to count as creatures. They occupy the damp side of stones, the pores of copper cushions, the brief meniscus left when dawn withdraws. In dryness they become scales, powders, transparent husks. Some remain in that state for decades, indistinguishable from mineral bloom. Water does not merely wake them; it restores the sequence they had been interrupted in. A speck begins dividing halfway through an old division. A filament resumes bending toward light. Colonies darken the undersides of quartz with green, violet, and iron-black films, laying down the pasture on which larger lives depend.

The copper cushions are colonies rather than single plants. Beneath their bristled surfaces, thousands of bead-sized bodies share moisture through a web of hollow fibers. The oldest cushions have spread only a hand’s width in a century. Their pale hairs comb water from the air, but also sense pressure changes caused by passing animals and distant cloud. Before mist climbs the western escarpment, whole slopes of cushions incline their filaments in the same direction. The movement is slight enough to be mistaken for wind.

Veil-plants live more quickly. Each ring is the exposed edge of a buried organism whose greater body lies flat between layers of stone. At night it manufactures its shining membrane from water-soluble tissue. By dawn the hoop has become a net cast into the air. Once the collected dew drains inward, the net digests itself. Small grazers sometimes arrive before dissolution is complete and eat the membrane from its rim, moving in careful circles. The plant tolerates this. Their feet carry reproductive dust to the next hoop.

The jointed reed-trees are inhabited in turn. Cool hollows inside their trunks shelter mites, pale thread-worms, and colonies of pollinators that spend the day folded into the seams. Their roots do not descend far. Instead, they spread laterally through fractures, sometimes joining distant trees into a loose hydraulic community. A tree that receives rain may feed another standing under clear sky. In severe years, the network abandons some members. Their trunks seal segment by segment, preserving reservoirs for the living grove. What appears to be a dead reed-tree may still contain water guarded by tissue no thicker than a fingernail.

The ivory grazers travel continually among these scattered provisions.

They have six legs because no four points of contact are sufficient on the shelves where they feed. The forward pair tests stone; the middle pair bears weight; the rear pair can lock into cracks when heat gusts rise from a ravine. Their mouths are wide, shallow rasps that remove a living film without scarring the mineral beneath it. From a distance they seem to eat rock.

Their mirrored backs are not smooth. They are covered in millions of tiny chambers that open and flatten in response to light. Under blue sky the chambers deepen and reflect blue. Beneath cloud they turn white. At sunset they take on rose, copper, and finally the violet-black of the shelves. Young grazers cannot control the effect well. Their backs flicker through misplaced colors, revealing them to predators, and so they travel beneath adults until their skins learn the horizon.

A procession has no permanent leader. Direction passes through the group as a pulse of pressure tapped foot to stone. The route may be remembered by no individual and yet persist for generations, stored as a rhythm in the herd: seven quick contacts where a channel bends, a long pause before an exposed crossing, three widely spaced beats near dependable water. A separated animal can sometimes recover its companions by pressing its lower body against the ground and listening for the inherited pattern.

At the dark pools they wait without conflict because disturbance is dangerous. The pool surface must first recognize a visitor as separate from dust. A grazer lowers a tufted drinking organ until the surface yields. If several push forward at once, the folded volume may return water unpredictably—against a wall, beneath the soil, or briefly into the air. The animals do not understand the geometry, but their etiquette is older than understanding.

Above them live the ray-flocks.

A ray-creature begins life in water. The transparent swimmers that fill rain channels are its larval form: quick, nearly invisible bodies feeding on organisms awakened from dust. Before the channel dries, a larva crawls onto warm stone and seals itself inside a brittle envelope. There it converts its swimming surfaces into broad porous wings. Months later, sometimes years later, sufficient heat wakes it. The envelope cracks, and a creature made for water enters the air.

Adults do not fly so much as enter agreements with currents. Their skins contain fields of pores that can be opened in patterns finer than muscle could manage. By admitting air through one flank and refusing it through the other, they slide, pivot, rise, or hang motionless beside a cliff. Seen from below, they resemble flat pieces missing from the sky.

Cloud-drinking is their great communal event. As a solitary cloud heaves over the rim, ray-creatures arrive from mesas many kilometers apart. They pass repeatedly through the white body, combing droplets into channels along their wings. During these gatherings they exchange minute packets of living tissue. There are no fixed sexes and no pairing. The cloud itself becomes the temporary body of the flock, carrying reproductive material among hundreds of individuals. When they emerge, each bears some portion of the next generation, to be released into the first suitable rain.

Their roosts smell faintly of hot metal and wet chalk. At evening they overlap on vertical stone, each fastening to the cliff with a ring of soft hooks. In sleep their pores open and close in waves. A roosting flock can alter the temperature of the rock by several degrees and may determine where condensation forms before dawn.

The tall ravine-drinkers move with greater deliberation.

Their bodies are narrow below and spacious above, as though an ordinary animal had been stretched by thirst. Four long legs end in broad, divided pads that bridge loose stones without shifting them. The neck rises above the reed-trees and folds twice when the animal rests. Along its sides hang pleated membranes where pollinators shelter from noon.

Its drinking filament is both tongue and sensory organ. Inserted into a reed-tree opening, it reads the chemistry of the suspended beads before taking them. Water belonging to a stressed tree is left untouched. Water carrying larvae is drawn only in part. A healthy trunk is drained to a level that stimulates further condensation without injuring its tissues. Thus the drinkers tend the ravines simply by feeding.

They are slow to mature and meet one another infrequently. Courtship may occupy an entire season and occur across several valleys. One animal leaves traces of aromatic oil inside selected reed trunks. Another discovers these months later and responds in a different grove. By the time the pair finally stands together, each has already learned the other’s health, route, diet, and approximate age through the water between them.

Their young are carried high against the folded neck, among the pollinator colonies. For their first year they drink moisture condensed on the parent’s skin. The insects clean them, and the young in turn provide warmth during cold highland nights.

The dust-colored predators are not numerous. No landscape this economical could support many.

They are broad, low beings with feet that distribute their weight so perfectly that even settled scree scarcely moves beneath them. Their eyes are poor. Instead, they perceive probable movement: heat remaining where a body rested, strain traveling through stone, changes in the behavior of small animals. They choose an intersection and wait.

Their patience is not stillness in the ordinary sense. While waiting, a predator slows until its breath may occur only a few times each hour. Dust adheres to secretions on its hide. Mineral films begin growing over its back. Veil-plants have been known to anchor against an individual that remained in place through several dawns. When prey finally approaches, the attack is a single expenditure—one surge, one closing of the mouth. A failed predator may need days before it can attempt another.

They kill rarely enough that a carcass becomes a local season. Grazers alter their routes. Pollinators arrive to drink salts from the eyes. Copper cushions beneath the bones darken and spread. The reed network may redirect water toward the site for years.

Then there are the custodians.

At a distance, one can mistake a custodian for a reed-drinker, a pillar, or the shadow of either. They are tall but not consistently upright. Their bodies have several resting geometries, and they fold differently according to heat, wind, and task. In strong noon light they become narrow, presenting little surface to the sun. In mist they open pale lateral tissues along the torso and limbs. These tissues gather moisture, exchange gases, and receive low-frequency vibrations from the structures.

Their skin is matte and finely patterned, usually the color of the stone most recently crossed. This is not camouflage in the grazer’s reflective sense. Dust and mineral salts are allowed to enter the skin’s outer dead layers, making each custodian a temporary record of its route. Ochers from the western rim, black grains from the arch field, rose quartz from the high shelves: a knowledgeable observer can read weeks of travel from the bands along a custodian’s arms.

They have no obvious face when resting. The organs used for sight, chemical sense, and electrical perception lie beneath a movable veil of skin at the upper end of the body. When attending closely, they draw this veil back. What appears is not a pair of eyes but a curved field of dark sensory points. Different portions watch different durations. Some register present light. Some compare it with light retained from earlier hours. A few are sensitive to the displaced images surrounding the oldest installations.

Their hands divide into many slender, boneless digits. They seldom touch the pillars directly. Instead, they place their hands near the surface and feel how heat, vapor, stress, and likelihood bend around it. To them a functioning pillar is not silent. It possesses something like tone: a layered pressure perceived in the nerves and cavities of the chest.

Custodians do not command the system. Command would be too crude and too quick. They offer it small alternatives.

Beside a condensation pillar, a custodian may unfold a ribbon of diagnostic tissue between two stones. Overnight the ribbon changes permeability according to minute shifts in dew. The custodian studies the resulting bands, then spends the next day altering the mineral balance of a nearby seam or shading one side of the pillar for precisely part of an hour. The pillar responds gradually. A cloud months later descends a little farther than it otherwise would have.

Repair is usually indistinguishable from waiting.

When a folded pool accumulates errors, reflections appear in the wrong order. Dust passes through the surface and returns before it fell. Animals drinking there may cast shadows into absent rooms. A custodian sits beside such a pool and introduces a sequence of disturbances: breath, fingertip, a warmed stone, a thread carrying a measured electrical pulse. Each disturbance asks where the pool currently believes its boundaries are. The answers may take days. Only after the folded watershed has described itself can it be persuaded to fit the basin again.

Custodians eat little. They take mineral films, the shed membranes of veil-plants, and dense nodules grown within certain reed-tree joints. They do not kill animals. This is not necessarily mercy; their metabolism is poorly suited to concentrated flesh. Much of their energy appears to come from symbiotic tissues that brighten imperceptibly under the plateau sun.

They sleep in the oval rooms beneath ledges, though sleep is not private for them. As a resting place rises from the floor and encloses the body, the room reads the traveler’s accumulated strains. Some memories are translated into changes in temperature and wall tension. Others are held outside the sleeper until waking. A custodian may emerge remembering a difficult crossing only as a pattern of warmth along one arm, then recover the event gradually over the next several days.

They seldom travel together because two custodians alter the local system merely by being near each other. Their electrical fields overlap. Their intentions make paths too legible. Condensation can gather around shared attention. When they do meet, they remain several paces apart and communicate through posture, shadow placement, and faint vibrations sent into the ground.

Much of what they tell one another concerns trends too slow for ordinary speech: a ravine becoming more hospitable over nine generations; an eastern pillar leaning by a fraction that is spatial rather than physical; a herd route beginning to coincide with an unstable fold. A conversation may begin at one seasonal meeting and conclude years later when the same custodians cross again.

They have names, but names are not sounds. A custodian’s name is a set of relationships: the route by which it first crossed the Long Incline, the pillar whose field marked its development, the particular future-shadow it can distinguish from others. Such a name cannot be spoken in one place. It must be demonstrated across distance.

The oldest among them are not necessarily the most physically aged. Age is measured by the duration one has learned to notice. A young custodian can see a cloud approaching. A mature one sees the wet season forming in the behavior of roots. An elder watches erosion choosing among shapes.

At the black field around the broken arch, even elders move cautiously.

They stand beside the slabs and observe shadows cast by things that are not there—or not there yet. The work can alter them. Some return from the arch with their movements fractionally misplaced, a hand beginning to reach before the need is visible. Others develop reflective patches in which the sky appears a few minutes old. These custodians often become guides, because dangerous ground recedes from their attention before it is encountered, and companions learn to follow the direction of their calm.

Whether they built the plateau’s system is no longer clear, perhaps not even to them. Their bodies are adapted to its fields. Their life cycles depend upon its water. The inhabited rooms recognize their nerves, but also the nerves of other sufficiently complex visitors. The pillars accept adjustment without acknowledging ownership. It may be that the first custodians made the structures. It may be that the structures gradually made custodians out of some earlier traveler.

And the structures themselves may live, though not in any way that settles the question.

They heal. They respond. They preserve preferences and modify them with experience. A vapor pillar exposed to centuries of changing cloud routes becomes better at inviting precisely those clouds the ravines can use. A heat-burying pillar distinguishes ordinary noon from dangerous accumulation. The rooms learn recurring bodies and prepare for them. Yet none reproduces, unless the slow migration of cracks and minerals toward certain empty sites is the beginning of reproduction on a geological timescale.

Around an old pillar, pearl-like material sometimes appears in the stone as threads. Over centuries the threads thicken and converge. Custodians mark such places but do not interfere. Some believe the plateau is extending its own system. Others believe buried components are surfacing. To the animals, the distinction has no meaning. Shade is shade; dew is water; a path that grows easier beneath the foot is followed.

Thus the plateau’s inhabitants are not gathered into settlements. They are gathered into consequences.

A ray-flock cools a cliff, and dawn water forms there. A reed network shares it. A long-necked drinker carries pollinators to the next ravine. Grazers clean the shaded films. Predators turn excess life back into a fertile patch. Custodians adjust a preference so that a cloud descends a generation later. Pillars bend losses into delays. The arch teaches erosion a shape, and every footstep begins, unknowingly, to trace it.

Each creature seems solitary when seen against the enormous stone. None is solitary in time.
