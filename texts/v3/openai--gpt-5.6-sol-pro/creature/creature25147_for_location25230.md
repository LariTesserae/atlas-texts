At first, it seems that only one thing lives in the Hollow City.

It has no name for itself. “Root” describes its shape badly and its history somewhat better. It is a plant only because no nearer category survives. It does not drink, leaf, flower, or seek the sun. It has converted all the familiar gestures of vegetation into slower, stranger equivalents. Sap has become mineral resin. Growth has become architecture. Breathing has become weather. Reproduction has become a civic ceremony enacted by empty streets.

Its body is not arranged around a single center. The black bud is important, but it is not a brain, nor even a heart. Life is dispersed through the walls in innumerable narrow threads. Each thread performs a little judgment. It thickens where strain persists, starves where pressure lessens, seals a wound, releases a chamber, redirects a grain of sulfur. No thread understands the city. Together they remember it.

The Root’s awareness, if awareness is the word, is tactile and delayed. It knows weight. It knows torsion, dryness, the arrival of salt through its rind. It knows a snapped fiber as a bright chemical disturbance that may require a century to subside. The touch of a traveler would enter it as warmth, pressure, and foreign moisture. Years later, capillaries near the touched place might constrict. Decades after that, resin might gather there. By then the traveler would be gone, but the Root would still be answering.

Thus the apparent emptiness is partly an error of pace. The organism is always occupied with itself. A plaza is crossed by pressures. Towers exchange tension with distant buttresses. Fine pulses travel along balconies that were once branches. Every district participates in a conversation too slow to be heard as speech, though fragments of it break into sound: a boom, a shriek, a rolling sequence of cracks. These are not cries. They are closer to syllables produced accidentally by thought.

But the Root may not be the city’s only inhabitant.

In the oldest walls are vertical seams denser than the tissue around them. Some fork near the ceiling into patterns like raised arms. Others descend into braided foundations and disappear. They are usually taken for supports. Yet they occur most often inside the narrow chambers called houses, and the grain of the surrounding walls bends toward them as it bends toward healed grafts.

These may be the old citizens.

They would not have been animals. They may have grown from temporary buds along the inner avenues: pale upright bodies with several tough, flexible limbs; crowns of sensory filaments; lower portions attached to the floor by cords fine enough to be withdrawn and planted elsewhere. They would have had no eyes. Sight would have been useless. Instead they may have read vibration through their feet and tasted the condition of the Root by pressing soft scars against its walls.

Their bodies were probably hollow. Resin moving through them would have stiffened or softened particular joints. They might have stood motionless for months, then worked continuously through a whole contraction cycle, cutting new channels while the tissue was pliant, placing wedges, tightening cords, scoring patterns that induced future branching. A generation of such beings could begin a doorway that only its distant descendants would finish.

They did not command the Root. Their relationship to it was more intimate and less equal. They were gardeners grown by the garden, hands extruded from the material they shaped. The Root produced them where internal strain required intervention; they altered its growth according to inherited forms; when their work ended, they fixed themselves in place and lignified. A citizen became a column. A gathering of citizens became a colonnade. Their last posture remained as structure after every softer distinction had vanished.

This would explain the absence of graves.

Death among them was not departure but reassignment. An old worker might enter a wall niche, extend its lower fibers into the floor, and wait while resin filled its cavities. Its sensory crown would harden into a branching brace. Its limbs would be bound into adjacent beams. For years it might remain partly responsive, feeling traffic and strain even as its body became architecture. There may never have been a moment at which it ceased to be a person and became a building.

Some could still be alive in that attenuated sense.

A hand laid against certain columns finds a faint irregular trembling unlike the broad pulse of the surrounding tissue. The tremor sometimes travels upward in clustered intervals, pauses, then repeats. Perhaps it is merely resin passing through an old knot. Perhaps a citizen enclosed ten thousand years ago continues to register pressure through a body now indistinguishable from the ward it supports.

If so, the city is not deserted. It is full of inhabitants who have become too patient and too large to recognize.

There are smaller lives as well, though “smaller” refers to duration and function rather than size. Beneath the floors lie dormant growths folded into dry scales. When a structural wound opens, chemical heat awakens them. They send translucent filaments into the crack, not feeding on the Root but continuing it. Some become patches. Some become valves. Some grow briefly into thorn-hard tools and detach when mature, as though the organism still expects hands to collect them.

The curled ribbons stored in the cisterns may be sleeping organs of another kind. They tighten in response to certain resonances. Given enough warmth, one might uncoil into a thin creeping stem that follows grooves through several districts, touching scars in a fixed sequence before rooting itself into a wall. Such a stem would resemble a messenger, though it carried no written message. Its body would be the instruction: where it paused, tissue would soften; where it anchored, a passage would begin to close.

Pollen is the city’s most numerous inhabitant and its least individual. Every black grain is a sealed proposal. It contains a minute arrangement of chemical possibilities, most of which will never be used. The grains lie in corners for centuries, piled like soot, waiting for the streets to breathe. Then they rise together.

During those long exhalations the city briefly fills with motion. Clouds pour beneath arches and ascend through hollow towers. Grains strike walls, rebound, settle on ledges, or enter the deep receptive cups. Almost all remain inert. A few dissolve against living scars and alter the growth around them. The change may be as slight as a new curvature in a corridor begun three hundred years later.

There may once have been many Roots beneath the salt field, each exchanging pollen through linked hollows or through a vanished atmosphere above. The city’s elaborate breathing could be the remnant of a conversation whose other participants are dead. It continues to send its black grains along routes that end in sealed chambers, faithfully addressing neighbors that no longer exist.

The central bud is different from all of these. It is neither citizen nor district, though it may someday become both.

Within its overlapping plates, tissues remain less committed than elsewhere. They have not yet decided whether they are root, limb, tower, reproductive organ, or something for which no present structure offers a model. The bud contains the Root before its forms separated. It is a compressed second city and perhaps a second species.

Its warmth is not evenly distributed. Deep between the plates, tiny zones wake and subside. One begins producing a pigment no light has ever touched. Another forms the rudiment of a hollow stalk, then reabsorbs it. A third rehearses the chemistry of water tolerance and fails. These are preparations, but also experiments: blind attempts to meet a world known only through mineral pressure and inherited instruction.

If the bud ever opened, it might not send a shoot upward. The old command to seek the surface may have been revised beyond recognition. It might unfold inward, filling the central district with soft new chambers. It might produce citizens again. It might release a forest of mobile stalks to cut the ancient city apart and train its fragments into another shape.

Or it may be doing so already, at a scale too slight to distinguish from waiting.

The Hollow City’s inhabitants, then, are not arranged into separate kinds. They pass into one another. Pollen becomes instruction. Instruction becomes tissue. Tissue becomes worker. Worker becomes tool, dwelling, brace, and memory. The Root contains them all without clearly possessing them, just as they shape the Root without clearly being separate from it.

Nothing there hunts. Nothing watches. Nothing sleeps in the animal sense. Yet the darkness is not vacant.

It is crowded with distributed life: with old citizens holding up ceilings in their final postures; with unborn organs dreaming chemically beneath the floors; with messages preserved as black dust; with wounds that remember being doors; with a whole city slowly thinking through the pressure of its own walls.

And at the center lives the most difficult inhabitant of all: not a creature, but a future that has remained alive long after the world required for it has disappeared.
