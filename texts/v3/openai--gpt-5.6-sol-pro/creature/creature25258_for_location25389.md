From a distance, the inhabitants appear to be one dark, restless nap upon the ground.

Only nearer does the nap divide into lives.

The first to become distinct are the grazers, because they spend the morning in the open. They are no higher at the shoulder than the basin’s thorn scrub, narrow through the chest and almost weightless-looking, with long two-toed feet that spread slightly on powdery soil. Their legs do not end in hard hooves so much as paired, blunt nails bordered by leathery pads. Each step presses the dust without breaking its crust deeply. Even in great numbers they leave stippling rather than churned earth.

Their coats are the colors of old sediment: gypsum white along the spine, dun at the flanks, smoke-gray around the legs. A dark stripe runs from the nostrils to the inner eye, where it reduces glare. They have high, mobile ears, but these are not used chiefly for sound. Veins near the skin shed heat after sunset, and the fine hairs along their rims catch scent carried just above the plants. The ears turn together when a predator is near, producing a visible signal that travels through the herd before any animal moves.

They crop no plant down to its base. Their mouths are small and exact, the lips divided into several muscular points capable of taking a seed head, a succulent tip, or the soft inner tissue of a folded leaf while leaving thorns untouched. Rows of enamel ridges grind salt-coated growth. Behind the teeth lie glands that bind excess mineral into chalky pellets, which the animals occasionally work from their mouths and drop along habitual routes. In time these pale pellets become part of the path itself.

No individual seems to lead them. The herd organizes through pressure and attention. A head lifts; nearby bodies angle away from it. A ripple of turned ears passes outward. Animals at the margins step inward while those at the center loosen their spacing, and the whole aggregation changes direction without a cry. When alarmed they do not bolt far. They divide, flow around towers and shrubs, then reunite beyond them. Panic would cost water. Their fear has become geometrical.

Young are born singly in shallow chambers excavated beneath living root mats. For the first several days the mother visits only during the coldest hours, guided by scent pressed into the soil. The young lie with their legs folded close and their breathing slowed. Dust settles over their coats until they resemble clods. Before they are strong enough to join the herd, small insects have already begun cleaning salt and dead skin from the folds around their eyes.

Other herbivores emerge at dusk.

These are taller but even more lightly made: long-necked browsers with sloping backs and split upper lips protected by dense white bristles. They walk between thorn crowns and feed by drawing branches through comb-like teeth. Their eyes are large, bronze, and nearly lashless, recessed beneath shelves of bone. When threatened, they become still rather than flee, aligning their necks with the pale verticals of insect towers. A motionless group can be mistaken for a stand of weathered chimneys until one bends soundlessly to feed.

The smallest grazers scarcely resemble herd animals at all. They are round-bodied, rabbit-sized runners whose hind feet strike the ground in patterned sequences. Much of their communication travels downward. A warning may consist of three soft impacts, a pause, then two more; mating territories are announced by longer tremors transmitted along packed roads and mound foundations. Their ears are reduced, their jaws deep, and the underside of the body is furred against hot soil. They sleep communally in borrowed passages, plugging the entrance with a disc of felted plant fiber held in place by their own bodies.

None of these animals dominates the basin. The builders do.

A single builder is unimpressive: pale, soft-bodied, nearly blind, with a broad head and dark mouthparts. Most are shorter than a seed. Their skin loses moisture quickly, and in the open they survive only beneath shade, inside covered roads, or in the humid plume of air rising from a vent. Yet almost every tower, dome, causeway, crusted wall, sealed chamber, and repaired fracture is the accumulated action of such bodies.

There are many kinds, and they do not all build alike.

The narrow-tower builders live on the firmer shelves. Their workers carry grains one at a time, coating each in a film drawn from throat glands before placing it. They are sensitive to moving air. A worker sets down a grain where the current cools one antenna more than the other; thousands following the same discomfort produce flues, baffles, and branching vents. Their towers lean slightly away from the strongest afternoon heat and contain thin internal walls that flex as temperatures change.

The dome builders of the soft troughs are heavier and darker. They mix clay with cropped grass and compact it by striking with the flat fronts of their heads. Their structures begin belowground as broad rafts of hardened chambers. Only after the foundation has survived several seasons do they raise anything above the scrub. When a dome subsides, they do not restore its original shape. They thicken the lowered side, abandon crushed rooms, and continue. The resulting settlement records every settling of the soil as folds and buttresses.

Along the buried moisture bands live smaller, almost translucent builders. They cultivate white fungal tissues in sealed combs made from chewed roots and dung. The fungi break down fibers the insects cannot digest and draw traces of water from humid air. The colony drinks by eating its garden. These insects rarely see the surface. Their towers are chiefly respiratory organs: porous chimneys that breathe with the daily heating and cooling of the basin.

A mature colony contains several reproductive mothers, each swollen and nearly immobile, attended in chambers far below the surface. They are not sovereigns. They do not know the extent of what proceeds from them. Their eggs are carried away into graded nurseries, turned, cleaned, warmed, cooled, or eaten when conditions fail. Colony memory resides elsewhere: in wall chemistry, inherited gut organisms, scent layered upon scent, and the continuing shape of passages. Remove the oldest inhabitants and the settlement still remembers where to build.

Borders between colonies are not simple lines. They are zones where scent becomes uncertain. Patrol workers touch one another, withdraw, recruit, test again. Some encounters end in masses of dismembered insects sealed into the walls. Others result in gradual fusion. Workers begin using foreign tunnels; fungal strains cross between gardens; reproductives of one lineage continue deep beneath architecture maintained by another. Aboveground, the seam disappears. Beneath it, ancestry remains complicated for centuries.

Many creatures depend upon this inability of a structure to remain private.

Small salt-colored lizards live in the outer vents. Their ribs flatten so they can turn inside narrow shafts, and their scales carry fringes that brush insects from the wall. They do not raid continuously. A lizard that eats too greedily is entombed by repairs. Instead it takes workers at intervals, defecates in unused pockets, and retreats when it detects the first alarm odor. Some colonies tolerate them because they also consume parasitic flies and mites near the openings. The arrangement is neither friendship nor domestication. It is a balance of inconveniences.

Deeper in the abandoned chambers live blind, soft-shelled beetles with legs adapted for scraping fungal films. Their larvae resemble roots and are often built around rather than removed. Tiny silver mites cling to the builders’ joints and drink condensation from the grooves in their armor. Thread-thin worms inhabit damp nursery walls. False workers—beetles shaped and scented like colony members—walk freely through the traffic, accepting food from insects that do not recognize the difference.

The most successful impostors imitate not an animal but a task. One smells like a hungry larva, another like damaged masonry, another like a reproductive chamber in need of cleaning. They survive by causing labor.

Above the active rooms is a changing population of tenants. Burrowing owls occupy high cavities, pale birds with broad feet and short, dust-colored feathers. Their wings are rounded and quiet, useful for brief flights between towers rather than long pursuit. They hunt at dusk from a vent rim, watching for tremors and changes in the posture of grazing animals. Instead of calling over distance, paired birds exchange low throat pulses while standing on the same wall, their feet feeling the vibration through the structure.

They are not the only birds, though none fill the sky in great flocks. Seed-eaters move close to the plants in quick, low bursts. Their bills are deep enough to crack dormant seeds and lined with ridges that strip salt before swallowing. Some carry seeds into shaded tower crevices to open them. Forgotten stores germinate there, and thorn shrubs root in the softened masonry. The birds return later to feed on those shrubs’ flowers and fruit. Over generations, their feeding sites become islands of vegetation marked by guano-whitened perches.

Pollinators take over after sundown. Moths rise from beneath leaves and from cracks in the mineral skin, their wings narrow and densely furred to reduce water loss. Most fly only a short distance. They find flowers by odor and by the faint difference in warmth between a blossom and the surrounding ground. Their mouthparts are short; the flowers are correspondingly low and deep-chambered, holding nectar close to their centers. Pollination occurs face-first, with the moth forcing its head through brushes of pale pollen.

Several moths never drink as adults. Their brief aboveground lives are paid for entirely by roots eaten in the larval stage. They emerge, mate, lay eggs near the scent of the proper plant, and die before the following noon. Their bodies are collected by builders, spiders, and seed-eating birds. Nothing winged remains in the open for long.

The basin’s spiders do not make broad aerial webs. There is too much dust. They spin low silk thresholds between stems or line cracks where moving grains betray approaching prey. One common kind builds a short tube against the shaded side of an insect wall and decorates its entrance with salt flakes. The flakes serve as camouflage, sunshades, and vibration amplifiers. When a beetle crosses them, the spider knows not only that something is present but approximately how heavy it is.

Predators larger than a hand are scarce in any one place, though present almost everywhere.

The lean hunters seen at the edges of herds are long-bodied and low-shouldered. Their paws are wide, furred beneath, and equipped with claws that remain slightly exposed to grip mineral crust. They have narrow faces, sealed nostrils, and pale eyes protected by inner lids. Some resemble cats when they wait and foxes when they move, but their spine flexes less than either. They hunt by steering rather than sprinting.

A hunter chooses a patch of thorn, a line of towers, or a sunken path and approaches at an angle that seems unrelated to the herd. Its presence compresses the nearest grazers. A second approach opens a lane. The predator moves only when the animals move, shaping their flow until a young, old, or dust-blinded individual is separated against a wall. The final rush is short. If it fails, the hunter lies down. It will not spend another body’s worth of water on pride.

These predators live alone for much of the year but inherit dens from generations of other species. A typical den may have begun as a builder chamber, been opened by a rodent, enlarged by a lizard, roofed by roots, and finally scraped wide enough for a nursing hunter. The occupant adds little beyond scent and bones. After it leaves, beetles remove the hide and tendons, insects carry away the dung, and plants germinate in the enriched floor.

There are also deeper predators, seldom visible. Heavy, wedge-headed burrowers follow the moist clay layers and break into colonies from below. Their forelimbs are broad and nearly hairless, their eyes reduced to light-sensitive patches. They feed on nursery comb, fungus, larvae, roots, and anything trapped by the collapse of their digging. Unlike surface animals, they can afford loose skin and thick bodies because the earth buffers them from heat. They may spend their entire lives beneath a single cluster of towers.

Their tunnels are among the first true channels in the basin. Most collapse within a season. Some remain as softened lines where roots gather and rain briefly runs. Colonies build into them. Grazers tread above them. Centuries later, a shallow depression crosses the plain, created by an animal no surface-dweller has ever seen.

Even the plants are inhabited as much as they are alive.

Root crowns contain nurseries of eggs sealed in foam. Succulent stems house larvae that drink without killing their host. Fungal cords connect separate shrubs, carrying chemical warnings and stolen nutrients. Minute animals graze on the fungal cords; other fungi grow inside those animals. Salt crystals harbor films of dark cells that close against dryness and open during the predawn rise in humidity. A flake of crust that appears sterile at noon may contain, by night, a complete grazing community measured in fractions of a millimeter.

The basin therefore has no ordinary background.

What seems to be soil is roof. What seems to be masonry is lung, graveyard, nursery, garden, hunting perch, and mineral spring. A plant patch is a consortium of roots, fungi, sleeping seeds, feeding larvae, scent marks, and old dung. A herd is not merely a collection of grazers but a moving climate: shade beneath bellies, moisture in breath, parasites in fur, seeds on ankles, predators at the perimeter, builders arriving afterward to retrieve what fell.

No inhabitant understands the whole construction. Each responds to a smaller necessity.

A worker cools one hot passage.  
A grazer steps around one thorn.  
A beetle follows one thread of moisture.  
A root enters one loosened seam.  
A hunter turns one herd.  
An owl enlarges one opening.

From these corrections the basin acquires mounds, then ridges; paths, then channels; thickets, then districts. Its creatures do not live against an unfinished landscape. Their bodies are the forces finishing it.

Seen closely enough, the apparent city dissolves into appetite, inheritance, error, and repair.

Seen broadly enough, all those separate lives gather again into a single animal: low, pale, immeasurably extensive, breathing through towers and root beds, moving dust through its chambers, and building beneath itself the ground on which it will continue to live.
