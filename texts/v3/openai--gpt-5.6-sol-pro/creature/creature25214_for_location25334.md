At first, the people of the Underleaf resemble workers seen through bad weather.

They pass beneath the lamps in oil-dark coats, bent under tools and bundles. Their heads are hooded against drips and wing swarms. Their limbs look thick, their backs irregular. One hears the knock of clogs on rootwood, the brushing of fiber capes, the small metallic music of hooks and buckles. In the obscurity they might be miners, factory hands, railway porters—the survivors of whatever industry first descended into the cavern.

Closer, the distinctions become less reliable.

Some of the apparent coats are not clothing but layered growths: waxed moss, overlapping scales, colonies of flat leaves trained to shed water. A hump may be a pack, a breathing organ, or a garden rooted into a harness worn since childhood. What looks like a hood may open its own stomata when the rain begins. Pale tendrils disappear beneath collars. Beetles move freely in the seams of sleeves and are brushed away only when they belong to the wrong species.

The townspeople call themselves the Kept, though the word also means *those who continue* and *those continually tended*. They are short enough to pass through old service corridors without stooping, broad through the chest, and difficult to count correctly in a crowd. Their usual posture is forward-leaning, compensating for the constant give of the streets. Their feet are wide and flexible. Most go barefoot on dry roots, feeling public signals through thick pads of scarred skin, but wear iron-rimmed sandals on catwalks.

Their skin comes in the colors of things kept from daylight: clay-gray, root-pale, amber, brown-black, the faded green of a cut stem. It is seldom bare. Fine vascular markings spread across it with age, branching especially around the throat, wrists, spine, and ribs. These marks darken when rain is near and flush visibly during speech. Whether they are inherited organs or symbiotic filaments is a question of vocabulary more than anatomy. A child is born with only a few. By adulthood, everyone carries a private map.

They have large eyes, but sight is not their most trusted sense. Their pupils remain wide even beneath workshop arcs, and many wear translucent inner lids against spores and sparks. Faces are read at close range. Intent is felt through the fingertips: pulse, tendon movement, throat vibration, the tremor of breath through the jaw. Strangers who speak without touching are thought evasive. Intimates may hold an entire conversation with forearms pressed together, inaudible even to someone standing beside them.

Their names are rhythms.

A person may possess a spoken workshop name for convenience, but the name recognized by law and kin is tapped against wood or transmitted wrist to wrist. It includes a base pattern received in infancy and alterations earned over a lifetime. Apprentices add a light return stroke. Pump workers carry a syncopation modeled on the engine phase in which they qualified. A person who survives a major crushing removes one beat and leaves its interval empty. The dead are named by striking only those absences.

No one in the Underleaf is entirely solitary. Each citizen carries a *keeping*: a deliberate community of smaller lives maintained on or within the body.

A kiln tender’s keeping may consist of heat-eating mites along the scalp, lung moss cultivated behind the collarbones, and a thumb-sized shell animal that cleans resin from the ears. Cistern workers host translucent leeches that warn of bad water by stiffening. Mechanics encourage wax lice beneath their nails; the lice emerge to feed on corrosion and can detect a cracked bearing before its sound changes. Couriers wear vibration-sensitive larvae in hollow beads against the ankle. If the larvae curl, something large is moving on the road ahead.

These creatures are not pets. Nor are they merely tools. They are inherited, exchanged at marriages, prescribed during illness, and mourned when lost. To say that someone is untrustworthy is to say their keeping will not stay with them.

Children begin almost uncolonized. They are smooth-skinned, loud, and astonishingly dry. For their first years they sleep in netted rooms above the floor swarms and wear caps to prevent moths from nesting in their soft hair. Their first public ceremony is not naming but inoculation. Relatives touch them with scraps of household moss, harmless eggs, throat cultures, and sap from the family doorframe. For several days the child fever-dreams while these lives establish their territories. Afterward, the family listens to the new rhythms in the child’s breathing and chooses which name-pattern fits.

The elderly are more garden than garment. Barklike ridges thicken over their joints. Their vascular markings develop tiny leaves in humid weather. Some must be pruned. Some carry old symbionts no younger keeper can support, and visitors come to them for vanished yeasts, antique medicinal molds, or a breed of cleaning beetle remembered by only one household. An elder may sit apparently asleep beside a warm pipe while six apprentices carefully harvest remedies from the folds of their coat.

Death is rarely a single moment. The Kept distinguish between the stopping of speech, the departure of the keeping, and the body’s acceptance by the root floor. These may occur days apart. A corpse is washed during the next rain and placed in a shallow cradle cut into a living buttress. Useful symbionts are coaxed away with heat and familiar tapping. The cradle then closes gradually. Years later, the person’s mineral buckles and tooth caps may appear embedded in a handrail or threshold some distance away.

The Kept share the town with animals that have also, in their fashion, become citizens.

Draft beasts know established freight routes and refuse newly cut roads before surveyors discover why they are unsafe. The oldest wear brass route plates bolted to their harness gardens. Market law grants them right of passage, right of browsing at designated verges, and the right to lie down without explanation during Root Turn. Drivers do not claim to own them. They own agreements with particular family lines of beast, renewed through feeding, grooming, and remembered paths.

Tunnel hounds belong to boiler crews collectively. Hairless, eyeless, and long as benches, they sense underground pressure through fan-shaped bones in their jaws. A sleeping hound may raise its head minutes before a pipe bursts. Their handlers paint warnings on their flanks in mineral paste, turning each animal into a slowly wandering noticeboard. They are affectionate toward machinery and often press themselves against engine housings with a tenderness they seldom show people.

The cistern fish are treated with greater caution. They are old, irritable, and perhaps more aware than anyone admits. Their whiskers explore the drainage network for hundreds of paces, entering kitchens, baths, and condensation traps through openings no wider than thread. During rain they exchange signals by striking water and pipe. Cistern keepers insist these patterns contain judgments about weather, pump condition, and human behavior. A fish denied its customary offerings may cause every drain in a district to clog at once.

Even the swarms have status. The polishing grazers are public cleaners until they cross a marked sill, at which point they become pests and may be swept into feed hoppers. Cable moths are protected during mating weeks because their shed wings are used in pressure membranes. Copper beetles belong to whichever switchbox they occupy, not to the owner of the building around it. Arguments over their sorted hoards have outlasted generations.

Then there are the plants, although in the Underleaf this category is too large to be useful.

The great trunk organisms are addressed as districts, landlords, weather, and ancestors. They do not answer in any manner the Kept agree upon, but they accept some structures and crush others. They alter sap pressure around habitual foot traffic. They open dry hollows around sustained heat. They sometimes seal a household in overnight without apparent provocation, then produce a new passage months later leading somewhere advantageous. Each has a temperament inferred from centuries of survival around it.

The eastern trunk is said to prefer percussion and tolerate iron. The cistern buttress dislikes flame but will bear enormous weight. The organism beneath the old foundry slowly extrudes foreign objects from its bark, so lost tools reappear there after decades, polished smooth and warm. Families negotiate with these beings through repeated use: walking the same stair, warming the same wall, tapping before cutting. Consent is assumed only while growth remains favorable.

Priests, where the word applies, are called Listeners. They tend no altar. They lie against selected roots for entire pump cycles with cheek and sternum pressed to the wood, recording shifts too slow or deep for ordinary feet. Their bodies are lean, their keepings sparse. Many have deliberately scarred channels into their ribs to improve conduction. They advise where a house may be enlarged, when a road should move, and whether an unfamiliar tapping belongs to machinery, animal, root strain, or something beyond the sealed boreholes.

No Listener gives a certain answer about the tapping below.

The town’s most respected people are not rulers but maintainers: pump stewards, street walkers, rain callers, symbiont keepers, bridge looseners, hatch wardens. Authority belongs to whoever can keep a necessary rhythm going. A council session is conducted around a shared beam, each speaker laying down a pattern through a weighted mallet. Interrupting means physically disrupting the beat and is reserved for emergencies or profound insult.

Their criminals are punished with stillness.

A minor offender may be forbidden to operate machinery for a shift, forced to watch others maintain what they neglected. Severe sentences involve the sealing of tools, the removal of door privileges, or confinement in a room where every surface is already occupied and nothing responds to the prisoner’s touch. Exile is rare. Beyond the lamps, a person may live for some time, but without a recognized rhythm the streets do not warn them, the beasts do not yield, and the town begins at once to digest their possessions.

The Kept fear stillness, yet they do not value motion for its own sake. Frenzy belongs to hatchings and panics. Proper life has recurrence. A good household door opens often enough to remain a door. A good friendship is renewed before its interval becomes uncertain. A good machine makes a sound that can be remembered in the body.

When artificial rain falls, distinctions briefly sharpen. The people throw back their hoods. Their gardened clothing unfolds to drink. Mites and beetles surface along their shoulders. Children run onto the root plazas with mouths open, while adults stand beneath gutters filling skin bags and ceramic jars. Draft beasts kneel. Moths burst from the cables. Cistern fish batter the dark water into foam.

For seven minutes the whole settlement glistens, and nearly every living thing reveals where it has been hiding.

Then the valves close. The last drops strike iron. Hoods rise, leaves fold, shells settle, workers return to the belts and valves. The streets resume their minute shifting beneath thousands of practiced feet.

From far away, the Underleaf seems to be inhabited by a town.

From within, it is harder to say. The people maintain the engines; the engines sustain the rain; the rain feeds the roots; the roots bear the streets; the streets carry the signals by which the people remain people. Remove any one of these, and what survives would have another name.

So the Kept do not say they live in the Underleaf.

They say they are among the things that keep it from closing.
