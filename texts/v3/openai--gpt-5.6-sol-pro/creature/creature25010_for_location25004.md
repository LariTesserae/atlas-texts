At first, the inhabitants seem to be the plants.

The frost-leaves that turn at dawn appear purposeful enough. The migrating groves seem to travel, however slowly. Vines seek water, roots operate latches, pillar-trees work looms and cutting frames. It is easy to imagine each trunk as a patient artisan and each garden as a household.

But no single plant contains enough of an inhabitant.

Cut down a pillar-tree and the work does not cease. The loom waits through several winters while neighboring roots thicken into its empty guides. A vine arrives from a courtyard wall and takes up the tensioning of the cords. Moss seals the cut stump. Eventually the loom resumes, advancing at nearly the same rate as before. Nothing has replaced the tree because the tree was never the whole worker.

Nor is the city itself exactly alive. Large portions of it may go dormant without apparent injury. A district can lose every standing structure to frost and still preserve its habits in buried channels, seed vaults, mineral stains, and roots thin as hair. Centuries later, condensation may return to the basin. The channels fill. Long-dry tissues swell. Walls rise where pressure beneath fitted slabs pushes them upright. The district begins again—not rebuilt according to a plan stored in one place, but remembered by thousands of partial inclinations.

The inhabitants live between these scales.

They are arrangements.

One may consist of a stand of black reeds, three linked cisterns, a row of porous towers, the roots beneath six streets, and a frost field several kilometers away. Another occupies a single courtyard but extends through a doorway into a season that has not yet reached the surrounding city. A third has no fixed territory at all. It passes from grove to grove as synchronized flowering, leaving one basin when the last capsules fire and appearing decades later where their seeds take root.

Their boundaries are not visible, and they do not always agree.

Two inhabitants may use the same tree. One receives the tree’s annual swelling as pressure in a pottery frame; another reads the same expansion as a count of winters. A reservoir can belong to the being that maintains its lining, the being that stores patterns in its water level, and the being whose distant roots drink from its leaks. These overlapping lives do not seem to experience contradiction. Possession here is only participation.

If they have bodies, their bodies are civic.

Their harder tissues are roads, retaining walls, lintels, clay pipes, stone pegs, and the black polished beds of temporary rivers. Their softer tissues are root mats, moss, wax seals, fungal cable, dormant seeds, and films of green beneath ice. Water carries impulses between the two. Frost supplies movement. Sunlight provides not only energy but orientation: a way for widely separated parts to agree on direction.

They have no central organs. What resembles sensing occurs everywhere.

A pressure drop enters the hollow stems of a fern field and is transformed into a volley of seeds. The recoil tugs buried fibers. Those fibers shift pebbles in a counting court. Elsewhere, perhaps years later, the altered pattern exposes dark stone to sunlight. The warmed stone melts a plug of ice, releasing water into a capillary rope. A gate closes in another district.

The inhabitant has perceived the coming weather and responded, though no part of it has known the whole event.

They sense distance through tension, time through growth, rain through weight, and cold through the expansion of trapped water. They sense the unreliable geometry of the plateau by losing contact with themselves. When a road folds and a root that should travel east emerges from a wall in the west, the resulting strain is marked in rings of abnormal wood. Groves gather around such discontinuities. White lichen is grown where the connection remains dependable; red lichen where it shifts after rain.

Their thought, if it is thought, is made of maintenance.

A cleared gutter is a preference retained across generations of moss. A repaired wall is an answer to pressure. The separation of seeds into beds is a distinction repeated until the distinction becomes part of the soil. They do not contemplate a cistern and then decide to close it. Rather, dust gathers, light diminishes, a shaded vine shortens, a counterweight descends, and the lid turns on its stone pivot. The decision is distributed through the circumstances.

This does not make them mindless. It makes their minds difficult to locate.

The counting courts are where they come closest to being legible. Each settlement possesses one, but the courts may be facets of a single faculty. Their stones move in response to frost and are returned by roots. Neither movement perfectly cancels the other. Small discrepancies accumulate: a pale stone displaced half its width, a black stone rotated to expose a vein, a row gradually becoming an arc.

Some patterns take thousands of years to complete. Some are undone before completion by a local acceleration of time. Some repeat from city to city despite the immense distances between them. A pattern begun in the western basins may appear at the oldest city before the western stones have moved, as though the court on the summit remembers the answer earlier than the question.

These patterns may be speech.

If so, the inhabitants do not speak in sentences. They speak in alignments, stored water, flowerings, opened routes, and the timing of shade. One city darkens a line of reflective roofs at sunset. Three centuries later, another city alters the species planted along an avenue. A migrating wood changes direction. A door in the summit city opens onto green cloud.

The exchange may constitute a greeting, an argument, or the recognition of a shared condition.

The upright branching forms glimpsed through the old doorway are not their ancestors in any simple sense. They resemble portable concentrations of the same life: temporary knots in which roots, tools, vessels, and purpose were gathered tightly enough to move at a visible pace.

They have narrow trunks divided into many jointed boughs. Some boughs end in grips of coiling fiber; others in fans of translucent leaf or clusters of stone beads. They carry baskets not because they possess hands and belongings, but because the baskets are detachable portions of themselves—seed, mineral, water, and memory enclosed for transit.

When unobserved, they cross the avenue. When watched, they become arrangements of wood.

Perhaps direct attention fixes them into the pace of the observer. Perhaps they move only in moments omitted from ordinary time. Perhaps they are merely the city’s recollection of mobility, preserved behind a doorway where the past remains active.

No such form walks the present plateau.

The inhabitants now travel by propagation. A traveling self may begin as a root advancing through a buried conduit. Where the conduit breaks, the root divides into threads and follows condensation along the stone. It may take forty years to cross a room. At the far wall it thickens, pries open a seam, and enters a street that sometimes leads to another city.

There it produces no replica of the organism from which it came. It recruits what already exists.

Local moss becomes its moisture-sensing surface. Old paving becomes support. A dormant mechanism contributes motion. Frost-leaves furnish pumps. The traveling inhabitant acquires a new body from the district, as a tune might acquire new notes while retaining its intervals.

For this reason, they are not born. They assemble.

They do not die, either, though they can become unable to continue as themselves. Drought may isolate their parts until their coordinated cycles fall apart. A temporal pocket may trap one process in a single instant while the rest proceeds without it. A shifting doorway may carry an essential root network somewhere unreachable. The remaining components are then taken up by other arrangements.

What is lost is not material but recurrence.

A certain gate no longer closes when the silver rains appear. A garden flowers without changing the pattern in its court. A cistern fills, but no distant roof flexes in answer. The stones that once formed a coherent sequence continue to move, yet the sequence has ended.

Their equivalent of grief may be the preservation of useless work.

Some cities maintain channels that no longer carry water. Groves send runners to sealed walls. Empty loom frames continue advancing ratchets, though no fiber has been stretched across them for ages. Every autumn, branches cut reeds and draw the bundles beneath roofs where the previous bundles remain untouched.

These acts are not errors. They persist too accurately. They may be the shape left by a relationship after one participant has vanished.

Other activities resemble curiosity.

Roots gather around doorframes whose destinations change. Frost mechanisms repeatedly open chambers in which time moves at dangerous rates. Seed capsules are positioned to fire into alleys that have narrowed beyond entry. On the central summit, fine roots spread over the reflective stone and stop where other stars appear. They form rings around those impossible lights, withdrawing when the ordinary sky returns.

The inhabitants do not avoid uncertainty. They cultivate interfaces with it.

They place gates at the boundaries of temporal climates. They grow marks that migrate with unstable routes. They extend capillary ropes down stairways deeper than the plateau. Their oldest tissues enter the dark doorway smelling of wet soil, while younger offshoots descend toward every city.

Possibly all these inhabitants are temporary organs of one greater being. Yet if the plateau is one organism, it is not a unified one. It contains competing rhythms, forgotten connections, local intentions, and parts that misread one another. It may be an organism in the same sense that a civilization is an organism: truly, but not exclusively.

Nor are the cities merely organs. Each possesses habits found nowhere else.

One city favors circular watercourses and closes every eastern gate at dusk. One grows cloudy ice into walls full of trapped bubbles, preserving sequences of ancient air. Another dismantles its outer streets before each rain and reassembles them afterward in slightly altered patterns. In a northern basin, all maintenance pauses when stars appear at noon. In the south, groves turn their flat crowns toward sounds that have not yet been released.

These differences endure. They amount to temperament.

The inhabitants may therefore be understood as communities of processes that have learned to remain recognizable. Their selves are neither bodies nor souls but continuities of response. They are what keeps happening in a particular way.

At evening, their awareness broadens.

Cooling stone contracts. Fibers tighten. Water withdraws into insulated depth. Frost begins at the edges of translucent leaves and travels inward along branching veins. Across the plateau, countless small changes enter root, channel, lever, court, and reservoir.

The cities become sensitive to one another through the cold.

A gate settling in a distant district alters tension in an underground cable. A reflective roof dims. A stone turns. Beneath the oldest city, the roots crossing the summit tighten by an amount too small to see. Doorframes stand open onto gardens, avenues, darkness, and other ages of the same land.

Then a knock, stored long ago, is released into the thin air.

Nothing turns toward it. Nothing startles. There is no startle here.

But frost advances through a field of clear leaves, and one after another they change their angle to the stars. A valve opens beneath an empty street. Water descends into a reservoir whose depth cannot be reconciled with the world above it.

Far away, a grove alters the direction of its growth.

This is how the inhabitants hear.
