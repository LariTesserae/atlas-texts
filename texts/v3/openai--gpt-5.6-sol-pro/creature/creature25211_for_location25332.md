The inhabitants are not, at first glance, a distinctive people. Nothing in them suggests a separate species or an ancient adaptation to the basin. They have lived here only a few generations, and many still carry features associated with places beyond the marsh: dark or ruddy skin, heavy brows, narrow noses, hair that curls tightly or hangs straight when dry. But almost nothing about them remains dry for long. Hair is cropped close, braided against the scalp, or plastered with mineral oil. Hands and feet are pale at the creases from constant wetting. Fingernails are kept blunt because long nails catch beneath roof laces and channel grates.

What distinguishes them is posture.

They rarely stand as though the ground were certain. Even at rest, their knees remain loose and their weight slightly forward, one hand near a wall. They sleep on tilting floors without rolling. They can feel a subtle change in a stair through the soles of their feet and notice when a lintel has lowered by the width of a fingernail. Outsiders walk heavily and reach for balance too late. Basin-born children learn to place a foot, test, and only then trust their weight.

Most adults are compactly muscled rather than large. There is little room for sweeping motion, and their strength develops through carrying jars close to the chest, bracing walls with the shoulder, turning clay, hauling themselves through maintenance slots, and holding awkward positions for long periods. They work with their whole bodies. A potter’s hip steadies the wheel plate. A drain cleaner hooks one foot behind a bridge brace. Two builders may press back-to-back in a narrow lane, one levering a shale slab while the other keeps a damp wall from bowing inward.

Their clothing is layered, close-fitting, and endlessly repaired. The inner layer is soft marsh fiber, beaten until it resembles coarse felt. Over it they wear short wraps coated with snail wax, clay oil, or rendered fungus grease. Long skirts and loose sleeves would drag in channels, so garments end at the knee and elbow. Calves and forearms are commonly bound with removable strips of oiled cloth. Kiln workers wear aprons of ceramic scales threaded on cord, which click quietly when they crouch. Almost everyone has a hood, though many prefer to let the rain strike their faces rather than surrender peripheral vision in a narrow lane.

Color comes from the place itself: smoke-black, iron red, shale blue, salt white, the bruised violet made from mud-snails. Bright dyes arrive by boat in quantities small enough to stain only cords, cuffs, or the edge of a hood. Such color is rarely wasted on a whole garment. A thread of yellow at the wrist may identify a household, a kiln lineage, an obligation, or a person promised safe passage through another family’s rooms.

They own little that is not useful, but usefulness here is broad. A beautiful jar is considered more reliable because someone has watched it closely. A well-shaped lamp niche keeps darkness from accumulating in a corner. A necklace of drilled roof fragments may also be dismantled to patch an eave. Children wear counters of clay and shell that become game pieces, weights, drain markers, and, in emergencies, wedges.

The basic social unit is not quite the family and not quite the house. It is the **course**: the group of people responsible for a connected passage of water from its first catchment to its final settling bowl. A course may include blood relatives, spouses, former spouses, apprentices, elderly neighbors, and anyone whose sleeping place rests against the relevant walls. Two people may call one another kin because the same roof water passes above their beds. Marriage matters, but shared drainage may matter more.

This makes residence complicated. A person belongs to several overlapping structures: the chamber where they sleep, the hearth whose warmth they receive, the cistern from which they drink, the kiln that fired their household vessels, and the watercourse they must clear during rain. Arguments over inheritance concern duties as much as objects. To inherit a room is to inherit its cracks. To inherit a jar is to inherit the roof that fills it.

No one possesses water outright. They possess the obligation to recognize it.

The most respected townspeople are therefore not necessarily kiln masters or traders, but those with long memories for flow: older men and women who know which white crust is harmless, which warmth beneath a floor means a deep seep has moved, and which new trickle is merely rain finding an old route. They are called to taste droplets, press cheeks against walls, or sit through a night listening to a cistern’s changing note. Their authority lasts only while their observations prove accurate. The basin is considered capable of correcting any person, regardless of rank.

There is no single ruler. Decisions are made by gatherings of affected courses, usually in a kiln yard after firing, when the ground is warm and people can sit without stiffening. The town does maintain several permanent offices. There are ridge gatherers, Rain Court listeners, hollow watchers, kiln tenders, and recutters of the outer steps. None is cleanly separated from ordinary labor. A Rain Court listener may spend the morning scraping snails from bowls; a ridge gatherer may also deliver children.

Children are everywhere but seldom underfoot. Before they can speak clearly, they learn the thumbprint signs for dangerous water. They make miniature channels in trays of wet clay, tilt them, obstruct them, and watch how the water divides. Their games involve passing silently through the town by different levels, or identifying households from the sound of runoff alone. They are discouraged from hiding in sealed places, not because hiding is wrong, but because an unwitnessed hollow is thought to invite filling.

Adolescence is marked by a first solitary rain watch. The young person spends a night beside a chosen channel and must report every change: temperature, smell, speed, grit, sound, brightness. The account is not expected to be dramatic. In fact, embellishment is frowned upon. Attention is the virtue being tested. A good report might be no more than: *The second thread stopped before midnight. The wall eye clouded. Three white midges gathered where no lamp was burning.*

Names often arise from such observations. Formal names given at birth are used by distant traders and in memorial recitations, but daily names change. Someone may be called Split Cup, Warm Step, Three Knocks, Blackwort Edge, or the equivalent of “She Who Heard the Lower Jar.” These are not necessarily jokes or titles. A name records where a person has become useful.

Speech is quiet and economical in the lanes. People announce themselves with clicks of the tongue, taps against ceramic, or brief humming notes that travel better than words through wet passages. Each course has its own approach signal. Loud voices are reserved for the ridge, the marsh, and emergencies. Indoors, raised speech suggests either panic or contempt for the walls.

Their music follows the same habits. They use water jars, clay clappers, taut fiber strings, breath flutes made from ceramic rather than reed, and low throat tones sustained beneath the sound of rain. Songs are rarely performed from beginning to end. Verses are inserted between tasks and may take days to complete. Some kiln songs are timed to the expansion of firing clay. Rain Court music is partly composed by rearranging catch jars so that droplets strike different depths.

Food is soft, dark, mineral, and often sour. Blackwort is eaten raw in thin folds or fermented into dense cakes. Snails are steamed in nested pots, their liquor saved for dye and sealant. Marsh fungi are dried above warm seep channels. Eels are not eaten, but traders sometimes bring fish, salt fat, seed meal, or strips of smoked meat. These are distributed first to children, the sick, kiln crews, and those keeping overnight vigil in clay pits. Meals are taken from shared shallow bowls because deep individual bowls hide cracks and waste heat.

Hospitality means giving a visitor a dry ledge, mild roof water, and an explanation of nearby channels. A guest who blocks a groove with a heel, moves a jar without asking, or closes an eye opening may be expelled immediately. Conversely, anyone who notices a new leak is owed food, even if they are an enemy.

Illness is understood through balance, warmth, and flow. Deep luminous water is administered in drops to those whose bodies have become “too still”: the feverless, the unresponsive, infants who do not cry, elders whose extremities remain cold. It may be medicinal, mildly toxic, or both. Healers use heated ceramic pads, snail dyes to trace swelling, suction cups, mineral poultices, and careful changes of sleeping inclination. Patients are often moved between rooms as treatment. A cough that worsens near one wall may ease beside another.

Death presents the most difficult kind of stillness.

The dead are not buried beneath the town; hidden decay would alter the waters. Nor is fuel abundant enough for ordinary cremation. Bodies are carried beyond the ridge on clay-coated litters and placed on low marsh platforms where cold, mineral water and insects gradually reduce them. Later, cleaned bones are returned. Some are ground into temper for a single memorial vessel. Others are set into visible wall niches, never sealing a conduit and never placed where runoff cannot touch daylight. The deceased is said to remain useful not as a spirit imprisoned in the wall, but as part of the town’s continued openness.

They fear entombment more than death. Their stories are full of rooms that filled too quickly, passages that narrowed while someone slept, and houses that mistook their occupants for empty space. Yet these tales are not told only to frighten. In many, the trapped person survives by making a sound the town recognizes: tapping a wedge rhythm, singing a course signal, or diverting one thin thread of water until it appears elsewhere.

The wall toads receive an almost familial tolerance. Residents learn their individual habits and sometimes name them after structural features: Low Arch, Crooked Drain, Fifth Step. A toad that remains in one channel for years may be consulted in the sense that its movements are watched. If it climbs unexpectedly, the lower drain is inspected. If it buries itself, a cold spell or pressure change is expected. No one claims the animal predicts events. They say only that it lives closer to the wall’s knowledge.

Blind eels are treated with greater solemnity. Whoever finds one carries it in both wet hands, and work along that course stops until it is returned to the seep. The finder then stays awake for part of the night. This is not regarded as worship. It is courtesy toward a warning.

The people’s central moral distinction is not between clean and unclean, nor even between truthful and false. It is between what is **attended** and what is **left hidden**. A mistake openly marked can be repaired. A crack disguised with plaster is a serious offense. A debt acknowledged may remain unpaid for years; a forgotten duty is intolerable. Their walls display failures deliberately: patches in contrasting clay, old channels left visible after diversion, broken roof scales incorporated edge-out so that their history can be felt by the hand.

This gives them a peculiar patience with change and a deep suspicion of perfection. They admire objects that can be opened, examined, relaced, rebalanced, or repurposed. A flawless sealed vessel is beautiful but unsettling. A house that has never shifted is watched more closely than one that settles a little each season.

From a distance, they may appear subdued—small wet figures passing among clay walls, heads bent under hoods, hands always occupied. Up close, they are intensely observant and not especially solemn. Their humor is full of leaks, leaning relatives, pompous jars, and people who claim to understand water better than water understands itself. They play tricks by changing the pitch of an empty catch cup or leaving a tiny clay face peering through a wall eye. Kiln workers sculpt obscene figures inside repair cavities where only the next repair crew will find them.

They are neither resigned to the basin nor masters of it. They do not imagine that enough labor will make the ground obedient. Their achievement is more provisional: they have learned to live without requiring obedience.

At night, when the sleeping tiers are unfolded and the last channels have been checked, bodies fill nearly every chamber. Some lie above cisterns, some beside warm walls, some in slings that sway with minute movements of the structure. A child may sleep with one hand resting in a groove so that changed flow will wake her. An elder may keep a jar close enough to hear its water settle. Beneath them, the basin presses upward. Around them, the ridge eases inward.

The town survives because its people never entirely surrender themselves to sleep. There is always someone listening: to a drain, a kiln, a toad, a wall, a jar, or the patient tapping of a neighbor correcting the angle of the world.
