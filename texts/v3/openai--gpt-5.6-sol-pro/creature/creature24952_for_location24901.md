The people of the Low Galleries are short more often than tall, though not from any single inheritance. Tall children learn to stoop early. Tall strangers strike their heads, fall from narrow stairs, and tire of turning sideways. Over generations, the Galleries favor compact bodies, strong backs, flexible knees, and hands that can work where the eyes cannot follow.

They are not uniformly pale. Daylight reaches them, and many spend their lives beneath it: stall keepers in the bright courts, gourd tenders, potters who judge glazes by noon color, and shaft workers whose faces are browned by light reflected down from above. Others are ash-colored from plaster dust or darkened by kiln heat and oil smoke. A person’s complexion often changes by district and occupation. So does the way they stand. Water carriers lean slightly away from the hip that bears the jar. Plasterers have powerful thumbs. Drain crawlers develop shoulders narrow enough to slip between old pipes. Elderly roof tenders possess a permanent, graceful bend, as if still passing under beams even in the highest vaults.

Almost everyone bears small scars on the crown of the head.

They dress in layers that can be removed, wrung out, traded, or used to stop a leak. The innermost clothes are soft woven grass or goat hair. Over them go short tunics, aprons with deep pockets, shoulder wraps, and strips of waxed cloth during rain. Long robes are impractical. Loose sleeves catch on tile corners and cage hooks. Bright dyes are precious, but bright patches are common: a square of saffron cloth at one knee, blue cord around an arm, red stitching along a hood. In a place of partial views, these fragments identify people before faces do.

Their footwear is soft-soled. Hard heels would make too much noise and slip on wet brick. Children often go barefoot until they are old enough to carry a lamp.

No one belongs simply to a house. A house is too temporary a thing. People belong to a chain of rooms, a cistern share, a work bench, a cooking smoke, and a set of remembered routes.

A family might sleep in three chambers that do not touch one another. The grandparents keep the dry room beyond the onion beds. Two sisters and their children occupy a former drain court reached by ladder. The goats are housed between them, above a potter’s firing cupboard. They gather in a fourth place to eat, unless rain has entered there, in which case they gather beneath a stair claimed jointly by six households. Kinship in the Galleries is therefore recited as a path.

“I am Sela of the Blue Cup, past the split pillar, under the fowl walk.”

“I am Torren, who eats at Sela’s fire.”

“I am Neme, born during the third filling of the Bent Cistern, sleeping now behind the tile of seven fish.”

Names of this kind change without shame. A person driven from a room by dampness may acquire a new name before the old plaster dries.

Children learn the Galleries through errands. Their first journeys are made with a hand on an adult’s belt. Later they are sent alone to fetch embers, salt, fungus cakes, lamp grease, or news of a rising cistern. They memorize turns by touch and sound: three cool pillars, a warm wall, the smell of goats, the floor that trembles beneath pounding grain. They know which holes are windows into neighboring rooms and which open into drops. They know not to step on pale plaster, which may be fresh, or on black plaster, which may conceal rot.

Their games imitate maintenance. They build miniature arches from broken tile, channel cups of water through dust, and compete to identify districts from echoes made inside jars. After rain they race leaf boats along the gutters until an adult catches them fouling a drinking run. The cruelest common prank is to tap from inside a cupboard while someone listens at the other side of a wall.

They stop doing this after their first real answer.

Listening is a respected skill. Before an opening is cut, a wall-listener places cheek, ear, and sometimes teeth against the plaster. They strike the surface with a padded mallet and interpret what returns: loose fill, hollow room, water, timber, breathing space. The best listeners claim they can distinguish a rat nest from hanging cloth and a sealed cistern from a chamber occupied in silence. They mark walls with small signs in charcoal and red slip: an ear, a wave, a closed eye, three fingers spread.

An ear means *listen again*.

A wave means *pressure*.

A closed eye means *leave sleeping*.

The spread hand means that someone has heard tapping from beyond but cannot tell whether it was deliberate.

There are people on the other side of some walls. This is known. They may be neighbors reached by no accepted route, or families belonging to a district whose passages closed generations ago. Sometimes trade is conducted through a hole no wider than a thumb. A coil of bronze wire passes one way; a packet of medicinal mold returns. Names are exchanged. Songs are compared. Then a season later the hole smells only of wet brick, and no tapping comes back.

These unseen neighbors are called the Counterwallers, though every district uses the same word for those on both sides. A Counterwaller, by definition, is someone whose wall is your wall.

The Low Galleries have no single ruler. Authority collects where water does.

Every dependable shaft has keepers. Every large cistern has measurers, witnesses, cleaners, and at least one person charged with remembering its lowest safe line. These offices pass neither strictly through families nor by election. They adhere to competence, debt, and presence. The one who wakes first to the sound of rain may command a hall full of people older and richer than themselves. During a downpour, argument becomes dangerous, so the rain captains carry white mallets and are obeyed at once.

In dry weather their power recedes. Then plaster guilds, herders, kiln owners, fungus growers, route wardens, and market callers recover their voices.

Water theft is punished harshly, but not always by violence. The worst offenders are declared “upstreamless.” No one must share with them from a private cup, no one must warn them of a new seep, and their jars are filled only after animals have drunk. The sentence can last three days or three years. Few endure it for long. Most confess, offer labor, and spend a season cleaning first-flow gutters with bare hands.

Ownership of walls is subtler. A family may own the face of a wall but not its thickness. They may plaster it, paint it, hang shelves from shallow pegs, and warm themselves against it. They may not drive a spike deeper than the first knuckle without calling witnesses. Beams belong partly to whatever they support. Drains belong to everyone below them. Light belongs to no one, though mirrors and bright plates may be inherited.

There are frequent quarrels over shadows.

The inhabitants eat grain porridge, onions, beans, fungus, herbs, eggs, goat milk, and meat on rare occasions. Fowl are more often eaten than goats. Crabs from the drains are toasted whole and ground with bitter greens. Children catch large beetles after rain and sell them by the string to keepers of birds and fish. Nothing edible is despised merely because it emerged from a wall, but everyone knows which wall it emerged from.

Cooking is done in small clay ovens whose heat is passed onward. An oven may warm a sleeping shelf above, dry tiles behind, and draw air through a fungus cupboard before its smoke enters a shaft. A badly placed fire is considered not only wasteful but antisocial. The ideal hearth performs four or five tasks and inconveniences no downstream neighbor.

Their potters are among their most honored craftspeople. A jar is never merely a jar. Its curve determines whether it can be carried around a particular pillar. Its lip decides whether a child can pour without loss. Its glaze may resist mineral bloom, encourage cooling, or reveal contamination by changing color. Many vessels are made with one flat side so they can stand against crowded walls. Others are narrow enough to be lowered through old pipe throats. Drinking cups often have ridged handles, allowing their owners to identify them in darkness.

They decorate useful things because almost everything remains useful longer than its maker lives. Painted animals run around cistern rims. Tiny faces peer from drain covers. Lovers scratch promises into roof braces. A common image shows a room inside a room inside a room, each with a little lamp. The innermost chamber is left blank.

Their songs are built for echo. Soloists sing short phrases and pause while the architecture answers. In some vaults the returning note arrives altered enough to form a second voice. Good singers know where to stand so that a lament becomes a chorus or a work chant travels around a corner. Drums are uncommon and disliked near old walls. Rhythm is kept with palms on thighs, ceramic clappers, or mallets struck against sound bricks set aside for that purpose.

A person courting another does not bring flowers. Flowers are too rare and too implicated in food. They bring a route: a newly discovered way between two familiar places, cleared of hooks and marked with chalk. To shorten someone’s daily journey by seven turns is an act of tenderness. To fit a lamp niche at their shoulder height is almost a proposal.

Marriages, when formally recognized, are agreements between water shares as much as between people. The pair exchanges stoppered cups. Each drinks from the other’s household cistern, then pours the last mouthful into a common bowl. Divorces are marked by returning the cups unbroken. Breaking one is a declaration of grievance and can divide a passage for years.

Birth takes place near warmth and water but away from the busiest echoes. Newborns are rubbed with oil and a little fine brick dust, said to help them “take the room.” For the first several days they are not given a route-name. They are called Little Guest, because infants are thought not yet to have chosen whether to remain.

The dead remain in the Galleries as well, though seldom as bodies. Space is too precious, and damp burial chambers are dangerous. Most are burned in shared kilns over several nights. Their ashes are mixed into memorial tiles, usually no larger than a hand. These are set into places the dead wore smooth in life: beneath a favored bench, beside a cistern, at the turn where they used to call greetings into the market.

Thus the dead are walked on, warmed, splashed, and polished. This is not disrespect. To be sealed in an untouched wall would mean exclusion from the ongoing place.

Some choose instead to enter the repair mortar. Their families carry pinches of ash to several work sites so that one person may become part of an arch, a stair, a goat ramp, and the rim of a public drain. Such dead are said to have “gone broad.”

Religion, if it can be separated from maintenance, concerns continuities rather than origins. The inhabitants do not speak often of whoever first made the Galleries. First makers are as difficult to imagine as first bricks. Their small shrines honor the Hand Before, the Breath Above, the Kept Drop, and the Room Not Opened.

The Hand Before is represented by fingerprints pressed into clay.

The Breath Above has no image; its shrine is a strip of cloth moving in a vent.

The Kept Drop is a covered cup that must never be drunk unless the district’s last cistern fails.

The Room Not Opened is represented by a little sealed door. Children are told there is nothing behind it. Adults answer the same, but less easily.

Not everyone is content below. A few become shaft-climbers. They maintain reflective plates, clear bird nests, replace cords, and ascend farther than ordinary workers dare. They wear narrow harnesses and carry hooked tools between their teeth. When asked what lies above, they give contradictory answers.

One says the shaft narrows until it will admit only light.

Another says there is a roof above the roof, hot enough to burn the palms.

Another claims to have heard open wind, a vast sound with no echo.

A woman named Hevri once descended with a green leaf broader than her face, wet with rain and unlike any plant cultivated below. She would not say where she found it. The leaf was divided into hundreds of pieces, traded, pressed into plaster, steeped as medicine, and finally lost. For years afterward, strangers claimed descent from those who had tasted it.

The shaft-climbers are regarded with admiration and suspicion. They are necessary, but they spend too long where the light has not yet learned the shape of rooms.

There are also those who go downward: drain delvers, warm-duct cleaners, rat hunters, and salvage crews who enter the buried floors exposed by collapse. They return coated in gray mud, carrying old tiles, intact jars, black timber, unfamiliar bones, or tools made from metals no current smith can name. They say lower chambers are hotter and quieter. Some contain architecture built for people of ordinary size. Others have doors only a child could pass through, or stairs whose steps are each as high as a person’s knee.

The delvers insist that the bats below are only bats.

They say this too quickly.

What lives in the Low Galleries, then, is not one people but an accumulation of people, as layered and repaired as the walls around them. Families vanish while their cup shapes remain. Dialects separate across blocked arches and reunite centuries later through a broken drain. Customs migrate by water. Songs arrive through masonry before their singers do. One district paints white circles around every safe doorway; another ties knots in hanging cords; a third refuses to eat mushrooms with forked stems because of a poisoning no one elsewhere remembers.

They are patient without being peaceful, ingenious without imagining themselves inventors, and conservative in matters where a mistake can drown a lower room. They distrust clean plans. A plan drawn with straight passages is a child’s fantasy or a fraud. They prefer knowledge carried in bodies: where to bow, where to brace a hand, where three people cannot pass at once, where a shout travels farther than a lamp.

Above all, they do not consider themselves trapped.

Trapped would imply another place plainly available and a door deliberately shut. The Galleries are the measure by which places are understood. Vastness is frightening because it has no supports. Open sky, if described to them, sounds like a ceiling collapse without end. A field is merely a room with nowhere to hang a shelf. A river is a catastrophic gutter.

Home is brick made smooth by feet. It is a sleeping recess exactly long enough for the body, a familiar drip sounding through the night, a warm pipe behind the back, a neighbor’s voice arriving through two walls, changed but recognizable.

When evening comes, the inhabitants appear by fragments. A hand shields a lamp. Bare feet cross a bridge and vanish behind baskets. A plaster-streaked face leans through an inner door. Someone laughs in a chamber that cannot be seen. A rain captain checks the knots on a cistern rope. Children carry warm bowls from the last column of daylight. High above, swifts climb toward a brightness the people know chiefly by its effects.

Then the lamps spread.

Not all at once, and never evenly. One glows behind a wicker wall, another beneath a stair, three more along the market gutter. Light catches eyes in apertures, goats on ledges, cats between bricks, geckos motionless beside painted saints. Through a thumb-hole in a sealed partition, an unseen neighbor pushes a twist of smoldering fiber.

Someone on this side cups it carefully, gives it breath, and makes another flame.
