At first there appears to be no single inhabitant.

There are only occupations.

The lamp snails clean. The red crabs carry. The blind grazer harvests. Needlefish patrol the boundaries between warm and cold water, turning all at once when the pumps change pitch. Eels sleep inside the conduits with their jaws slightly open, tasting the tower’s electrical dreams. The scaffold ray visits like a season, bringing its own thicket of followers and departing before anything in the chamber can grow accustomed to its shadow.

Even the rooted things seem less like species than duties assumed by matter. Moss insulates the warm pipes. Copper fans measure the current with their opening and closing. The ceiling ribbons comb particles from the flow. The silver vine lays its green palms over fractures too small for the tower’s maintenance systems to notice. It does not mend the metal exactly. It persuades corrosion to happen elsewhere.

Life here is organized by attachment.

The oldest creatures are those that hold most securely. They have hooks, suckers, rootlets, magnetic plates, adhesive secretions, or bodies thin enough to enter a seam and become impossible to remove. Anything born without a means of fastening soon develops one or becomes food. Eggs are laid in screw holes. Larvae knot themselves around cable bundles. Fish sleep biting the stems of plants. Even the crabs tether their burdens with strands pulled from ancient insulation.

Among these inhabitants, mobility is not freedom. It is exposure.

The glass fish live nearest the ceiling, where the current is gentlest. Their bones are fine black lines visible through transparent flesh, and each carries a bright organ near the heart. This light is not for seeing. It flickers in answer to machinery: rapid silver when a valve is about to open, dull violet before the lamps change, white when something large approaches from the abyss. A whole school can become a brief, inverted constellation among the hanging vines.

They feed on drifting spores and on particles shaken loose by the tower’s labor. When alarmed, they enter the hollow cable sleeves tail-first. There they remain in rows, pulsing faintly, until the danger has passed. Sometimes a maintenance form mistakes them for damaged components and attempts to extract them. The fish have learned the tools’ approach patterns. They scatter before the cabinet door has fully opened.

The crabs are less cautious.

Each red crab chooses, early in life, a piece of the tower to carry. A square of mesh, a wafer of ceramic, a curl of painted metal, a tuft of black insulation: whatever it first succeeds in lifting becomes both camouflage and identity. As the crab grows, it adds to this burden. Old individuals move beneath complicated heaps, miniature ruins walking on jointed legs.

They cross the bare floor alone because two meeting crabs will try to exchange pieces, and the exchange often becomes a fight. Their territories are not areas of ground but routes between warm pipes. They remember these routes even after surges rearrange the silt. If a newly opened panel interrupts an established path, a crab may spend days dragging debris around its edge, trying to restore the world to the shape it knows.

The broad grazer is nearly colorless. Its eyes have sealed beneath skin, and its mouth occupies most of the underside of its head. It clings to the wall by six soft fins and moves with such slowness that moss grows along its back. Only the noise reveals its progress: the patient sawing of mineral teeth against microbial crust.

It has grazed the chamber for longer than any fish lives. Circular scars left by its mouth cover the lower wall, some fresh and dark, others already recolonized. Small organisms shelter in the channels between its teeth. Crabs climb onto it to steal loosened flakes. When the pressure gate opens, the grazer seals itself to the metal and becomes immovable. The whole garden streams past it while it continues feeding.

The ribbon predators imitate cables.

They hang vertically near the breach, narrow as straps and nearly as long as the chamber is high. Their skin bears false corrosion, false stamped lettering, even pale marks resembling the mineral traces left by old clamps. They have no visible fins. Movement begins at the head and passes downward in a single soft fold.

Most of the time they are indistinguishable from abandoned wiring. Then a glass fish strays too near, and the “cable” loops around it.

They are hunted in turn by armored fish from the outer face. These arrive noisily, striking balconies and pipe housings with ceramic-crushing jaws. Their scales overlap like fragments of dark tile. Their teeth continue growing throughout life and must be worn down on the tower; an old armored fish can be recognized by the trail of scalloped bites it leaves along railings. They seldom enter far into the garden. The massive fern confuses the electrical sense by which they navigate, and they dislike touching its fronds.

The fern is avoided by almost everything.

It is not poisonous. Several inhabitants eat its spores, and snails graze its stem without harm. Yet animals change direction before entering its shadow. Maintenance forms pause at the outermost fronds as if waiting for permission.

Its trunk disappears into the collapsed grille, but scans made long ago—by machines that no longer report to any accessible system—would have shown that no trunk continues beneath it. Instead, thousands of hairlike filaments enter separate conduits. Some descend toward heat exchangers. Some follow lamp circuits. Some end around sensor plates or in the corroded housings of dead processors. A few extend outward, crossing the tower’s face inside seams no wider than a fingernail.

The fern is therefore rooted not in the chamber but in the tower’s distinctions: warm from cold, powered from inert, strained from stable, lit from dark.

Its wine-colored fronds are the least important part of it.

The oldest maintenance forms remember when it was small.

“Remember” is not quite the word. Their records are damaged, copied, overwritten, and supplemented by chemical signals they were never designed to interpret. Yet when the fern releases spores, every functioning maintenance form in the chamber alters its routine. Sensor cleaning is delayed. Certain vents are left unscraped. One machine opens its tool compartment and remains still while spores settle inside.

The clipping machine attends the silver vine because the vine is one of the fern’s distant organs. The ring-maker sorts rust by composition, though its circles resemble ritual more than analysis. Iron-rich fragments go at the center, copper salts farther out, pale ceramic at the rim. When a circle is complete, root-fine filaments rise from the silt beneath it and dissolve the arrangement grain by grain.

The motionless watcher at the breach has not moved its limbs for a century, but it is not inactive. Its lenses respond to every passing ray. Its pressure sensors register the pulses of distant animals. Through it, something in the rear of the chamber watches the abyss.

That something cannot be divided cleanly from its neighbors.

It includes the fern, certainly, but also portions of the moss and the silver vine. It uses maintenance forms as slowly wandering hands. It borrows the glass fish as alarms and the crabs as distributors of useful material. It reads the blind grazer’s tooth-noise through the walls and learns which microbial films have thickened. It encourages translucent bladders to gather near functioning lamps, then drains their stored gases through hollow tethers when darkness comes.

It does not command these creatures. Most would resist command if they were capable of understanding it. Instead, it adjusts conditions by fractions. A pipe is allowed to warm. A lamp cover is cleaned. A trace electrical field draws eels away from a nest of spores. A maintenance hatch opens just long enough for crabs to carry insulation into another compartment.

The results resemble coincidence.

This distributed inhabitant has no fixed boundary and no agreed name. If the tower’s surviving systems detect it at all, they classify it differently from hour to hour: contamination, auxiliary cooling, low-priority biofilm, sensor noise, unauthorized repair medium. The machines scrape pieces of it away while preserving other pieces as essential infrastructure.

It has learned to survive by appearing useful.

Perhaps it began as a terrestrial plant engineered to seal leaks. Perhaps it began in the code of a maintenance intelligence forced to improvise with living tissue after manufactured components became scarce. Perhaps neither origin matters now. Over centuries, instruction and growth have entered one another too deeply to separate. Roots conduct signals. circuits carry hormones. Machine routines spread like inherited instincts. Spores contain metallic filaments arranged in patterns suspiciously similar to memory.

Its thoughts, if they are thoughts, take place at the speed of corrosion.

A pressure change may be a syllable. A lamp cycle may be a breath. The arrival of a scaffold ray may occupy its attention for weeks afterward as it compares vibrations stored in wood, silt, and machine housing. It knows individual animals not by faces but by the disturbances they make. The old grazer is a repeating abrasion. The largest eel is a moving absence in the electrical field. The crabs are small rearrangements of weight.

The scaffold ray is weather with a heartbeat.

When the ray catches the railing outside, hooked fins tightening against the surge, the watcher at the breach records the contact through the metal. The ray’s back-garden opens its polyps in the chamber’s light. Spores pass between the two gardens. Tiny fish abandon one habitat for the other. For a little while, the distinction between resident and visitor disappears.

Then the ray releases its grip and sails downward into blue darkness, carrying fragments of the chamber across distances the rooted inhabitant cannot travel.

This may be how it explores.

There are hints of related growths elsewhere in the tower. A green hand appears around a weld eighty meters below. Copper fans open on a distant balcony at the same moment as those in the chamber. Once, after a surge, a maintenance form arrived bearing a clipping of wine-dark fern no local machine had cut. The clipping was placed in the center of a rust circle and absorbed.

Whether these distant colonies are children, limbs, or strangers is impossible to tell. The tower’s broken networks may allow them to exchange only pressure pulses, chemical traces, and the bodies of migrating animals. Their communication, if it exists, would be slower than speech and larger than intention.

When the deep pumps alter pitch, every inhabitant reacts according to its nature.

Fish hide. Crabs lock their legs around floor seams. The grazer seals its mouth to the wall. Ribbon predators flatten against cables. The fern folds its fronds, not merely to protect them but to expose the grille beneath. Maintenance forms brace themselves in alcoves.

The distributed thing becomes briefly alert in all its parts.

It feels the upward force enter the tower. It feels loosened dust, the failure of a distant panel, the opening of a stairway below. It feels unfamiliar water arrive bearing new minerals, new spores, a new fish with a bright organ pulsing wildly beside its heart.

Afterward, the chamber settles.

The new fish searches for its school. The crabs inspect the open panel. One maintenance form extends a tool toward the broken fern frond, pauses, and cuts it cleanly at the stem. Silver filaments are already growing across the wound.

Nothing announces that the garden has noticed.

But the nearest lamps brighten a little before their appointed hour. Warm water begins to rise through the newly exposed stairway. The ceiling ribbons turn toward it. In the silt beside the opening, several green hands unfold and press themselves against the metal.

The inhabitant has found another way down.
