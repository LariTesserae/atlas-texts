No single creature can be said to own the Black Cup. Its inhabitants overlap without quite meeting, arranged in layers of moisture, root, sound, and altered distance.

The most obvious are the **rim-dwellers**.

They live facing inward.

The trees put most of their leaves toward the darkness, as if the empty center were a sun. On their outer sides they are sparse, ordinary things: low crowns, dull bark, leaves toughened against plateau rain. Inside the Cup their trunks broaden into fluted resonant chambers. Their black leaves hold water in long gutters, releasing drops at regular intervals. A tree may wait minutes between drops, but it rarely misses its appointed beat.

These are sometimes called **bell trees**, though they do not ring. Their roots do.

Beneath each tree lies a living knot of wood and fungus wrapped around seams in the plateau stone. When water pressure changes, the roots tighten. The resulting tremor passes through buried threads to the next tree and the next, around the basin. Thus the trees keep a slow account of one another. A fallen tree is not immediately replaced. For decades the gap remains, and the surrounding vegetation alters its dripping and shuddering to sound around the absence.

Among the black leaves roost **dewbacks**, warm-scaled gliders with loose folds of skin from wrist to flank. They are broad as open hands and nearly weightless. When resting, they hang head downward and collect condensation along the ridges of their bodies. Before flight they shake themselves dry, producing a quick three-part rattle. They never glide directly across the center. Instead they enter the open air on long curves, repeatedly turning back toward the rim, though these curves sometimes carry them farther than the basin is physically wide.

Dewbacks retain large silver eyes. The eyes do not respond to light. Each contains a suspended chain of mineral grains and functions as an exquisitely sensitive balance organ. A dewback can feel the changing tilt of the seven structures through the minute rearrangement of those grains. During rare silences, every roosting dewback closes its eyes.

The pale tree crabs are less delicate. They work constantly at the root buttresses, tapping with one foreleg and listening through the others. Their shells are soft and chalky, strengthened by fibers stolen from fungal mats. They test roots for hollowness, clear clogged water channels, and cut away any growth that creeps too near the bare central mud.

They are gardeners, though nothing instructed them to be.

If one of the crabs is carried outside the Cup, it continues tapping for several days. Without the basin’s returning echoes, it becomes increasingly agitated. At last it digs a narrow hole, braces itself inside, and taps until it dies.

The **undertone frogs** occupy water films too thin to hide them. They are small, blunt animals with skin like wet ash. A frog’s throat sac extends deep into its chest and surrounds several flexible bones. Its call is felt more readily through the feet than heard by the ears. Different colonies hold different notes. Together they produce chords that drift clockwise around the Cup.

Young frogs call too sharply. Many are eaten before they learn the proper depth.

Their chief predator is not silent. It cannot afford to be.

The **counterfeit** is a long-bodied climber with six padded limbs and a loose velvet coat. Its head has no visible mouth when closed. Along its ribs are vibrating membranes with which it reproduces almost any common sound: water striking bark, beetle wings opening, crab taps, the throat pulse of a receptive frog. It approaches prey under a layer of familiar noises.

An inexperienced counterfeit copies too perfectly. Perfect repetition is rare in the Cup and readily detected. Older animals introduce errors: an extra drop, a wingbeat slightly out of time, the faint scratch of a claw concealed beneath a frog note. The greatest hunters can imitate not just a creature but a location. One may crouch above a frog while making itself seem to approach from below the soil.

Counterfeits also hunt one another. Their courtship and their predation are therefore difficult to distinguish. Both involve elaborate lies followed by sudden contact.

Much of the constant insect noise comes from **velvet files**, colonies of small black beetles that travel nose to tail along branches. No individual beetle produces more than a dry click. A file of several thousand becomes the sound of distant machinery. When a line reaches a gap, the beetles at the front stop while those behind continue clicking. The pressure of accumulated bodies determines whether the colony turns, bridges the gap, or divides.

No queen has been found among them. Reproduction occurs only during the basin’s wet decades, when several files converge on one of the seven moss collars. There they exchange larvae, fungus, and pieces of their own shells until the colonies can no longer be told apart.

Above them all circle the long-winged **measurers**.

They are not bats, though travelers call them that. Their wings are translucent, their bodies naked and warm, and the bones of their narrow faces form movable acoustic forks. They do not send out simple cries. Each releases a braided pulse containing dozens of close frequencies, then listens to the frequencies returning by different paths.

From these replies they construct the Cup—not as a map, but as an ongoing sensation of depth and interval.

The measurers are responsible for much of the apparent emptiness at the center. They pass through it constantly, but in strict lanes that cannot be seen. A young measurer entering the wrong lane may return at once, strike the ground years later, or disappear while its echoes continue circling for several nights.

Their colonies remember safe routes as songs. When the seven structures lean and the invisible geometry changes, the songs must change with it. An old route cannot simply be abandoned: echoes still travel through it. For generations the measurers include warnings in their calls, marking paths that no longer exist underfoot but remain acoustically open.

Below the turf is another population.

There are translucent worms that drink the films between grains of stone, mites that graze on warm fungi, and slow shelled things that spend an entire lifetime moving from one tree root to the next. Deeper still, where the plateau’s fractures hold water, the fungal partners of the rim vegetation form a nearly continuous tissue.

This tissue is older than any living tree.

It has no central mass. It cannot be dug up whole. It experiences the Cup as pressure, mineral chemistry, and recurring vibration. During the synchronized shudders, every major root contracts against it. The fungus answers by changing the flow of water and sugars around the ring.

In this way, the rooted life and the calling life are joined. The animals keep the soundscape. The plants keep the timing. The fungus keeps the memory.

They are not a single organism, but neither are they fully separate. A species that fails to contribute to the basin’s patterns seldom persists. Its calls interfere with hunting, migration, dripping, or the measurers’ lanes. Predators find it. Roots close beneath it. The Cup edits its inhabitants patiently.

Then there is the inhabitant of the open center.

The rim creatures have no special call for it. Every sound made in the Black Cup includes it already.

It has no visible body, no odor, and no warmth detectable apart from the general warmth of the basin. It does not disturb the air. Nothing has been seen feeding there. Yet the lives around it behave as though perpetually attending to something enormous and close.

The seven narrow structures seem to define its condition. Whether they imprison it, shelter it, or allow it to exist at all cannot be determined. Their outward lean suggests strain. Their murmur is not uniform: listened to over many nights, each structure can be heard carrying a separate portion of one continuous, immensely slowed vibration.

The creature may therefore be distributed among them.

Or the towers may be teeth around its mouth.

Its ordinary movements are too slight or too displaced to register as motion. A pressure here, a delayed echo there, a seed returned untouched after centuries—these may be its breathing. The central vacancy may not be vacant. It may be occupied so completely that smaller matter cannot establish lasting paths through it.

The curved strip on the mud is associated with it, though no one knows how. It resembles neither bone nor tool. At close range it produces no sound, but all echoes divide around the cylinder above it. The rim animals treat that column as part of the central inhabitant. Even falling insects twist away from it.

During the rare silence of the seven structures, the inhabitant crosses the Cup.

What crosses may be no more than one portion of it: a foot, a thought, a narrow organ extended between places that the towers usually keep apart. The ground bows under weight without receiving an impression. The movement is steady and grave. It does not hunt. It does not pause at trees. It passes from one side of the ring to the other over the course of a night.

The creatures remain still because their noises are no longer needed. Water hangs because the intervals by which drops fall have been suspended or borrowed. Condensation stops because, for those hours, the basin’s warmth belongs entirely to the crossing thing.

When it reaches the far side, the seven structures resume one at a time.

Then every animal answers together—not in alarm, exactly, and not in worship. The sound is closer to confirmation.

The Cup is measured again. The paths are restored. The roots resume their pressure. Drops complete their falls.

Whatever crossed has arrived where, in ordinary space, it already was.

And the many small lives at the rim return to their long labor: keeping a world of noise around something that must remain in perfect dark, giving shape through echo to a presence that cannot safely be seen.
