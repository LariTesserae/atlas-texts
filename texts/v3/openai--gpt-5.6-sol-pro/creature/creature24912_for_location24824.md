At first the inhabitants seem slight.

Seen against the glare, crossing a roof or leaning above a cistern, they are narrow dark interruptions in the brilliance: bent backs, wrapped heads, hands spread on stone. Distance makes them look furtive. Proximity corrects this. They are not timid, and not especially small. They have merely learned to keep every part of themselves near a surface.

They stand with knees unlocked and feet turned slightly outward, as if balancing on something that floats. Even on level rock they rarely put their full weight down at once. A hand trails along a wall; a shoulder rests against a door bend; toes test a stair before the heel follows. Children learn to climb before they learn to walk across open ground. In the central oval, where there is sometimes nothing to hold, they become visibly uneasy and move in quick diagonals from edge to edge.

Their feet are broad, strong, and often bare inside the settlement. The soles grow pale and ridged from cold rather than dark and callused from heat. They can feel water gathering below a paving slab, and old people claim to distinguish ordinary seepage from an approaching inversion through the bones of the ankle. Leather shoes are worn for journeys beyond the basin, but within the town soles are considered dangerously deaf. During the coldest weeks, they bind their feet in bird skin with the feathers turned inward, leaving the toes partly free to grip.

Their hands are more remarkable. The fingertips are blunt, the nails kept very short, and the palms are crossed by fine white scars from stone, shell, and ice. Nearly everyone is missing a little skin from the knuckles. They handle wet rock with extraordinary tenderness. Before lifting a wall stone, a builder lays both palms against it, not ceremonially but to learn whether it is held by frost, weight, or the pressure of hidden water. A stone taken incorrectly can tear away half a building. A stone understood correctly can be eased free by a child.

No one works alone on load-bearing walls. Two people may move the stone, but six or seven touch the surrounding structure, sensing its minute shifts. They communicate in murmurs, finger taps, and changes of pressure. A hand pressed flat means *hold*. Two fingers crooked into a seam mean *water here*. A thumb drawn across the wrist means *the weight has gone elsewhere*. Their building language is partly spoken and partly transmitted through the thing being built.

This has shaped ordinary affection. They touch often, but seldom embrace in the open-armed manner of lowland people. They lean. A parent and child rest foreheads against the same stone. Friends sit spine to spine while repairing nets. Lovers sleep with one person’s foot tucked behind the other’s knee, so that any tremor wakes them both. To be trusted is to be used as a brace.

Their skin varies from smoke-brown to a pale mineral olive, but exposed faces share the same weathering: red across the cheekbones, bluish around the eyes, cracked at the mouth. The glare makes them squint deeply. In old age, these folds remain even in darkness, giving elders an expression of intense, private amusement. Many have clouded patches in the eyes. Complete blindness is uncommon, though loss of distance vision is not; the basin rewards those who can read what is near.

They protect their sight with narrow masks of hide, bone, or thin slate. The simplest are strips pierced by horizontal slits. More elaborate ones have movable flakes that can be angled toward snow, water, or cliff. Some are lined with crushed crab-shell pigment, rust-red or black, to soften reflected light. A stranger may mistake these masks for signs of rank. They are not. The most intricate often belong to people whose eyes are already damaged.

Hair is worn close to the head or braided flat beneath wraps. Loose hair catches ice, blocks drain slots, and startles nesting birds. The common head covering is a hood made from stitched bird skins, its crown whitened with chalk and its inner edge rubbed with oil. Feathers are not displayed in plumes. They are hidden where they keep heat.

Their clothes look bulky only when dry. Most garments are layered close: bird skin, woven hair, softened hide, felted moss where it can be spared, and narrow pockets filled with loose down. Sleeves fasten tightly at the wrist. Belts carry plugs, wedges, cord, a bone scraper, and several family stones. Nothing hangs freely. Ornament is attached by stitching or pressure, never by chains that might ring against rock.

The family stones are the nearest thing they have to permanent names.

Each is small enough to close in one hand. Some are naturally striped; others bear shallow pits, edge-notches, copper stains, or a smear of shell red renewed each year. A household keeps several: one for those living together, one for its dead, one for debts and exchanges, sometimes one for a room that no longer exists. When a dwelling migrates, its stones move first. They are placed in a dry niche while walls are dismantled around them, then carried to the next arrangement.

People do have spoken names, but these are light and changeable. A child may be called for a gesture, a flood, a bird that nested above the birth chamber, or the peculiar behavior of water that week. Such names alter as the person alters. The family stone does not state who someone is. It states which continuities will be carried when everything else shifts.

Kinship follows work more closely than blood. Those who repeatedly rebuild the same sleeping chamber become its household, whether they are siblings, widows, former rivals, or children left by travelers. A person belongs to the people whose roof they know how to take apart in darkness. Partnerships can last a lifetime, but rooms do not, and so commitment is expressed through remembered arrangement: knowing where another prefers the sleeping shelf, how much draft they tolerate, which possessions must remain above flood level, and where their dead should eventually be set.

Children are not forbidden from dangerous places. There are too few places without danger for that to be useful. Instead, danger is divided into learnable textures.

They are taught which ice sings beneath a thumbnail, which only shivers; which walls may be warmed with the body and which will shed their faces if touched; where crabs climb before a floor-flood; how a stable stone differs from a merely motionless one. They play by rebuilding miniature houses in gutters and letting the water choose which survive. They race glass gnats with mirrored chips. They dare one another to lie at the edge of the central hole until they hear something from an earlier town.

Very young children often speak of those sounds without reverence. They say they hear coughing, quarrels, grinding stones, a baby refusing sleep. Adults do not correct them. The past here is not imagined as solemn. It is simply crowded.

The inhabitants seldom raise their voices. Their language has many close consonants made against the teeth and palate, and few open calls. Meaning is carried by breath, timing, and the direction of the face. In a cramped passage, one can hold a long conversation without speaking above the sound of seepage. At a distance they use hand signs, flashes from wet slate, or lengths of cord drawn through wall holes.

They sing, but usually into stone.

A singer places the mouth near a cavity, drainage throat, or fitted joint and lets the structure carry the tone. These songs are felt more than heard. Some travel through three or four adjoining houses as a faint vibration in sleeping platforms. Others disturb water in bowls, raising rings that allow those nearby to follow the rhythm with their eyes. Loudness is considered crude not because silence is sacred, but because loud sound tells nothing about where it has gone. A good song remains accountable to the walls that receive it.

Their festivals are correspondingly difficult for outsiders to recognize. There may be no gathering, no bell, no central fire. On a chosen day, everyone turns one wet stone in a wall so its pale underside faces outward. The basin brightens by degrees. Oil lamps are placed behind sheets of ice, and whole passages glow. Food is passed from roof to roof. Children stain their palms red and leave prints on temporary ice partitions, knowing the marks will vanish by morning.

Their food is spare, oily, and mineral-tasting. They eat crab flesh, eggs, preserved birds, curds from the compact pack animals brought seasonally through the pass, and cakes made from traded grain ground with dried moss and shell meal. Fat is more valued than meat. Salt arrives from elsewhere, though certain bitter salts are scraped from the basin walls and used medicinally.

The many-jointed wall creatures are not eaten, even during hunger.

No law forbids it. The refusal is more intimate than law. The creatures share warmth with the settlement and move through portions of it no person can reach. They are treated as the town’s inward hands. When one dies, its dark body is pushed deep into a wall cavity with scraps of leather and crab shell. Builders mark that section by turning one stone sideways. For years afterward they may say that the wall “has an elbow there” and avoid placing brittle ice above it.

The creatures tolerate the inhabitants without submitting to them. They steal tools, gnaw sleeping mats, and sometimes carry family stones into inaccessible gaps. Such thefts are taken seriously. A missing stone is not replaced at once. People watch the animal routes, leave oily bait, dismantle walls if they safely can. Occasionally the stone returns polished, warmed, or marked by teeth. This is considered an alteration to the household rather than damage.

The white crabs are handled more casually. Children scoop them from gutters. Cooks sort them by shell hardness. Forecasters watch them for hours, noting not merely whether they climb but which legs they lead with and whether they pause beneath overhangs. A crab entering a sleeping room is tossed out. A hundred crabs entering together will empty the house faster than an alarm.

The long-winged birds occupy a stranger relationship with the people. They nest close enough to touch, yet resist ownership entirely. Their soundless throat pulses are believed to pass through human teeth during sleep. Some inhabitants wake with aching jaws and an urgent certainty about weather, death, or the placement of a roof. Most such certainties prove useless. A few save lives.

Bird tending is undertaken by people with steady hands and damaged sight. They clear ice from nest mouths, remove dead young, and count eggs by touch. In return they take feathers, abandoned shells, and sometimes the bodies of adults killed by cold. No bird is deliberately caught. When famine forces the taking of eggs, the person responsible later sits beneath the empty niche until the parent birds return. This is not an apology. It is a way of remaining present for the consequence.

Glass gnats are never touched.

Children try, of course. They close fists around flashes and open them on empty palms. Bowls are set beneath swarms, nets raised through them, wet slate coated with oil. Nothing is caught. At most, a gnat seems briefly to become a droplet or a fleck of ice, though no two witnesses agree which.

Those who study the gnats are the town’s nearest equivalent to scholars. They are called, in translation, the Between-Watchers. They sit where falling water crosses hard light and record formations on thin flakes of shale. Their notation resembles cracks spreading through ice. They forecast freezes, inversions, the safest hours for opening cisterns, and the rare days when a wall can be moved without drawing water after it.

They do not agree on what the gnats are.

Some maintain that each is the visible edge of a decision made by water. Others say they are discarded possibilities: every gnat a freezing that almost occurred. A few argue that the swarms are living creatures that feed on brightness and leave no bodies because darkness consumes them completely. The oldest Between-Watchers tend to become less certain, not more. They point out that the gnats sometimes gather around grieving people even when no water falls nearby.

Death is not hidden, but neither is it made central. The dead remain in their dwellings only until the basin offers a suitable crevice. This may take days or an entire season. During that interval, the body is wrapped, kept cold, and included in household routines. Tools rest beside it. Meals are eaten nearby. If rebuilding becomes necessary, the dead are moved with everything else.

Before placement, the family stones are pressed one by one against the body’s lips. The smallest stone is then tucked into the wrappings, while the others return to the living. Thus every household carries an incomplete set.

Those who fit the dead into the basin’s edge are highly respected builders. They must understand how seepage will pass, how ice will thicken, and how the added body will alter pressure in the surrounding rock. A grave badly placed can split open a channel or flood a lower room. A grave well placed becomes useful for generations: strengthening a retaining face, cooling a store niche, clarifying runoff.

No one says the ancestors protect the town. Protection would imply keeping it as it is. The ancestors are said to *press* upon it. They enter through meltwater, mineral film, and the slow inward lean of walls. When a familiar route closes, an old grave clears, or a room lifts unexpectedly on sun-thickened ice, people may attribute the change to a particular dead person—especially one known in life for impatience.

Age brings authority, but not command. The eldest inhabitants remember multiple arrangements of the basin. They know that a wall now used as a nursery was once part of a drain, that a stair descends into an old roof, that three houses considered unrelated still share foundation stones from a collapsed communal chamber. Their memory prevents ownership from becoming too simple.

Disputes therefore concern use more often than possession: who must keep a channel clear, whose warmth is thawing the neighboring wall, whether a nesting shelf may be roofed, where flood sediment should be stored. Judgments are temporary and written for the current shape of things. A decision may remain binding “until the west gutter climbs,” “until the dead woman’s shoulder clears the ice,” or “while the oval holds sky.”

The closest thing to a ruler is the person who calls for a wall to be unmade.

Anyone may notice danger. Few are trusted to decide that a structure has reached the end of its present usefulness. Such a person must distinguish fear from movement and movement from failure. They lay an ear to the stones, watch the nearby water, consult the crabs, and then touch the wall in silence. If they begin removing their own belongings from the adjoining rooms, others follow.

Their authority lasts only until the stones are safely distributed.

The central hole belongs to no one. Infants are carried around it. The dead are never passed over it. Builders avoid aligning long seams toward it, although no one can say whether this is engineering or custom. At dawn, when it exhales, people sometimes hold masks, tools, or family stones in the cold breath. At noon they cover loose feathers and ash lest the inward pull take them.

A few individuals become devoted to the hole. They are not priests. They clean its rim, keep water from sealing it, and listen at hours when everyone else sleeps. Prolonged listening changes their speech. They begin using obsolete names for rooms, referring to stairs where blank walls stand, or asking after people dead for centuries. Yet they are also uncannily good at predicting where old foundations will emerge after a collapse.

The town neither venerates nor confines them. It gives them work dismantling walls.

Beyond the basin, the inhabitants are poor travelers. Open distances exhaust them. They dislike forests most of all: too many vertical forms, all moving at their crowns, none offering trustworthy stone. In low valleys, the sound of rivers makes sleep difficult. Rain feels disorderly to them because it falls only downward. They are astonished by buildings intended to stand unchanged for generations and privately regard mortar as a kind of panic.

Those who leave often return with iron, grain, oil, cloth, stories, and an altered gait. For months afterward they walk too heavily. Children laugh at the way they trust the ground. Eventually the basin teaches their feet again.

What lives there, finally, is difficult to separate into people, animals, water, and town.

The white crabs read floods before the forecasters do. The wall creatures retain heat and carry household histories in their teeth. Birds inhabit the architecture’s smallest intentions. Glass gnats occupy the hesitation between states. The dead brace the edges. The living move stones from one temporary necessity to another. Even the water, rising against gravity or hardening in light, behaves less like a substance than a population with obscure customs.

And the town itself appears alive not because it resembles a single animal, but because it never preserves one body.

It molts roofs. It seals and opens passages. It draws water inward and sends people climbing across its skin. It takes the warmth of sleeping creatures into its walls. It incorporates the dead without ceasing to rearrange them. No map captures more than a phase of it; no house can be inherited except as a sequence of remembered forms.

Its inhabitants do not call this endurance.

Endurance, to them, would mean remaining under pressure without changing.

They call it fitting.
