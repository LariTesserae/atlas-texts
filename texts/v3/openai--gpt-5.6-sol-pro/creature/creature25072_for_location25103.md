At first it seems that hands live in the Ninth Seam.

Hands, and the work that hands perform.

They emerge brown, red, soot-dark, clay-pale from every aperture: small hands threading roots through lattice; broad hands holding walls upright while resin cures; old hands with copper wire splinting the fingers; infants’ fists opening and closing in bowls of rainwater. A hand may remain visible for hours while the rest of its owner stays folded behind a partition, and it becomes easy to think of the wrist as a root and the person as something planted elsewhere.

The inhabitants themselves encourage this impression. They do not insist upon being seen whole.

Wholeness, to them, is temporary and somewhat indecent. A body fully extended occupies space that could support a shelf, a cistern, a bed of peppers. They grow compactly. Their knees and shoulders are polished not by the Seam but by one another. Their toes spread wide and muscular from gripping ropes, damp rungs, and the rims of pottery. Most can sleep upright, curled around a jar, or suspended in slings narrow enough to be mistaken for tool loops. They turn sideways without thinking. They can pass one another in a gap scarcely wider than a skull by exchanging breath, posture, and burden in a practiced sequence: one exhales, one bows, one lifts the basket, one slips beneath the raised elbow.

No one crosses the Seam alone. Even solitude is done cooperatively.

Their skin carries the close air on it. Sweat rarely evaporates; it runs along channels painted across their bodies in ash and plant oil, dripping from elbows into catch-cups tied at the wrists. These markings resemble irrigation plans, though whether the builders copied the watercourses from their own bodies or their bodies from the watercourses is uncertain. Their hair is generally bound tight with reed fiber, but some wear it long and braided into living vines. After enough seasons the vine roots into the braid. Such people must sleep wherever the plant allows, and are honored as anchors.

Their clothing is architecture in its soft phase. Bark cloth wraps are stitched with pockets for seeds, pegs, glaze chips, coals sealed in clay, and slips of leaf bearing measurements. Aprons are unfolded at need into hammocks or rain catchments. Shoulder capes harden under resin and become roof tiles after their wearers die. Nothing intimate remains merely intimate for long. A cradle becomes a grain sieve. A wedding mat becomes a wall. A shroud is mortared into a cistern so the dead may continue holding water.

They have names, but few names belong to one person.

A name belongs first to a task. The person measuring the eastward lean is called Red Cord. Whoever carries the kiln flame is Ember-Mouth. The keeper of the final unset brick is Ninth Breath. These names pass from worker to worker during the day and may cross generations without pause. Beneath them are quieter names, made of touch rather than sound: three taps on a wrist, a thumb drawn across the brow, a particular way of taking weight from someone’s shoulders. Those are used among kin and cannot be overheard by echoes.

To speak a private name aloud would give the chamber another worker.

Children are not taught construction as a separate skill. They are born amid it and at once become part of its measurements. Newborns are laid briefly against unfinished surfaces; the span from crown to heel determines the dimensions of a run of bricks. Toddlers sort shell from bone by taste. By the time a child can stand, they know which ropes may be held, which must never be touched, and which are taut only because someone years from now will fasten the other end.

They are not punished for mistakes of workmanship. A crooked tile, an accidental opening, a seed dropped into the wrong gutter—these may be instructions. The elders gather around such errors and wait to see what answers. If a root enters, the wall is redesigned around the root. If water pools there, a cistern is declared. If an echo supplies a measurement, the measurement is tested against three cords, two memories, and the behavior of the black lizard.

But there are mistakes the Seam cannot reinterpret. Finishing a circle is one. Counting floors is another. These are met not with anger but immediate, communal terror. The offender is wrapped in wet cloth, held by many hands, and kept from speaking until the dangerous completeness has passed. The builders fear finished things because finished things have nowhere to grow. A closed form may become an outer wall, and an outer wall would tell the mountain how small it is.

Their society has no rulers in the ordinary sense. Authority travels toward the place of greatest strain. Whoever feels a platform bend becomes, for that moment, the person obeyed. A child may command fifty adults if the child’s cheek is pressed to a pipe and hears pressure changing within it. An elder may be ignored if their instruction arrives after the roots have chosen otherwise. Decisions are not made in chambers—there is no room for councils—but sung along work lines. A proposal begins as a short phrase. Other workers alter its rhythm as it passes. If the phrase returns unchanged, it is considered suspicious. If it returns richer but still usable, work begins.

The oldest inhabitants are difficult to distinguish from the Seam.

Resin glosses their skin. Moss grows in the creases of their backs. Their feet have broadened around ladder rungs they no longer leave. They remember walls that have not yet appeared and forget chambers still occupied. Some speak only in dimensions. Some sit with an ear against the clay and report the hammering of the miniature builders below all hearing.

Age is not counted in years. A person is said to be “three roofs deep,” “older than the bitter cistern,” or “born before the stair unfolded.” Death, likewise, is not treated as departure. There is nowhere to depart to. When someone stops breathing, the body is washed in circulating rain and watched carefully for instructions. A stiffened arm may indicate the angle of a brace. Curled fingers may define the mouth of a drain. Bones become chimes, needles, levers, or kiln ribs. Teeth are set into warning strings. The skull is usually placed behind a wall with a speaking tube at its mouth.

Sometimes, much later, the tube gives advice.

Whether this is the dead person, an echo, or a voice from a deeper Seam is not debated unless the advice proves structurally unsound.

Their kinship is organized by burdens shared. Two people who have held the same failing terrace are siblings thereafter. Those who survive an upward rain become a household, regardless of prior relation. Marriage consists of constructing something neither person could inhabit alone. Separation is accomplished by dismantling it without causing collapse, and is therefore rare.

They are tender with one another in ways adapted to congestion. Affection is the quiet repositioning of a tool so someone can reach it in darkness. It is taking another’s place beneath a load without waking them. It is knowing which orchid must not hear a beloved’s voice. Lovers seldom embrace face-to-face; there is not enough room. They rest back-to-back in the heat, each supporting the other like walls braced from opposite sides.

Their feasts are small and continuous. A pepper passed along a scaffold, bitten once by each worker. Taro paste scooped from a communal leaf with clay-stained fingers. Clear gutter crabs steamed in folded flowers, though only after they have shed the floor plans on their shells. Fruit is opened cautiously. Water has been found inside fruit before, waiting with the pressure of an entire storm. One overripe gourd drowned a sleeping tier; ever since, gourds are tapped and listened to before cutting.

The white crabs are not exactly livestock. They are junior surveyors. Builders give them pollen, protect their gutter routes, and copy their shell diagrams onto bark cloth. The diagrams are often impossible: rooms larger inside than outside, staircases returning to days before they were built, foundations resting on sleeping people. These plans are not rejected. They are stored until the Seam makes room for their truth.

The black lizard is treated with greater caution. Children call it the Inspector of Newness. Adults pretend not to call it anything, because it vanishes when named. Its appearance means a surface has become real enough to stand upon but not yet real enough to remember footsteps. Before stepping where it has stepped, builders place a palm flat against the glaze. Sometimes the surface is warm. Sometimes they feel, beneath it, an immense answering palm.

The feathered thing above has no settled description.

Some say it is a bird too large to enter the mountain and therefore forced to exist only in pieces: one wingbeat in the rafters, one shadow, one feather, one eye glimpsed in a lamp basin. Some say it is the future animal for which the summit is being built, nesting backward through time. Others believe there is no bird at all—that the feathers are leaves from a plant growing in a version of the Seam where plants have learned to fly.

Ninth Breath, the keeper of the unset brick, is always chosen from those who have heard the wings and not looked up.

This keeper has no fixed age or sex. During the dimmest hour they crawl through the pauses between bodies, carrying the final brick against the chest. It is a poor brick by ordinary standards: slightly warped, underfired, and impressed with more fingerprints than can belong to the person who made it. Ninth Breath finds the gap and sits beside it while the Seam inhales.

The duty is not to insert the brick.

The duty is to endure the desire to insert it.

At that moment every builder feels the temptation. The open space seems intolerable. Through it comes the hot wind, the pressure of all that is not yet shaped, the touch of the countless outer hands. Tools shift toward the gap. Mortar swells. Nearby roots reach for it. Even the miniature workers on the Unfinished Peak abandon their baskets and turn their minute faces upward.

Ninth Breath holds the brick and does nothing.

That restraint may be the greatest work performed in the Seam.

Yet the builders are not its only inhabitants. Something lives through them, though it is unclear whether it is the mountain, the chamber, or the labor itself.

It has no organ except what they construct for it. The gutters are its vessels. The ovens are its fever. The downward flames are its buried sight. Its thoughts travel as strain through scaffolding and return as measurements in songs. It dreams in floor plans on crab shells. It tests possible bodies by growing unassigned plants. Its memory is carried by echoes, which is why echoes sometimes remember instructions before anyone has spoken them.

The builders may be its cells, or its midwives, or the small clay figures through which it imagines having hands.

They disagree about this amiably while working.

A few believe they are building the mountain. A few believe the mountain is building them: narrowing shoulders, strengthening toes, bending generations into shapes suited to its interior. Some claim the immense hands beyond the transparent walls belong to their own descendants. Others say each builder has one enormous hand outside, pressing inward, while the body within is merely the fingertip’s dream of itself.

The miniature builders are not discussed often. Every inhabitant has looked at them; no inhabitant admits how long.

Up close, the tiny figures differ from one another. Some carry baskets. Some brace ladders. Some sleep beneath terraces. One keeps a tiny unset brick beside a hole too small for light. Their rolled-clay heads bear dents that resemble faces only when viewed from the corner of the eye.

Now and then a builder in the full-sized Seam recognizes a miniature posture as their own.

Now and then a miniature worker looks up first.

This is perhaps why the inhabitants labor so relentlessly. Not because they expect completion, and not simply because the mountain requires it, but because every act of building grants space to someone nested farther within. A wall raised here becomes a shelter there. A spilled cup becomes another world’s monsoon. A kiln flame gives heat to an unseen generation. They live under the pressure of infinite dependence.

And still they laugh.

Their laughter is brief, practical, often triggered by the Seam’s more intimate absurdities: a root stealing a needle, a crab presenting a plan for a room shaped exactly like a crab, an echo issuing instructions in the hiccupping voice of a baby. Laughter travels farther than speech. It enters pipes, jars, and miniature caves. Sometimes, long after the joke has been forgotten, laughter comes rising from the Unfinished Peak in voices too deep to belong to anything so small.

Then the builders pause, listening.

They place their wet hands against the nearest wall.

From the other side, countless hands press back.
