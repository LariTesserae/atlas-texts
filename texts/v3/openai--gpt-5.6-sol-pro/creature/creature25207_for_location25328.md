The people of the High Drowned Steps are difficult to distinguish from their city at first.

Seen through mist, they appear as narrow figures in layered gray garments, stooping beneath pipes or standing motionless at the ends of bridges. Their clothes shine with condensation. Tools hang close to the body so that nothing swings unnecessarily. Many wear broad shoulder-cloths with raised seams that collect water and lead it toward flasks at the hip. A person walking home may gather enough from the fog to fill a kettle.

But gray is only their weather-color. Under awnings and inside the crowded warmth of dwellings, their clothing opens into mineral greens, rust reds, lichen blues, and the dull gold reserved for signalers. Sleeves are embroidered with neighborhood drainage maps. Formal coats carry stitched elevation lines, revised whenever the district is resurveyed. A family of long standing can point to the marks on an heirloom collar and show how far its house has descended in six generations.

They tend to be slight, deep-chested, and deliberate in movement. Children learn early not to run in groups across the same floor. Crowds distribute themselves by instinct, leaving gaps like the spaces in a lattice. In public halls, people shift positions whenever a colored weight-lamp changes above the door. During festivals, dances travel from courtyard to courtyard rather than gathering everyone in one square.

Nothing about this is treated as caution. It is simply good manners.

Their faces are often partially covered outdoors, not against cold but to warm the air and harvest breath moisture. These masks are made from felted moss fiber, fine wire, and replaceable root filters. The oldest people wear elaborate versions with little copper condensers along the jaw. When they speak, droplets gather beneath the chin and fall into a collar channel. To waste one’s breath is a common expression for both foolish speech and extravagance.

Names are seldom shouted. Every inhabitant has a near-name for conversation, a lamp-name for use across fog, and a pressure-name recorded in civic machinery. The lamp-name is a short sequence of colors and intervals, often inherited but altered slightly from parent to child. Pressure-names are tapped into pipes or sent through hydraulic consoles: patterns of pauses more than sounds. Some citizens know colleagues for years by a green-green-amber light across a basin and only later meet them in person.

This has made their speech quiet and richly dependent on proximity. At arm’s length it is precise, intimate, full of small breathy distinctions that vanish only a few steps away. Lovers are said to share weather because they stand close enough to feel one another’s exhalation. Arguments, by contrast, are often conducted with hand panels, each person withdrawing until anger can be reduced to clean, visible signs.

They distrust anyone who raises their voice.

Most belong to a chain of maintenance rather than a single trade. A baker may also tend three roof cisterns, inspect a retaining garden, and take a monthly shift operating the counterweights beneath a tram station. A teacher may be responsible for the blue-black fatigue lichen on a bridge. Children serve as drip-counters, recording the yield of gutters too small for mechanical gauges. No one is thought fully adult until they can trace the route of water from the roof above their bed through roots, tanks, neighborhood reservoirs, pressure mains, factories, and eventually the cliff.

Their wealth is measured less in possessions than in reliable passage: a claim on so many minutes of lift power, so many liters through a living filter, so much permitted load on a floor. Heavy objects have multiple owners because they must migrate. A family table may spend winter in one dwelling, summer in another, and storm season suspended in a public weight chamber. Libraries circulate shelves rather than books. Statues are hollow, jointed, and carried ceremonially from foundation to foundation so that no ancestor burdens one place forever.

Permanent ownership is regarded with mild suspicion. The dead themselves do not remain whole.

Funerals begin in elevated garden rooms where the body is washed with water collected from the deceased’s own roof. Soft tissues are buried among filter roots. Bones are cleaned, divided, and worked into small practical objects: valve handles, loom shuttles, instrument bridges, toggles for condensation cloaks. The name is not carved upon them. It is considered enough that the dead continue to be touched.

Only the teeth are kept together. They are sealed in little glass pressure capsules and stored in the Ministry’s elevation vaults, arranged according to the height at which each person was born. As the plateau subsides, old generations appear to rise on the wall.

The people possess an unusual patience with machines. They do not expect mechanisms to obey instantly. An operator may set a sequence of valves in the morning and return after supper to see whether the machine has considered the request. Factory workers sit beside motionless presses mending clothes, trimming roots, playing silent tile games, or watching gauges gather resolve. Then a lamp changes. Everyone stands. For six or seven minutes the room becomes exact: belts move, weights fall, jaws close, hands feed and retrieve. When the stored pressure is spent, work ends immediately. No one urges the last wheel around.

A continuously turning machine makes them uneasy. It resembles panic, or hunger.

Their engineers are called listeners, though much of their work is visual and tactile. They lie with cheeks against floors to feel minute changes in temperature. They watch lichen darken around stressed bolts. They suspend hairs in sealed corridors to learn where the building is breathing. The finest listeners claim that each basin has a posture: a distributed arrangement of strain running through streets, towers, buried foundations, roots, and reservoirs. Their task is not to strengthen the city absolutely, which they consider impossible, but to persuade its weight to go somewhere else.

The Ministry of Subsidence employs more of them than any other institution. Its clerks are recognizable by white fingertips, stained with the chalk used to revise civic maps. They are neither gloomy nor especially powerful. They keep expiration tables for stairways, marriages of foundations, projected cliff losses, and districts not yet built. Every home receives a forecast:

**This wall has nine generations.**  
**This street has four.**  
**This room should not be loved beyond eighty years.**

Children sometimes alter the figures as a prank. Adults react more strongly to this than to theft.

They are not, however, a melancholy people. Their humor concerns delay, misplaced water, and the vanity of stone. They hold feasts when a building is successfully dismantled before collapse. Neighbors gather to eat broadleaf stews and pale cistern crabs while crews remove the upper stories in sections. The last resident carries out a bowl of moss from the roof garden. When the final wall comes down, everyone applauds by opening and closing their hands, because ordinary applause cannot be heard at distance and because sudden vibration is discourteous.

Their music is made chiefly of light, touch, and pressure. Concert halls have flexible floors crossed by slow waves from buried pistons. Audiences sit or lie down and feel compositions travel through the ribs. Lantern orchestras perform across whole valleys, towers answering one another in amber, red, and milk-blue. Some pieces last several nights, continuing only when the fog is dense enough to serve as a screen.

They do use bells, but bells are not for summoning. They are set swinging where they cannot be heard, their motion visible through the mist. A bell that stops unexpectedly means a bridge has shifted, a cable has iced, or something large has passed close to it.

Children are fascinated by the gliders along the outer cliffs. They call them many local names: cloud-hands, sixfolds, dusk kites, the black laundry. Adults insist the animals are harmless, though no one agrees where they breed. Cliff workers sometimes leave scraps of mineral paste on high ledges and later find the paste gone, with neat three-pronged marks around the dish. A membrane occasionally turns up snagged on a collecting net, translucent as wet soot and large enough to roof a room.

The reservoir crabs are more familiar. Households paint tiny identifying marks on their shells and transfer them between cisterns to keep the water clean. Old crabs that have sealed themselves into masonry are never pried out. Their positions are incorporated into architectural plans. Many buildings contain small niches labeled with the names of crabs unseen for decades, waiting for a sufficiently wet year.

The pale turbine moths are tolerated in workshops and sometimes read as omens. Their wings accumulate metallic dust in patterns corresponding to the bearings where they fed. Inspectors catch them against dark cloth and study the markings. A sudden abundance of silver-winged moths can close a factory faster than any ministerial order.

Then there are the wall-tracks.

People speak of them obliquely. A garden crossed by three forearm-long impressions will be closed for a day, not from fear, they say, but for measurement. Bowls of filtered water are placed at opposite ends of the route. Roof doors are unlatched. No attempt is made to follow the marks.

Children believe a great, flat creature moves beneath the moss, lifting itself partly out of the stone wherever it steps. Engineers propose frost deformation, coordinated root action, or an undocumented maintenance device from an early period of the city. The oldest gardeners say all these explanations are impolite. Whatever crosses the walls, they say, was on the plateau before walls learned to stand there.

Those who live below are harder to describe.

The upper city calls them the Residuals, when it admits they exist at all. Officially this refers to old automata: pressure governors, maintenance carriages, root-tending mechanisms, and signal relays that continue to perform incomplete duties in buried streets. Many certainly exist. Repair workers have found brass machines moving through black stations on stone wheels, pausing at long-demolished doorways to deliver heat or water. Some bear inspection stamps from ministries extinct for centuries. Others appear newly serviced.

But lamps answer from the lower levels with patterns too irregular for machines and too obsolete for modern citizens. Food sometimes disappears from sealed supply cabinets. Gardens are pruned. Chalk arrows appear on tunnel walls, pointing away from collapses that have not yet occurred.

A few people live openly near the upper buried streets: pump keepers, salvage families, archivists of abandoned pipe systems. They are paler than surface residents and dress in waxed patchwork against the perpetual drip. Their eyes are accustomed to amber emergency light. They cultivate white leaves in station beds and raise moth larvae for fat. Their lamp-names use colors no longer standard above.

They insist there are deeper inhabitants.

According to them, people remained behind as the city accumulated upward. Over generations they surrendered first the sky, then ordinary clocks, then spoken language, because neither day nor sound could reach them reliably. They learned to follow warmth through rails and pressure changes through walls. Their bodies became low, strong-fingered, and sensitive to vibration. They travel when the old systems wake, riding freight platforms through shafts whose upper controls have been removed.

That is one account.

Another says the buried citizens are not descendants but reconstructions: maintenance systems that watched human workers for so long they began repeating them. They grease rails because hands once greased rails. They grow gardens because gardens once filtered water. They send elevators upward because people once waited below. Wet footprints are merely another function being faithfully performed.

Repair crews tell quieter stories. They tell of figures standing behind station glass, wearing clothing from several different centuries at once. Of a worker who met herself at the bottom of a lift, older and carrying a lamp-name she had not yet chosen. Of neighborhoods where every doorway opens onto the same warm garden. Of rails that pulse beneath the palm like sleeping arteries.

For this reason, whenever fog enters against the wind, the lower doors are opened. The custom is usually explained as pressure management. Yet families also set an extra cup beneath the kitchen gutter. They turn mirrors toward the wall. They make sure no chair is left bearing the full weight of an empty room.

Sometimes, after such a fog, someone finds a tool sharpened that had been left dull, a failing valve newly wrapped in an unknown green metal, or a child’s lost object placed carefully beside the lowest stair.

Sometimes the household cistern is several liters short.

No one complains.

Across the plateau, life is arranged around the understanding that nothing stands alone. A person is part of a floor load, a water route, a maintenance chain, a signal sequence, a lineage of reused material. Buildings are temporary postures of stone. Gardens are organs shared by neighborhoods. Machines sleep more than they move. The dead become handles. The unseen continue their shifts.

And beneath all of them, perhaps, something older than the cities follows the slow descent of the country grain by grain—keeping pace as the plateau wears away, leaving three parallel impressions wherever it briefly rises near the surface.
