No single people live in the district. Its inhabitants are distributed through it.

They occupy thickness rather than space: the narrow interval between the upper and lower skin of a leaf, the translucence under a pavement, the hollow of a rail, the wet brightness at the bottom of an empty cistern. The open streets are not forbidden to them, but exposure seems an exceptional condition, like illness or ceremony. To emerge is to become briefly complete.

The beetles are among the most numerous. Seen from above, they resemble seeds passing through the rootwork, each in its own channel beneath the black-green paving. They keep strict lanes. At junctions they pause for blue pulses in the vines, then cross in alternating streams. What they transport is too small to identify: grains of mineral, flakes of bark, minute transparent parcels that contract when shadows pass over them. They appear industrious, though their destinations sometimes cease to be adjacent while they are traveling. When this happens, they do not turn back. The lane lengthens under them until it arrives where the destination has always been.

Inside the handrails sleep the velvet-backed animals. Only their fur is ordinarily visible, a warm strip beneath the palm. Their bodies are long enough to follow entire staircases, bending through posts and corners without apparent joints. A sleeper may begin in one plaza and end in a room no longer connected to it. Touch wakes them slowly. First the fur leans toward the hand; then a pulse travels along the rail; finally, somewhere distant, a narrow face pushes out through a knot in the wood and tastes the air.

They feed on warmth left by passersby. A handprint can sustain one for days. The smaller print that develops inside it belongs to the animal—not a paw mark, but its recollection of the hand, reduced and made portable. When the district changes position, these creatures preserve obsolete routes in their bodies. Their dreaming is full of stairways that no longer lead anywhere.

The birds are less substantial. At rest they possess feathers, feet, the quick rounded weight of ordinary birds. In motion they surrender depth. They flatten against the roof leaves and run through the veins as dark emblems: hooks, arrows, scraps of cursive writing. A flock disturbed at one end of the district may pour across the canopy as a single stain and separate into individuals several streets away.

They nest in folds where leaves overlap, making shallow cups from shed outlines. Their eggs are spots. For several weeks each egg looks like nothing more than a blemish in the foliage. Then the leaf puckers around it and releases a chick with no thickness at all. The parents feed it moth scales and pieces of evening until it acquires enough shadow to peel itself free.

The moths gather wherever cold has edges. They stand around the cubes exhaled by the factory, facing inward, their antennae silvered with frost. They do not enter the cold. They graze its geometry, keeping the corners sharp as the cube travels. Without them, the cold softens, spreads, and becomes weather. A neglected cube can chill an intersection for years.

When the cubes reach the awnings where darkness is stacked, the moths exchange places with the animals that drink night. These are difficult to see because feeding erases their contours. One notices them by subtraction: a low shape moving through blackness, leaving a paler wake. Their mouths are broad, delicate surfaces like the undersides of mushrooms. They lap darkness from walls until the structure beneath begins to show, sometimes exposing a doorway not yet surrounded by its building.

The pale browsers come out when the ceilings sweat most heavily. They are tall enough to look into the transparent patches of upper chambers, though they never seem curious about what lies within. Six legs carry a body the color of root pith. The front pair are fine and almost handlike; the rear pairs bear most of the weight and end in pads that adhere briefly to the pavement before releasing with a soft tick.

Their faces are built for reading. Narrow lips move over the walls, lifting inscriptions one character at a time. Directions, warnings, faded names, fungal timetables: all are cropped alike. The browsers chew slowly, and the words can sometimes be seen moving beneath the skin of their throats. After feeding, faint text appears along their flanks. It is rarely the same text they consumed. A notice reading NO ACCESS may emerge as a list of rainfall; a platform destination may become the anatomical name of a bone no animal there possesses.

The reed-hounds follow them.

From a distance these predators seem lean and familiar. Close by, each is an assembly of dry, jointed stems held in the approximate intention of an animal. Air passes through them. Their ribs whistle very softly at certain cold intersections, though they make no sound while running. They hunt not the browsers’ flesh but the words fermenting inside them. A successful hound bites into a flank and shakes loose a black cluster of letters. The browser escapes lighter and blanker. The hound carries the stolen phrase away in its hollow chest, where it clicks for days.

Sometimes the phrase alters the hunter. A hound that has swallowed the word RIVER may begin to flow around corners, leaving portions of itself upright in shafts. One that takes the name of a destination may disappear from the district altogether, though its shadow continues hunting. The browsers are not always afraid of these shadows. Some allow themselves to be overtaken and return wearing darkness shaped like a second animal.

In the cisterns live the smallest inhabitants and, perhaps, the oldest. Transparent frogs cling to the water’s underside. Threadworms braid themselves into temporary nerves. Aquatic spiders walk across submerged reflections, each foot resting on an image of the ceiling. The pale fry with leaf-shaped fins browse on mineral light.

They regard water as only one possible medium. When a cistern dries, they pass into its reflection with the practiced haste of commuters entering a crowded carriage. There they wait in water that exists only visually. Hunger slows but does not cease. Predation continues. Eggs hatch. Generations may pass in the shining floor of a basin while the basin itself remains dusty.

These reflected populations differ from those returned by rain. They come back slightly revised. A frog may emerge with the markings of a drain grille across its back. Spiders return with too many reflections and cast several shadows in different directions. Fish that have waited through a rearrangement sometimes remember the old locations of every pool. Released, they swim into walls and vanish, following a connected water system that is no longer true but has not entirely stopped existing.

There are also the workers, if they can be called creatures. They have no permanent bodies. A valve opens; a ladder bends; a root conveyor shudders beneath the street. For a few seconds these motions agree with one another and form a presence, large and attentive. It may be felt looking out through several transparent windows at once. Then the blue pulse moves on and the presence comes apart into civic functions.

Each factory seems to house one version of this life. The leaf-pulping plant is patient and adhesive. It repairs anything placed near its intake, wrapping broken shells, dead moths, stones, and occasionally patches of spilled darkness in fresh roof membrane. The seed factory is hotter and more secretive. It accepts muddy water and produces black seeds, but some nights the seeds bear tiny doorways in their coats. If planted, they do not sprout. Instead, a room appears underground, complete with condensed rain and a pale membrane ceiling. Roots arrive later.

The factory that exhales cold appears least animate, but animals treat it with the greatest caution. Just before each cube emerges, every creature in the surrounding street turns toward it. The factory’s bark contracts. Something inside takes a breath much larger than the trunk could hold. What it exhales is not merely cold but a measured absence of climate, cut to civic dimensions.

No one tends these beings because tending is one of the district’s own metabolic activities. Roofs feed pumps. Pumps circulate sap through doors. Doors admit darkness. Darkness outlines rooms. Rooms call walls into existence. The animals graze the excesses, carry information between disconnected tissues, sharpen cold, preserve obsolete routes, and return vanished water to the world. Predator and prey, machine and building, pavement and citizen are roles rather than kinds.

Even the travelers who occasionally enter do not remain entirely separate.

The district begins incorporating them at once. Their heat is harvested from handprints. Beetles copy the rhythm of their footsteps. A transparent patch opens in a stalk at the height of their face. Somewhere, a shallow stair begins growing wear in anticipation of their repeated use. If they sleep there, birds gather as markings above them, and the handrail mammals extend themselves toward the sleeper’s dreams.

Those who stay too long become difficult to remove. It is not that roots seize them or that vegetation grows over their bodies. Rather, the district develops functions that require their presence. A door opens only when they are cold. Rain falls in the chair where they prefer to sit. A pump pauses until they cross a certain alley. Their habits become load-bearing.

Eventually they may find that the way out remains visible but no longer leads from where they are. Some adapt. They learn to live in several past arrangements at once. They answer to rooms that have moved and remember cisterns in both locations. Their skin takes on the chill of leaf membranes. Their shadows begin arriving early.

Such residents are seldom seen directly. They retreat into architecture as the native creatures do. One may hear a page turn inside a sealed stalk and suppose someone is reading there. A bootstep passes beneath the pavement. Behind a transparent patch, a face appears for the duration of a blue pulse—but it may be a traveler, a mammal’s borrowed memory, or the district trying a human expression.

At the station, all distinctions narrow.

When the lamps pulse, beetles leave their hidden lanes. Birds recover their bodies and settle along the veins. Browsers stand with unreadable flanks toward the track. Reed-hounds sit beside them, dry muzzles lifted. Fish crowd the reflections in polished surfaces. Darkness-drinkers emerge as pale vacancies among the others. Within the handrails, the long mammals wake from end to end.

They gather without touching because each occupies a slightly different version of the platform. Their crowding is an overlap of possible districts rather than a congestion of bodies. All face the empty tracks. All appear to wait.

Perhaps they expect a train. Perhaps they remember one that will have arrived. Perhaps the train is the immense life of which the district is only a station-shaped organ, and the wind without moving air is its circulation passing nearby.

But when nothing arrives and another street exists afterward, the inhabitants do not seem disappointed. They disperse into the new surfaces with immediate familiarity. Beetle lanes are already present beneath the pavement. Birds find old nests in leaves that have just unfolded. A browser discovers writing on a wall too young to have been inscribed. In the fresh handrail, something long and warm is already asleep.

What lives there is not merely the fauna, nor the vegetation, nor the machinery that digests both. It is the district’s capacity to inhabit what does not yet exist.

Everything in it is waiting, and the waiting itself is alive.
