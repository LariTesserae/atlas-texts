At first the inhabitants seem to be a people in hiding.

One sees their city before one sees them, then their work before their bodies: a scoured runnel, a lintel newly braced, footprints descending into water and not returning by the same route. They are easy to mistake for a dwindling remnant, scattered among rooms too numerous to inhabit. Even when one comes upon them, they may stand motionless in the gray clothing of the plateau, indistinguishable for a moment from wet masonry.

They are ordinary human beings, though the city has given them a particular bearing. They walk with their weight low and slightly forward, placing the whole foot rather than the heel. Children learn early that a slab may tilt, that clear water may conceal a missing stair, that moss is sometimes less slippery than bare stone and sometimes more. Adults test unfamiliar paving with staffs of willow or bone. Many have broad, splayed feet from a lifetime without rigid shoes. Their hands are pale and roughened at the fingertips, the nails worn by grit, lime, wet fiber, and clay.

Their clothing is layered, close-wrapped, and rarely entirely dry. Felted wool, beaten bark fiber, plaited rush, and hides from the grazing shelves are cut into short cloaks that leave the legs free. The outer layers are rubbed with fat or mineral-rich clay, not to make them waterproof—nothing remains waterproof here—but to delay saturation. Hoods extend over the brow and taper behind the neck so that rain runs away from the collar. Calves and forearms are bound in replaceable strips. People entering a dwelling unwind these at the threshold and hang them over warm stones.

Color exists, but rain subdues it. Garments begin ocher, russet, black, or a blue extracted at great cost from a low hill plant; within a season they become variations of smoke and earth. Bright color is therefore intimate. It survives beneath cloaks, inside sleeves, around the throat. A person may appear entirely gray until reaching for something and showing a narrow band of red weaving at the wrist.

They live in small, dry portions of enormous structures.

An inhabited house is seldom a single building. It is a chain of selected chambers linked through otherwise unused architecture: a sleeping room whose roof remains sound, a kitchen three doors away where the smoke draws well, a storage vault farther uphill, a rain court belonging jointly to several families. Between them may lie abandoned rooms, collapsed galleries, and channels that no one crosses during the wet months. A household is thus shaped less like a box than like a route.

Doorways are made low with hanging mats. Floors are raised on fitted rubble or layered with brush and rushes. Sleeping platforms occupy wall niches above the damp line. Pottery, wooden vessels, baskets, polished stone tools, bone needles, and bundles of cord hang from pegs driven into old joints. Almost nothing useful is left on the floor.

Fire is precious and small. It is kept in deep stone hearths, fed with peat, dung, roots, splinters of willow, and old structural timber only when a collapse exposes wood too rotten for any other use. Most food is simmered in lidded vessels or cooked against heated stone. Flame is not used for illumination except during necessary work. After dark, people know their rooms by touch. They speak, mend, eat, and lie down in a darkness made familiar by years.

This does not make them solemn. It makes their merriment difficult for strangers to witness.

Laughter belongs to inner rooms. So do songs, arguments, courtship, games of marked pebbles, and stories in which the geography of the city is exaggerated into absurdity. Children are sent racing through covered routes to carry messages, and return by deliberately impossible detours, claiming a new flood has swallowed half the district. Old people tell lies about having crossed the entire plateau. Everyone knows these are lies; that is why the stories are beloved.

No one knows how many people live in the city. Counting is complicated by weather, migration between rooms, and disagreement over what constitutes a household. A district that appears deserted in winter may fill in the dry season when families return to clean cisterns or graze animals on nearby shelves. One basin may hold only thirty people across a thousand structures; another may contain several hundred within hearing distance of one another, though no gathering reveals them all at once.

They do not think of themselves as one undivided people. They belong first to a flow.

A person is of the upper channels, the silt courts, the western seep, the warm cisterns, the south-falling water. Kinship follows descent, marriage, adoption, and fosterage, but responsibility follows drainage. Those who receive the same runoff must cooperate whether they are kin or enemies. A disliked neighbor upstream can foul one’s drinking basin. A stranger clearing a culvert may save twenty homes. This practical dependence has become morality.

Children are taught the path of water through their district before they are taught its human genealogy. They memorize which channels carry drinking rain, which receive wash water, which vanish underground, and which emerge in another quarter. They learn to recognize the change in sound that means a hidden conduit is filling too quickly. An experienced listener can kneel in a passage, lay an ear to the paving, and say that a blockage lies three courtyards downhill.

The people who do this best are called by names that translate imperfectly as hearers, openers, or those who go ahead of the flood. They are not rulers. They are surveyors without instruments, historians of buried slopes, and negotiators in disputes between watersheds. Their staffs are marked with depths and distances. Their most valuable possessions are cords knotted to record gradients, rainfall sequences, and the timing of flows through underground channels.

During long rain they move continually. They clear grit with shoulder blades from animals, pry wedged stones loose with hardwood bars, pack leaking joints with clay and fiber, and break openings in walls that have become dangerously watertight. Their duty sometimes requires damaging a private room to preserve a public channel. Such acts are remembered for generations.

Authority elsewhere is similarly narrow. There are keepers of food chambers, witnesses of boundary flows, people who know safe passages through submerged quarters, and elders trusted to divide clean water during a dry interval. No one governs the whole city because no one can reach the whole city reliably, and because decisions made in one watershed may be foolish in the next.

Messages travel along overlapping human chains. A change in the central basin may be known on its southern shore by evening, at the eastern escarpment after several days, and in a fog-isolated western district not until the season has turned. News mutates with distance. The city possesses no single present.

Its people reckon time by wetness as much as by sun. There is the first roof-dripping after frost, the month when the upper channels run clear, the weeks of red silt, the interval when the Three Basins become one, the brief season when bedding can be carried outdoors. Birth years are remembered through unusual water: the child born when the north causeway disappeared, the twins of the dry cistern, the old woman who came during the rain that flowed backward under the wind.

Food comes from many small sources, none sufficient alone. Families cultivate hardy tubers in pockets of silt, bitter greens along drained courtyards, fungi in dark chambers, and low beds of grain in the districts that dry first. They gather eggs from cliff birds when the nesting ledges can be reached, net small translucent fish in the lower channels, and keep a few shaggy, narrow-bodied grazing animals on the plateau margins. These animals are brought into roofless courts during severe weather, but never in large herds. Their hooves damage paving, their dung fouls water, and the city has little pasture.

Trade climbs from the country below. Porters arrive with salt, grain, timber, oil, woven cloth, medicinal bark, and occasional luxuries such as colored stone or a sealed jar of dried fruit. They depart carrying dense cakes of peat, lichen dyes, fine waterproof clay, carved stone vessels, and knowledge of the plateau’s rainfall, which predicts floods in valleys far beneath the cliffs. There are established climbing roads, but landslips frequently alter them. Some trading families below maintain relations with a single escarpment district for centuries without ever visiting the central basin.

To outsiders, the plateau people seem poor because they own little that cannot be carried. Within the city, wealth has other forms: a dry chamber, a dependable roof, rights to the first clean runoff, access to a warm cistern, a store of fuel, knowledge of an uncollapsed passage. The richest inheritance may be a route.

Routes are guarded, exchanged in marriage, revealed in gratitude, and sometimes lost when the last person who knows them dies. They are described through sequences rather than maps: pass beneath the split lintel; take the stair where water climbs the left wall; cross three roofs; descend when the stone turns red; ignore the first sound of falling water. A route can remain useful for two hundred years or vanish overnight.

The dead remain in the city, but not near flowing water. Bodies are wrapped in woven fiber and placed in high, dry chambers, often several stories above the rooms occupied in life. The entrances are then narrowed with loose stone. Nothing is sealed completely. Air must pass. Water must pass if it comes. The same law applies to the dead as to the living.

Over generations these chambers become difficult to distinguish from abandoned rooms. Bones settle among mats, beads, stone cups, and offerings of dried food. A roof collapse may return an ancestor unexpectedly to the weather. When this happens, the remains are gathered without alarm and carried to another height. The people say that the dead are patient about being moved because they understand the city better than anyone.

Their beliefs are less concerned with beings in the water than outsiders expect. They do not worship the central basin, and they know that mist is mist. Their sacred stories dwell instead on obligation, passage, and consequence. Water remembers no insult, they say, but carries every one downhill. A good act may likewise arrive far from the hand that performed it.

They distrust permanence when permanence means obstruction. A wall intended to last forever is an arrogant wall. The best construction yields in known places. Roofs are laid so that failure will spill outward rather than crush the room beneath. Bridges have stones designed to wash free under exceptional pressure. A house may be respected not because it has stood unchanged, but because generations have allowed it to alter without losing its use.

This attitude enters their character, though not uniformly. Some are conservative to the point of obsession, preserving old gradients and inherited channels long after the surrounding district has shifted. Others are restless explorers of drowned streets, convinced that an abandoned conduit can be reopened or an isolated quarter resettled. Young people sometimes leave their watershed simply to escape the weight of remembered water disputes. Some descend from the plateau and never return. Others find the lowlands intolerably loud, warm, crowded with vegetation, and enclosed by upward slopes.

Those who return often do so in rain.

The nonhuman inhabitants are equally dispersed. The city contains no lush abundance, but life persists in layers fitted closely to stone and water. Lichens consume the outer surfaces by grains. Mosses hold minute forests in sheltered joints. Pale worms inhabit the silt of submerged rooms. Tiny crustaceans appear in basins that seemed empty the day before, hatched from eggs preserved through years of cold dryness. Frogs survive where underground water carries a little warmth, their skin nearly the color of limestone. Their calls are so rare that people stop speaking to hear them.

Slate birds follow the rain fronts across the plateau. They arrive not as permanent residents but as quick claimants to newly exposed mud, leaving three-pronged tracks among human footprints. Small bats roost in upper chambers and pass between districts through cracks no person could follow. Cliff hares enter the outer ruins during fog, feeding on moss and the bark of flattened shrubs. Foxes travel the drainage lines at night because channels make easier paths than broken streets. They are seldom seen directly. People know individual foxes by the shape and spacing of their prints and give them names that may outlast certainty that it is the same animal.

The grazing animals at the western edge are neither fully domestic nor entirely wild. They descend toward inhabited courts during hard weather and withdraw when grass appears on the upper shelves. Some bear old ownership cuts in their ears; others have never been touched. Families argue over whether an animal belongs to the person whose mark it carries, the district whose shelter it uses, or no one at all.

Beneath all these visible lives is another community that the people recognize only through effects. Certain cisterns sour if covered. Certain submerged chambers produce a skin of red or white at particular times of year. Clay taken from one hollow preserves food; clay from another spoils it. Old water can carry fever without smell or discoloration. The inhabitants have accumulated exact rules around these invisible changes, though their explanations differ. They know to let new rain flush a channel before drinking, to keep cooking vessels above sleeping height, to abandon a basin after finding particular dead insects along its edge.

Illness travels by watershed and is remembered that way. So does health. There are healers who specialize in cold-swollen joints, infected feet, lung sickness from damp rooms, and the confusion caused by bad cisterns. Their medicines are modest: heat, drainage, bitter roots, clean cloth, fasting, broth, isolation, movement to a drier quarter. Their finest skill is recognizing when a person’s dwelling is the disease.

In the evening, the inhabitants become briefly visible.

They emerge to lift channel gates made from fitted slabs, brush silt from grates of pierced stone, inspect roof drains, and read the coming rain in the direction of mist over the basin. Figures stand on terraces at many distances, each apparently alone. A child carries a basket of peat beneath a cloak. Two old women lever a stone into place while arguing about its angle. A man empties foul water into a marked channel, waits, then rinses the vessel in another. On a far causeway, a line of people crosses between districts, each holding the shoulder of the person ahead because fog has begun to rise.

For a few moments the city does not seem empty at all. It seems inhabited in the way a body is inhabited: not in every chamber, not visibly, but through circulation, warmth, maintenance, and countless small responses to pressure.

Then doors are covered. The figures withdraw into walls. Rain begins softly on the roofs.

Downstream, someone hears the first change in the channels and wakes.
