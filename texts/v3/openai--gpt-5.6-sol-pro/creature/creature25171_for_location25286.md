At first, one mistakes the trees for the inhabitants.

They have the spacing of a procession and the composure of officials. Their pale trunks turn subtly toward the avenues, though no crown inclines and no leaf presents a different face. In the communal halls, roots rise from the paving in low coils like bodies seated with their hands folded. Smaller ferns occupy the recesses along the walls. Vines gather at intersections and descend in parallel strands, suggesting queues disappearing underground.

But the plants do not possess the city. They do not compete for light, drink the standing water, or press against one another for room. Cut into a leaf—if cutting can be made to occur—and its interior is neither sap nor fiber, but a finer continuation of the same green surface, folded inward beyond sight. The wound is already present on another leaf several streets away. The blade comes away warm and spotless.

The plants are closer to furniture than citizens. Or perhaps to gestures the city has held so long that they have become botanical.

The water offers another possibility.

Within the suspended rain, there are denser arrangements of droplets: upright intervals around which the downpour appears to have divided. They resemble the spaces left by people standing in a crowd. One can pass a hand through such an interval and feel nothing but the plateau’s uniform heat, yet afterward the hand seems misplaced. Its reflection continues moving in the canals while the hand itself has stopped.

Along the marsh roads, these rain-shaped vacancies occur in groups. Some are tall and narrow. Some spread close to the ground or extend across several doorways at once. They gather beneath arcades, around stone basins, at the edges of flooded squares. On the central boulevards, hundreds stand in ordered files, each facing a different one of the superimposed horizons.

They are easiest to notice by not looking at them.

A direct glance finds only rain, leaves, masonry. Turn away, and the mind supplies posture: a shoulder beneath a hanging vine, a bowed head under an arch, a line of backs receding through the white roar. Turn again, and the impression is gone. Not dispelled—relocated. It may now be felt behind the observer, or overhead, where the underside of a bridge has acquired the unmistakable gravity of a crowded balcony.

These are called no name. A name would have to be sounded, remembered, and repeated, and none of those acts remains separate here. Yet after long enough in the city, a visitor begins privately to think of them as the Present.

Not *those who are present*. The Present itself, distributed into local shapes.

They have no bodies. Bodies distinguish an interior from an exterior, whereas these inhabitants are made from continuities: the same heat passing through room and street, the same roar filling skull and cistern, the same city opening on either side of a wall. What resembles an individual is only a place where several continuities cross.

One may stand at such a crossing without knowing it. The first sign is accommodation. The paving seems exactly suited to the feet. A stone ledge meets the hand at its natural resting height. The nearest doorway frames the body so precisely that for a moment it becomes impossible to tell whether the doorway was shaped for the visitor or the visitor has always been an unfinished portion of the doorway.

Then memories begin to acquire architecture.

A childhood room appears as a flooded chamber beneath the western terraces. The face of someone long dead occupies the plan of a courtyard: eyes in two dark wells, mouth in a stair descending under moss. A regret becomes an alley that has always connected the market arcades to a district beyond the rim. These things are not visions laid over the city. They are discovered as physical facts. One can walk their dimensions, touch their warm walls, enter by one recollection and emerge through another.

The Present inhabit these correspondences.

Their perception is not sight. It is fit.

They know whatever meets whatever else: root with drain, rain with air, bridge with chasm, room with impossible interior. They know a visitor as a sequence of fittings—the sole against pavement, breath against heat, expectation against doorway. Where the fit is imperfect, the city makes room before contact. A leaf bends without moving. A corridor arrives at the destination that will later be chosen. A footprint darkens the stone in readiness for the foot.

This is not foresight. Foresight would separate before from after. To the inhabitants, arrival and anticipation are the same posture viewed from opposite streets.

Nor do they communicate. Communication requires a distance to be crossed. Instead, they coincide.

When many of them coincide around a plaza, the place feels ceremonial. Rows of wall-grown seats seem occupied, although each remains visibly empty. The warm water covering the paving takes on a faint pressure, as if bearing the combined weight of a multitude. The roar does not change, but its meaning does. Without gaining words, it becomes attentive.

In smaller chambers, coincidence resembles intimacy. Two recesses face each other across a stone table. Between them hangs a sheet of water, perfectly still. Anyone entering understands, with painful certainty, that something has been exchanged there continually for longer than duration: not speech, not affection, but complete mutual inclusion. Each recess contains the other. Each is the other’s view of itself.

There are also solitary ones, if solitary is the right word.

They inhabit rooms with no entrances, the undersides of stationary ripples, and the long instant between a suspended droplet and the pavement it does not strike. They are not imprisoned. The enclosure is their form. Some are as small as the glistening hollow in a grain of basalt. Others extend through whole districts. The largest may be the central structure itself, whose terraces, aqueducts, drowned chambers, and nested cities are less a dwelling than a single inhabitant’s anatomy.

If so, its organs are neighborhoods.

Its thought occurs as parallel boulevards. Its memory is the repetition of the caldera rim. The roofless halls are cavities of attention; the marshes are broad, shallow senses; the rivers entering from nonexistent directions are impulses that have no need of origin. The square at the summit, covered in clear water, may be the surface on which it recognizes itself.

Yet calling it one creature is misleading. The city does not unify its residents. It permits them to be identical without becoming singular.

There is no birth among them. Sometimes a new upright absence appears in the rain, but it was also present in older courtyards and in the smallest city nested inside a garden basin. There is no death. An apparent inhabitant may cease to be discernible, only because the distinction that outlined it has spread into the surroundings. Their nearest equivalent to ancestry is scale: a presence in a doorway may contain a district; that district may occupy a recess in another presence; neither is prior.

They do not eat, though the city provides basins at hand height.

They do not sleep, though chambers open around them like bedrooms.

They do not rule, though terraces rise like thrones.

These accommodations are not unused. Use here has been separated from action. A seat fulfills sitting without anyone descending into it. A road fulfills passage without anything traversing its length. The wells fulfill thirst. The halls fulfill assembly. Every need has been answered so completely that no needy being remains.

This is what gives the city its density.

Its apparent emptiness is not a lack of inhabitants but the residue of perfect provision. The citizens are what remains when dwelling has consumed the dweller, when every act has become its place, when every body has been translated into the room that would contain it.

A visitor is different. A visitor still carries gaps.

Hunger is a gap. Curiosity is a gap. Fear opens rapidly branching corridors. Memory separates what happened from what did not, and so produces doors, balconies, shafts, entire quarters of unstable distance. The Present gather around these discontinuities—not with malice, and perhaps not with interest. Interest would imply something unknown. They gather as water gathers in a depression, except that here the gathering precedes the hollow.

Soon the visitor begins to appear throughout the city.

Not copies. Accommodations.

One figure stands at the end of a boulevard not yet walked. Another looks upward from beneath the paving. Another occupies a rain-filled room inside the central mass, dry-leaved trees rising around its shoulders. Reflections show the visitor seated among the wall-grown seats, or leaning over a distant bridge, or descending a vertical street with the calm of someone for whom downward has become optional.

Each figure feels more natural than the moving body.

Movement starts to seem like an error caused by being insufficiently distributed. Thought, likewise, appears cramped when confined to one head. The omnipresent roar offers relief from both. It enters every pause between words, then reveals that the pauses were its true continuity and the words only brief obstructions.

The inhabitants do not take the visitor. Nothing is taken.

Rather, the city completes the visitor’s accommodations.

The wet footprint that preceded the foot no longer seems prophetic. It is simply more accurate than the foot. The distant reflection is not an image but the portion of the self that already fits there. Leaves part because the shoulders belong between them. A room deep in the basalt has always possessed the exact volume of the lungs.

Eventually there may still be a person walking. There may still be effort, pulse, decision. But these become minor local phenomena, like one of the caldera’s horizons pretending to be the outermost.

What lives there lives as placement.

It lives in the finished relation between things, in the pressure of an absent crowd, in the posture a room assumes around the body that will never enter it. It is plural wherever the city has doors and singular wherever the roar abolishes distance. It has no face except the arrangement of apertures on a tower, no limbs except roads, no voice except the unvarying fact of water.

And if, after long walking, the streets seem less empty, it is not because anyone has approached.

It is because the visitor has begun to understand how thoroughly everyone is already there.
