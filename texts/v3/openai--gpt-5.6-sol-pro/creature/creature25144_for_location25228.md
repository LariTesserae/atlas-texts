### The Workers

At first it seems that nothing lives in the Green Works.

Anything large enough to be noticed has learned to keep low. There are no grazing herds crossing the open floor, no animals standing clear against the sky. Even the birds vanish as soon as they descend. A snipe becomes a brief tearing sound and then is gone into sedge. A rail is only a cry moving through reeds. Frogs speak without showing their mouths. The inhabitants are present chiefly as effects: a trembling stem, a bubble in black water, seed husks scattered beneath a perch.

The first and greatest inhabitant is the floor itself.

It is not one creature, though it behaves at times like a single broad, slow body. Its skin is moss and interlaced grass. Its tougher tissues are sedge root, willow fiber, and reed rhizome. Beneath these lie the pressed remains of former seasons, still retaining their structure in the dark: leaves flattened into brown paper, stems softened but not erased, root systems through which newer roots have grown. The dead are not buried apart from the living. They are used as scaffolding.

This body breathes unevenly. Rain enters it with a whisper and is held. Air passes down through cracks and old vole runs. In the deeper layers, where oxygen fails, small makers of gas consume what the upper growth has discarded. Their bubbles gather under root plates until they escape into the central lane with a gulp. Elsewhere water is drawn upward through thousands of narrow stems and given to the air one invisible portion at a time.

No part directs the rest. Each reed attends only to water, light, damage, and the pressure of neighboring reeds. Yet whole stands lean together before the wind. They clatter in sequence, rise in sequence, shed pollen and seed within the same few days. Their coordination is not thought, but neither is it accidental. They share weather, soil, ancestry, and the buried channels along which moisture travels. That is enough to make a multitude act like machinery.

Among the roots live the smaller workers.

Black beetles polish the tunnels they repeatedly travel. Pale larvae inhabit the submerged fiber, chewing what has already begun to soften. Water beetles keep private reservoirs beneath overlapping leaves and row through them on bright-edged legs. There are worms thin enough to occupy the films around roots, mites no larger than grains of peat, springtails that gather on still pools until the water appears dusted with soot. Snails wake after rain and climb into the upright world, rasping algae from reed stems. As the air dries, they descend, leaving silver routes that last only until the next condensation.

The voles live in warmth within the cold mat. Their chambers are lined with shredded grass and built above one level of water but below another level of weather. Generations may be born without seeing the basin from edge to edge. To them the Green Works is not open country. It is a dark architecture of edible walls, crowded passages, root pillars, and sudden wet rooms. They know storms as pressure and vibration. They know hunting birds as a shadow that briefly plugs an entrance.

Frogs occupy the boundary between that hidden interior and the light. Their backs have the color and broken texture of wet peat. They sit with their bellies in cold seepage, waiting for whatever the reeds dislodge. When they call, the root mat carries the sound. One answers another across a distance that appears empty, and soon the ground itself seems full of throats.

In the deepest pockets, small fish endure the basin’s withdrawals. They are dark, narrow-bodied things, economical in movement. When water contracts, they gather beneath the overhang of roots where the last cold remains. They feed on larvae, on drowned midges, sometimes on smaller versions of themselves. Rain enlarges their world without warning. A seam opens, two pools become connected, and for a few nights they travel through corridors that will shortly cease to exist.

Above them, the reeds are inhabited at every height.

Spiders fasten lines between stems, rebuilding after each hard wind. Their webs are most visible before sunrise, when droplets reveal structures that daylight will erase. Caterpillars fold leaves around themselves. Aphids crowd the tender shoots, and small wasps search among them with quick, exact movements. Midges turn above the pools in revolving columns, each individual rising and falling while the whole swarm remains in place like a wheel mounted on air.

The birds use this vertical abundance without ever fully belonging to it. Warblers pass through the stems as if through a forest made narrow. They brace one foot on each reed, their weight scarcely bowing either, and take insects from the undersides of leaves. Rails inhabit the denser center, stepping on roots concealed beneath water. Their long toes distribute their weight; their striped bodies divide into bars of light and shadow. They are more often heard than seen and seem, when they finally cross an opening, improbably solid.

Snipe sleep with their bills laid along their backs. When startled, they leave explosively, carrying mud on their feet and fragments of the hollow’s life in their feathers. In evening light, starlings arrive from the surrounding country. They do not settle at once. They become a single dark organism above the basin, contracting, widening, turning without an apparent leader. Then the organism breaks into thousands of birds, and the reeds receive them.

Their roost is quarrelsome and hot. Stems bow beneath the accumulated weight. Droppings strike the leaves and enter the water, returning distant fields to the basin as nourishment. By morning the starlings have gone, leaving feathers, noise remembered in silence, and a richer film on the roots.

The old willows support a slower population. Fungi enter their split trunks and reduce the heartwood to damp red meal. Beetles enlarge the passages. Moss seals the outer bark, and willow roots pass through the remains of willow branches, so that parent, offspring, and fallen limb become difficult to distinguish. A branch may lie still for years before producing a line of shoots. What appears dead at one end flowers at the other.

There are hunters, though few announce themselves. A heron sometimes stands at the central lane, gray enough to resemble one of the willows raised briefly upright. It waits without joining the clatter around it. Harriers travel low over the sedges, reading movements too slight for a human eye. At night, small owls work the margins. Their prey disappear cleanly, but not entirely: fur becomes lining, bone becomes mineral, blood enters insect and root.

Nothing here possesses the whole place. Each inhabitant knows a particular Green Works.

For the fish it is a chain of temporary darknesses. For the vole it is a buried city. For the warbler it is a set of swaying perches and concealed cups. For the snail it is a wet interval between rains. For the reed it is light above, water below, and the pressure of its own kind on every side.

Taken together, they form the resident of the basin: a dispersed creature with no fixed boundary and no central mind. Birds leave it and return. Water enters and vanishes. Stems rise from the bodies of former stems. Its smallest members replace themselves in hours; its willows persist through centuries by lying down and rooting again. It eats sunlight, stone dust, rain, old fiber, and its own dead. Its waste becomes its foundation.

It does not sleep, although portions of it become still. In winter the visible machinery pales and collapses, while work continues in the peat. In spring the floor drives up new green points through last year’s wreckage. Summer fills the air with wings and pollen. Autumn lowers seed into the same wet seams that received it before.

Thus what lives in the Green Works is not hidden because it is scarce. It is hidden because it is everywhere, distributed through the mat, the water, the stems, and the open air. The seeming emptiness is merely the space above its body.

Underfoot, it is continuously feeding, drowning, hatching, rooting, rotting, and beginning again.
