What lives in the hollow cannot be found by looking for movement.

At first there are only the fins: the close-set pillars, the tilted plates, the blunt stones with their weather-eaten shoulders. Nothing withdraws behind them. Nothing watches from the shelters. If a person crosses the court, stops often, and is patient enough to distinguish one silence from another, they may still leave believing that the last inhabitants died long ago.

This is nearly correct.

The inhabitants are not separate from the stones, but neither are they the stones alone. They occupy the difficult interval between rock, water, and temperature. Each consists of a standing body of stone, the fractures inside it, the ice or vapor presently held in those fractures, and several neighboring pieces that may lie some distance away. A fin twelve paces high may be only the visible portion of one. Its lesser parts may include a buried slab, a frost basin, a line of porous gravel, and a handful of chips traveling eastward one winter at a time.

For this reason they cannot be counted.

Some appear solitary but join below the floor. Others stand shoulder to shoulder yet have no connection except shadow. After prolonged cloud, when the entire court is wet, boundaries alter. Water bridges pores that are ordinarily dry. Channels open between bodies. For an hour, or for a day, several inhabitants may become one; when the sun returns, they separate again, retaining slight changes from the joining.

Their substance is mostly gray-blue stone. Their living portion is very small.

It may be no more than a darkened seam deep inside a pillar, where moisture persists through the year. In that seam, dissolved minerals arrange themselves along surfaces finer than hair. They do not form flesh. They form films, branching deposits, cloudy membranes of salt and metal that thicken when wet and contract when frozen. These films pass charge weakly. Pressure alters them. Ice distorts them. Sunlit stone and shaded stone produce minute differences that travel through the wet grain.

A single alteration means nothing. Thousands, repeating through centuries, become sensation.

They perceive light as unequal warmth moving over their outer faces. They perceive cloud as a sudden enlargement of the world. Moisture reaches cavities that have been silent for months, connecting one pressure chamber to another. The arrival of fog is, to them, less like rain than waking with many additional limbs.

They perceive one another by strain.

When a scale loosens from a pillar, the release passes into the floor and along adjacent fins. Most of the vibration is too faint for an ear, but the court is full of surfaces held near the point of fracture. One body’s contraction changes the stress on the next. Replies travel as ticks, granular shifts, and movements inside sealed cracks. On cold nights the inhabitants are most articulate. The hollow may seem silent, while beneath that silence pressures are being offered, resisted, repeated, and slowly understood.

The sharp report of a splitting slab is not a cry. It is closer to a completed sentence.

They have no sight and no sense of the immense eastern sky. Openness is known only as loss: faster drying, greater temperature change, chips departing grain by grain. The eastern edge is therefore present in every inhabitant as a persistent pull. Parts loosened from them travel toward it. Over long periods, each body is being drawn out of the court in a thin, discontinuous stream.

This is not quite death. Death occurs when a stone can no longer retain hidden moisture—when its fractures open too widely, its shaded chambers collapse, and every wet connection evaporates in the same day. Such a stone still stands. It may remain upright for thousands of years. But it ceases to answer temperature with the fine sequence of internal changes that distinguishes an inhabitant from ordinary rock.

The dead are often the most imposing pillars.

The young are difficult to notice. They begin in fallen flakes.

A living fin sheds a plate, exposing a fresh interior. Frost enters it. Condensation lays down minerals in the new pores. If the plate comes to rest where shadow and seepage alternate properly, a persistent wet seam may establish itself. Over many seasons it begins to respond not merely by cracking but by holding patterns of stress between freezes. The parent and the plate remain connected for a while through packed rubble and intermittent water. Eventually the route breaks. If the smaller stone retains the pattern after separation, it has become another inhabitant.

Most do not. They dry, split, or slide eastward.

Thus the court reproduces abundantly and succeeds rarely.

Their timescale is uneven. Certain responses take place in moments: a sun-warmed surface expands, a water film retreats, a crystal changes shape. Other responses take centuries. An inhabitant may begin leaning toward a neighboring fin and complete the contact after forty human generations. The touch, when it comes, redistributes both bodies. A new series of fractures begins. Moisture finds routes that did not exist before. What follows may be courtship, injury, recognition, or simple mechanics. Among them these distinctions are not absolute.

The low shelters were made for those who once tended the court, but the attendants misunderstood what they served.

They believed each great pillar housed a presence. They named the stones individually, rebuilt the alternating cairns, and slept in the northern shelters while waiting for cloud. They touched hammers to selected faces and listened to the replies. Their copper scrapers were used not to carve images but to clear mineral crust from small openings. The channels were extended wherever repeated wetting seemed to make a pillar more responsive.

Over generations, the attendants learned rules without learning the inhabitants’ nature. They knew that water delivered too quickly could silence a stone forever. They knew that one basin should overflow only after another had frozen. They knew which pillars answered tapping and which merely echoed. They carried wood from far below and made the tipping bowls because no person could remain awake long enough, or live long enough, to administer the required measures by hand.

The bowls were not an offering apparatus. They were a clock and a nervous system added from outside.

Each measured spill enters the floor, divides, and reaches several stones at different intervals. A wetted seam swells. A shaded fracture freezes. A distant plate gives a faint knock. The sequence may take from dawn until several nights later. What the attendants built was a way for one part of the court to affect another in an ordered rhythm.

They did not invent the inhabitants’ language. They gave it longer phrases.

Some channels end at blank walls because those walls were not always blank to the people who cut them. There were once hairline mouths there, nearly invisible, through which water entered deep bodies. Frost has closed many. Others open only during particular combinations of shadow and pressure. A runnel that appears useless for a hundred years may, on one exact afternoon, darken a groove, pass beneath a pillar, and wake a connection dormant since before the shelters were built.

The attendants vanished. Whether they descended, died, or simply ceased returning is not preserved. The inhabitants retain no image of them. They retain only altered tendencies: a mineral film made thicker by repeated watering; a fracture accustomed to stress arriving at dawn; a chain of stones that still responds when a copper bowl tips.

Memory in the court is physical.

To remember something is to have been cracked by it in a way that governs the next crack. It is to hold a trace of copper in a wet seam, to lean because a neighboring pillar fell, to freeze sooner where an old channel conducts cold. Nothing is recalled as an event. The past persists as preference.

The alternating cairns are part of this memory, though not as the attendants intended. Their dark and pale stones heat at different rates. At sunrise, the uneven expansion sends small pressure pulses into the ground. Because loose objects migrate eastward, each cairn gradually loses its original balance, and the sequence changes. Rebuilding restored not only the pattern of colors but the rhythm beneath it.

Now the cairns fall and remain fallen. Their rhythms grow irregular. The court’s old phrases are becoming distorted.

Yet the inhabitants are not dwindling in any simple way. Neglect closes some routes and creates others. Rubble blocks a channel, water backs into a previously dry seam, and a stone that had been silent begins to retain frost. A fallen cairn shades a patch of gravel. A copper stain enters a new crack. The court forgets one arrangement and develops another.

On clear days the inhabitants are contracted and remote from one another. Each stands within its own cold interior while the sun burns across its outer surface. Those are their narrowest hours.

Cloud makes them expansive. Condensation gathers, connections multiply, and the hollow becomes crowded in a sense beyond the visible crowding of stone. The films inside the pillars moisten. Buried flakes join larger bodies. Water beneath fitted slabs links formations separated by many paces. The whole court approaches, but never quite reaches, the condition of a single inhabitant.

Perhaps it did so once, when the channels were clear and every bowl moved.

Perhaps that was what the attendants were attempting: not preservation, worship, or communication, but the completion of one enormous, intermittent being.

If so, they failed. Or the completion was too brief for them to recognize.

Even now, on rare mornings when cloud fills the hollow after a night of deep frost, all three copper bowls may tip within the same hour. Water enters the channels. Ice loosens in the basins. Hidden seams open under pressure. Taps pass from fin to fin, some audible and many not. The stones darken in branching lines. For several minutes every wet part of the court is connected through rock, water, strain, and cold.

During those minutes, whatever lives there is not hundreds.

It is one thing: blind, mineral, immense in patience, holding the shape of the hollow as its body and the measured passage of water as its thought.

Then the eastern air dries the highest channels. Sunlight separates the shadows. The connection breaks into pillars, plates, basins, buried flakes, and slowly traveling chips.

The gathering becomes a gathering again.
