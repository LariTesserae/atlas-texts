At first the inhabitants seem to be the ants, because they are the ones most often abroad.

They come in several sizes and several kinds. Fine black workers travel in threads through cracks too narrow to admit daylight. Larger red-brown ants move openly along the roots, pausing at intersections to touch one another with their antennae. Weaver ants inhabit the upper levels, where leaves have been pulled into chambers and stitched with pale larval silk. Their city is suspended, flexible, and green. When wind passes through it, whole rooms tilt together.

None of these ants knows the settlement entire. Each knows a warmth, a sequence of scents, the pressure of bodies ahead, the nearness of food or danger. A worker may spend her life between a nursery under one buttress and a fruit hollow three roots away. To her, the rest of the city is not unknown so much as nonexistent. Yet through thousands of such partial lives, routes form and vanish with something like intention. Traffic thickens a passage. Damp clay gives way beneath it. Roots follow the moisture. A road becomes a tunnel, then a columned gallery, without anyone having imagined either.

The queens live farther in. They are seldom seen: heavy-bodied centers of dark chambers, attended by daughters that clean them, feed them, and carry away their eggs. During floods, the brood moves before the water arrives. White larvae and translucent pupae pass upward in careful jaws, one by one, while lower rooms fill with cold brown light. The queen herself may remain until the damp reaches the nursery walls. Around her, panic has no cry. It appears only as speed.

The termites are less visible and, in some districts, more numerous. Their walls conceal them almost completely. Behind membranes of clay, pale workers move in continuous contact, blind or nearly blind, soft as kernels soaked in water. Soldiers stand at breaches with enlarged heads and jaws, plugging openings with their own bodies while workers repair the edges around them. Their structures are delicate only from outside. Within, they are kept at a steadier humidity than the open floodplain, and chambers are shaped by air as much as by earth.

They cultivate darkness. In the deeper pockets they tend fungus through chewed plant matter, feeding it with wood, leaf fiber, and dung gathered grain by grain. The fungus is neither crop nor tenant in any simple sense. It digests what the termites cannot and is pruned, eaten, spread, and protected by them. Its pale nodules rise in the chambers like a second, subterranean settlement. Mites live among it. Tiny flies steal from it. Threadlike worms graze on its margins. The termites’ garden is already a crowded wilderness.

Below both ants and termites, the worms inhabit the city vertically. Their passages descend through fresh silt into older, darker ground. They emerge at night or during rain, swallowing the new surface and leaving it behind as coiled towers. They are builders without districts, opening shafts that later carry air, roots, water, springtails, and ant scouts. A worm may erase a boundary simply by feeding through it. It may join two chambers whose occupants had never met. In dry weather it withdraws deep into the earth, knotted in a moist pocket, while others continue to use the hollow of its labor.

Springtails gather wherever decay is wet enough to soften. They resemble ash until the ash leaps. At the edge of a pool they feed on algae, spores, and the invisible films that form on mud. One falling drop can scatter hundreds. They return almost at once, so lightly that the water’s skin bears them. Mites move among them in red, amber, and ivory forms: hunters, scavengers, parasites, feeders on fungus and rot. To the larger inhabitants these creatures are little more than motion in the soil. To one another they are herd, rival, prey, and weather.

Beetles work the heavier materials. Some arrive polished and dark, plated against the abrasion of narrow tunnels. Some carry horned heads or shovel-shaped forelegs. They bury fruit pulp, roll fragments of dung, scrape fungus from wood, and lay eggs in provisions that will become both cradle and meal. Their larvae live as pale, folded things beneath the city, blind mouths enlarging chambers around themselves. When they emerge as adults, they break through ceilings and leave round apertures that may later serve as doors for species much smaller than they are.

Flies occupy the settlement briefly but in great numbers. They discover sweetness almost as soon as it begins to ferment. Their maggots turn fallen fruit into loosened pulp; then, for one warm interval, the adults rise together. Their wings fill a courtyard with a metallic haze. By evening the cloud has dispersed into the forest, leaving empty cases, footprints in the fruit skin, and a new wealth of prey.

The hunters live according to these gatherings.

Small geckos wait on the root faces where ant roads cross open clay. Their bodies are the color of silt, bark, or lichen according to where they pause. Only the eye remains conspicuous: bright, lidless, measuring distance. A gecko may remain still through the passage of hundreds of insects, then take one moth with a movement too quick to divide into beginning and end. Its droppings fall into the streets below, where ants dismantle them.

Tree frogs inhabit the folded leaves and water pockets. By day they seem grown from the vegetation: green backs, pale bellies, toes widened into wet discs. Their breathing is almost the only sign that they are separate from the leaf. At dusk they descend toward the cisterns. A male calls once, softly, and waits for an answer through a silence full of smaller lives. Eggs laid over water swell into clear beads. Tadpoles drop into temporary pools and race the evaporation, feeding on algae and one another before their basin disappears.

The narrow brown snake is not a resident in the manner of the colonies, but it returns often enough to belong to the city’s memory. It enters without respecting roads. Its body passes over walls and through roofs, reading vibrations through its jaw. Ant traffic reverses before its head appears. Frogs flatten into their folds. Geckos vanish behind root bark. Sometimes the snake finds nothing. Sometimes a struggling bulge travels slowly down its length as it withdraws into the grass. In either case, the emptied streets remain empty for a while, as if absence itself had left a scent.

There are quieter inhabitants still: nematodes in films of water between grains; rotifers waking when a puddle returns; algae waiting as dust; fungal threads extending through walls and dead wood. Seeds lie dormant under entire districts. Bacteria occupy every damp surface, digesting the settlement molecule by molecule. They live in guts, in pools, in clay, in fruit, in the thin seam where a dead wing touches mud. Nearly every visible inhabitant carries another population within it.

And over all of them lives the fig.

It is not merely roof or foundation. Its roots drink from the same channels the animals excavate. Their waste feeds its root tips. Its tissues thicken around repeated disturbance, closing some passages and reinforcing others. Fine roots enter abandoned chambers like searching fingers. They branch where the soil is rich, die where it dries, and leave hollow molds that become tunnels. The fig’s fruits summon birds and bats from beyond the settlement; their leavings bring seeds, insects, and distant minerals. Inside each fig, minute wasps complete lives in hidden flowers, inhabiting another city folded within the body of the first.

The fig experiences the settlement slowly. An ant road that endures for generations may be, to the tree, a brief region of warmth and aerated soil. A buried wall becomes pressure against a growing root. A flood is recorded in a new band of sediment, then entered and bound. The tree shelters the inhabitants, but it also consumes their city by growing through it. No hostility attends this. To be supported and to be overgrown are often the same event.

So there is no single citizenry beneath the roots. There are colonies nested within territories, bodies within colonies, gardens within bodies, and sleepers sealed in the mud until rain restores them. Some inhabitants live for an afternoon; some queens for years; some roots may outlast hundreds of generations moving beside them. They do not agree upon the scale or purpose of the place.

What they share is not allegiance but consequence. Each alters the conditions under which the next must live. A termite closes a breach and makes a ledge for moss. Moss keeps clay damp enough for a worm. The worm opens a shaft that drains a nursery but floods a beetle hollow below. Beetle pulp feeds flies; flies feed frogs; frogs feed the snake; the snake leaves a skin that mites reduce to fragments. Nothing acts for the city, yet the city arises from all of them.

Its true inhabitant may be this exchange itself: the ceaseless passing of shelter, moisture, food, and danger from one body to another. It has no body of its own except the temporary shape of the hollow. Each flood buries part of it, each dry season opens it again, and every living thing enters a structure already being remade by lives it cannot perceive.
