The first mistake is to think the Garden is inhabited by separate creatures.

It is inhabited by a weather.

The black trunks, the borers inside them, the sails flung between their crowns, the people crouched behind braided doors—all belong to the same moving system. Wind carries food to the plants, plants give chambers to the borers, borers foretell the gusts, and the gusts decide where every rooted or roofed thing may remain. Nothing truly lives *in* the Rattle Garden. It lives briefly at one angle to the wind.

### The wind trees

The largest inhabitants are the so-called trees, though they have neither wood nor sap. Their woven trunks are colonies of threadlike organs fused around an empty core. Young trunks are soft enough to bend, dark gray and faintly warm. With age they blacken, separating into cables that rub against one another and produce the deep notes heard throughout the hollow.

No two mature trunks sound alike. Pitch depends on height, the width of the inner chamber, the number of borers living in it, and whether the root collar is still whole. The people recognize important trees by sound long before they see them. A tree may therefore retain its identity after migration, even if half its crown has broken away.

They are patient only in appearance. Beneath each collar, mineral filaments creep continuously through cracks in the shale, testing for heat and reactive salts. These filaments do not drink. They corrode. A settled tree slowly digests the stone around its base until it has both fed itself and weakened its own anchorage. Eventually the right gust frees it.

An uprooted tree is not helpless. Its lower branches stiffen into hooked spars, while the crown folds asymmetrically to catch crosswinds. It cannot direct itself with precision, but it can influence the manner of its tumbling. Some travel only a few paces. Others circle the chimney for hours, booming whenever they strike the rock, before wedging themselves into a new slope.

The oldest trees are not the largest but the most traveled. Their trunks are scarred smooth on one side and crowded with knots of regrowth on the other. Families sheltering near them tie small identifying cords high on the bark. If the tree migrates, the cords go with it. Years later it may return from another chamber of the hollow bearing the weathered tokens of people now dead.

### The small nations of the crowns

The sail creatures live almost entirely in the air. Each consists of a hard central body surrounded by four or six transparent fins, pleated so finely that they seem to vanish when turned edge-on. They have no wings and little strength. Their art is surrender with adjustment.

By tightening one fin and loosening another, they choose which part of a turbulent current will claim them. A swarm that appears to be scattered randomly is often sorting itself by age, readiness to breed, or tolerance for heat. The youngest remain in rising columns above the fissures. Breeding adults gather in the colder downward streams. The dead, unable to alter their sails, collect in certain sheltered cracks in drifts like translucent husks.

Their predators are the net-casters. These narrow animals cling folded against the trunks, nearly indistinguishable from broken twigs. When a swarm passes, a net-caster springs outward and opens its limbs. Filaments stretch between the joints, each tipped with backward-pointing barbs. It may catch twenty sails in a single leap. If it misses the next trunk, it reels in its own net, curls around whatever it has taken, and lets the wind carry it until it collides with something solid.

The six-legged runners belong to the floor and lower walls. Their reflective alarm plates are not meant to hide them. They are meant to confuse distance. When several flee together, each becomes a sequence of copper-white flashes appearing in different places. A predator lunges at the apparent movement and strikes stone.

Runners often live near the human shelters. They eat seeds caught in cordage and the tiny parasites that gather in masks. They are not domesticated, but certain families leave warm ceramic tiles near their doors for them. A shelter with many runners is considered sound and clean. Sudden abandonment by the animals is a warning of gas, unstable rock, or a change in the vibration beneath the floor.

The leaf grazers are slower and stranger. Suspended beneath the crowns, they turn their ring-shaped mouths against the mineral crusts that form on the leaves. Their polishing benefits the plant, and a healthy tree may support hundreds. During migrations they clamp down and travel with it. Thus each old wind tree carries its own dark, hanging pasture.

### The drummers within

The borers are the Garden’s most numerous animals and, in some ways, its nervous system.

They hatch in the warm seams of young trunks and chew inward, sealing the entrance behind them with compressed fiber. A colony may spend its entire existence without seeing open air. It feeds on the inner walls and on chemical deposits carried through the tree’s threads. Pale, soft-bodied and without eyes, borers perceive the world through pressure.

Their knocking is not random. They signal breaches, predators, new growth and changes in tension. The approach of a hard gust compresses the outer fibers of a trunk, and the colony responds before the wind reaches the hollow. Neighboring trunks touch through their crowns and pass the rhythm onward. The warning that travels across the Garden is therefore made jointly by plant, animal and weather.

The people have learned part of this language.

Children are taught to place the side of the head against a trunk and distinguish ordinary feeding taps from strain calls. A rapid clustered knock means the crown is twisting. A slow beat moving downward means the collar has begun to fracture. Silence in a tree that should be full of borers is more alarming than either.

Borers are also eaten, but never indiscriminately. A trunk stripped of its colony loses an early-warning sense and is more likely to split without notice. Harvesters open only marked chambers, taking the largest animals and plugging the cut afterward with black fiber. Skilled harvesters can judge a colony’s size by tapping the trunk with a ceramic hammer and feeling the answering tremor through their fingertips.

### The people behind the stone teeth

At a distance, the Garden’s people look slight and somewhat stiff, their bodies wrapped in layered cords and overlapping plates. Bare skin is seldom visible except around the eyes and hands. Their coverings are not robes. Loose cloth would fill with seeds, catch the wind, and become a sail. Clothing is close-bound: narrow bands wound around limbs, scale-like pieces of polished leaf tied across the shoulders, and weighted belts that press downward when a gust tries to lift the wearer.

Their masks are the most carefully made things they own. Each has several dry filtering layers separated by lattices of hair-fine copper. The outer layer catches leaf grit and larger seeds. The inner layers force air through crooked passages where finer particles lodge. Between breaths, tiny suspended clappers shake the filters. A good mask gives its wearer a faint private rattle.

Masks are never shared casually. The shape of a person’s breath alters them over time, and the inner cords acquire an intimate pattern. To give someone a mask is an act associated with adoption, marriage, rescue or mourning.

The people do speak, but the Garden makes ordinary speech unreliable. Their voices are low and close, used within shelters or mouth-to-ear. Outdoors they communicate through gesture, tugged cords and percussive signals. Two people walking a dangerous path may remain joined by a line tied at the wrist. A squeeze, a double pull or a sudden slackening carries more clearly than a shout.

Their names have several forms. There is a spoken name for quiet places, a sequence of taps for distance, and a knot by which the person is represented in records. The knot-name is considered the truest because it can outlast both voice and body. It encodes household, birth chamber, the gust cycle of one’s arrival, and later additions such as apprenticeship, migration or debt.

A person’s belt is not a complete history in the sense of a written chronicle. It is closer to a landscape of obligations. One cluster records who braced the roof during a collapse. Another remembers cord lent for a child’s tether. Certain knots mark access to food shelves, safe clefts, borer colonies or thermal boxes. To cut such a belt is not merely vandalism. It is an attempt to sever the wearer from society.

### A village that must not gather

No settlement can become dense, so community exists across distance.

A household may be only two adults, several children, an elder, and whoever has been stranded there by a recent wind shift. Relatives commonly live on opposite sides of the hollow. They maintain contact through route cords and tuned frames. At agreed intervals, each household plucks a particular line. The note carries poorly through the overall clamor, but its vibration passes into adjoining stone or connecting fibers. News travels from shelter to shelter as patterns of tension.

This dispersed life produces no central square, council house or market. Important decisions are made in sequence. A proposal begins as a knot added to a public line. Those who agree tie supporting loops beside it; those who object twist the line in another direction. Because the Garden may rearrange paths while a decision is being considered, consensus can take many days. Urgency has its own blunt signal: three severed cords hung from a warning frame.

Leadership belongs less to warriors than to listeners, knot-readers and people who remember the behavior of air.

The most respected are the gust-keepers. They maintain records of migrations, vent openings, crown collapses and the songs of warning frames. A gust-keeper can stand beneath the western wall, hear one frame singing beyond the chimney and estimate which passage will become dangerous. Their knowledge is exact but never treated as certainty. The Garden punishes certainty more reliably than ignorance.

Children are tethered until they learn how to fall.

This is not regarded as confinement. A child’s first independent crossing of an exposed chamber is celebrated, though quietly. The child wears a belt containing blank lengths of cord and must return with one knot tied for every warning correctly recognized: a borer alarm, a runner flight, a change in vent odor, a crown beginning to rotate. Those who return with few knots are sent again in a calmer season.

### Hunger, craft and the absence of wetness

Food is brittle, oily, fibrous or powder-dense. Roasted borers split like seeds. Grazer eggs are scraped from leaves in hard amber clusters. Charge-mats are peeled, beaten free of grit and warmed until they curl into dark wafers. The inner tissue of a young wind plant is pale and layered, with a sharp metallic taste.

Eating is slow because masks cannot remain open for long. People take small bites in sheltered alcoves, closing the mask between them. Communal meals occur inside sealed rooms after everyone has brushed and shaken the seeds from their clothing.

Their bodies conserve their own inner moisture with extraordinary strictness. Skin is waxed by natural oils and further sealed with mineral powders. Exhaled vapor is trapped in the mask and recovered along its inner lining. Waste is sparse, dry and immediately carried to hot cracks where it is rendered harmless. To bleed openly is dangerous not only because of the loss, but because moisture awakens every dormant thing in the nearby dust.

A wound draws seeds.

For this reason healers work swiftly with heat, pressure and tight wrapping. They carry hooked copper tools for extracting anything that has already begun to unfold in flesh. Their medicine is cautery, splinting, abrasive cleaning and the careful use of reactive salts. Tenderness exists, but it must often be expressed through firmness: a hand clamped over a wound, a cord pulled tight, a hot blade held steady.

### The enclosed dead

Death is treated as a final change of shelter.

The body is bound immediately, first at the mouth and chest, then along every limb. It is enclosed in alternating layers of leaf plate, salt and compressed black fiber. The knots used are the reverse of those tied at birth. The aim is not to preserve appearance but to keep the dead from becoming fertile ground for whatever seeds entered during the last breaths.

Some bodies are placed in deep dry clefts. Others are incorporated into shelter walls, especially if the dead person was a builder or gust-keeper. There is no belief that the stone imprisons them. Rather, to brace a wall after death is considered a continuation of household duty.

The dead person’s history belt is not buried intact. Its knots are divided among those who inherit the obligations recorded there. For years afterward, a child may wear part of a grandparent’s life at the waist without possessing the whole of it. No one person is permitted to own an entire memory.

### What the Garden makes of them

The inhabitants are not silent, grim survivors in a hostile place. They are alert, exacting and often playful. They imitate the Garden’s noises, set harmless seed cases whistling outside one another’s doors, and compose rhythms that make neighboring borer colonies answer. Young net-casters are sometimes coaxed into leaping through loops for wagers. Children polish runner plates and release the animals into shafts of white light to watch them scatter brilliance across the walls.

Their beauty is tactile and acoustic. A well-made knot is admired by running it beneath the thumb. A desirable voice is one that carries clearly through a mask. Music is judged not only by what it sounds like, but by how it causes trunks, roofs and tuned lines to respond. The finest performances recruit the hollow itself: a struck frame calls to a tree, the tree’s borers answer, a gust catches the seed cords, and for a few moments no one can say which part was composed.

They do not imagine permanence as stillness. Stillness is death, blockage, a vent gone cold, a colony fallen silent. What endures must loosen, migrate, shed pieces and catch elsewhere.

A household is permanent if its cords are retied.

A law is permanent if it can be translated into a new pattern.

A person is permanent if some obligation of theirs remains under another person’s hand.

And the Rattle Garden is permanent because it never keeps its shape.
