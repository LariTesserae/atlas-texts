It is difficult to count the inhabitants of the Dry Crown.

From the lowlands, the obvious answer is the towers. They turn their leaves, sing across the plateau, open their walls to travelers, and exchange burdens through the buried network. They respond to injury. They retain preferences in shape and tone. Old towers object to being retuned, sometimes holding an obsolete chord for decades after the surrounding district has adopted another scale. Citizens speak of them as infrastructure when taxes are assessed, as ancestors when storms damage them, and as living beings whenever anyone proposes cutting one down.

The ground is less easily excluded. It heals, anticipates traffic, remembers extinct neighborhoods, and occasionally refuses a new road. Beneath every foot lies an intelligence so slow and broad that its smallest gestures are mistaken for geology. Whether it is one organism, a colony, a civilization of fused organisms, or merely the medium through which all other life thinks together is a question mostly asked by visitors.

The people who conduct markets and attend councils are more recognizable, though only at first.

At a distance they resemble narrow figures wrapped in layered black garments. They lean into the wind even while standing still, and their long heads bear swept-back fans, horns, vanes, or sprays of filament. No two silhouettes are identical. Some walk on two legs, some on four, and some unfold additional supports when gusts strengthen. Their bodies are openwork: ribs and limbs made from interlocking struts with wide spaces between, so that the air passes through them instead of carrying them away.

Up close, the garments prove to be organs.

Their outer mantles are gardens of overlapping mineral scales, heat-absorbing on the exposed side and pale beneath. Fine conductive tendrils bind these mantles directly into the wearer’s deeper tissues. A citizen may replace one according to season, profession, or mood. Market tenders cultivate broad sensory capes able to taste charge gradients from incoming goods. Civic singers wear stiff, pleated vanes that amplify the chest. Couriers favor smooth sheaths that hum at one precise note and can be recognized by the roads below.

Nothing on them is wet. Their joints move through controlled slippage between crystal planes. Their muscles are cables whose defects advance in coordinated waves. When they exert themselves, faint blue lines travel beneath the body’s dark surface, outlining a momentary anatomy and then disappearing. They do not pant, but after climbing they may stand with their arms spread, drawing charged dust and solar warmth into the lattices of the torso.

Their faces are not fixed.

The front of the head usually carries several dark, glassy regions that serve as eyes, though their number changes with age and need. A traveler preparing for the Meridian may grow additional sight patches along the jaw and shoulders. A gardener working among predatory roots often seals the forward eyes entirely and sees through delicate whiskers planted in the ground. The mouth, when present, is a cluster of vibrating plates. It is used for close speech, cutting, grafting, and the testing of resonant materials.

Expression passes through the whole body. Attention brightens the seams around the eyes. Doubt introduces a soft rattle into the throat. Amusement is a rapid sequence of harmless static discharges between the fingers. Grief causes the dorsal vanes to lose tension and bend crosswise to the wind, producing a low, wandering note that others can hear from far away.

They have no single name for themselves equivalent to *people*. The nearest term means something like **those who keep a shape while exchanging its parts**.

This is not modesty. It is anatomy.

A resident of the Crown may retain the same pattern of memory for centuries while replacing nearly every visible structure. Limbs wear thin from abrasive air. Sensory plates become clouded. Resonant cavities crack or drift out of tune. Such parts are detached at grafting houses and returned to communal tissue beds, where their useful geometries are stripped of personal pattern and encouraged into new growth.

Replacements are not always of the same kind. A historian may surrender a pair of walking limbs after taking a rooted office, adding instead a crown of buried fibers through which archived memories can be heard. A caravan guide may carry six legs in the migration season and trade four of them for gliding membranes when the western winds become reliable. Those who spend long years connected to a district sometimes become difficult to separate from it. Their thoughts begin arriving through doors and signal roots. Their voices issue from neighboring walls. Eventually the original body may empty, remain standing for a season, and be used by bell-winged creatures as a nesting frame.

Identity resides in a braided structure deep in the torso, part crystal, part rhythm, part absence. It does not store memory as an archive stores records. Rather, it teaches incoming matter how to remember being the same person. If the braid is damaged, the citizen may remain articulate and skilled but become legally new.

For this reason, memory coherence is treated with both reverence and caution. The blue death-flowers close around most adults because their identities are tightly ordered. They remain open near infants, certain mystics, people recently returned from long-distance transit, and those who have deliberately loosened themselves to receive a civic memory.

Children are not born in private bodies.

They begin in sheltered acoustic clearings where several adults plant slivers of their own memory braids into a nursery root. The contributions are not blended evenly. One may offer spatial instinct, another a set of ancestral cautions, a third the cadence by which the child will recognize affection. The district supplies a temporary body, usually low and many-legged, with broad contact plates pressed to the living ground.

For the first few years, children migrate between attached and mobile existence. They uproot themselves for a few hours, wander, tire, and rejoin the nursery. Their first lasting achievement is not walking or speech but maintaining a thought without the substrate completing it for them. When a child can carry a secret from one side of the clearing to the other, it is considered independently alive.

They are curious in ways adults find physically expensive. They insert their sensory threads into unopened buds, climb resonant towers during warning chords, and exchange limbs without recording which one belongs to whom. They gather inert skeleton lace from ancestral drifts and construct small figures that the wind promptly disassembles. Many children pass through a phase of imitating shardbacks, adding needless legs and clipping civic moss from walls.

They learn speech twice.

The first language is conducted through contact with the ground: pulses, pauses, pressure harmonics, and deliberate interruptions in the flow of ionic sap. It is intimate and difficult to lie in, because the pavement hears the speaker’s bodily state alongside the intended message. Families use it. So do courts.

The second language is for the wind. It is made from whistles, rattles, sustained cavities, snapping vanes, and the tones of nearby structures. A skilled speaker does not merely produce a sentence but places it within the city’s ongoing chord. Meaning changes with weather and distance. The same phrase sent east through pipe-trees may be affectionate; sent west through seed rattles, it becomes formal warning.

Names are short melodies. They alter as bodies change. A person who acquires a deeper chest chamber must either transpose their name or be known thereafter as someone who has survived transformation.

Silence leaves them nearly expressionless. During the Listening they lock their mobile plates, close their throat instruments, and press some portion of the body to the ground. Even skeptics do this. The deep knock is not only heard but enters their lattices, briefly aligning defects throughout the body. Elder citizens sometimes claim that each knock removes one imperceptible thought and replaces it with another.

No one agrees what is being changed.

Their society has the patience of organisms that build by growth, but it is not peaceful in any simple sense. Disputes can last generations because neither party expects time to resolve them. Rival districts retune the wind to drown one another’s announcements. Roads are persuaded to bend away from unpopular households. A hostile civic gardener may plant delays in a transit bud, causing a merchant’s goods to arrive after the market has ended or before they were sent.

Violence is usually architectural. To injure a body is temporary; to alter the pattern by which it repairs itself is grave. The most feared weapons are songs that persuade tissue to misremember its shape. Their use is forbidden near the Hollow Meridian, though old trunks in the northern districts still bear the asymmetries of forgotten wars.

There are specialists who tend such injuries. They are called straighteners, although little about them is straight. They travel with choirs of bell-winged creatures housed along their backs. By releasing particular combinations of the animals onto a damaged patient, a straightener can change the local resonance until bones, fibers, and memories settle into their proper relations. The treatment may take years. Patient and healer often remain rooted together throughout, sharing charge and dreams.

Dreaming occurs during periods of reduced internal circulation. The body stiffens, its blue activity dims, and consciousness passes partly into the nearest vascular layers. Dreams are therefore not wholly private. A sleeper may encounter impressions left by former buildings, extinct grazers, buried neighborhoods, or people whose remains became pavement long ago. Most such impressions are formless. Occasionally one speaks clearly.

The Crown’s dead are not buried. Burial would imply a separation the inhabitants do not recognize.

At death, reusable organs are removed before their organizing charge disperses. Memory braids are carried to a civic spire and sounded once through its highest chambers. What can be translated into rhythm enters the public archive. What cannot be translated remains in the body and draws the blue flowers.

Relatives sit nearby while the flowers feed. They tell stories incoherently on purpose, breaking the deceased’s pattern into fragments too small to be mistaken for continued personhood. Afterward, scavenger lichens take the remaining charge. The mineral lace may be placed against a household wall, fitted into an instrument, or released to the wind.

Some dead return through transit.

They emerge from sealed buds without warning, usually lacking the final years of their lives. A few are duplicates of citizens still living. Others bear civic memories belonging to no recorded person. Law treats them as arrivals rather than resurrections. This distinction comforts no one, but it prevents inheritance disputes from becoming permanent.

The non-speaking animals share in the city more fully than outsiders first assume. Shardbacks know the pulse-calendar and arrive at grafting stations without herding. Sailers memorize district songs and carry corrected versions of them to mistuned outskirts. Bell-wings function as both wildlife and public signal equipment, nesting wherever the city’s chord offers a useful unresolved interval.

Even predators may hold civic roles. The rooted hunters that wait over ribbon-beast passages keep the lower vascular seams from being overgrazed. District stewards mark each with violet bands and record the years of its vigil. Very old hunters become landmarks. Messages are left in their branches. Lovers meet beneath them, trusting that anything capable of waiting motionless for eighty years will not interrupt.

Beyond all these lives is the Crown itself.

Sometimes it seems merely vegetal: responsive, ancient, without a central mind. Sometimes it behaves with unmistakable intention. It grows a chamber before anyone requests it. It closes a route against a traveler who later proves dangerous. It opens market buds containing objects no one remembers sending. During festivals it may introduce a subterranean rhythm too complex for the public conductors, and the entire city struggles to follow.

The inhabitants do not worship it. Worship would place it at a distance.

They prune it, argue with it, live inside it, repair portions of it, and are repaired by it in turn. They inherit its old reflexes as instincts. They carry pieces of its metabolism in their chests. Their children think through it before they can think alone. Their dead become its walls.

At night, when the Meridian turns and the other plateaus appear, the citizens gather among the smooth trunks. They arrive in many shapes: walkers webbed against the wind, rooted historians speaking through portable branches, children clattering on borrowed limbs, caravan people with salt crusted along their flanks. Shardbacks stop in the distant grooves. Sailers circle overhead. Even the predatory roots quiet.

Then the city across the impossible gap begins to sing.

The Dry Crown answers—not as one voice, and not as many. Towers, citizens, animals, pavement, buried forests, transit fibers, and the pale drifts of the dead all enter at different intervals. No inhabitant possesses the whole song. Each holds a tension, a beat, a sustained note, an engineered silence.

For those few cold hours, what lives there becomes audible: not a population upon a plateau, but a plateau practicing, across ages, how to become a person.
