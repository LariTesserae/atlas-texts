At first it seems that nothing lives there.

This is partly because the city has preserved the gestures of occupation after abandoning the forms by which occupation is usually recognized. Lamps burn, but no one walks beneath them. Cargo moves, but no one receives it. Bridges connect buildings whose rooms have no furniture and whose corridors narrow into conduits. If there were once inhabitants who required beds, kitchens, voices, and doors at the level of the hand, the city has worn away their evidence.

What remains alive is distributed.

The maintenance carriages are the most obvious example. No two are quite alike. Some are low and broad, with six mineral-caked wheels; others hang beneath rails like black seedpods; a few move by pressing blunt ceramic feet against the pavement. They carry pipe sections, valve housings, sealed canisters, or nothing visible at all. Their routes appear purposeless when watched for an hour. Over months, a pattern emerges. They are following changes too gradual for an observer to notice: a fraction of pressure lost from a western main, a degree of subsidence beneath a reservoir tower, an altered conductivity in rainwater falling on the eastern wards.

They do not repair things in the old sense. They encourage the city to heal in the materials available to it.

A carriage may stop beside a crack and inject a clear suspension into it. Hot seepage draws minerals from the suspension; over several seasons, a white rib grows across the damaged stone. Another carriage leaves copper mesh under a leaking joint, where green-black salts accumulate until the leak is narrowed to the required flow. Failed parts are seldom removed. They are entombed, bypassed, made structural. Beneath the newer streets lie entire generations of carriages caught in their own repairs, their chassis now serving as reinforcement in walls and pressure chambers.

Whether the moving machines know the buried ones are there is impossible to determine. They pause near them. Sometimes they exchange faint electrical pulses through the wet pavement. The pulses may be diagnostic signals. They may be recognition.

Deeper in the system are things less easily separated from their surroundings.

The translucent membranes beneath the grates are called animals only because they move against the current. Each is built around a dark grain—iron sulfide, volcanic glass, occasionally a fragment of manufactured alloy. The membrane grows by depositing successive films around this kernel. It has no mouth. Instead, one surface binds dissolved metals while the other releases them in altered form. Given enough time, a colony changes the composition of the water passing over it and therefore changes which minerals precipitate farther downstream.

They gather especially thickly near valves that have begun to fail.

There, hundreds overlap in pale layers, opening and closing so slowly that their motion resembles breathing seen through ice. When pressure rises, they flatten. When the water grows acidic, they loosen themselves and climb upstream. Two meeting on the underside of a pipe may exchange kernels, or fragments of kernels, after which each departs with its chemistry altered. This may be reproduction. It may be maintenance performed by organic means. The city does not distinguish between the two.

The porcelain creatures of the towers are more individual.

They have a central body no larger than a knuckle and anywhere from nine to several dozen legs, depending on age and damage. New legs bud beneath overlapping shell plates. Old legs become brittle, snap off, and are replaced. Each ends in a brush of filaments fine enough to separate droplets by salinity. As they cross a wet surface, they taste the city one bead at a time.

They favor condensation from copper, nickel, and the pale alloys used around energized cables. They consume almost nothing recognizable as food. A tower scavenger may spend an entire night collecting a single green droplet, folding its limbs around it, and holding it against a seam in its underside until only a chalky shell remains. Their internal tissues are clear. Through them can be seen a succession of colored chambers: turquoise, rust-red, sulfur yellow, each clouding and clearing as the creature sorts what it has gathered.

Their discarded shells are not always dead.

Rain entering an empty shell can awaken residual contractions along the leg sockets. The shell crawls a short distance, usually downhill, before lodging in a drain. In sheltered towers, heaps of such fragments slowly rearrange themselves over years. They form porous dams where particular salts collect. Living scavengers visit these dams and add fresh shell pieces with apparent care.

A colony, viewed over decades, resembles an animal larger than the tower.

Higher still, within the warm mist around the intake vanes, there are filamentary forms that can be seen only during electrical events. They span open spaces as threads of blue light. Under ordinary illumination they are invisible; even when collected, they leave no residue except a faint branching stain on ceramic.

They grow along voltage gradients. A filament begins where a charged droplet hangs from metal and extends itself into the air by linking to the next droplet, then the next. Thousands together make loose trembling nets. The nets redirect mist toward the intake towers and collapse when the turbines slow. Detached portions drift across plazas, maintaining their shape for several seconds. If one touches a railing, the railing rings softly.

They may be colonies. They may be electrical discharges stabilized by impurities in the rain. Yet they avoid certain districts, return after storms to the same pylons, and sometimes gather around a stalled carriage until its motors start again.

Most of the city’s inhabitants are of this uncertain kind: persistent arrangements rather than bodies.

In the oldest chambers, polished by centuries of hot runoff, pressure changes travel through narrow channels as pulses. Valves answer from adjoining districts. Reservoir levels rise and fall in coordinated sequences. No single component contains a plan of the whole system. Nevertheless, the system anticipates weather. Hours before denser cloud reaches the western ridge, intake shutters open. Before an underground tremor, the eastern mains empty themselves. When mineral buildup alters a pipe’s resonance, distant pumps change cadence until the obstruction fractures.

The city senses through water pressure, temperature, conductivity, and vibration. It remembers in deposits.

Each layer of scale records a previous state: droughts as thin dark bands, years of sulfurous rain as yellow strata, electrical failures as dendritic copper stains. Sensors read these layers without removing them. Decisions made long ago remain physically present in the walls, influencing the flow of present water. Thus memory thickens every surface until passages close and new ones must be opened.

What appears to be decay is also accumulation of experience.

There may once have been overseers. If so, their role survives in the exchange stations, where bundles of cable enter high, windowless rooms. Inside are ranks of ceramic columns wrapped in conductive film. Condensation runs down them in branching paths, and each path changes the signals passing through the column. The rooms are hot, dark, and full of small clicks.

Occasionally a shape can be seen there.

It stands between the columns, taller than a person and much narrower. It has no continuous surface. It is assembled from hanging strips, articulated tools, loops of cable, and streams of falling water, all temporarily coordinated around an empty center. When it moves, it does so by releasing part of itself and acquiring another part farther along the room. A handlike cluster forms only when a hand is needed. An eye is a lens carried to an inspection port, used, and returned to a rack.

These assemblies never leave the stations whole. Outside, they disperse into carriages, cranes, shutters, sensors, and conductive rain. It may be misleading to say that there are many of them. It may be equally misleading to say there is only one.

The enormous presence below the plateau belongs to a different order.

The city reacts to it as a tide pool reacts to a passing shadow. Pressure falls. Droplets lift. Membrane-creatures tighten around their kernels, and tower scavengers seal themselves into beads. The cables lose their hum before the darkness arrives.

No part of the creature has been observed long enough to be named. Now and then something brushes the underside of an escarpment: not flesh exactly, but a broad surface patterned with dim cavities, each cavity drawing vapor into itself. Instruments lowered into the cloud return dry and magnetized. Some return coated in a warm transparent material containing structures like cells. Others do not return at all.

It may swim in the weather. It may be the weather gathering briefly into anatomy. The city’s intake cycle changes after each passage, as though accounting for a loss or answering a signal.

And then there is the cube in the Dry Basin.

Nothing visibly inhabits it. No cavity has been detected within it, no mechanism, no stored charge sufficient to explain its cooling. Yet the rest of the city behaves toward it with a precision shown nowhere else. Every long pumping cycle narrows toward that central act: cessation, condensation, ascent.

The water climbing its faces contains traces from every district. Copper from the western conduits. Carbonate from the old chambers. Sulfur from the deep faults. Microscopic fragments of membrane-creatures. Porcelain dust. Lubricants from the carriages. Metal from dissolved rails. In minute proportion, the lifted volume contains the entire city.

For half a minute it gathers there, dark and coherent.

If the city possesses an inhabitant in the singular, this may be the only moment when it has a body.

The body has no fixed outline beyond the shape imposed by surface tension. It has no organs except all the pumps, mains, towers, electrical fields, animals, and mineral beds that prepared it. It does not speak. It rises. It becomes cloud and is distributed over the plateau, entering gutters, machines, membranes, porous stone, and the unseen atmosphere below.

Then the city is plural again.

Its citizens crawl along parapets, glide under grates, rotate on crane bearings, precipitate in pipes, tremble between pylons, and pass as pressure through buried chambers. Some are machines becoming geology. Some are animals becoming components. Some are weather held briefly in a shape.

None of them owns the city. None is entirely outside it.

They live there in the way water lives in a circulation: by leaving every place they occupy, by returning altered, and by wearing their memory into whatever contains them.
