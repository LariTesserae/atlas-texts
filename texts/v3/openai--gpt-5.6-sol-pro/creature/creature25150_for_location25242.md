At first, what lives there seems to be only the green.

It lies in separate, close-kept countries: a cushion folded over a wet crack, a shield of moss beneath a leaning slab, a few handspans of sedge rooted where meltwater briefly disappears. Each patch is dense enough to conceal its own weather. Frost shines on the upper leaves while, a finger’s depth below, black soil remains soft and water moves among pale roots. The plants do not grow upward so much as inward and across. They hoard warmth, moisture, shed hairs, insect husks, and crumbs of stone. A whole thicket may be lower than a boot sole.

The oldest inhabitants are therefore easy to mistake for surfaces. Tiny willows run through the cushions like buried cord, their exposed twigs twisted by decades of wind. Mosses drink the morning’s frozen condensation without ever seeming wet. Bellflowers open only when sunlight reaches them, each pale bloom held close to the ground as if listening. Red-stemmed sedges occupy the margins, and the noon fern waits tightly furled among warm stones until the proper few minutes arrive. Then it opens, briefly and completely, before closing against the returning cold.

Within these low gardens live multitudes whose lives may never extend beyond a single damp seam. Copper-colored beetles push through flower throats and vanish under petals. Fur-heavy bees climb rather than fly, hauling themselves from bloom to bloom with deliberate legs. Midges dance in columns no taller than a hand, tethered to the moisture that bred them. Pale moths spread themselves flat against basalt, turning their wings into instruments for gathering heat. When a cloud crosses the sun, the entire small population slows. When brightness returns, movement resumes almost at once.

The basin’s larger grazers are gray, quiet creatures built for uncertain ground. At a distance they resemble stones slipping uphill. In motion they can seem harelike, but their faces are blunt, their ears abbreviated, and their feet divide into broad little hooves that find purchase on fresh scree. Their fur is blue-gray in shadow and silver along the back in full sun, so their bodies alter with every step between light and darkness.

They feed apart from one another. A dozen may occupy the same sedge island without forming anything that looks like a herd. Each lowers its head and cuts rapidly at the low growth, pausing now and then with jaws still moving. Their silence is not emptiness but attention. A pebble displaced far across the basin can lift every head at once.

Their paths reveal more than they do. The tracks do not run straight. They pass from cushion to seep, from seep to shaded wall, avoiding wide expanses of dry gravel even when those offer easier footing. The animals know where water remains beneath apparently dry stone. They paw frost from moss, nibble the lacquered willow leaves, and rest with their flanks against basalt that has stored an hour of sun. At night they gather near the walls, though not for comfort in one another. Each chooses its own piece of mountain and presses close to that.

Their shed fur persists in cracks and shelters, snagged on sedge stems or worked by insects into nests. Long ago—or perhaps only last season—human hands gathered the same fur and laid it over stone floors.

Beneath the warmest slabs live the six-toed lizards. They are white enough to disappear against old snow, except for a moss-green line along the spine. Their bodies are long and low, made for entering fissures that retain the mountain’s deeper, steadier temperature. At dawn they wait under thin flakes of translucent rock, dim shapes beneath mineral windows. Sunlight passes through and warms them before the air itself has softened.

Once active, they follow brightness with extraordinary precision. They emerge along the moving edge of sun, hunt among flowers for beetles and bees, then relocate as shadows change. Their six splayed toes grip both glassy stone and moss. If startled, they do not always flee at once. They flatten, whitening into the glare, until the green spinal stripe resembles a narrow thread of vegetation. But they never remain after the western wall’s shadow reaches them. Before then, each has vanished into a crack whose entrance may be no wider than a knife cut.

Above all these lives the patient danger of the narrow-winged birds.

They belong less to the basin than to the rising air beyond its lip. The precipice lifts them. They circle for hours with wings held rigid, dark marks against the saturated sky, and seem almost stationary until their shadows race over the ground. They make little sound. A cry would arrive as warning, and they have no need to call to what they hunt.

They watch the boundary between brilliance and shade. In full glare the grazers and lizards are sharply outlined; from the ground, the sky behind the birds is difficult to look into. When one drops, it becomes speed without spectacle—a folding of wings, a brief rush close to stone, gravel scattering from a desperate turn. Sometimes the bird rises carrying something. Sometimes it lands out of sight among the green islands. Afterward, the surviving grazers remain frozen until the basin’s smaller sounds begin again.

No creature here lives everywhere. Each inhabits a chain of tolerable places.

The bees possess warm hollows. The midges possess wet seams. The lizards possess the traveling sunlight and the deep cracks. The grazers possess routes between green patches. The hunting birds possess the updraft and the glare. Even the plants occupy only the hidden cups where water pauses. Between these territories lie stretches of stone that may be crossed but cannot be lived in.

The people who use the basin understand the same arrangement.

They are not permanent inhabitants, or not anymore. Their presence survives in three shelters crouched beside the eastern wall. The shelters imitate the living things around them: low, close, sunk into hollows, exposing almost nothing to the air. Their makers chose no commanding view. They built below the level at which the wind becomes dangerous, fitting slabs together without mortar and roofing them with poles darkened by hides and smoke.

Whoever comes there knows stone, weather, and the movements of the gray grazers. They know which moss can be cut without stripping the whole cushion, which seep continues longest after sleet, and at what hour the central stone bowl will fill. Their tools are bone, sinew, wood, hide, and volcanic glass. A green glass knife, chipped keenly enough to part hair or root fiber, remains in one wall. Bone pins wait beside it. The wooden ladle at the water bowl has split and been bound with sinew by someone unwilling to discard a useful thing merely because it was damaged.

Their hearths are small. They burn dung and braided roots, fuel gathered a little at a time. Smoke has blackened the undersides of the roofs, but there is no deep midden, no thick accumulation of ash, no sign of generations remaining through winter. The visitors sleep on sedge and grazer fur. They hang hides from stone hooks. Perhaps they wait for animals crossing the ridge. Perhaps they collect plants available only during the basin’s brief flowering. Perhaps the shelters are a refuge on a route between valleys now hidden beneath cloud.

They leave little behind because everything carried so high has cost effort, and everything found there has a use.

Their cairns are scarcely taller than the surrounding rubble. Two balanced stones may be enough to say: step here, not there; the slab holds; the next patch hides water; the white edge beyond is not snow but open air. The markers do not impose a road. They record a negotiation.

When people occupy the shelters, they too must follow the sun. A hand emerges from the entrance to test the air. Frost is shaken from a hide. Someone carries the repaired ladle to the stone bowl during the short hour of melt. Someone watches the flying hunters and the feeding grazers, reading their positions as signs. At dusk, everyone lowers their voice, banks the mean little fire, and withdraws beneath fur and stone. The mountain does not permit wakeful grandeur after dark.

Yet the basin’s most persistent residents are those almost impossible to see.

They are roots moving water through darkness. Larvae enclosed in damp moss. Spores waiting inside cracks. Mites among willow scales. Seeds that may remain inert through many seasons before one warm interval allows them to open. Fungi thread through the soil beneath each green island, joining what appears separate above. In the compact shelter floors live remnants of other seasons: hairs, pollen, dried cells, the dormant eggs of insects brought in on bedding or hide.

Life there survives by withholding itself.

It stays beneath, between, behind, or close against. It does not waste height. It does not trust open water. It does not assume yesterday’s wet seam will be wet tomorrow. It wakes in portions as sunlight travels across the floor, so that the basin does not possess one morning but many: one for the east-wall moss, another for the beetles, another for the lizards beneath their stone windows, another hours later for the fern.

By late afternoon, almost everything living has shifted westward or withdrawn. Flowers close. The bees crawl into cover. The grazers leave the shining patches and feed along the cool boundary. Lizards disappear before shadow can trap them. The hunting birds rise away on the last usable air.

Then the visible inhabitants are gone, and the place seems vacant again.

But beneath a moss cushion, water is still being passed from cell to cell. Under a slab, a white lizard’s heart continues at a winter-slow rhythm. Gray bodies breathe against the wall. Insect eggs endure inside frozen stems. Roots hold themselves in the young mountain’s fractures. Frost grows inside the abandoned shelters, tracing the fur and sedge left by human sleepers.

Nothing there is securely established. A falling slab may erase a garden centuries old. A new crack may create another. Meltwater may abandon one colony and awaken seeds in bare gravel twenty paces away. The inhabitants live not in a settled landscape but in the mountain’s ongoing rearrangement.

They are creatures of the interval: between cloud and sky, thaw and freeze, sun and shadow, fracture and soil. They live there by becoming small enough, low enough, quiet enough, and patient enough to occupy the brief kindnesses of stone.
