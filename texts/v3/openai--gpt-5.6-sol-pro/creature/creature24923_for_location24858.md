Nothing in the hollow is wholly itself.

The creatures there are not malformed, exactly, nor young in the ordinary sense. They are complete enough to hunger, flee, breed, and die. Yet each carries some feature intended for a world that has not arrived: fins for deeper water, lungs for colder air, claws curved to grip bark where no forest stands. They live as rehearsals live—urgently, without knowing they may be revised.

The glass-backed skimmers are born in the wandering pool by the hundreds. Their eggs resemble clear grit and are often mistaken for the beginning of sand. They hatch whenever the pool’s floor changes composition. The young emerge already swimming, six fins spread around them like petals around a transparent seed.

Inside each skimmer’s glassy carapace, a thin black organ turns toward the nearest arch. It does not matter which arch is closest, nor what that arch shows. The organ follows it as a flower follows light. Skimmers that survive long enough develop faint images beneath their shells: coastlines, constellations, branching rivers, the outlines of aquatic beasts far too large for the pool. No two bear the same image. When they leap together, the air above the water flashes with a hundred possible worlds.

They feed on whatever the pool is about to become.

The cup-crabs are more deliberate. Their bodies are chalk-pale and soft beneath shells shaped like overturned bells. Each spends the first part of its life searching for a particular droplet—not merely water, but water of the correct weight, temperature, and memory. Having found it, the crab turns its shell upward, settles the droplet into the bowl, and carries it for the rest of its days.

They guard these droplets fiercely. They polish them with their mouthparts. During dry intervals they cluster shell to shell, making a trembling mosaic of separate waters. If frightened, they tip their bowls over and flee, but afterward they will search the stone for hours, tapping every bead of moisture as though calling a lost name.

A crab whose droplet is taken through an arch changes direction at once. It walks toward the cliff and does not stop. No one knows whether it falls. The vapor hides the final step.

Higher in the air live the needle-wings. Individually they are almost invisible: a jointed body, a dark pin of a head, two wings fine as scratches in glass. Their true body is the chord produced by the swarm. Every insect contributes one narrow vibration, and together they maintain a note that braces the warm air against the hollow’s uncertainties.

The pitch alters for folds, for storms, for the opening of the central petals. It also changes in response to events that have not yet occurred. Sometimes the swarm screams an alarm into an empty hollow. Hours later a ridge cracks, or a grazer falls, or an unfamiliar shadow passes beneath the cloud. At other times the chord becomes soft and intricate, almost tender. On those days one of the white trees is preparing to cast off a membrane.

The trees themselves possess no wood. Their porous trunks are inhabited from top to bottom by colonies of minute, heat-loving things that build chambers of white mineral around themselves. What looks like a single tree is therefore a crowded tower: millions of soft bodies, each blind, each scarcely larger than mist, sharing warmth and chemical rumor.

The black crown-membranes are communal organs. They drink cloud, taste changes in pressure, and gather traces of what passes through the arches. When a membrane detaches, it carries part of the colony with it. The falling piece does not seek fertile ground. It seeks a place where the hollow is incomplete.

The oldest tree’s bronze tags were not placed there by vanished workers. They grew there.

Deep inside that colony are tiny organisms that secrete metal when exposed to certain silences. Each time the central construction opens and sound is abolished, another tag begins beneath the trunk’s surface. Over years it works outward and hangs free. The tags are smooth because they are not labels. They are hardened intervals—records of every occasion on which the hollow ceased to hear itself.

There are seven trees because seven is the number the weather recognizes. If one dies, rain thickens until a crown-membrane takes hold somewhere new. If an eighth appears by duplication, the two nearest trees slowly lean together and fuse, mingling their inhabitants. For several days afterward, drops of bronze run down the joined trunk.

The long-legged grazers come and go, though “come” and “go” have uncertain meanings here. They descend from ridges too small to conceal them and vanish into cloud before reaching the cliff. Their split hooves test each step for solidity. Their combed mouths peel fresh mineral foam from the stone, exposing darker matter beneath.

Amber light moves in their veins according to no rhythm of the body. It brightens when an arch shows daylight and dims when the black sea is visible. Very old grazers have maps of faint light beneath their skin. The maps depict paths across expanded versions of the hollow, including slopes that exist only during the ninth cloud cycle.

They are gentle with most smaller things. Cup-crabs shelter between their hooves. Needle-wings settle along their backs without being disturbed. A grazer will even pause beside a stranded skimmer and lower one luminous leg, allowing the creature to slide down the wet hide toward water.

Their red mites are less gentle. The mites regard each grazer as both country and god. They cultivate the hide, clear its wounds, prune its parasites, and drink minute amounts of amber from its veins. When threatened, they rise into the air and form the image of an animal no grazer has ever seen.

The image is always the same: a massive, many-antlered thing with its head turned as if listening behind it.

Folds fear that shape.

A fold has no stable anatomy. At rest it is a length of damp darkness, faintly ribbed, lying beneath an arch where several histories overlap. When hungry, it enters one shadow and emerges from another. The crossing leaves it altered. A fold that enters beneath a tree may emerge with pores like the tree’s trunk. One crossing from a grazer’s shadow may give it hooves; another may fill it with harmless glass fins. It sheds these borrowed structures after the hunt, leaving them twitching on the stone.

Folds do not bite. They cover.

What happens beneath one is hidden even from the arches. When the fold withdraws, its prey may be dead, duplicated, reduced to an earlier stage of life, or absent except for a damp silhouette. The fold feeds on the difference between what was caught and what remains.

For all this, folds are not the hollow’s most dangerous inhabitants. That distinction belongs to the liverwort under the overhangs.

Each translucent patch is one organism interrupted into colonies. Its dark droplets are pieces of a distributed memory. Rain allows the colonies to think together. Water films connect them; signals pass from overhang to overhang; and for a few minutes the liverwort becomes aware of the entire hollow.

Then its blue spores rise.

The spores are not reproductive in the usual sense. They carry away whatever the liverwort has just understood. Needle-wings inhale them. Grazers swallow them with mineral foam. Skimmers absorb them through their bright gills. Thus the inhabitants sometimes know things they have never learned: where the pool stood yesterday, which arch has changed, how many unseen herds passed in the fog.

This knowledge fades within hours, leaving only behavior. A crab avoids a stone it has never visited. A grazer mourns beside an empty patch of ground. A fold waits beneath a shadow that will not exist until afternoon.

And beneath them all lives the slowest thing in the hollow.

It has no boundary separate from the dark stone. The central petals are lodged in it like old instruments left in a wound. The ridges are its unfinished folds; the pool is moisture gathering in a shallow organ; the arches are senses that look not outward but sideways, into adjacent conditions of itself.

It does not yet know whether it is a landscape, a machine, or an animal.

The concussions below are its attempts at a pulse. The opening cracks are mouths it forgets to keep. Every sealed fissure is a thought abandoned before rain can enter it. It experiments with hardness, drainage, height, and life because these are the means available to it for discovering its own shape.

The things in the hollow live upon this uncertainty. Some may be its parasites. Some may be organs. Some may be memories of the makers, translated badly into ecology. The seven trees could be gauges. The grazers might be maintenance. The folds may be errors taught to hunt. Even the clouds seem partly endogenous, exhaled from the cliff and drawn back across the stone like breath reconsidered.

Now there is the groove.

At first only the cup-crabs notice it. They gather along its edges, holding their droplets high. Skimmers crowd the point where the pool spills over. Needle-wings descend until their chord trembles against the running water.

The groove lengthens by grains. Water enters it and does not hesitate.

Something threadlike stirs in the flow—too pale for a root, too purposeful for foam. It has no eyes or limbs. It turns downhill because downhill has, at last, become a fact. As the first current carries it toward the cliff, the white trees open their black crowns. The grazers lift their heads. Even the folds draw close to the arches and become still.

The hollow has produced many creatures.

This is the first one for whom it has produced a direction.
