At first, the inhabitants of the Whiteworks are difficult to distinguish from the works themselves.

A figure standing beside a condenser may appear to be another inspection housing: pale, smooth-sided, beaded with water. Someone crouched at a canal lip resembles a folded piece of glazed equipment. In the glare, faces disappear behind reflective veils and limbs are broken into fragments by wet light. Only when they move does the city disclose them—slowly in the open, quickly through shadow, always with the practiced caution of creatures who know that heat, pressure, and water are never merely background conditions.

The people are narrow-bodied more often than broad, with long hands and strong, splayed feet. This is partly inheritance and partly the shaping of a life spent on slick inclines, ceramic steps, pipe galleries, and stone polished by centuries of runoff. Children learn to lower their weight before they learn to hurry. Adults walk with knees slightly flexed and toes testing every surface. A Whiteworks laborer can cross a flooded apron without splashing and descend a mineral-sheathed stair by feeling the vibration through the soles.

Their skin ranges through dark umbers, reds, violet-browns, and mineral pallors, but little of it is exposed at noon. Bare skin overheats in the scattered brilliance, and moisture sitting on it does not evaporate efficiently. Most people wear loose, double-layered coverings of translucent ceramic fiber. The outer layer is white, silvered, or faintly blue; the inner layer is dark and absorbent. Air moves between them as the wearer walks. Sleeves open along the underside of the arm, and collars stand away from the neck. Garments are cut to shed water into channels at the hips rather than letting it collect at elbows and knees.

The common sun hood has a broad, shallow crown and a veil composed of thousands of tiny reflective scales. From outside it looks opaque. From within, it breaks the basin into tolerable shades of gray. Workers who must read signal mirrors wear black-rimmed lenses with rotating polarizing plates. Their eyes are not necessarily more delicate than those of lowland people, but they are trained to notice different things: a blue lamp wavering in steam, a flash repeated from three districts away, the darkening of a wet wall before a fracture opens.

Clothing marks occupation less by color than by surface. Hydraulic keepers wear matte cloth so reflected light does not conceal leaks. Canal tenders wear glossy coats that animals cannot easily grip. Shadow inspectors carry panels of hard white armor over the shoulders and spine, protecting them when they open infested cavities. Breeders wear aprons filmed with living cultures whose smell calms the creatures under their care. Old workers accumulate repairs in differing ceramics, so that a person’s coat becomes a mosaic of districts, guilds, and former duties.

Almost everyone carries a water knife.

It is not primarily a weapon. It is a flat ceramic tool with a hooked tip, used to clear an overflow, split a swollen conduit skin, scrape scale from a gauge, sever the adhesive threads of a canal animal, or open clothing around a steam burn. The best are black and thin enough to ring when tapped. Families pass them down. A knife that has saved a cistern is marked with a blue notch; one that has opened a wall colony is drilled through the hilt, allowing light to pass.

Speech exists, but the city has made it modest. Voices are useful indoors, at close range, during the intervals when pumps rest. In open districts, people speak with their bodies.

Their public language combines hand signs, mirrored flashes, and impacts transmitted through built surfaces. A palm held low means that a walkway is vibrating. Fingers curled toward the wrist warn of rising pressure. Two flashes and a pause request passage across a freight line; three descending flashes announce contaminated runoff. Lovers converse from opposite balconies by angling small pieces of dark glass into the evening lamps. Children tap gossip into pipes until an irritated technician opens a purge valve and floods the whole conversation with static.

Names have several forms. There is the intimate spoken name, often soft and brief. There is a civic sign-name, designed to be seen at a distance. There is also a pressure mark: a personal rhythm tapped through floor, rail, or cistern wall. Old people may recognize friends by the feet before they see them.

The Whiteworks do not divide themselves into neighborhoods in the ordinary sense. People belong to watersheds.

A household is registered by the route its water takes: which roof gathered it, which filters received it, which canal accepted the overflow, and where it left the inhabited districts. Kinship follows these routes as much as blood. Those whose runoff joins before the lower settling terraces call one another basin-cousins. Marriages between watersheds are celebrated by temporarily crossing two household drains, though the connection must be severed before the water grows still. Adoption is marked by teaching a child the inspection path from roof to outfall.

Homes are spacious but not closed. A room must breathe, drain, and admit a blade of direct light. Walls stop short of ceilings. Cupboards have perforated backs. Beds are slung above shallow floor channels and are rolled up each morning so their undersides can be examined. Possessions are kept few, hard, and legible: glazed bowls, wire frames, fired tablets, woven mineral fiber, sealed packets of microbial food. Anything that swells invisibly or rots in darkness is regarded with suspicion.

Nevertheless, the people are not austere. They decorate by deposition.

A family may divert one thread of mineral-rich water over a relief for generations, allowing a portrait to emerge beneath translucent layers. Doorways are edged with bands of colored scale. Memorial walls grow outward year by year as relatives add solutions containing copper, sulfur, iron, or powdered glass. Some rooms are famous for their rain acoustics: awnings and bowls arranged so that each heavy drop produces a distinct note. Since uncontrolled sound is rare, deliberate sound carries great emotional force. A single struck vessel can quiet a crowded court.

Their food comes from vats, cisterns, pens, and canals. Dense microbial cakes are steamed in leaf-thin ceramic envelopes and served with sour mineral gels. Pale shell-grazers are raised for soft inner tissues and for the reflective plates they shed as they grow. Thread swimmers are eaten coiled and hot, their branching organs removed for medicines and dyes. Larger livestock are kept in humid galleries where they browse films cultivated on rotating stone drums.

Meals tend to be strongly flavored. Sulfurous broths, metallic salts, bitter ferments, and sharp acidic pastes cut through the oppressive warmth. Coolness is a luxury more prized than sweetness. Wealthy households serve chilled condensation gathered from high night surfaces. Guests drink it in tiny cups and hold each mouthful before swallowing.

No one wastes water, but neither does anyone hoard it. To possess water is to be responsible for its onward movement. A miser in the Whiteworks is not someone who refuses to share; it is someone who lets a private cistern linger too long.

The most respected citizens are often those who understand passage: hydraulic judges, gradient readers, fracture listeners, and keepers of old routes. A gradient reader can stand in a plaza that appears perfectly level, pour a spoonful of water, and tell which buried channel has shifted. Fracture listeners lie with one cheek against warm stone and interpret the tiny pulses of pressure below. They are summoned when a wall begins sweating in an unfamiliar pattern or when puddles tremble without any scheduled turbine movement.

Shadow inspectors occupy a more uneasy place. They enter spaces everyone else is required to expose but prefers not to imagine. Their work begins with light. Narrow mirrors are inserted through slots, then scent threads, then articulated probes. A neglected cavity may contain only clear condensation and a few harmless discs. It may instead be packed solid with pale jointed bodies, interlocked so densely that the colony has become a second architecture inside the first.

Such colonies are not always destroyed. If they are stable and the wall can bear them, inspectors may cut new vents, establish a controlled outflow, and register the mass as a resident system. Some of the oldest inhabited buildings contain generations of human households outside and generations of cavity creatures within. Their heat, salts, waste, and movements pass through one another. Whether the building belongs to the people, the colony, or the spring beneath both is considered a legal question with no permanent answer.

Children are fascinated by these interior lives. They keep harmless wall animals in perforated jars and race shell-grazers along wet tiles. They build miniature canal systems after rain, arguing over whose overflow has stalled. Their first serious civic lesson is to recognize dangerous stillness. An adult sets out several bowls: one flowing, one recently filled, one beginning to thicken. The child must identify them without touching. Later comes the lesson of opening a pressurized vessel—never facing the seam, never trusting silence, always providing the contents somewhere to go.

Illness in the Whiteworks often begins as an error of balance rather than an invasion. The body salts become wrong. The skin ceases shedding heat. Symbiotic microbes in the mouth or gut change under an unfamiliar water supply. Physicians therefore ask first where a patient has been drinking and which district’s rain touched them. Clinics contain flowing diagnostic tables where blood, saliva, and runoff react in branching ceramic grooves.

Burns are common. So are mineral lesions around the eyes and joints. Older workers may have fingers stiffened by years of dissolved salts, or pale branching scars where steam crossed the skin. Many replace damaged joints with ceramic interfaces and flexible black ligaments. Prostheses are not hidden. They are polished, etched with watershed marks, and fitted with vibration-sensitive pads. Some veteran canal tenders can detect a blocked outlet more readily through an artificial foot than through the living one.

Death does not exempt anyone from the law of flow.

Bodies are not buried; the ground is too hard, wet, and chemically active, and sealed tombs would become dangerous. Nor are they simply burned, since fuel is costly and smoke hangs unpredictably over the basin. Most are taken to dissolution courts near the outer channels. There, under awnings open to rain, the body is given to carefully managed colonies. Water passes continuously over the remains. Minerals are separated, useful compounds recovered, hard tissues fired into small glazed pieces.

These pieces are set into household drains, public steps, signal towers, or the walls of the deceased’s former workplace. The dead continue in the city not as preserved likenesses but as surfaces over which water travels. To say that someone “still receives the rain” is both consolation and literal fact.

Not all inhabitants are citizens in the human sense.

The city’s animals have their own forms of tenure. Shell-grazer colonies inherit favored shelves. Stilt-legged filter herds return to the same plazas in response to pressure tremors below the paving. Predators maintain routes along shaded undersides of bridges, and experienced pedestrians know where not to put a hand during rain. Canal swimmers are transferred between districts with ceremony because each population has adapted to a slightly different chemistry. An animal moved carelessly can die, clog a system, or breed so rapidly that a conduit becomes unusable.

Some creatures live in close partnership with individuals. Small scavenging discs cling beneath workers’ shoulder capes and remove microbial buildup from the fabric. Vibration-sensitive, many-legged animals are kept in engine houses; they flatten themselves before dangerous pressure changes, offering warning earlier than instruments. Wealthy couriers ride narrow six-limbed beasts bred from the plaza filterers, though the animals cannot bear heavy loads and refuse routes whose paving transmits unfamiliar signals.

There are also things below the city that no one calls livestock.

Pipe colonies occupy the warm mains in sheets many meters long. Deep-well organisms alter the chemistry of industrial water and sometimes respond to pumping cycles as if anticipating them. When a plant changes rhythm, the organisms may change with it. Engineers speak of training a well, angering a spring, or allowing a turbine line to sleep. Official manuals avoid such language, but veteran crews do not.

Before restarting an old work, they feed it.

A measured broth is introduced upstream. Valves are opened in a particular sequence. The crew waits for the expected color to appear in the inspection runnels. Only then are the turbines engaged. Plants awakened without this courtesy have been known to foam, overpressure, or send masses of transparent tissue into the municipal canals.

In the abandoned districts, stranger populations persist. Descendants of maintenance animals have grown too large for modern conduits. Pale predators pass through walls honeycombed by dissolved mortar. Roofless pressure halls shelter stilt herds whose flank flashes no longer match those of the domesticated plaza animals. Scavenging discs have fused into rippling communal sheets that move only at night. Salvagers enter these places in pairs, trailing overflow lines behind them so that they can always find their way back by following moving water.

The people who live nearest the ruins are considered peculiar even by other Whiteworks inhabitants. Their garments are patched with ancient glaze. Their sign dialects use obsolete machine warnings. They know channels omitted from civic maps and recognize rain by taste. Some claim that the oldest factories are not abandoned at all—that their buried cycles continue too slowly to register, one movement perhaps every season, one pressure exchange every generation.

At evening, the distinctions among all these inhabitants become less certain.

People leave the white shelter of their roofs. Canal animals rise toward the lamps. Shell-grazers loosen from the hot shelves and begin their patient circuits. Filter herds cross the open plazas, their flanks pulsing with messages that children imitate from the causeways. Predators unfold beneath bridges. Shadow inspectors mark tomorrow’s openings. Condenser keepers walk the rooflines, turning channels toward night collection.

From above, human movement is only one pattern among many: hooded figures pausing at signal lines, livestock threading between ponds, freight sleds gliding without drivers, colonies shifting along luminous water.

They do not dominate the plateau so much as participate in its metabolism. They keep the water moving because it keeps them alive, and because stillness belongs to something else. They open their buildings to light because every shadow invites another form of residence. They maintain the engines, cultivate the pipes, harvest the animals, feed the wells, and set their dead where the rain can reach them.

The Whiteworks inhabitants are caretakers, dependents, engineers, prey, breeders, and mineral deposits in progress.

Given enough time, each of them becomes part of the watershed.
