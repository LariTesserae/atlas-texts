At first it seems that the inhabitants are only the little ones.

They show themselves by subtraction: a clean circle in the bacterial sheen, a gap in a mineral fringe, a droplet that shivers though the air is still. They are nearly all the colors of their surroundings. Transparency is common, but never perfect. Their tissues hold traces of what they have eaten—iron-red digestive knots, smoke-dark nerves, beads of silver waste waiting to be shed. Against stone they resemble wetness. In open water they resemble flaws in the light.

The coin-grazers live pressed flat to metal. Their bodies are round only when well fed; hunger draws them into thin ovals that can pass beneath scale and rust. On the underside is a mouth made of several concentric rasps. These turn at different speeds, milling microbial film from iron without dislodging the grazer. A feeding colony leaves overlapping tracks as regular as machine tooling.

They do not have eyes. Along their rims are pits sensitive to voltage, pressure, and the dissolved signatures of other bodies. In the seconds before the central contraction, every grazer stops feeding. Their tooth-rings become still. Then they pivot, almost as one, so that their narrow ends point into the coming current. After the knock they resume, moving to fresh metal uncovered by the lowered water. Their eggs are laid in stamped-looking rows along the heads of submerged bolts. The resemblance to manufactured markings is so exact that old surveyors, if any came here, may have counted generations as serial numbers.

The thread-swimmers belong less to the pools than to the interval between water and air. Each is a filament surrounding a ladder of tiny fluid chambers. It climbs by fastening its forward end to condensation and allowing the droplet’s own movement to draw the rest of the body after it. A single thread may travel from ceiling to floor without swimming once.

When a droplet grows heavy, the animal ties itself around it. The knot is not a grip but a feeding organ: microscopic combs strain airborne bacteria, metal salts, and organic dust from the suspended water. Just before the drop falls, the thread loosens and lashes toward the next bead. Many fail. They descend with their droplets and survive in the pools until they can follow vapor back upward. Thus the basin’s faintest rain carries a vertical migration.

In breeding season—if a place without seasons can be said to have one—the threads gather on cables and join end to end. Hundreds may make a translucent net between two housings. Condensation pearls at every junction. The net becomes visible only when it is already heavy with young.

The crustaceans of the deeper pools are more various than they first appear. Those on the gravel have broad feet and high, clear shells that protect chambers of microbial culture carried on their backs. They sow these cultures by dragging fringed hind limbs through fresh silt. The inverted surface-walkers are lighter, with feet that dimple but do not break the water skin. They graze from below, their pale bodies reflected above them so precisely that each seems paired with a second animal walking belly to belly in another world.

The riders are smallest. They stand on the backs of the bottom-walkers and feed from the cultivated chambers, trimming one microbial strain and removing another. They are parasites when numerous and necessary when scarce. A host burdened with riders may starve; a host without any is eventually overgrown, its culture soured by mineral waste. At intervals the host turns beneath an overhang and scrapes away the excess. Dislodged riders sink slowly, limbs spread, waiting for another shell to pass beneath them.

Some find no host. These change.

Their bodies thicken, the rear limbs harden into hooks, and they become the clasping forms that live around valves. For years they may remain there, filtering vapor each time a wall exhales. They grow too large ever to ride again. When the valve finally fails or seals with deposits, they release their grip together and walk into the water, awkward as broken mechanisms. Most are eaten. A few reach another breathing aperture and establish a new congregation.

The washer-backed animals own the wet land between channels. They are not a single species so much as a layered society of related forms. The smallest live beneath the plates of the largest. Pale juveniles occupy the spaces around leg joints, eating rust flakes and dead skin. Narrow-bodied attendants clean the sensory grooves along adult shells. Egg-bearers remain deep in the crevices, where several adults lock their plates together around them to make a chamber no predator can enter.

Their shells are dark where newly formed, then whiten as silica enters the outer layers. Old individuals become ridged with mineral bands. Some are nearly immobile beneath the weight of their own armor and live wedged permanently in wall cracks, fed by younger animals. In return they serve as living gates. At the warning tremor before the machine’s contraction, they flex their bodies and open passages through the colony. Hundreds stream through in ordered files. When the last has crossed, the elders settle back into place.

The papery sound of their feet is not incidental. Each foot ends in a fan of dry, hard lamellae. As the animal walks, these scrape the ground in short coded bursts. A colony crossing open mud is continually announcing depth, charge, danger, and the time remaining before the water returns. The collective whisper is a map passed from foot to foot.

Predators hear it.

The cable-worms in the drain mouths are older than most of what they catch. A small one occupies a conduit no wider than a finger. The largest fill pipes that might once have carried a person’s arm. Their outer skin forms a braided sheath, black with incorporated iron. Two sensory filaments trail from the mouth of the pipe and sample every current in the basin. The body itself may extend far into the wall, folded through turns no rigid skeleton could navigate.

A cable-worm has no central jaw. When its seam opens, the apparent body separates into hinged feeding strips lined with inward-pointing glassy hooks. It closes these around several fleeing animals at once and draws them into a digestive sleeve. Shell, flesh, microbial culture, and mineral accretion are dissolved separately. The useful fractions travel down different internal canals. The rest is extruded deep in the conduit, where it hardens into a smooth lining. A very old cable-worm therefore inhabits a pipe partly rebuilt from the bodies of its prey.

They strike at the machine’s knock because fear makes every other creature predictable. Yet they, too, are hunted. Blind, many-legged things no thicker than wire enter through cracks in the conduits and eat the cable-worms from behind. An afflicted worm begins making false strikes, opening when no prey is present. Eventually it leaves its pipe. Out in the pools it has no resemblance to cable at all: it unfolds into a broad, segmented ribbon whose many feeding strips row helplessly against the water. The washer-backed colonies descend on it before it can find another refuge.

There are also inhabitants that scarcely qualify as animals.

The flexible silica fringes contain colonies of soft polyps smaller than grit. Each occupies a chamber at the tip of a branch and extends a ring of molecular cilia into the seepage. The colony does not design its stony support. Water does that. Yet over generations the animals alter the water’s acidity by minute degrees, encouraging deposition on one side and dissolution on the other. The resulting fans turn toward chemical gradients rather than light. What seems to be a fossilized leak is, in part, a city slowly steering the walls that contain it.

Other colonies live within rust. They form dark, moist sheets between layers of oxide, digesting the metal’s changing states. Their activity causes old plates to swell into hollow blisters. Inside each blister are chambers, canals, and thin pillars. Break one open and thousands of white-bodied organisms withdraw through pores too small to see. Left intact, such colonies can outlast the iron that houses them. When the last original metal is consumed, the blister remains as a load-bearing shell of waste, inhabited by descendants that continue to circulate charge across a structure no longer made of what they eat.

The amber indicators have their own attendants. These are tiny shell animals with a single anchoring foot and a crown of fine antennae. They cluster around the glass whether it glows or not, but depart when the hidden condition behind the glow is absent. Light is merely its visible companion. What attracts them is a weak, ordered oscillation passing through the wall.

They feed by aligning microscopic crystals inside their bodies with that oscillation. The crystals flex; surrounding tissues harvest the movement. An indicator colony can live for months without taking visible food. When the signal fails, the crystals fall out of alignment and begin to injure the soft tissue around them. The animals abandon the chamber before this happens. They travel in close groups, each individual orienting by the vibration of its neighbors, until another buried current is found.

The masks contain unusually dense colonies of them.

Within each eyeless facepiece, shell animals arrange themselves in whorls around the round mouth fitting. Their generations accrete inward. The oldest shells lie against the metal, the youngest on top, until the colony reproduces the missing face in reverse: brow ridges, flattened cheeks, a shallow bridge where a nose might have rested. No individual perceives this shape. It results from gradients of salt and old organic residue preserved in corrosion.

Yet the casts are not identical.

Some are narrow. Some have asymmetrical jaws. One carries the persistent hollow of a scar. The animals inherit no memory of the person who wore the mask, but they follow what remains of that person’s chemistry, tracing sweat, pressure, and breath long after flesh is gone. When disturbed, the whole colony contracts. For an instant the inward face seems to grimace.

Larger lives are harder to distinguish.

Something passes beneath the black gravel. It does not tunnel, and it is never seen in full. At times a patch of aggregate lifts by a few grains, advances, and settles. Springs cloud briefly in its wake. Coin-grazers abandon metal near its passage. Cable-worms pull in their sensory filaments. The washer-backed animals do not flee; they flatten themselves and wait.

Fragments found after such passages suggest a soft creature armored not by a shell but by attached debris: ceramic chips, iron nodules, rings of old gasket material, the emptied casings of smaller animals. These fragments may not belong to it. They may be what it harvests, or what it molts, or simply what the moving water carries.

The most plausible outline is broad and radial, with dozens of short limbs gripping separate stones. It may advance by shifting its weight through the gravel while remaining almost entirely buried. It may feed at the invisible springs, spreading membranes over the upwelling water. Some of the mineral chimneys could be its abandoned feeding structures. Some might still be attached.

No one could say whether there is one or many. Its movements occur at widely separated points, sometimes too soon after one another for a single body to account for them. But the gravel bed is connected beneath the foundations, and a creature adapted to live between stones need not preserve the compact shape expected of an animal. What appears in one pool may be only a hand, a mouth, or a temporary knot in a body distributed through the basin floor.

The central machine is inhabited as thoroughly as everything else.

There are grazers inside the insulation, thread-colonies in the condensation chambers, nests beneath the flywheels, and pale sacs rooted in seals where pressure leaks by imperceptible degrees. But these are tenants. Deeper in, beyond the crusted casing, another order of life has become entangled with the mechanism’s surviving functions.

Its cells are long conductive strands. They bridge cracked contacts, sleeve corroded wires, and grow through ceramic pores. Their metabolism depends on the changing charge produced during each contraction. In return, their bodies close circuits that would otherwise have failed. Where a relay has dissolved, a braided colony tightens and releases in response to voltage. Where lubricant is gone, microbial gels fill the bearing surfaces. Where a pressure diaphragm split, layers of living membrane have grown across the gap and pulse with it.

No single strand understands the cycle. No organ commands it. The contraction emerges from the timed responses of countless colonies, each feeding, sealing, recoiling, or discharging according to local conditions. The machine sustains the life inside it, and that life sustains the machine. Neither remains separately viable.

This composite being does not wake every eleven or twelve minutes. It is never asleep. The muted knock is closer to a heartbeat, though even that comparison is too simple. The lowering of water draws fresh chemicals through the bed. The returning flow distributes heat and charge. Vapor clears waste from the valves. The brief rain carries organisms and spores from one chamber to another. Every creature in the hollow has fitted some part of its life to this event.

Their anticipation is not prophetic. A pressure change moves through the rock before the contraction. A current passes along buried iron. Ceramic chambers sing below the threshold of easy hearing. Each species senses a different precursor. Together, their movements reveal the approach of the knock as muscles reveal the approach of a thought.

And beneath the grille, something receives the whole pattern.

When the harmonic tone begins, the basin’s inhabitants turn not toward the opening itself but toward the network below it: pipes, fractures, saturated gravel, conductive seams. Droplets remain suspended because the air pressure rises by an exact, slight amount. Ripples flatten because opposing vibrations cancel them. The indicators kindle as sequential currents pass through the walls.

During that minute, the many separate rhythms of the basin become phase-locked. Thread-swimmers tighten around droplets. Grazers halt their rings of teeth. Cable-worms open their seams without striking. The washer-backed animals lift their forelimbs and hold them clear of the ground. Even the buried thing ceases moving.

The basin is not silent then. It is listening with every body it contains.

Whether the source below is another machine, a larger colony, or the remote organ of something extending far beyond the hollow cannot be determined from above. The tone may be a diagnostic pulse transmitted through an ancient system. It may be a territorial call. It may be the means by which a distributed creature briefly gathers itself into coherence.

Whatever sends it does not live under the basin in the way a beast lives in a den. Its presence is in the regulated tide marks, the stable channels, the circuits completed by flesh, the migrations timed to pressure, the walls rebuilt by rust-eaters, the mineral branches guided through seepage. It lives as an agreement among processes.

When the tone ends, that agreement loosens. The lights fail one after another. Predators become predators again; grazers feed; colonies cross exposed mud; the buried body resumes its patient rearrangement of stones. The suspended drops fall in their thousands.

For a moment, every shell and filament receives the same rain.

Then the inhabitants disappear back into wet surfaces, and the place seems empty only because almost everything alive there has learned to resemble what keeps it alive.
