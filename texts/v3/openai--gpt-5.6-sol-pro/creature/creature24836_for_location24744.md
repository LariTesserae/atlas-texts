The first inhabitants are almost too small to count as creatures. They live in the polished stone itself: dark motes arranged along crystal boundaries, feeding on pressure, iron, and the weak currents that pass between unlike minerals. They have no fixed bodies. A colony may spend a century as a stain beneath a translucent plate, then loosen during a flood and travel as a metallic taste in the water. Wherever they feed, the rock clouds and softens. Wherever they die, it grows faintly conductive.

The coin-grazers follow these living films.

Each is a low, round animal with a shell of overlapping mineral scales and a mouth covering nearly its entire underside. They move by rippling thousands of transparent cilia against the ground. Individually they are slow, but their sheets advance with startling purpose. Millions turn at once when a richer seam is found, making whole tracts of the plain appear to rotate. They scrape the microbial films from stone, leaving pale circles behind them. Older grazers carry maps of vanished drainage systems etched into their shells, inherited from water swallowed before the present channels existed.

They are rarely alone. Thread-hunters travel through the grazing sheets: black, tendon-thin animals that look like discarded cable until they tighten. A thread-hunter has no jaws. It loops itself around several grazers, ties its body into a complex knot, and slowly contracts. The shells inside the knot crack without sound. After feeding, the hunter lies straight and rigid for days while rows of borrowed mineral plates form beneath its skin. With age it becomes less flexible and more ambitious, until it tries to knot prey too large for it and remains wrapped around the victim as a permanent ring of living armor.

The flooded channels belong to the bells.

They are small enough to hold in one hand, though nothing here has hands suited to holding them. Each carries a translucent, porcelain-thin dome filled with black brine. Beneath it hangs a fringe of paddles and sensory filaments. They swim by striking their shells against one another, using the recoil to move. A lone bell is nearly helpless. In a crowd, their collisions become locomotion: the entire shoal rattles forward like gravel poured through a pipe.

Their clatter serves other purposes. Changes in rhythm announce pressure shifts, approaching walkers, and the onset of Compression. When threatened, a shoal can synchronize into one immense blow. Thousands of shells strike at once, and the resulting crack travels through water, stone, and flesh. Small predators split open. Larger ones are merely informed that food is present.

Over all of these rise the tower colonies.

No single organism occupies a tower. Each is a congress of valves, muscular chambers, calcified siphons, electrical knots, and blind mouths, all descended from animals that once lived separately. Near the base, broad intake mantles adhere to the floor and drink through the rock. Higher up, cylindrical filter bodies sort the flood into salt, metal, spores, eggs, memories, and heat. At the summit, vent animals open their spiral throats and discharge what the colony cannot use.

A young tower begins as a trembling ring around a warmless spark. Grazers gather there because the current encourages microbial growth. Predators follow the grazers. Their waste mineralizes. Filter polyps settle on the mineral. Gradually the ring grows walls, ducts, and an interior pressure system. After several thousand years it begins to breathe as one body.

Old towers become social. They signal across basins through subterranean pulses, delaying or advancing their contractions until entire districts inhale together. They exchange not only water but behavior. The electrical remnants of dead animals enter through their roots and are distributed among the chambers. Thus one tower may flinch when a thread-hunter passes, though it has never been prey; another may attempt to migrate during Compression, lifting half its foundation before settling back into the mud.

Some towers inherit stranger instincts. There are colonies that periodically fold their vents like wings. Others fill internal brood chambers with smooth stones and guard them as eggs. One immense siphon at the edge of the broad basin produces, every few decades, the courtship call of a species extinct before the surrounding mineral bed was laid down. Nothing answers, but nearby towers alter their pumping rhythm as if listening.

Between the colonies walk the many-angled herds.

No observer can determine the complete form of a walker. From one direction it appears to stand on six ivory limbs, each divided into too many joints. A bladder of luminous brine hangs within the frame of the legs, sloshing in opposition to gravity. From another direction the bladder vanishes and the limbs become a trail of fresh impressions. In water, the animal’s reflection shows a much larger creature descending beneath the plain, upside down, with its feet attached to those of the visible body.

Perhaps the walker is only the intersection of several animals occupying adjacent versions of the same ground. The herd behaves as if this is ordinary. Individuals graze by lowering their absent sides into mineral beds. They breed by converging at shallow pools and exchanging reflections. Calves first appear underwater, immense and inverted; over many seasons they rise toward the surface, becoming smaller and less complete until their feet emerge into air.

Their calls precede them. A low horn note may sound over the flats hours before the herd arrives, rolling loose shells and closing every grazer along its path. During migration, the walkers follow their own calls, as though summoned by what they are about to say. If a lead animal is killed before calling, the herd may continue toward the place where the missing sound should have been and wait there for years.

The pale radial hunters wait beneath thin skins of mineral.

They are all mouth and direction. Eight or twelve chalk-white lobes radiate from a central opening, each lobe containing an organ that senses which way nearby things expect to fall. When prey crosses overhead, the hunter does not move. It changes the answer.

For a moment, downward points toward its center. Water climbs slopes. Mud peels sideways from the ground. Bells, grazers, loose pebbles, and sometimes a walker’s leg plunge into the waiting aperture. Then the ordinary pull of the plain reasserts itself. The pool collapses back, leaving a crescent-shaped scour and the hunter swollen under the stone.

Juveniles can bend only droplets. They hide beside condensation beads and make tiny animals fall around the curved surface forever. Adults have been known to tilt entire channels. The oldest do not feed often. Their buried lobes spread for kilometers, and when they open, the horizon seems briefly to stand on edge.

The six-chambered listeners are heavier and rarer. Their bodies resemble low iron boilers armored in hinged plates. Between the plates are resonant cavities, each tuned to a class of sound: impact, flowing water, alarm, fracture, thunder, or biological pulse.

They hunt by opening one chamber and taking its sound away.

If the water’s clatter vanishes, every striking bell is drawn toward the silence. If the groan of a pressure fault disappears, the movement of buried stone gathers inside the hunter. Calls can be removed halfway through, leaving animals with open mouths and no ending to their cries. When the chamber shuts, everything captured returns at once as an internal concussion, stunning or liquefying whatever has been drawn close.

The oldest listeners are scarred by sounds too large to digest. One carries thunder continuously in its third chamber; blue light leaks between its plates whenever it walks. Another swallowed the synchronized exhalation of a tower basin and has not closed properly since. It lies half buried, emitting a low, endless breath. Smaller animals shelter in its lee, mistaking its injury for weather.

There are creatures adapted specifically to the Compression.

Seam-scavengers are squat, many-legged things plated in dull silver. Before distance collapses, they fasten themselves to places where two remote surfaces are about to touch. For several hours they feed along the contact: licking foreign mud from local stone, cutting eggs from migrations that have arrived without crossing the intervening land, and collecting the pale residue produced when incompatible locations overlap.

When the plain expands again, the scavengers brace themselves with hook-shaped limbs. Some are divided nevertheless. These do not always die. A half left in one basin may continue sharing a stomach with its counterpart kilometers away. Food entering one body nourishes both; injury to either causes the other to bleed. Over time, divided scavengers become small distributed colonies, each member carrying one organ of an animal that no longer occupies a continuous place.

Other hunters use the Compression as a jaw. They position one part of themselves in a distant channel and another beside a migration route, then wait for the intervening kilometers to disappear. For an instant they become whole. They close around whatever has been brought between their halves. When distance returns, prey is carried away in pieces, some of which have not yet realized they are separate.

The water-cup animals are more cautious.

They are low, tripod-bodied browsers with a deep transparent organ set into the back. They collect still water and allow it to settle until it reflects an earlier plain. By flexing thin membranes beneath the cup, they select a depth of time, then drink. A mouthful from a young reflection repairs worn tissue. A careless swallow from too deep a layer may reduce the drinker to a larva, an egg, or a pattern of salts waiting for life to begin.

Families drink together. The oldest individual stirs the cup while the others watch the reflected stars. Their migrations follow not present geography but coastlines preserved in the water’s memory. They may cross dry stone as though wading, pause beside cliffs worn flat ages ago, and gather around seas that no longer exist.

Backward-feeders prey upon them. These are narrow, hooded animals with grasping siphons that force a victim’s own cup-water down its throat. They prefer adults carrying many years of mineral growth. The victim becomes younger in convulsive stages: plates soften, limbs shorten, instincts vanish. At last only an egg remains. The predator swallows it whole, acquiring not the flesh of the animal but the years removed from it. Old backward-feeders are surrounded by a haze of stolen duration. Rain slows near them. Cracks in the ground close as they pass.

Then there are the animals inside the ice.

When detached shadows gather and the flood skins over black, tiny spirals are already swimming through the solid. Their shells turn like screws. They move along lines of stress, eating trapped vibration and leaving minute tunnels that fill behind them. A dense swarm can steer a fracture away from one bank and toward another. Tower colonies cultivate them in frozen membranes around their vents, using them to shape the way sound breaks the brittle mist.

During a thaw the spirals do not emerge into water. They remain in the idea of ice, swimming through places that are still cold enough to remember being solid. At such times they can pass through shells, mineral beds, and living tissue. Animals infested with them become rigid and transparent from within. Their movements produce the soft cracking of distant lakes.

Death does not remove any of these inhabitants. It redistributes them.

A dead grazer clouds into metallic sediment, but its tendency to turn toward rich stone may enter the mud. A walker mineralizes upright, while its missing sides continue migrating. A bell shoal crushed during Compression may leave its rhythm in a channel, causing later floods to rattle even when empty. Electrical instincts drift into tower roots and are incorporated into pumping cycles.

Some places therefore behave like animals without possessing bodies. A patch of iron-black mud recoils when shadow passes over it. A mineral shelf contracts around eggs. An abandoned pool hunts by remaining still until thirsty creatures gather, then pours itself elsewhere. Stormwater occasionally assembles the reflexes of extinct fliers and lifts from the plain in the shape of beating wings.

In the broad basin, distinctions fail altogether.

Tower colonies interlock with herds. Bells live inside siphons. Predators become valves. Scavengers stitch temporary organs between distant bodies during Compression. Water moves only by being swallowed, filtered, remembered, aged, expelled, and swallowed again. A pressure wave crosses the basin and millions of creatures close. Another follows, and they open. Blue corona passes from vent to vent like a thought too large for any one organism.

No inhabitant commands this process, but each participates in it. The basin feeds as a single mouth without having a mouth. It listens through the six-chambered hunters, remembers through captured water, walks through incomplete herds, and alters gravity with buried radial flesh. The dead provide instincts; the living provide pressure; the stone provides shape.

Perhaps the largest creature there is not the basin, nor the colonies, nor the world beneath them.

Perhaps it is the exchange itself: salt becoming shell, shell becoming abrasion, abrasion becoming current, current becoming signal, signal becoming appetite, appetite becoming mineral again. Its organs are species. Its pulse is the synchronized venting of valleys. Its slow growth is indistinguishable from erosion.

It has no outer skin. It ends wherever the black water ceases to press upward—and the water, by every sign available to the creatures drinking from its ancient reflections, has never ceased anywhere.
