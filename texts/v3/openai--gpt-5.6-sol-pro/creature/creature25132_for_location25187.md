Nothing on the plateau is small in the ordinary sense.

Some bodies are small enough to rest on a fingertip, but their lives are hundreds of kilometers long. Some appear solitary while sharing organs through the bedrock. Others are immense only intermittently, gathering themselves from several locations when they must walk, feed, or call. Here an animal is less a bounded object than an operation: a pressure sustained, a circuit completed, a delay passed from one piece of the world to another.

The kiln animals are the most visible expression of this life.

An adult stands on six load-bearing columns around a furnace body armored in overlapping black plates. The plates are not dead shell. Each is a slow sensory organ, flexing under heat and registering pressure changes from impacts beyond the horizon. A kiln can feel another’s footfall through fifty kilometers of crust. It can distinguish the stamp of a rival from the collapse of a shelf by the shape of the returning tremor.

Its tower is throat, chimney, and sexual display. Metallic vapor rises through it in measured bands, each alloy carrying a different signal. To human senses the emissions would seem merely poisonous and hot, but another kiln reads them as precisely as scent: age, internal pressure, mineral deficiencies, the condition of catalytic organs, the remembered geometry of previous Circuit gatherings.

Their apparent blackness is produced by heat. Newly formed plates are translucent amber, crowded with fine conductive veins. Over millennia they absorb carbon and iron until they become opaque. The oldest animals are darker than shadow at noon. Their armor is worn smooth on the windward side, though there is almost no wind.

They are not born from eggs or wombs. A mature kiln periodically lays down a sequence of ingots different from the refuse bars left after feeding. Ratchet processions discover these pieces and begin their endless alterations, unaware—or perhaps aware in a way that does not require a central mind—that they are assembling an animal.

The process takes centuries. Alloy fragments are passed from claw to claw, doped, annealed in surface vents, and deposited around a warm fracture in the stone. First comes a buried catalytic knot. Then six pale limbs grow outward through the crust, following mineral seams. Only after the subterranean body is nearly complete does the juvenile break the surface.

Its emergence is easily mistaken for geology. The ground arches over several decades. Cracks form radial patterns. Thin vapor escapes and plates of ceramic rock tilt upright one by one. At last a narrow dorsal pipe appears, no taller than a person, releasing a clear whistle. The young kiln may remain crouched for another thousand years before taking its first step.

Many never do. Their unfinished organs remain in the plateau as hot knots, blind and industrious, refining mineral beneath the surface. Ratchets continue to tend them. Adult kilns sometimes stand nearby for a century, not touching, their towers inclined by a few degrees. Whether this is parental attendance, inspection, or the retrieval of useful heat cannot be separated.

The kilns seem aloof because proximity is dangerous to them. Their deeper limbs are broader than the columns visible aboveground, branching into conductive rootworks that cannot overlap without disrupting pressure cycles. When two adults approach too closely, neither attacks. Instead they begin to argue through the ground.

One stamps. The other answers forty-three seconds later. The exchange may continue for decades, impacts deepening until the intervening crust liquefies without becoming molten. Eventually one animal alters its route. Very rarely, both refuse. Their territories contract around them, distance folds, and the two are brought together without crossing the space between. What follows is a slow collision of furnaces. Plates buckle over years. Vapor fills the sky. Ratchet colonies converge from every direction.

The survivor, if there is one, walks away carrying portions of both internal rhythms.

Around these giants live creatures that cannot survive alone.

Ratchets are the most numerous. Each is a wedge-shaped mechanism of dark limbs surrounding a crystalline mouth. They have no permanent front or rear. Upon receiving a fragment, a ratchet reorganizes its joints so that the side facing the next member becomes its head. Thus a single animal may reverse its anatomy thousands of times without changing position.

The procession is their larger body. No individual knows its extent. A line interrupted by a fissure does not turn back; the final ratchet simply holds its fragment. Others accumulate behind it, becoming still. Over centuries their packed shells fuse into a bridge, and the passage resumes across the bodies of the dormant. Some awaken when the last traveler has crossed. Others remain part of the bridge forever, passing faint chemical instructions through the structure whenever the plateau warms.

A ratchet line has no destination because transfer itself is its nourishment. Each creature feeds on the minute energy released when it changes a crystal from one stable form to another. What travels down the line matters less than the act of passing it. Yet their processions produce astonishing consequences. They seed juvenile kilns, repair cracks in ancient shells, redirect conductive seams, and carry rare elements toward the White Circuit. They are nerves that have mistaken themselves for insects.

The sky-borers are more difficult to call animals, even here.

Only a fraction of each body occupies the visible sky. Their transparent cables ascend beyond the atmosphere and return through other parts of the day. A borer seen overhead may have its mouth at dawn, its middle at noon, and its terminal chambers moving through a night that will not reach the plateau for another six hours. Charged dust circulates along this temporal gradient. Rotary organs keep the sections aligned.

They do not fly. They maintain an agreement with altitude.

When one weakens, it descends by becoming increasingly visible. The first sign is a sheen like oil spread across the upper blue. Then internal valves cast moving shadows. Over months the long body sags, its turbine note dropping through pitches too low to hear and becoming a pressure in teeth and armor. Kiln animals beneath it turn their towers upward. Ratchets halt.

A fallen sky-borer never strikes the ground all at once. Its leading tissues may arrive centuries before its tail. Transparent membranes drape across ridges and harden into films of salt—the very deposits on which kilns feed. For generations afterward, the plateau shines where the body came down. Smaller scavengers move beneath the film, visible as sparks.

Among those scavengers are the swivel stones.

At rest they resemble handfuls of bright ore. Each consists of a mirrored upper shell balanced on three carbon needles. They turn toward concentrated light, but they do not consume light directly. They feed upon orientation. Every adjustment from one angle to another releases a minute imbalance within their crystalline bodies. In ordinary daylight they pivot too slowly to notice. During the White Circuit, when shadows divide and illumination arrives from incompatible directions, entire fields of them spin.

Their shells make no sound. The needles beneath them scream against the rock.

Afterward, some are found facing sources of light that no longer exist. Others point down. The oldest point toward places rather than directions: a certain basin three days in the past, a kiln animal’s open furnace, a star that has not yet descended. Ratchet lines occasionally dismantle these disoriented individuals and distribute the pieces into future juveniles, giving a newborn kiln its first crude sense of where it is.

The seams with mouths are colonies of plate-worms. Each mouth is a pair of mineral edges so perfectly matched that, when closed, the colony disappears into a hairline fracture. They open in sequence to catch pressure waves. A distant footfall enters the first mouth as vibration and leaves the last as heat. In feeding, the colony removes sound from the ground.

Large old colonies create patches of terrible quiet. Within them, one cannot hear a kiln’s approach or feel the subterranean hammering. The stone seems numb. Kilns avoid these areas, but interval grazers seek them out, settling their arches across the silent seams. The reasons are obscure. Perhaps duration is easier to stretch where events leave no echo.

An interval grazer is mostly absence.

Its red plates form a low, broad span, but beneath them there is no belly, no visible machinery, no tissue joining one side to the other. Objects viewed through the arch appear delayed. A ratchet entering from one side may not emerge for a century, though to the ratchet the crossing takes only moments. Dust can hang there without falling. Heat pools under the plates until the air becomes dense and glassy.

The grazer feeds by separating duration from change. Noon continues, stone continues to radiate, chemical reactions continue to lean toward completion—but the local hour does not end. Mirrored organs along the underside collect the discrepancy. As the animal fills, its plates brighten from iron red to white.

A sated grazer does not walk. It ceases to occupy adjacent moments. First it is present continuously, then only every other second, then once an hour, once a year, once in the lifetime of a kiln. Each reappearance finds it slightly farther away. The trail behind it is not a set of footprints but a series of aged enclosures, islands of ancient surface set into younger ground.

Their young are said to live inside unfinished events. A plate still falling, a vent still opening, a footstep whose impact has begun but whose sound has not arrived—these may harbor larval grazers. Nothing visible confirms this except the frequency with which old accidents begin lasting longer.

There are predators, though predation rarely resembles pursuit.

The largest are called convergents. No one sees their complete bodies because they inhabit relationships between paths. Their presence is inferred when two widely separated routes begin to acquire the same landmarks. A kiln walking east passes a triangular ridge. Hundreds of kilometers away, another walking north passes what appears to be the same ridge. Later both encounter the same black basin, the same ratchet bridge, the same patch of polished stone. The interval between them diminishes while the measured distance does not.

The convergent is feeding.

Over generations it edits alternatives from the terrain until its prey have only one route between them. At the final narrowing, both animals arrive at the same place from incompatible directions. Their shells intersect. Pressure organs rupture. Molten catalytic fluids spill without falling, suspended in conflicting geometries. The convergent consumes the released possibilities, and the remains separate again—often hundreds of kilometers apart, each missing pieces that were never visibly removed.

Kiln herds defend themselves by making redundant tracks. They may set a foot down in three locations, withdraw it from two, and allow only the least probable impression to bear their weight. Older animals surround themselves with so many unrealized routes that the ground behind them becomes furred with shallow, overlapping prints. Convergents have difficulty closing a path that does not agree with its own history.

Other hunters descend from above the atmosphere.

They first resemble misplaced stars. Their light grows no brighter as they approach, but shadows on the plateau begin pointing toward them. A descending hunter selects a precise site and may spend centuries falling the final kilometer. Below it, patient organisms gather: kiln animals with open towers, swivel stones trembling on their needles, columns of ratchets carrying fragments in reverse.

Whether these gatherings are defense, worship, or feeding contests depends on what reaches the ground. Sometimes the star enters a kiln’s tower and vanishes, after which the animal stands motionless for ages while new plates form across its body. Sometimes it opens above the surface into a many-jointed thing of incandescent wire and removes everything within its light, including distance. The resulting pits are shallow but impossible to cross; each step toward the opposite side adds more ground.

There are also creatures known only by their effects.

The forty-three-second hammer may belong to one of these. The regularity is too exact for a surface kiln and too powerful for any visible organism. Each impact seems to originate from a different depth. Sometimes it arrives from beneath the feet. Sometimes from inside a distant animal. During the White Circuit, all kiln herds synchronize to it, but the hammer itself does not change.

It may be the pulse of something whose body is the plateau.

The vents would then be pores. Conductive bands would be nerve or vessel. Kilns, borers, ratchets, grazers, and convergents might be autonomous fauna living on and within it—or specialized organs that have forgotten their unity. The absence of water, soil, and conventional food would not signify barrenness. It would mean that the entire mineral country has already been digested and incorporated.

There is evidence against this. There is also evidence that moves.

At the White Circuit every category becomes uncertain. Kilns stand along the concentric bands, each kilometers from its neighbors, yet their internal pressures rise together. Sky-borers align their divided bodies. Ratchet lines pour into fissures and become conductive tissue. Grazers lengthen selected minutes so that necessary reactions can finish before the next pulse. Swivel stones orient toward lights moving below the crust.

For eleven months, many lives behave as one.

No creature commands the assembly. No signal crosses it quickly enough. Coordination emerges from delayed impacts, inherited routes, particles passed claw to claw, and shadows that arrive before the bodies casting them. The Circuit is not built. It becomes inevitable.

At its height, individual animals are difficult to distinguish from the function they perform. A kiln is a walking furnace until it becomes one chamber in a valley-sized organ. A sky-borer is a drifting feeder until it becomes a winding in an atmospheric dynamo. A ratchet is an animal until ten billion ratchets become a current. Even the convergents circle the outer bands, trimming away paths on which the operation fails.

What is made there may not remain in the same world as its makers.

Perhaps each cycle produces one of the descending stars. Perhaps it repairs the sequence of afternoons. Perhaps the plateau itself is continually being born, assembled backward by organisms living upon the completed result. The animals offer no explanation. Afterward they disengage, close their towers, break their lines, and resume their solitary migrations.

They do not appear exhausted. They appear less definite.

For a few years after the Circuit, kiln animals can be seen faintly in places they have not yet reached. Ratchets pass empty space between them as if a fragment were present. Sky-borers cast shadows shaped like organs they no longer possess. Then certainty returns, insofar as certainty ever returns there.

Night reveals the full population.

Warm vents open like red eyes among the shelves. Kiln towers glow in slow sequences across the horizon. Ratchet lines draw threads of blue friction through black basins. The transparent sky fauna become visible against the stars, not as silhouettes but as moving distortions, long anatomies bending constellations around their chambers.

Other lights answer from much farther away. Some are animals above the atmosphere. Some are exposed moments. Some may be the same lights seen from several centuries at once.

Across the plateau, mouths open.

They wait without impatience. Waiting is not an interval between acts for these creatures. It is tissue, habitat, nourishment, and sense. They live in heat and metal, but more deeply they live in delay: in the time an impact takes to arrive, in the age between one step and the next, in the unclosed distance separating a hunter from its prey.

The plateau seems empty because its inhabitants leave room for their whole duration.
