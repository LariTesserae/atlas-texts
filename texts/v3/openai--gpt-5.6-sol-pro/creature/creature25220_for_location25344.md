What lives there is difficult to count.

The swifts live there plainly. So do the frogs, the beetles, the gray burrowers, the mosses, and the birches that have abandoned the shape of trees. But these are inhabitants in the ordinary sense. They enter, hatch, feed, sleep, die. Their bodies can be found.

The older inhabitant is not found so easily.

Climbers call it the Low One, though nothing agrees on whether this name refers to a creature, a presence, or the mountain itself. It is called low because all signs attributed to it occur close to the ground. Grass bends without wind. Water changes course beneath a boot. A patch of moss rises and settles as if something under it has drawn breath. Small objects left in the second stone cup sometimes move from one side to the other, but never climb over the rim. Nothing belonging to it stands upright.

No one claims to have seen the Low One whole.

At times it seems very small. A movement passes beneath the dwarf birches, no larger than a stoat and too deliberate to be runoff. There is a glimpse of a narrow gray back, slick with moisture, vanishing between stones. Tiny five-pointed prints have been reported in the silt after rain. They resemble the forefeet of a salamander, except that the outer digits are too long and sometimes leave scratches like writing.

At other times it appears distributed across the basin. A person kneeling beside the weeping wall may notice that droplets are striking the pool in a sequence rather than at random. The rhythm continues in the underground gurgling, then returns from the eastern chimney as a softened whistle. If the person taps the ledge, the sequence stops. Tap again, and it resumes somewhere beneath the central slabs.

In that moment the Low One seems not to be an animal under a rock, but a listening arrangement of rock, water, roots, and hollow air.

There are stories that it has a body in the chambers below the old tarn bed. In these accounts it is pale, jointless, and very long. It lies folded through the mountain’s internal passages as an eel lies among submerged roots. Its skin is thin enough for dark stone to show through it. Water enters along its flanks and leaves through fringed openings beneath its throat. It has no eyes, having never required them. Instead, its skull—or what passes for one—is a broad plate pressed to the underside of the basin floor.

Every hoofstep reaches it.

Every rainfall touches it in a thousand places.

When it moves, it does not crawl so much as alter its pressure within the mountain. A chamber fills; another empties. Water rises unexpectedly through one crack and vanishes down another. Loose stones knock together far below. The sounds that return from the walls may be echoes shaped by tunnels, but the old climbers say the Low One is imitating what it hears. It has listened for so long that stone taps, hoofbeats, wing-claps, and human syllables have become the only vocabulary it possesses.

It repeats poorly. Or perhaps it repeats with care, discarding whatever it considers unnecessary.

A shout comes back as a single flute note.

A name returns as three taps.

Laughter may descend into the floor and not be heard again for years, until water striking the southern spring reproduces its cadence in miniature.

Those who believe in a smaller creature describe it differently. They say the thing beneath the birches is the Low One itself, not merely an extremity. It is squat and dark, with limbs arranged for clinging rather than walking. Its hide carries moss in the folds. Mineral crust whitens its back, and fernlike gills lie flat against its neck. The long outer fingers can be spread against stone to detect vibrations. When still, it is nearly indistinguishable from a wet block threaded with roots.

According to these accounts, it emerges only in cloud.

It comes from a drainage hole near the western wall and moves around the basin’s margin, never crossing the open center. It drinks not by lowering its mouth but by laying its whole underside against saturated moss. Beetles disappear beneath it. Pale worms are taken delicately between two fingers. It may remain in one place for hours while water beads over its back.

Swifts tolerate it. They sometimes land near it to pull mineral-coated fibers from its skin for their nests. Rodents pass beneath its resting limbs. Frogs climb onto it during storms and call there, mistaking—or not mistaking—it for warm ground.

Goats will not approach it.

They often detect its presence before people do. Their green-stained knees straighten. Their ears turn toward a patch of apparently vacant moss. Then the herd withdraws in silence, preferring the exposed slabs to the living margin. Eagles, circling above, show no interest. Either they cannot distinguish it from the basin or they have learned that it is not something carried away.

The two descriptions may both be true. The small, low body seen in mist may be only what the deeper animal puts aboveground: a feeding organ, a sensing limb, or a bud produced for the wet season. There are organisms in the basin that have no single center. The birches share buried stems. Moss breaks and continues as itself. Fungal threads pass from root to root beneath several paces of earth. The Low One may follow this older pattern.

Its visible bodies, if they are bodies, do not appear to live long. Occasionally a gray, mineral-stiff shape is found pressed into a crack after winter. It has fingers but no mouth, ribs but no spine, and an interior filled with clear gel. By midsummer it has collapsed into a skin no thicker than a dead leaf. The surrounding moss grows unusually green.

Yet the noises beneath the slabs continue.

The Low One does not seem hostile. It has caused deaths only in the manner that weather and old stone cause them: without pursuit. A hollow slab has broken under a climber. A sudden diversion of water has loosened footholds in the eastern chimney. Once, according to the charcoal marks kept in a village far below, the basin filled during a cloudburst and did not drain for nine days. Throughout those days, something struck the underside of the shelter floor at dusk.

Still, people bring it gifts.

They place bright pebbles in the untouched cup because color is rare underground. They leave bird bones because bones preserve the memory of flight. Beads are offered for their holes, each one a small imitation of a passage through stone. The horn figures are always carved lying down. Some resemble goats, others people, others long many-limbed forms that cannot be identified. Upright figures are considered insulting or foolish.

The Low One appears to value none of these objects in the way a bird values glitter. It does not carry them off. Over decades, however, they rotate in the basin. Bones sink into mineral film. Pebbles are turned so that their brightest surfaces face downward. Beads become threaded on fine white roots that were not visible when they were placed there.

The second cup is never cleaned.

To clean it would be to erase the slow arrangement by which the inhabitant answers.

Those who sleep in the stone shelter sometimes wake with the certainty that someone is lying beneath them, matching the position of their body. Turn onto one side, and a faint grinding follows under the floor. Stretch a hand, and water ticks from the wall in five quick drops. Most climbers dismiss this in daylight. Cold, altitude, and continuous noise make patterns where none exist.

Even so, few sleep there twice.

The Low One is least perceptible in fair weather. Under a hard blue sky, the basin becomes merely a basin. The silt dries. The frogs vanish. The runnels diminish. Insects occupy the sunlit stones, and the swifts overwhelm every subtler sound. Whatever lives below withdraws with the water, down into pressure, darkness, and the long cold filtration through the peak.

Rain gives it coherence.

As the channels fill, separate signs begin to join. The wet wall darkens like an opening eye. Moss shifts over buried hollows. The central slabs answer one another. Frogs call from temporary pools, and beneath their wooden notes comes a lower interval, too slow to be heard as a voice but strong enough to be felt through the knees.

Then the whole basin seems to be one shallow organ fitted into the mountain’s crown.

The empty center is its listening surface. The green ring is its sensitive edge. The eastern chimney is its throat. The spring far below is where its gathered waters finally leave it, altered by years in darkness.

Whether that makes the Low One a beast, a colony, or merely an old habit of the mountain is a distinction important only to visitors.

Everything that lives there has learned the same posture.

The birches kneel. The grasses flatten. The goats lower themselves to feed. The swifts press into holes. The frogs bury themselves in mud. Even people crawl through the chimney and sleep beneath a roof barely raised from the earth.

High things are broken or taken.

The inhabitant endures by keeping close to stone, spreading sideways, and allowing the summit to appear empty above it.
