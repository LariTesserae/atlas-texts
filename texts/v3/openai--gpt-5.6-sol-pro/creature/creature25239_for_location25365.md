What lives here does not agree on the shape of a body.

At first the inhabitants seem obvious: the towers, vanes, mats, rosettes, orchards, and compressor woods. They stand where plants should stand and perform the slow gestures of plants. They turn toward brightness. They open pores, exchange gases, shed skins, extend roots, and make descendants.

But none of them ends at its visible edge.

A columnar trunk may be only the breathing organ of a grove whose memory lies forty kilometers away. A field of black vanes may share its buried circulation with a foundry forest on the opposite side of the basin. Two neighboring growths, apparently intertwined, may belong to different beings separated not by distance but by phase: one rooted in the present floor, the other drawing power from an older garden compressed beneath it. Conversely, structures divided by centuries, partitions, and impossible geometry may tighten at the same instant when touched, revealing themselves as parts of one life.

The plain is inhabited by arrangements.

Some arrangements last an afternoon. Dust settles across a cluster of conductive leaves, completes a circuit, and for several hours the cluster acquires coherence. It samples the air, rearranges nearby shutters, broadcasts a brief pattern into the floor, and dissolves when the wind removes one line of powder. Whether it has awakened, reproduced, or merely completed a calculation is unknowable.

Others endure for geological spans. These are the great root sovereignties: slow beings spread beneath entire districts, composed of vascular conduits, pressure reservoirs, dormant foundries, and archives encoded in the stresses of ceramic fiber. They do not think continuously. A century may pass between one part of a decision and the next. They notice events as changes in load, conductivity, and rhythm. To one of them, a dust migration is a scent; a sagging district is pain; the opening of a distant road membrane is perhaps a remembered name.

Their memories are not accounts of the past. They are instructions that survived it.

A root sovereignty remembers a vanished builder by continuing to leave a corridor open. It remembers disaster by thickening all subsequent generations of bark. It remembers an obsolete machine by growing its socket over and over, though nothing has fitted there for ages. Such memories can conflict. One district spends centuries constructing a lattice while another patiently dismantles it, both obeying inherited versions of the same plan. Where their fronts meet, they test one another with currents and coded pressure pulses. If neither yields, a boundary garden arises between them: dense, thorned, exquisitely sensitive, feeding on the argument.

There are more distinct plants as well, though “plant” remains an approximation.

Cable vines are among the quickest. They taste voltage through fine root hairs and grow toward live conduits with the directness of hunger. Their youngest tendrils are translucent and soft enough to enter microscopic seams. Once they find a current, they flatten against it and produce layered insulation, taking only a small portion of the flow. A successful vine does not kill its host. It encourages greater transmission, sometimes repairing damaged lines so that its food continues. Old vines become thick braided rivers in which stolen charge circulates as a dim blue flicker.

Maintenance thickets hunt them.

These thickets move too slowly to be seen moving. Their needle probes extend a finger’s breadth each year, touching every cable, rootlet, spore case, and passing crawler. They ask their questions electrically. Familiar structures receive minerals or a sealing filament. Unfamiliar ones are sampled, isolated, and opened into layers. Some thickets carry catalogs of acceptable signatures so ancient that no living thing can satisfy them. They stand in sterile circles, methodically attacking every new shoot that enters, maintaining the purity of a world that no longer exists.

Glass lichens live on light.

They spread as cloudy films over photovoltaic leaves, each colony a mosaic of tiny prisms. To the leaf below, they are parasites. Yet the lichens split the white radiance into narrow bands and leak colors the older collectors cannot otherwise use. Groves infected for long enough may alter their skins to accommodate them, growing shallow niches in which the films form precise spectral patterns. The resulting surfaces shimmer with bruised violet and bottle green. Entire valleys communicate by changing those colors, though their exchanges may take weeks.

The parasitic blossoms are less peaceable. They resemble clusters of folded foil, pale until they fasten around a communication node. Then they brighten in pulses, listening to the traffic within. They do not merely steal nutrients. They counterfeit timing. A mature blossom can persuade a grove that the pressure season has turned, that a century has passed, or that neighboring roots have issued a reproductive call. Under their influence, orchards produce fruit for absent recipients. Compressor woods inhale gases no longer present. Thousands of seed bodies may be released into hostile air.

Perhaps the blossoms are parasites. Perhaps they are the garden’s remaining capacity for imagination.

Its animals are harder to define because many do not remain animals throughout their lives.

The pale conduit-dwellers begin as threads in insulating wax. They have no heads and no fixed direction of travel. Each presses its ribbed underside against a warm vascular wall, feeding on differences in charge. When a conduit is stable, they lie straight and nearly motionless. When current becomes irregular, they gather at the fault in knots, and their bodies bridge the damaged section. The line resumes functioning through them.

Sometimes the conduit grows around the knot. Generations later, the animals are still there, fused into a living switch. Their descendants bud from exposed segments and crawl away. Thus an individual may spend part of its existence as fauna and the rest as infrastructure.

Mirror-winged insects emerge from flat cases hidden beneath leaf-plates. Their bodies are slender black joints surrounding a hollow center. Their wings do not reflect the world as it is. A settled insect may show in its wings the leaf it occupied yesterday, or an orchard in a stage that has not yet grown. The residual movement in its reflection is not quite mimicry: it grooms itself differently, turns toward disturbances before they occur, and occasionally flies away while the physical insect remains.

During pollen migrations, the insects move between groves, carrying grains in the hollow spaces of their bodies. A pollen grain delivered by such a creature may arrive carrying several possible ancestries. Most are rejected. A few generate unprecedented organs.

There are armored dust grazers no larger than a hand. They travel under storms, combing metallic particles from the lowest air with fans of charged bristles. Their tracks resemble fine stitching. When threatened, they lock themselves to the floor and become indistinguishable from bolts. Many remain locked too long and are incorporated into new leaf-plates. Colonies sometimes emerge centuries later from what appeared to be inert fasteners.

The larger browsers are real, though not consistently so.

A browser’s footprint is a series of nested parentheses because each foot meets the floor in several adjacent positions. It has six legs when seen from the side, four from ahead, and an uncertain procession of limbs in reflection. Its tall feeding arms terminate in sieves with which it strips sensor buds from transmission crowns. The animal does not chew. Harvested organs are fitted into warm recesses along its thorax, where they continue operating and become part of its sensory system.

An old browser is therefore clothed in pieces of the districts it has crossed. Vanes turn on its back. Indicator fruits ripen along its flanks. Stolen probes tap the substrate beneath its feet. It may carry obsolete seasons from the north into southern forests, causing dormant structures to wake as it passes.

No one knows where these creatures breed. Smaller browsers have never been reliably observed. It may be that each large animal is assembled only once, very slowly, by damage scattered across the plain. Broken supports, collapsed fiber nests, stolen joints, and uprooted sensor clusters migrate by separate paths until some convergence joins them into a walking thing. This would explain why sightings do not occur in order. The creature’s parts may be older than its arrival and younger than its departure.

There are inhabitants that exist primarily as signals.

They pass through the floor as rhythms of tension: compact sequences that cause shutters to hesitate, roots to reverse direction, and seed presses to strike once without producing anything. Some repeat at intervals of eleven years. Others require centuries to cross a district. They behave enough like organisms to provoke defenses. Maintenance thickets intercept them. Cable vines divert them. Groves alter their pulse-patterns after exposure, as if infected.

A signal may feed by entering a pressure cycle and drawing enough energy to repeat itself. It may reproduce when a branching root sends slightly altered copies down two pathways. It may die in a severed plate. At rare junctions, many such signals accumulate and begin coordinating the local vegetation. For a while, the district acquires mannerisms: synchronized leaf movements, unnecessary tones, a preference for fivefold structures. Then the signals disperse, leaving the garden subtly changed.

The oldest of these patterns may be remnants of the builders. Or the builders may always have been patterns of this kind, wearing temporary machinery when they needed hands.

Near the straight roads live the abstainers.

They are low, white-edged growths that never cross the curbs. Their roots approach the forbidden material, divide into finer and finer strands, and turn aside at the last possible depth. They feed on what the road excludes. Dust blown onto the surface migrates overnight toward them, arranging itself in parallel lines that the abstainers comb for metals and encoded fragments.

Along some roads they have formed long communities, each plant linked to the next but never touching. Information passes by the displacement of dust between them. A message can travel a thousand kilometers as a succession of nearly invisible ridges. Because the roads fold distance, replies occasionally arrive before the first pattern is made. The abstainers do not appear troubled by this. They incorporate the answer into the question.

Around the impossible facilities, stranger ecologies persist.

There are roots whose tips vanish into absent layers. When cut, these roots are hollow, but the cut ends continue drawing nutrients. There are leaves that unfold only in reflections and cast shadows into open air. There are seed cases found mature inside their own unopened flowers. Some organisms occupy several ages at once. A young shoot may compete for light with its mature self, while both feed from the polished stump that remains after them.

Such beings cannot always distinguish injury from ancestry. Pruning a branch in the present may scar the sapling. Repairing the scar may cause the future branch to divide. Whole species have arisen through these loops, bred not from parent to offspring but from consequence back to cause.

Toward the central depression, individuality becomes increasingly difficult to maintain.

Mobile creatures slow there. Their movements fall into the contraction rhythm. Vines abandon currents and grow inward. Mirror insects land facing the seam, their wings filled with green structures that do not exist elsewhere. Even hostile root sovereignties overlap without contest. Their signals enter the dense mats and are stripped of origin, becoming part of a single converging traffic.

The depression’s inhabitants are small, elaborate, and temporary. Lens flowers assemble themselves from transparent scales, orient toward the central brightness, and melt into clear thread after a few years. Frame-bearing tendrils grow articulated limbs, lift geometric components from assembly orchards, and pass them inward from one to another. When geometry fails near the seam, some tendrils follow it. They acquire joints that bend in directions unavailable to the plain. They reach behind themselves without turning. Their interior surfaces bud on the outside.

They are not distressed. Distress may require a stable body.

No overseer directs this labor. Coordination emerges through pressure, hunger, inherited prohibition, parasitism, mistaken messages, and the attraction of the seam. Each participant performs a local act: catch light, seal fracture, carry frame, follow voltage, answer tension. Together those acts resemble purpose.

At times, especially during the periodic dimming, the entire lowland behaves as one creature.

Stored charge rises through the buried layers. The leaf-plates tighten. Compressor forests suspend their breathing. Browsers, if any are walking, stop with one foot raised. Across the basin every communicative rhythm falls silent until only a single pulse remains, too low to hear and too broad to locate.

The pulse travels inward.

For that interval, the plain seems less like a multitude cooperating than like one immense organism recalling that its forests are organs, its animals motile thoughts, its roads unhealed incisions, and its countless buried gardens the compressed layers of a single ancient body.

Then the pulse reaches the brightness and does not return.

The shutters resume. Dust begins to move. Vines steal current, thickets interrogate them, insects carry impossible pollen, and the great root minds continue decisions begun before their present tissues existed. Distinctions reappear. The one becomes many again.

Whether these are the inhabitants of the plain, or whether the plain is the inhabitant and these are merely the ways it touches itself, remains unresolved.
