No creature there sees the city whole.

Each knows it as a sequence of pressures: a wall against the left flank, a warmer taste ahead, the tremor of a feeding crown snapping shut, the sudden drag of water when something large enters a nearby shaft. To live there is to possess no horizon. It is to inherit a route through matter.

The most numerous inhabitants are the **pale binders**, soft-bodied animals scarcely distinguishable from the mucus they produce. At rest they resemble scraps of translucent skin folded into cracks. Their organs are visible only when food passes through them: a smoky thread moving down the center of the body, dividing, fading. They have no eyes and little armor. Along their undersides are rows of adhesive lobes, each able to grip a separate grain.

A binder spends its life repairing the immediate world without knowing it repairs anything. It grazes over shell dust, bacterial felt, and mineral flakes, swallowing what is edible and coating the rest in a secretion that hardens in seep water. Its trail becomes a thin, flexible pavement. Other animals cross it. More debris adheres. In time the pavement thickens into a ledge, then a wall.

Young binders are nearly liquid. They pass through pores that adults cannot enter and emerge in sealed pockets where the water has gone stale. There they feed on bacteria until their bodies widen. If no moving animal reopens the pocket, they die within it, but their decay produces gas and acid; the gas fractures the mineral film, the acid softens its edges, and sometimes the chamber opens after all. Even their failures make passages.

Older binders accumulate foreign matter on their backs. Shell scales, crystal needles, shed mouthparts, and grains of volcanic glass become embedded in their outer mucus. No two carry the same covering. Some resemble tiled roofs. Some are shaggy with the hollow jaws of worms. A very old one may be so crusted that only its feeding end remains soft. When it moves, the city’s floor appears to move with it.

Above them live the **tube-throats**, whose crowded houses form the pale columns. The animal inside a tube is little more than a digestive cord, a muscular collar, and a crown of filaments. It begins life free-swimming, though “swimming” is too forceful a word: the larva turns slowly in the water until chemistry tells it that another tube is near. Then it fixes its tail beside the adult and secretes a first brittle ring.

Every later ring records a feeding season. Warm, sulfurous weeks make broad dark bands. Cold intervals make thin translucent ones. Sudden fear leaves faults where growth stopped and resumed. The oldest tubes contain the chamber’s unwritten history, though no inhabitant can read it.

The tube-throats share more than walls. Where their tubes touch, pressure passes through the colony. One crown recoils; its withdrawal dents the water and flexes the neighboring tube; the next animal withdraws in turn. The response can race across a district faster than any predator. Sometimes it begins with danger. Sometimes a settling crystal strikes the right surface. Sometimes the first animal withdraws for no apparent reason, and ten thousand neighbors obey.

For those few seconds the city loses its fringe. The columns become bare, chalk-white, lifeless. Predators find nothing to seize. Then one filament appears, then another, and the entire settlement opens like frost growing backward.

Between the tube-walls move the **hinge-runners**, needle-legged crustaceans with bodies no larger than mineral grains. Their shells are incomplete: narrow plates joined by wide intervals of naked membrane. The plates protect them only when they fold themselves into angles. A hinge-runner fleeing through a tunnel seems to collapse successively—head under thorax, abdomen beside head, legs drawn through last—then unfolds on the other side.

They are scavengers, thieves, and accidental masons. They drag scraps into crevices to eat in safety. The edible portions vanish, while shell, tooth, spine, and indigestible film remain. Binders seal these heaps. Tube-throats colonize them. Generations later, a mound of hinge-runner refuse may divert an entire current.

Their young are carried beneath the abdomen in glassy sacs. The embryos develop slowly enough that mineral dust sometimes skins the sacs before they hatch. Mothers scrape the coating away with their hind legs. Those that fail carry pale, solid pearls until the dead brood breaks down inside.

Hinge-runners communicate by tapping shared surfaces. The signals do not travel far, but within a single wall they are precise: food, obstruction, hunter, fertile body. A crowded wall is never still underfoot. It is alive with minute percussion.

They fear the **thread-mouths**.

A thread-mouth is a ribbon worm that can enter an opening narrower than its own head because its head has no fixed width. It advances by pouring itself forward. The front of the body thins into several exploratory cords, each tasting a different crack. When one finds prey, the others withdraw and the whole animal follows that single cord.

It does not always kill directly. A thread-mouth may lie for days inside the loose armor of a binder or between the plates of a hinge-runner, drinking slowly. Infected animals continue their habits. They feed, drag debris, reinforce walls, and carry the hidden worm into neighborhoods it could not otherwise reach. Only when the host becomes too weak to hold a current does the thread-mouth consume what remains.

Afterward it may wear the emptied body for a time. Not as disguise—there is little sight here—but as scaffolding. The old legs catch against tunnel walls, anchoring the worm while it reaches for another animal.

Larger than these, but less numerous, are the **well-jaws** beneath the plazas. A well-jaw spends almost all its life buried vertically in soft sediment with only its circular mouth exposed. Around that mouth the floor is kept unnaturally clear. Its cilia push away falling dust. Its hidden limbs rake aside anything that might reveal the edge of the burrow. Thus each well-jaw makes a smooth open space, and open space becomes a warning every mobile animal learns bodily.

The mouth does not snap. It caves inward.

When prey crosses the center, the well-jaw suddenly enlarges its throat and draws in water, sediment, and victim together. It sorts the mouthful below. Edible matter passes down. Sand and shell are packed into pellets and expelled through a separate chimney several body-lengths away. Those pellets support their own bacterial films, attract grazers, and sometimes become the foundations of towers. The predator creates a neighborhood with the waste from maintaining its emptiness.

Some hinge-runners exploit it. They wait at a plaza’s edge for the inward current, leap into it, seize loosened scraps from the well-jaw’s mouth, and kick away before the throat closes. Most attempts succeed. The unsuccessful ones are why the behavior remains profitable to the predator.

In the vertical water move the **bell-bodies**.

At first they seem delicate even by the standards of the pocket: clear medusae, no larger than thumbprints, with bells too weak to beat. But weakness is not quite what they possess. Their flesh contains rows of tiny chambers lined with salt-moving cells. By filling one row and emptying another, they alter their density and their balance. They can rise without pulsing, tilt without turning, or stop at a chosen height where the upward seep exactly bears their weight.

Their descent is the nearest thing the city has to weather.

A feeding bell darkens as its internal chambers concentrate salts. It sinks through the towers with filaments trailing below, each filament fine enough to enter side streets the bell itself cannot approach. The strands carry adhesive bulbs and knots of venom. A hinge-runner touching one may be held in place while the filament shortens grain by grain. Sometimes the captured animal is drawn upward. Sometimes the bell continues to descend, hauling its prey through several districts and stripping away legs against the architecture.

Small animals recognize the first dimming of a bell-body. Tube-throats retract. Binders flatten. Hinge-runners enter holes too narrow for their own armor and endure the pressure until the shadow passes. The warning moves ahead of the hunter as a chain of local disappearances. From a viewpoint no resident possesses, one would see whole avenues empty in widening rings beneath it.

Bell-bodies reproduce near the warm fissure. They press soft clusters of eggs against mineral chimneys, where the temperature is barely higher but reliably so. The hatchlings do not resemble bells. They are creeping, many-mouthed films that spread over the chimney and feed directly on bacteria. Months later portions of the film swell, detach, and drift upward as transparent bells. The parent form and the child form never meet as recognizable kin.

At the seep itself, individual animals become difficult to name.

There are **smoke-snails**, their shells reduced to soft internal spirals. There are grazing disks that move on rippling carpets of cilia. There are mouthless worms filled with sulfur-eating bacteria, pale except for the dark organs in which their symbionts are kept. There are tiny hunters shaped like open hands, standing on five limbs while the sixth waits to close. There are colonial bodies that look like mineral stains until a passing shadow causes every pore in them to contract.

Many of these creatures are less single than they appear.

The mouthless seep-worm, for instance, is born with a digestive tract. As a larva it feeds in the ordinary manner, swallowing bacterial cells from the walls. Later it seeks the chemical boundary where methane-rich water meets oxygen-bearing cold. There it ingests a final population of microbes. The microbes enter its tissues. Its gut closes, then dissolves. From then on, the worm is an envelope carrying a chemical garden it can neither leave nor survive without.

If the seep weakens, the bacteria consume the worm from within.

If the seep strengthens, they multiply until the worm must grow or split. Adults sometimes tear crosswise, each half sealing around part of the bacterial population. What seems like reproduction is also the redistribution of hunger.

Farther from the fissure, along the Fold, live the **long-turners**, though they cannot turn.

They are narrow segmented animals whose rigid bristles point backward. Once they enter one of the Fold’s lower apertures, reversal is impossible: the bristles lock against the walls. They must continue inward until the passage widens, ascend through the crowded interior, and leave through an upper hole. Their bodies are shaped by this circuit. Their front ends tolerate warm, mineral-heavy water; their trailing ends tolerate cold. A long-turner stopped halfway across the Fold can survive with its body in two chemical worlds.

They graze as they go, clearing the lower passages and coating the upper ones with waste. Their migration maintains the direction of flow. The direction of flow favors their migration. Neither came first in any useful sense.

Young long-turners begin the circuit before their bristles harden. Some attempt to reverse and can. With each molt, backward movement becomes more difficult. Adulthood is reached when the city’s route becomes anatomy.

Occasionally one grows too large for the widest chamber. It advances until both ends are wedged, then remains alive as a fleshy obstruction. Other creatures climb over it. Binders coat its exposed back. Tube larvae settle beside it. When it dies, its body decays inside a sleeve of mineral and mucus, leaving a new conduit through the Fold. Traffic has turned a traveler into infrastructure.

No inhabitant is entirely protected from burial. The chamber’s walls slump by degrees too small to flee from. A district that was level one season may tilt; a low corridor may become a trap for falling silt. The creatures most suited to this are the **breath-borers**, blunt animals with shovel-shaped heads and extensible tails.

When buried, a breath-borer does not dig itself free. It swallows sediment ahead and packs it behind while extending its tail toward moving water. Glands at the tail’s tip secrete a lining that keeps the narrow shaft from collapsing. As the animal descends or shifts sideways, the tail lengthens upward. Eventually only the tail opening remains at the surface, opening and closing like a tiny mouth.

Other creatures shelter around these breathing chimneys. Bacteria grow on their exhalations. Tube-throats attach to the hardened rims. Predators wait beside them for whatever emerges. If the breath-borer dies below, the tail lining remains, and seep pressure may turn the empty shaft into a warm spring. A single buried body can determine the arrangement of a district generations later.

Death here rarely remains a body for long.

Soft tissue clouds with bacteria. Scavengers enter through membranes rather than tearing them. A dead bell-body collapses over towers like clear fabric and is eaten from both sides. Tube-throats leave their houses as empty vertical graves, but larvae settle on their rims, binders coat their interiors, and long-bodied animals use them as passageways. Shell plates persist somewhat longer. They migrate through the city one accidental drag at a time, becoming roofs, blades, paving, armor, and finally mineral dust.

Only eggs are treated as if they must remain distinct.

Many inhabitants produce them in transparent sacs fixed to the sheltered sides of walls. A whole district may become furred with embryos. Inside each sac, cells divide slowly in the cold, pause when the seep falters, and resume when warmth returns. Some eggs take longer to develop than the structure supporting them takes to survive. Their wall falls, their district is buried, their sac is carried through a crack, and they hatch somewhere no adult of their kind has lived.

A few eggs are older than the streets around them.

They are passed from surface to surface by collapse. Their shells incorporate successive mineral skins, giving them dark concentric halos. Most contain nothing living. Some contain larvae held in such deep suspension that life and preservation are nearly the same condition. The opening of a cold rupture, the arrival of methane, or the pressure of a passing body may wake them.

Then a creature absent for many generations returns to the city without having come from elsewhere.

When true ice falls through the ceiling, all these lives rearrange around it.

The ice is not merely food or shelter. It is an impossible surface: hard yet vanishing, cold even to the cold-adapted, carrying trapped gases and green remnants from above. Binders creep beneath it and fix shell dust to its underside. Hinge-runners scrape organisms from its cloudy veins. Tube larvae attach to it despite having no future there. Bell-bodies hover above it, made buoyant by the fresher water melting from its edges.

The ice produces a temporary caste of opportunists. Creatures normally separated by temperature, current, or predation crowd within antenna-lengths of one another. Eggs are fastened to it because bacterial films bloom there. Hunters arrive because prey has gathered. The shard becomes a roof, plaza, feeding ground, nursery, and source of freshening water.

Then it thins.

Structures attached to it descend. Eggs tear loose. Tube colonies topple. Fresh water disperses. The animals do not mourn, but for several feeding cycles they continue to follow routes that no longer lead around anything. Their repeated motion keeps those routes clear. Thus the vanished ice survives as streets.

Taken separately, none of these creatures is remarkable for intelligence. Most possess only knots of nerve sufficient to distinguish touch from hunger, warmth from poison, open water from a closing wall. They do not know the Fold as a landmark, the seep as a center, or the plazas as a class of dangerous place. They have no image of enclosure. They cannot conceive that another ocean lies above them.

Yet together they remember.

They remember predators as avenues maintained by generations of flight. They remember currents in the orientation of tubes. They remember collapses in bridges made from fallen walls. They remember favorable chemistry in crowded layers of eggs. They remember extinct bodies in the dimensions of tunnels first bored around them. Their memory has no witness. It is embedded in what remains passable.

The city itself is therefore the largest thing that lives there—not as a single organism, and not merely as a metaphor. It breathes through thousands of chimneys. It feeds at the seep. It recoils district by district. It seals its wounds with fear, waste, bacterial film, and newborn bodies. Its skeleton is continually secreted by those sheltering inside it, while its flesh is replaced too quickly for any part to claim permanence.

Its thoughts, if they can be called thoughts, are changes in traffic.

A bell darkens. Corridors empty. Pressure shifts. Tube crowns close. Silt falls into the unused water. Binders cross the new layer. Larvae settle. A wall begins.

A fissure opens. Cold pours in. Burrowers exude mucus. Small bodies crowd the breach. Their combined drag slows the current. Dust catches. The opening narrows. Those nearest it are entombed, and the others survive behind them.

A seep pulse warms the chamber. Microbes divide. Grazers gather. Hunters follow. Eggs clear. Towers add pale rings. The floor bows beneath the new weight.

Nothing commands these responses. Nothing names them. The whole remains unaware in every one of its parts.

Still, when the sediment presses inward, it presses against something that persists—not an animal, exactly, but a dense agreement among appetites. A habitation that can exist only by being inhabited. A body made from the prevention of burial.

And in every narrow living hollow, some soft creature braces itself against the closing world, feeds, and keeps the dark open.
