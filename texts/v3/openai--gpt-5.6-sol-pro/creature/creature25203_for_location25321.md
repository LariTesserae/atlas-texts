The people who live there are difficult to recognize as a people at first. There is no costume shared across the basin, no single ancestry, no emblem painted on every door. From a distance they look merely practical: pale commuters in dark coats, mechanics with rolled sleeves, children in waxed capes, growers carrying trays of seedlings between factories. They have the compressed, weatherproof appearance of the city’s plants. Their clothes are layered rather than heavy, reinforced at knees and shoulders, crossed with straps from which hang gloves, chalk, a folding ground-pole, a cup, a small level. Their boots have broad articulated soles. Most carry two kinds of key and several things that are not keys but become useful when a door has changed its mind.

They seldom hurry on foot.

This is not caution exactly. It is attentiveness. A native walker places the heel lightly and lets the sole listen before committing weight. On familiar streets this becomes unconscious, a faint hesitation that gives crowds their peculiar flowing gait. People spread around a newly raised seam without looking down. They touch handrails not merely for support but for news. A rail can tell them that a tram is coming, that a pump has started three blocks away, that a retaining wall is under strain, that someone is hammering illegally in a cellar.

Visitors mistake these gestures for nervousness. In fact the citizens are not especially afraid of the ground. They distrust rigidity more.

A person who refuses to alter a plan when a corridor has bent, who insists that a boundary remain where it was yesterday, who fastens both ends of a bridge and calls the job finished—such a person is considered reckless. Their language has many unflattering words for needless firmness. The worst describe someone who would rather break than yield and who expects everything around them to break as well.

Children learn flexibility before they understand why it is valued. They play games in which the rules migrate from one side of the yard to the other. They build houses of slotted pieces and then score one another not for height or beauty but for how much distortion the houses endure before becoming unusable. In school they are taught to distinguish the tremor of a passing freight train from the slower pulse of rising earth. They memorize evacuation scents. They practice waking in darkness, finding whichever door still meets the floor, and leaving without taking anything that requires two hands.

They are also taught not to mock someone who has been caught.

The ground’s enclosures are usually gentle, but the experience marks people. A worker may stand still too long while checking a machine and find both boots clasped to the ankle by hardening mineral. A sleeper in an illegally parked cab may wake to stone grown over the wheel wells and halfway up the doors. Extraction crews arrive with heating coils, ceramic saws, and blankets. They speak continuously to the trapped person, even when there is no physical danger. It is said that the earth does not swallow; it persuades the body that motion has become impossible. Long after rescue, some people cannot bear tight shoes or chairs with arms.

The oldest inhabitants have bodies shaped by decades of correction. Their balance is excellent, their ankles thick, their palms crossed by pale pressure scars from railings and tools. Many have one leg fractionally stronger than the other from living through a long neighborhood tilt. They can sleep while a room changes angle. They wake when it stops.

They do not measure age only in years. Someone may say she was born before the East Kilns lifted, apprenticed during the Three-Day Drainage, married in the winter when the red tram route folded under the tannery, widowed after the settling of the ninth reservoir. These are not catastrophes in the telling. They are strata. A life accumulates not as a straight path but as a sequence of adjustments made and survived.

The city attracts particular kinds of outsiders: engineers who discover that calculation here must remain humble, refugees for whom impermanence feels honest, gardeners interested in plants that grow without soil staying put, and people who have lost property elsewhere and no longer wish to love anything that pretends it cannot be taken. Some stay because the city makes room by deforming. A workshop narrows; an upper floor acquires an annex; a market shifts over three streets; a household absorbs another bed. Residence permits are tied to contribution, kinship, and anchor access, but no one can point to an unchanging parcel and say, absolutely, *this is mine*.

Ownership is therefore less like possession than maintenance. To own a room is to keep its joints oiled, its second exit clear, its pipes available to those downstream, and its load within the tolerance of whatever lies beneath. Neglect dissolves one’s claim faster than debt. A tenant who notices a new crack and marks it properly may have more authority in a building than an absentee proprietor whose name remains on an obsolete map.

Families are similarly structural. Some are formed by blood, some by apprenticeship, some because several households share the same thermal loop and have spent too many winters regulating one another’s heat to remain strangers. The common domestic unit is called, in translation, a bearing: the group of people expected to distribute one another’s burdens. A bearing may occupy adjoining rooms or be scattered through a pump house, a greenhouse roof, and two apartments reached through a loom hall. Members inherit obligations before objects. They inherit inspection rounds, burial anchors, mushroom shares, school escorts, rights to warm pipe walls, and responsibility for elderly neighbors when ramps become stairs.

Privacy exists, but it is made rather than assumed. Pipes pass through walls. Voices travel in metal. A family argument can emerge faintly from a radiator in a clinic across the block. People hang felt baffles and grow thick moss in interior grilles. Important conversations take place while walking outdoors, where the city’s countless small vibrations break speech apart. Lovers seek places beside presses, tram junctions, and drainage falls—not for romance in the usual sense, but because there they cannot be overheard through the structure.

Courtship often begins with small acts of reliable adaptation. Someone resets another person’s crooked door without making a ceremony of it. Someone remembers that a corridor has lost a step and reaches back in the dark. Paired plumb lines at weddings are not symbols of perfect uprightness. Each hangs separately. The promise is to keep observing the same changes and to tell the truth about what is leaning.

Their beauty standards follow the same principle. They admire ease under altered conditions: a person who can dance on a settling platform without anticipating every movement, a face that remains open during a warning pulse, hands capable of delicate repair while the workbench drifts out of level. Clothing is patched visibly, often with contrasting structural cloth. A good patch should show where strain occurred and how it was answered. Concealed damage is shameful. Mended damage may be ornamental.

This does not make them endlessly patient or communal. District rivalries can survive long after the districts themselves have exchanged streets. People steal heat, falsify load records, redirect seepage toward neighbors, and move anchor markers at night. Some landlords deliberately let access routes become difficult, then claim a room has ceased to be habitable and evict its occupants. Smugglers exploit jurisdictional shifts, waiting for a warehouse wall to cross a legal boundary without moving a single crate.

The most serious crimes involve false stability: painting over strain marks, damping warning bells, locking a cellar exit, disguising young crust as tested pavement, or certifying rigid work where flexibility was required. Such acts are treated harshly because their victims may be distant and unknown. A badly fixed beam can carry force through the web and injure someone six streets away.

Their courts must therefore reconstruct not only what happened, but where. Proceedings begin with maps dated to the minute. Witnesses identify anchor points, pipe tones, light codes, prevailing tilt. Judges sit with surveyors and neighborhood maintainers. Verdicts sometimes assign no prison term but impose years of public inspection duty: the offender must become responsible for noticing the sort of danger they chose to hide.

Many residents work in trades that would elsewhere be considered obscure branches of engineering but here are as ordinary as baking. There are listeners who diagnose foundations by placing the jaw against walls; route shepherds who walk ahead of mobile markets and negotiate passage; stair correctors; hinge fitters; seepage clerks; map mourners who archive streets after they cease to exist; and room midwives who attend buildings during major deformation, removing furniture, loosening partitions, and helping inhabitants re-enter once a new shape has settled.

Those who tend foundation animals occupy an uncertain status among technician, physician, and priest.

They are called softwrights. They work without sharp tools whenever possible. When one of the creatures rises beneath a construction site, the softwrights wash clay from its exposed surface and wait to see how it responds to air. No two are exactly alike. Some resemble flattened limbs, others folded mats of pale tissue threaded with stone. A few contract when touched. A few slowly orient toward boilers, kitchens, crowded sleeping rooms. Their keepers chart these preferences, though they avoid saying the creatures think.

Children do not share this restraint. They give them names.

A foundation animal incorporated into a home becomes part pet, part ancestor, part load-bearing uncertainty. Residents leave warm basins near exposed ridges of its body. They stroke places where the mineral skin remains flexible. They blame it, affectionately or not, when cupboards narrow and bedrooms develop curved corners. Some animals produce slow sounds below hearing that cause shared dreams in the households above: tunnels full of summer air, white roots moving through darkness, a pressure like memory without images.

Whether the creatures create the ground or merely rise with it is the central unanswered question of the basin. The citizens argue about this in canteens, schools, laboratories, and municipal chambers. Some believe the animals are organs of a single vast life beneath the plain. Some say they are temporary arrangements of mineral fiber, no more singular than eddies in water. Others insist they are the city’s first inhabitants and that all construction is, legally and morally, a form of tenancy.

The animals themselves offer no ruling. They harden, bear weight, and flex.

The human inhabitants are less comfortable with the eels. Foundation animals can be built around; eels violate categories. They appear in sealed sumps, roof tanks, lift wells, condensation traps, sometimes in the shallow bowls left overnight by subsiding floors. They are always healthy. Their stomachs are usually empty. Attempts to mark them fail because marked individuals disappear, while unmarked eels appear in the same container minutes later.

Maintenance workers have developed etiquette around them. If an eel is found, machinery is stopped, lights are lowered, and a wet route to the nearest drain is improvised. No official regulation requires this. It is simply done. To handle one roughly is believed to invite plumbing failures, though the statistical office has repeatedly found no evidence. The statistical office’s lower archive has flooded four times.

The hares in the cable galleries are tolerated because they chew only obsolete insulation, or seem to know which insulation is obsolete before the engineers do. Foxes are admired as superior surveyors. People watch their roof routes after a settling and copy them. Soot birds are less beloved. Their colonies can choke ventilation towers, but removing them all at once changes airflow and sometimes causes warm chambers to cool too quickly. So the flocks are thinned, redirected, bargained with through food and baffling. A district’s dawn is often the sound of thousands of mineral-dark wings stirring inside its walls.

No creature here is simply wildlife. Every species becomes infrastructural, and every infrastructure begins, eventually, to behave like a habitat.

The dead remain part of this arrangement. Burial is less a withdrawal from the city than a change in duty. Bodies are wrapped in structural cloth and placed in low reinforced beds beneath cemetery gardens. Mesh spreads outward from each grave, binding soil, paths, nearby walls, and sometimes tram supports. Names migrate when markers do. Families visit an anchor reference, not always the visible stone.

It is common to say of a dependable person that they will hold a whole street after death.

There are disagreements about whether this is dignified. Some choose furnace reduction and incorporation into ceramic ducts instead. Their ashes become part of the glazed inner surface through which drinking water or warm air passes. Others prefer the reed systems, where nutrients move through roots, fish, laundries, and gardens until no one can say where the body ended. The city offers no clean separation between remembrance and reuse.

Religion, where it exists, attends to emergence more than creation. Its shrines are temporary frames erected around things coming up: an unknown root, a buried machine, a wall of clay carrying ancient-looking insects, a set of rails that no archive records laying. Devotees do not ask first what these things mean. They clean them and watch. Their central discipline is withholding explanation until the object has had time to become itself.

This patience is not universal. Many people have no interest in mystery. They work ten-hour shifts correcting ceramic channels, collect children from strain class, argue about cabbage allotments, and curse when the bathroom door turns oval again. The extraordinary becomes domestic quickly. A pale creature flexing in the pantry wall is less important than whether it has pinched the hot-water line.

Humor is dry, local, and based on delayed consequences. A person leaving for the night says, “Keep the room where it is.” A shopkeeper whose entrance has lifted beyond reach hangs a sign below it: OPEN, CONDITIONS PERMITTING. When two agencies dispute jurisdiction over a migrated corridor, residents paint a third boundary and declare themselves an independent stairwell. Children tuck tiny civic flags into new ridges before surveyors can stake them.

Music favors vibration. Much of it is felt through benches, floors, and rails rather than heard. Ensembles play entire districts by striking tuned sections of disused pipe while listeners sit in connected buildings. Because the structural web alters with temperature and settlement, the same composition cannot be performed twice. At festivals, tram service pauses and the lines become long instruments. People place gloved hands on them and feel rhythms arrive from beyond the visible horizon.

Their dances are quiet as well. Feet slide, test, withdraw, and land. Partners exchange balance without locking arms. Older forms include moments when everyone stands motionless while the platform moves beneath them. Outsiders often miss the instant when the dancing begins.

At the Unmade Square, the citizens become most unlike the practical image they present. They gather at the railings before work, after school, during meal breaks. Surveyors watch instruments; growers release spores; children wait for insects; philosophers wait to be disproved. Nobody owns what rises there, though many institutions have attempted to claim authority over it.

The children are the square’s closest students. They name each brief country and argue over its borders. They track streams with bits of thread, build bridges from matchwood, rescue pale insects from floods no wider than a hand. Adults warn them not to touch the surface, but the warning is inconsistently enforced. Most adults remember touching it themselves.

Now and then a child claims to have seen people in the miniature landscapes.

Not tiny people exactly. Upright disturbances. Processions of grit moving against the water. Clusters gathered beneath moss fronds. Marks along a ridge like foundations for rooms too small to enter. By the time an adult looks, rain has rounded the marks or the whole country has folded under.

The official explanation is scale-seeking: the human mind mistakes random arrangements for habitation. The unofficial response is to place the child’s drawing in the square archive, alongside thousands of others.

The city’s inhabitants do not consider themselves temporary. This may be their deepest distinction. They know no wall is fixed, no route final, no river faithful, no map innocent of age. Still they make preserves, teach old songs, keep appointment books, plant pear trees, and quarrel over inheritances. They undertake works intended to last for generations, though lasting here never means remaining unchanged.

To endure is to continue participating.

Perhaps this is why the city itself sometimes seems inhabited by something larger than its citizens but composed of them: not a mind beneath the plain, not the Broad Works as a literal organism, not the foundation animals joined in secret thought, but a distributed creature made of mutual correction. Its nerves are rails and handrails. Its slow muscles are pumps, roots, and buried structural cloth. Its memory is held in dated maps, scars, obsolete doors, cemetery mesh, and the unconscious gait of people crossing uncertain ground.

At night, when a warning bell sounds and a block begins to realign, windows brighten in sequence. Residents wake, dress, unlatch second exits, check pipes, lift sleeping children, move tables away from narrowing walls. Softwrights descend. Tram crews isolate the lines. Foxes leave the roofs before anyone sees why. In the dark under several buildings, a foundation animal bends by a fraction.

No one commands the whole response. It passes from room to room through light, scent, pressure, and practiced touch.

By dawn the block has changed shape. A bakery opens through what was yesterday its storeroom. Two families discover they now share a landing. A school loses one gate and gains access to a greenhouse stair. People complain, measure, negotiate, carry kettles across the new connections.

Then they begin the work of living there, which is also the work of keeping the place alive.
