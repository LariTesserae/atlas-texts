No creature of the High Green lives alone.

Even the great growth itself is inhabited down to its smallest functioning parts. Its clear sap carries pale swimmers that trim clots from the vessels and gather wherever tissue has been bruised. Threadlike worms graze the walls of its water chambers. Blind, translucent crustaceans drift through internal cisterns, feeding on pollen washed down from the surface. In the warm fermenting dark beneath them, soft colonial things cling to the roots and shine blue when disturbed.

Whether these are parasites, organs, or guests is impossible to say. Remove some of them and the plateau sickens. Allow others to multiply and whole districts blacken.

The High Green is not one mind in any way a person would recognize. It does not look toward danger or remember an individual wound. Yet injury travels through it. Pressure in one district changes the opening of pores a hundred miles away. A burned scar may cause distant masts to remain closed for a generation. When rain touches the eastern rim, buried vessels begin tightening in the west before any water reaches them.

Its awareness, if awareness it is, moves slowly except when it does not. A settlement can stand safely for eighty years and then be rejected in an afternoon.

The people who live upon it call themselves, in several languages, by names that mean approximately **the Light-Footed**, **Those Above the Breath**, or simply **Guests**.

They are lean, broad-toed people, often long-limbed from a life of climbing platforms and crossing springy ground. Children learn balance before distance. Their walk has a peculiar rolling care: heel scarcely touching, weight tested and withdrawn, each step listening through the sole. Outsiders stamp because they expect earth. The plateau-born place their feet as if greeting an animal that may be asleep.

Most wear layered wraps of woven fiber, loose against the heat and tightenable when the evening swarms emerge. The outer cloth is stained with resins that repel siphoners but attract small blue mites; the mites eat the larvae of more dangerous parasites and are tolerated in hems and hair. Travelers often carry living colonies of them in perforated beads at the neck.

Their masks are their most distinctive possessions. These are light frameworks of rib and chitin covered with replaceable gauze. They keep out seed dust, biting clouds, and the invisible irritant released when the ground is frightened. A mask may be plain, but many bear long cheek vanes or painted throat patterns modeled after harmless flying animals. Such decoration is practical as well as beautiful. Predators that strike at familiar silhouettes sometimes hesitate long enough for a person to drop flat.

A household is rarely more than a few related families sharing platforms around a hardened scar. Their dwellings are always unfinished. Supports can be shifted. Walls can be folded. Roofs can be cut loose and made into sleds or sails. Water vessels hang rather than stand, because a vessel resting in one place too long may be overgrown, tipped, or slowly swallowed.

Possessions are judged by whether one person can lift them.

The elderly carry the heaviest things: memory, routes, obligations, and the names of changes in the ground.

A route-name is not merely a direction. It may encode the season when a ridge becomes soft, the pitch of insects near drinkable water, the smell of a predatory flowering, and which mast should appear on the left at sunset. A skilled route-keeper can speak for an hour without repeating a landmark. Since paths alter, these recitations are revised after every journey. A route remembered too rigidly is considered more dangerous than no route at all.

Their maps are songs.

Low notes describe broad folds. Clicks mark seams. A held breath means exposed country with no shelter. Sudden changes in rhythm warn that the ground responds to vibration. Children practice maps at dusk, when their voices are almost lost in the chorus, beating the contour against their own ribs.

They do not believe the evening sound belongs to one species. To them it is an assembly.

The first voices are made by seam-clickers: thumb-sized animals with flat bodies and one enlarged limb that strikes the plate beneath them. They emerge in their millions to claim tiny feeding territories. Then come the reed-throats, soft green creatures that inflate sacs along their sides. Burrow drummers answer from below. Winged glassbacks contribute a pitch too high for many adults to hear but painfully clear to children.

Larger animals enter last.

The long-legged grazers send pulses through the living ground by flexing hollow structures in their feet. These animals are called **dawn shears** in the western settlements. They stand twice the height of a person at the shoulder, though most of that height is leg and arched neck. Their heads are narrow, lidless, and fringed with sensory threads that taste fresh growth. At night the plateau raises a fine nap of nutrient-rich filaments. The shears move into position before dawn and harvest it in long, precise swaths. By full daylight the crop has withdrawn or dried.

Dawn shears cannot easily rise after falling. They sleep upright, locking their joints, and dislike ground that sways under wind. A startled herd does not stampede together; each animal chooses a different direction, opening across the plain like fingers. The very old travel alone. Their backs become gardens of moss, ticks, mites, nectar bulbs, and little hook-footed scavengers that spend their whole lives on one host.

The people follow shears without domesticating them. A herd reveals safe ground and impending weather. Condensation can be gathered from a resting animal’s flanks if approached beneath its field of vision. Shed mouthplates provide blades finer than stone. When a shear dies, however, no one approaches until the plateau has taken its share. The ground beneath the carcass may remain hungry for days.

Other grazers seldom touch the surface at all. Sailbacks ride thermals above the plateau, descending only to drag comb-like mouths across the tops of flowering stalks. Their young are born in the air inside membranes that unfold as they fall. During calm weather adults gather around the bladder towers, hooking themselves to the ribbed walls until the wind returns. Seen at dawn, hundreds may hang there with their sails folded, like dark fruit.

The bladder towers have inhabitants of their own. Their translucent interiors are warm, humid, and threaded with ladders of tissue. Needle fliers puncture them to lay eggs. Round-bodied groomers seal the punctures afterward and eat any egg placed too deeply. Long snails travel inside the walls, visible only as shadows. Some towers remain clear; others become clouded with entire food webs and eventually collapse under the burden.

Among the surface multitudes are the red carriers. Each is no larger than a finger joint, but their roads can be wider than a person’s outstretched arms. They transport wax, carrion, spores, dry foam, and the eggs of other species. Their colonies are distributed across many miles rather than gathered in a single nest. One seam houses larvae, another breeders, another the pale swollen bodies that store water. Chemical messages pass continuously between them.

The carriers maintain certain wounds in the plateau instead of allowing them to heal. Around these wounds grows a sweet scar tissue harvested by both insects and people. Destroying a carrier road is therefore not a small act. It may dry a cistern, starve a settlement, or leave a district vulnerable to boring grubs. Travelers step over the roads. If the file is too broad, they wait.

The most feared burrowers are rind whales, though they are no true whales and seldom exceed the length of three people. They move within the upper tissue by forcing the layers apart, leaving a slow ridge on the surface. Their bodies are slick, boneless, and ringed with cutting mouths. Usually they consume dead tissue. During drought they rise toward vibration.

A traveler who sees a ridge turning to follow does not run. Running excites both burrower and ground. Instead, one throws a resonant peg as far as possible and strikes the surface once. The whale follows the answering tremor. If the peg lands in sensitive tissue, the plateau may close on it, and sometimes on the whale as well.

Predators exploit every such entanglement.

The stalk mimics are perhaps the easiest to recognize, which is not to say they are easily recognized. They stand rigid for days, narrow-bodied and ribbed, with folded limbs resembling dry floral sheaths. Genuine stalks lean slightly toward the prevailing wind. The mimics lean toward traffic. Their blossoms are mouths seen from above. When a flock passes, they burst apart into jointed hooks.

More deceptive are the shade-bearers. These low, broad animals cultivate living mantles on their backs. Their moss is cool. It smells of damp shelter and produces edible nodules. Small creatures gather underneath, especially at noon. The predator does not hurry. Its edges lower so gradually that the gathering prey continues feeding until daylight has narrowed to a line.

Old shade-bearers become almost charitable. Too slow to hunt effectively, they shelter more life than they consume. People sometimes recognize individuals and rest near them, never beneath. After death, their moss mantles may survive and spread, becoming islands of true soil.

Gall-tongues live longer and are less forgiving. Their bodies fill the cavities of ancient galls, conforming so perfectly to the interior that dissection reveals no clear shape. Only the feeding filament emerges. It may extend for hundreds of paces, green at first glance, complete with false leaves and beads of counterfeit dew. It catches beetles, fliers, and sometimes a human hand. Cutting the tongue is useless unless one can do so before it contracts.

The plateau’s human inhabitants test uncertain vines with a tuning fork. Plant tissue gives back one note. Flesh gives another.

They also share the High Green with people who are no longer entirely surface dwellers.

At breathing pits, where heat and mist rise from inside, small communities suspend themselves beneath the outer plates. Their platforms hang on fiber ropes in red-lit chambers. They collect condensed water, cave wax, blind animals, and mineral nodules pushed up from the roots. Surface people call them **the Below-Faced**, because they climb with their bodies toward the dark and their backs toward the sky.

Generations in the chambers have made them pale and powerfully built through the shoulder. They navigate by echo and by changes in moving air. Many wear clicking plates on the tongue. They know the hidden water system better than anyone, but their routes are perilous: vessels tighten without warning, chambers flood during distant storms, and some passages are temporary spaces between muscles of the greater growth.

The Below-Faced say there are chambers so large that their ceilings make weather. They speak of warm rain, internal lakes, forests of colorless cilia, and root columns descending beyond the reach of dropped lights. They say something grazes those roots from underneath.

Surface people treat this last claim with respectful doubt.

No one doubts the pale hunters of the upper air. These six-limbed creatures circle beyond ordinary fliers, transparent except for their eyes and the dark cords of their digestive tracts. They hunt by falling through swarms with their membranes furled, opening only near the surface. A single pass can leave a trench of stunned insects.

They are drawn to flame because they descend from ancestors that fed around lightning wounds. Fire means injured tissue; injured tissue means rising prey. Thus when a settlement’s fire cup cracks, the first danger is not burning. It is the whitening of the sky as hunters turn their bodies toward the light.

Occasionally one of these creatures is found alive after a storm, its membranes tangled in the ground hairs. Killing it is forbidden in many settlements. It is shaded, wetted, and released from a high mast if possible. The hunters travel farther than any person, and ornaments have been found embedded in their transparent flesh: foreign pollen, salt crystals, slivers of blue stone, once even a worked metal ring.

During flowering years, distinctions between resident and migrant disappear.

Creatures rise from sealed chambers that have been quiet for decades. Eggs hatch in old baskets. Dry shells split along riverbeds. The sky fills with animals no living route-keeper has named. Some breed once and die within hours. Others arrive on the torn canopies from distant regions and remain after the bloom, founding strange little populations that may endure for centuries.

The people gather beneath the masts but do not celebrate without fear. Flowering means water, food, courtship, trade, and renewal. It also means that the High Green is rearranging itself.

Settlements exchange children of marriageable age, tools, songs, mites, preserved sap, and news. The Below-Faced emerge in numbers. Sailbacks descend low enough to touch. Dawn shears stand together in great silent groups while the canopy opens above them.

Then comes the overwhelming chorus, in which every living thing seems to occupy a necessary interval.

The people sing too. Their part is small and may not matter. They sing route-songs altered into gratitude, death songs with no names in them, and long pulsing tones copied from the grazers. They beat the hardened scars with padded rods and feel answers travel away beneath their feet.

Some insist the answers return.

Not immediately. Not in a form that can be repeated. Yet, after a human rhythm has been sounded for many nights, a breathing slit may begin opening to the same measure. Seam-clickers may shift their cycle. A distant district may flower in a line resembling the pattern beaten into the ground.

This is why the wisest inhabitants hesitate to say they live *on* the plateau. The burrowers live in it. The fliers live from it. The red carriers maintain it. The grazers prune it. The people wound, tend, listen, and remember. The High Green shelters and consumes them all.

They live in one another’s habits.

And beneath every footfall, wingbeat, feeding cut, birth pulse, and evening song, the ancient growth continues its immeasurably slow life—neither landscape nor god, neither host nor beast, but the vast participant to which every smaller creature is, for a little while, an answer.
