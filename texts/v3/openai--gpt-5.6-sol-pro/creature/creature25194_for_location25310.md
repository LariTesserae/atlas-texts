The inhabitants are difficult to divide into creatures, colonies, and processes. On the plateau, an individual is often only a temporary arrangement: a shell joined to its neighbors for a wet season, a membrane extruded by something buried far below, a mobile organ belonging to a colony kilometers away. Life does not rise conspicuously from the land. It lies against it, enters it, and borrows its forms.

The most numerous are the **shale-backs**.

A shale-back is a flat animal made of nested plates around a soft, pressure-filled core. Seen from above, it resembles a palm-sized flake of dark stone. Its front and rear are not obvious. It can reverse the direction of its organs over several hours, turning what had been a feeding edge into an excretory one and proceeding without rotating its body.

Beneath each plate is a fringe of microscopic drinking tubes. At condensation, the tubes emerge and sweep moisture from the rock. Other mouthparts rasp at lichen and collect metallic dust. Much of what a shale-back consumes is not food in the ordinary sense but material for maintenance: silica for shell, iron for charge-bearing blood, clay to seal cracks, trace salts for the ceramic tissues of its joints.

Alone, it is nearly insensible. Its nervous system can distinguish pressure, dampness, slope, electrical potential, and the chemical presence of its own kind. In a mass, however, shale-backs become something more elaborate. Their overlapping shells transmit pulses from one body to the next. A disturbance at the edge of a colony travels inward as a slow tightening, branches around damaged areas, and returns with information altered by every animal it crosses.

The colony does not think quickly. It may take an afternoon to notice a shadow and several days to decide that a channel is drying. But it remembers routes longer than any member lives. Migration knowledge resides in the habitual alignment of millions of bodies, in chemical residues laid beneath them, and in pressure-sensitive eggs buried along the way. If a generation is destroyed, those eggs hatch facing the old direction.

Their young begin almost liquid. They seep into cracks as translucent films, feeding on mineral bacteria until enough shell material has accumulated around them. Only then do they climb out and join the moving surface. The elderly become increasingly stonelike. Their plates thicken, their internal chambers narrow, and eventually they cease to travel. Colonies pass over them. Lichen enters their joints. In time, there is no useful distinction between the body and the shale beneath it.

On the cliff faces live the **pale fans**, animals shaped by the danger of projecting into the wind.

Their main bodies are narrow sealed cords, chalk-white or faintly blue, with dozens of limbs pressed close along their sides. Each limb divides into soft adhesive fingers sensitive enough to find pores in the stone. They spend dry periods folded under ledges, layered together like roots exposed by erosion.

Moisture changes them. Membrane combs unfurl from their backs, turning whole escarpments feathery. The combs strain fog, charged dust, spores, and the minute airborne young of other species. Fluids swell through the animals’ bodies; dormant organs begin working; reproductive chambers open. For a few hours they are almost lush.

When the rock dries, they detach in groups. Their bodies flatten, internal bladders expand, and the many limbs lock into a broad framework. They glide without beating or calling. A cliff may release a million at once, yet from below the event appears to be no more than a pale alteration of the air.

They do not choose destinations individually. Each feels the electrical tension of its neighbors through the thin atmosphere, and the flock continually corrects itself. Those at the edges fold or extend a limb; that minute change propagates across the group until the entire living sheet turns. Sometimes they cross a valley and find no damp stone. They cannot climb again after landing. Such failed migrations leave drifts of white bodies at the foot of a wall, where scavenging films dissolve them over decades.

Higher still are the **sky-rings**.

Each is a translucent sac surrounding a torus of muscular tissue that rotates continuously while the animal is active. The ring is neither a wheel nor an organ driven around an axle. Waves of contraction travel through it, giving the appearance of rotation and moving gases through layered internal chambers. There, atmospheric compounds are separated, concentrated, and stored. Charge gathered from cloud and aurora sustains much of the process.

A solitary sky-ring is unstable and shorts itself in turbulent air. For this reason, they travel in chains. Conductive tendrils link animal to animal, distributing charge and adjusting buoyancy. Some chains contain only dozens of bodies; the oldest may extend beyond sight.

Their relationships are provisional. Individuals join, separate, exchange stored gases, transfer embryos, and sometimes digest weakened neighbors without interrupting the chain’s passage. There is no permanent head. Leadership moves toward whichever body currently has the clearest electrical gradient. In a changing wind, this role may pass along the chain like a traveling spark.

They descend during the continent-wide wettings. Threads lower from them and touch ridge tops, carrying charge into the ground. Shale-backs crowd around these points. Buried organisms open conductive tissues. For a few hours, sky and stone become parts of one circuit.

The organisms beneath the silica domes are less easily named. What appears to be one **refinery colony** is an alliance of several forms.

Deep siphoners draw warm brine upward from fractures. Membrane animals separate dissolved substances. Tubular grazers patrol the channels and consume blockages. Weaver organisms take carbon-rich discharge and pull it into black insulating fiber. Small six-limbed carriers distribute pellets between chambers, guided by flavor, field strength, and inherited sensitivity to mass.

No member understands the whole. Indeed, no member can survive the whole. The hot brine that feeds the siphoners would kill the weavers; concentrated sulfur lethal to the carriers is required by the membrane animals. Their interdependence has become architectural. Each generation precipitates new silica partitions, seals old chambers, redirects flow, and leaves behind a structure that constrains the behavior of the next.

The dome itself is dead secretion, but not merely shelter. Its thickness regulates heat. Its ribs conduct vibration. Tiny differences in curvature determine where condensation gathers and where eggs can remain unfrozen. A mature colony has a shape produced by thousands of generations of blind correction. Damage one side, and for centuries the organisms inside will alter their secretions until the temperature and pressure gradients are restored.

Among the most visible workers are the **pellet bearers**, though each is scarcely longer than a finger. They carry condensed mineral nodules in ventral baskets and travel the same lanes throughout their lives. Their route may seem absurdly indirect, bending around a stone that eroded away ten thousand years before. They retain the bend because the path is not learned from landmarks. It is encoded as a sequence of pressures, inclines, and magnetic changes transmitted through their eggs.

They do not know whether a pellet contains iron, sulfur, carbon, or waste. Sorters at the destination determine that by swallowing it. Inside a sorter, rows of muscular chambers vibrate at different frequencies. A pellet moves until its density resonates with a chamber wall, then is expelled through the corresponding duct. The sorter is less an animal carrying a machine than an animal whose digestion has become classification.

Across open flats move the **dust barrels**. They are low, thick-bodied filterers with circular mouths and no fixed face. They advance by inflating chambers along their undersides, pressing themselves forward a few centimeters at a time. Air, grit, spores, shell fragments, and microbial crust pass through them. Useful particles are retained. Cleaned grains emerge behind in paired lines.

Their migrations can last centuries. A calf may be born on a route begun by its remote ancestors and die before reaching its seasonal end. Predators therefore do not chase them. The predators are embedded along the lanes.

These **waiting mouths** spend almost their entire existence underground. A mature one may have a body extending hundreds of meters through cracks, though only a small pressure-sensitive trap lies near the surface. It feeds perhaps once in thirty years. When a barrel passes overhead, the trap does not snap. Sudden movement would waste energy and warn the herd. Instead it adheres to the barrel’s underside and releases enzymes slowly. For months the prey continues traveling, carrying the predator with it. By the time it weakens, the predator has grown a new buried network along the route.

The largest inhabitants have no common size or final form. They are called **ravine bodies** only because ravines are where their presence can be recognized.

A ravine body begins as a colony of threadlike organisms around a geothermal seep. The threads fuse, mineralize, and divide labor. Some become armor; others become fluid conduits, thermal exchangers, electrochemical chambers, sensory cables, or anchoring tubes. Over thousands of years the colony closes across the gap above it. Sediment gathers on its back. Shale-backs migrate over it. Plants root in it. Entire refinery colonies may establish themselves in the warmth leaking from its joints.

Whether it remains a colony or becomes one animal is not a question the plateau answers. Its organs share fluids and genetic material. Injury to one region produces compensating growth in another. Yet sections can die, detach, and persist independently for centuries. Two ravine bodies meeting underground may exchange tissues and thereafter respond to pressure as one.

They perceive the world as load, heat, chemical flow, and strain. A footstep would mean nothing to them. A changing aquifer is a season; the settling of an escarpment is an event; the cooling of a deep fracture may be experienced as something like pain.

Their migrations are acts of anatomy performed upon the landscape. Feeding tubes withdraw from exhausted cracks. Anchors dissolve their own mineral bonds. Internal chambers flood or empty to shift weight. The body takes decades to produce a single effective contraction. During this time, organisms living on its back may complete hundreds of generations without realizing that their ground is moving.

A ravine body’s death can last millennia. Its rot warms a valley. Its conduits become rivers. Its armor collapses into terraces. Eggs and dormant tissues continue operating after coordination has ceased, so that portions of the dead creature pump, sort, and respond long after the whole has become geology.

Most life, however, is smaller than sight.

Pressure eggs are stacked kilometers deep in porous strata. Mineral films advance along crystal boundaries. Thread colonies inhabit the warm skins of buried stones. Organisms no larger than cells rotate magnetic grains into alignment and use them as communal sensory organs. Others remain dormant until stress fractures the rock, then bloom in the crack for the few hours before it seals again.

These hidden beings account for much of the continent’s metabolism. The visible animals are only the exposed tools of a deeper ecology.

There may be no mind on the plateau in the sense of a single creature looking outward and knowing itself. Yet the continent possesses memory. Migration lanes persist. Colonies compensate for damage. Electrical warnings cross valleys. Sky-rings, shale-backs, buried threads, and refinery domes alter one another’s behavior during the great wettings. Information is stored in shell orientation, channel shape, dormant eggs, mineral concentration, and the tension of living cables in stone.

Nothing commands. Nothing surveys the whole.

Nevertheless, when moisture comes, the inhabitants answer one another. The cliffs release their pale multitudes. The ground darkens and begins to travel. Domes open. Buried organs wake. Long chains descend from the cloud lid. Ravine bodies increase their deep, inaudible pulse.

For those few days, each creature becomes part of a coordination older than species. Then the water withdraws, the connections break, and the inhabitants return to their separate scales of time.

They fold down. They seal themselves. They become slopes, ledges, stains, dust, and weather.

And beneath that composure, they continue their work.
