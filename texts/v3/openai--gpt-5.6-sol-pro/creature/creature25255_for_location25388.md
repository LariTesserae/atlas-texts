The people of Bellmouth are difficult to distinguish from the ward’s equipment.

Seen outside their shells, they are broad, jointed silhouettes moving hand over hand along cables. Their suits are assembled from generations of incompatible manufacture: ceramic breastplates, brass locking rings, layered rubber, woven metal, pressure bladders patched with bacterial resin. Hoses loop close to the body so the currents cannot seize them. Tools are clipped flat against thighs and ribs. Helmets tend to be small-windowed and heavily ribbed, giving every worker the blunt head of some armored larva.

Nothing dangles. Nothing is ornamental unless it can survive being dragged through a turbine throat.

The oldest suits bear more metal than fabric. They have been inherited so many times that no single body fits them. Their wearers pack the empty spaces with gel and bind their joints until movement becomes a deliberate series of hinges. Families recognize these suits by dent patterns and weld scars. A helmet may have a personal name even when the person inside it does not.

In the inhabited shells, the people look smaller.

Bellmouth’s air is so dense that voices carry poorly and breathing is visible as labor. The residents have heavy chests, thick necks, and the habitual stillness of those who know every exertion changes the atmosphere around them. Their ears are often scarred. Many are partially deaf; nearly everyone suffers some private tinnitus, and total hearing loss is not regarded as a profound disability. Children learn pressure-sign before spoken language. They communicate by touches to the wrist, jaw, sternum, and the metal surfaces around them.

A conversation may pass through a table.

A warning may arrive through the floor before anyone knows who sent it.

Names have spoken forms, but the truer names are rhythms. Three quick taps and a drag. A firm pulse followed by two faint ones. A rolling knuckle pattern felt through a handrail. These signatures travel farther than voices and are less easily confused with machinery. The dead retain theirs. For years after a person is gone, friends may encounter an old automated signal or a loose valve beating against a pipe and stop because, for an instant, it has called them by name.

Most Bellmouth residents are descended from workers who were never meant to remain. Pressure engineers, separator crews, ore sorters, penal maintenance gangs, debt laborers, divers born in upper stations that no longer exist. The distinctions between those origins survive in customs nobody can fully explain. Some households shave their heads before a purge. Some refuse to eat anything taken from a westward current. Some weld a thin coin inside every door, payment for a rescuer or bribe for the pressure itself.

There are few conventional families. A household is defined by shared air.

Anyone assigned to the same pressure shell breathes the same recycled atmosphere, contributes moisture and microbes to the same filters, and depends upon the same failing seals. This intimacy is considered stronger than blood. To accuse someone of “breathing elsewhere” means more than disloyalty. It suggests they have shifted the burden of their survival onto strangers.

Infants are kept in warm suspension cradles because ordinary beds place too much strain on their developing lungs. They sleep floating in saline gel while pumps imitate the slow pressure changes of the ward. Before they can walk, they learn to brace. Adults test them without warning by shaking a wall plate or opening a narrow valve. A Bellmouth child reaches for a handhold before looking for the cause.

Their games imitate disaster. They race discarded bolts through model currents. They identify districts by vibration alone. They hide in empty condenser drums and send coded taps to be found. One game, forbidden and therefore common, requires a child to let go in an exterior training tank and trust the others to catch them before the return grille.

Adolescents make their first unshelled crossing along the Procession. They go in groups, but each must release the departure cable alone. For several seconds the current owns them completely. Those who return often claim to have felt the shape of the whole ward through their suit: every pump stroke, every intake, every distant collision conducted through the water. Adults do not contradict this. Bellmouth allows its young certain beautiful lies.

The working population is divided less by trade than by what kind of danger they have learned to ignore.

Valve listeners spend their shifts pressed against bulkheads, interpreting machinery through the bones of the face. They can tell bearing wear from trapped debris, a cracked rotor from an animal caught in an intake. Many have callused foreheads from leaning into sounding plates. In old age they continue to tilt toward walls, listening even in sleep.

Current riders tend guide cables and map the ward’s shifting flows. Their charts are not geometric. They are schedules of temperature, taste, vibration, and delay: iron-hot at second pulse; leftward pull after gate seven; dead pocket beneath the cracked drum. They are admired for an instinct that resembles recklessness. Some can cross the central water with no propulsion, folding their weighted limbs at precise moments and allowing themselves to be passed from current to current.

Scrapers remove the living mineral films from electrical wells. The work is delicate because the films are neither wholly biological nor wholly deposit. They grow in branching sheets, black at the base and cream along their conductive edges, and react to nearby voltage by contracting almost imperceptibly. Scrapers insist the oldest colonies anticipate their tools. They harvest them in sections, leaving enough behind to preserve the circuitry’s familiar load.

Sealwives and sealmen inspect inhabited shells. They are permitted to enter any room, interrupt any ceremony, wake any sleeper. They carry chalks that mark surfaces even in sweating air. A white circle means a flaw can wait. A cross means evacuation. A solid white patch means the compartment is already dead, though the people inside may still be speaking.

Then there are the reclaimers, who enter sealed homes after pressure has equalized.

They recover metal, records, tools, teeth, suit components, and the engraved strips on which households keep their histories. They never open an interior door directly. First they knock, not because they expect an answer, but because something inside may be resting against it under tremendous force. Reclaimers are regarded with a mixture of disgust and necessity. They know how every family arranges its private rooms. They know who hoarded oxygen cartridges, who maintained illegal dry cabinets, who died holding whom.

Bellmouth has no cemetery. Bodies are too chemically valuable and enclosed space too scarce. The dead are reduced in heated digesters, their recoverable salts returned to the communal systems. Bone minerals become filter media or ceramic patch. Metals from implants go back to workshops. A small portion of each person’s carbon is pressed into a black tablet and fitted into the household wall.

Funerals are transmitted through the infrastructure.

At the appointed time, the pumps alter phase by a fraction. The change passes through pipes and handrails as a low double pulse, followed by the dead person’s touch-name. Everyone who feels it is expected to stop and take hold of something. Thus grief moves through the ward physically, entering palms, teeth, scar tissue, and old fractures.

Not everyone living in Bellmouth is acknowledged in the registries.

There are drifters in the abandoned belts who no longer maintain air rooms. They inhabit suits continuously, sleeping in wall clamps and exchanging spent breathing cartridges at unattended stations. Their bodies have grown into their harnesses. Pressure sores calcify beneath seals; fingers become permanently curved around grips. They speak rarely, preferring impact codes improvised from pipe noise. Officially they are vagrants. In practice, they know routes no map records and are summoned whenever something must be reached through a broken district.

There are also the unopeners: inhabitants of old residential cylinders who sealed themselves generations ago and never resumed contact. Their windows remain fogged from within. Their pressure gauges tremble in narrow living ranges. Power continues to be drawn. Occasionally a fresh repair appears on an exterior seam. No one knows how many people remain inside, or what shape their society has taken. Messages sent through the hull receive only slow, repetitive taps, possibly language and possibly machinery.

Around all these human lives exists another, quieter population.

The blind shrimp are tolerated because they gather before certain kinds of pipe failure, clustering where warmth or chemistry changes by amounts too slight for instruments to register. Workers watch them as surface sailors once watched birds. A sudden exodus from a seep mouth can halt an entire shift.

The pale brick-worms are less welcome. They tunnel through refractory lining and pack the holes behind them with glassy secretions. In some furnaces this weakens the wall; in others, the wormwork is the only thing holding ruined masonry together. Maintenance crews argue over whether to poison them. Usually they postpone the decision until the worms decide it first.

The gelatinous fish in the central void are called thoughts. They gather near operating substations and orient toward pressure pulses. A person drifting alone may find one keeping pace just beyond the helmet lamp, folding whenever they fold, descending whenever they descend. Current riders say thoughts do not follow bodies but intentions. This is superstition, though experiments have been inconclusive.

The things that clog the intake screens are called veils.

A veil may be hundreds of meters across and almost without substance: clear membranes, lines of muscle, scattered knots of sensory tissue. Drawn against a grille, it becomes briefly visible as a trembling sheet full of dim internal sparks. Crews cut it free in sections. The pieces continue contracting for days and sometimes arrange themselves around warm cables.

Some believe the veils descend from the upper ocean. Others believe they are born in the ward from bacterial skins, metal ions, and stray electrical current. Scrapers have found patterns in transformer growths resembling immature veil tissue. They do not report this often.

And beneath everything is the knocker.

Bellmouth has many explanations for it. An ancient pressure engine entombed before the cavern was opened. A trapped plate of basalt flexing under the tides. A second ward, deeper and older, signaling upward through the rock. A living thing whose body is too large to enter the hollow, so that only one slow organ touches the cavern floor.

Children are told it sleeps while the turbines run. Valve listeners say this is false. Through sensitive plates, some claim they can feel the knocking at all times, hidden beneath the ward’s mechanical weather. It does not keep one rhythm. Over decades it has shortened the interval between blows.

A few listeners begin answering.

They do so privately, during maintenance pauses, striking the floor with hydraulic tools in patterns no shift schedule requires. Most deny it when questioned. Some disappear into lower service shafts soon afterward. Their suits are occasionally recovered, neatly clamped to the rock, empty and still sealed.

Whatever lives below has not yet entered Bellmouth in any form the ward can recognize.

But in a city where every name is a rhythm, regular knocking cannot remain meaningless forever.
