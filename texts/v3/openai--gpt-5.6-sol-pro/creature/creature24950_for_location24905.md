What lives there cannot be separated cleanly from what happens there.

The black crusts along the fractures are the smallest and most legible inhabitants. They are seldom thicker than soot, and when dormant they are nearly indistinguishable from the metallic tarnish left by the valley vapor. Under magnification, however, the crust is divided into innumerable compartments. Some are angular and mineral-walled; others flex slowly, as if their boundaries were films under tension. No compartment contains a nucleus, mouth, seed, or other recognizable center. Each is almost empty.

They do not spread toward warmth, nourishment, or one another. They spread toward events.

Hours before a fault opens, a dark branching pattern advances across the stone above it. Its finest lines arrange themselves along stresses too slight for instruments to detect. Where two future fractures will intersect, the crust gathers into dense black knots. Where a ridge will rise, it draws long parallel bands. Thus its colonies resemble maps, but maps of a country that does not yet exist.

The crust does not survive the event it predicts. When the seam opens, heat and violet radiance consume every visible part of it. What remains is a white filament sealed into the newborn rock. These filaments occur in layers through the plateau, each one preserving the shape of a former colony at the instant of rupture. Sometimes thousands lie inside a transparent cliff, one behind another, making pale thickets in the glass.

Whether these are corpses is uncertain. They may be the crust’s mature form.

During the intervals of absolute stillness, faint charges travel along the buried filaments. A cliff containing many of them may answer a distant cliff with a pulse of cloudy light. The exchange is very slow. One signal can take an entire pause to pass from the foot of a mountain to its summit. When movement resumes, the signaling ceases, though the last pulse may remain caught in a crystal point for days.

The translucent sacs appear afterward.

They rise from waxy salt in places where underground discharges have crossed a concentration of white filaments. At first each is only a clear bead. It inflates without drawing in the surrounding vapor, creating its own interior volume as the mineral skin expands. The largest stand waist-high, taut and faintly faceted. Inside them is neither gas nor liquid, so far as can be determined, but a pressure slightly less than absence. Dust placed against the skin jumps away.

A field of sacs does not form randomly. They appear in arcs, branching rows, and incomplete rings corresponding to buried colonies below. At irregular intervals their skins tighten in sequence. The contraction runs from one sac to the next like a thought passing through separate transparent heads.

They have no obvious means of reproduction. When they collapse, the iridescent skins adhere to the ground and are soon covered by fresh stone. Yet long afterward, black crusts often emerge along faults that pass through those buried skins. It is possible that crust, filament, and sac are successive phases of one life: the first anticipating change, the second enduring it, the third carrying something from the completed fracture into the next.

But this is only their visible anatomy.

The greater part of the organism—if it is one—is the interval it keeps empty.

This is most apparent in the roomlike formations. Before the walls of a chamber condense, dust is driven from a sharply bounded volume. Vapor will not enter it. Loose fragments striking its invisible surface turn aside, sometimes gently, sometimes with enough force to become embedded in distant rock. For a few seconds or several hours, a chamber exists without walls: a hexagonal exclusion in the open air.

These clear volumes have behaviors.

They orient. Their narrowest side turns toward regions of approaching pressure. They divide at constrictions, producing two smaller hollows that drift apart before mineral matter gathers around them. They join by extending passage-shaped absences toward one another. When several are present, they assemble into sequences of rooms, shafts, stairs, and courts. The geometry resembles architecture because architecture is another practice of preserving shaped emptiness inside mass. Here the emptiness comes first.

Once enclosed, it changes.

The new chamber becomes warmer than the rock around it. Its interior swallows sound, except for tones produced by nearby fractures. Those it strengthens and holds. A person entering such a space might hear the descending note of a broken escarpment days after the escarpment had vanished. In older chambers—old meaning perhaps a week—the accumulated tones overlap until they become a pressure on the teeth and the bones of the face.

No body waits inside. Nevertheless, the chamber is not vacant in the ordinary sense. Its emptiness has structure. Fine dust released near the center outlines folds, branching lobes, and narrow internal corridors that do not correspond to the surrounding walls. These patterns shift when distant mountains rise. When two chambers face each other across a basin, their internal folds often change together.

The chambers may be organs built around creatures made of exclusion. Or the chambers themselves may be temporary shells around portions of a single, plateau-wide inhabitant whose substance is not matter but the removal and arrangement of matter.

Its movements are visible only indirectly.

A sequence begins with a stair descending into solid ground. Days later, a door-shaped void opens on a ledge many kilometers away. Then a ring of upright slabs forms beyond the next ridge. During a period of stillness, the stair, door, and ring align—not by sight lines, but through the deep stone. A region between them becomes unusually quiet. Heat gathers on its highest points. Loose crystals turn toward its center.

When the pressure wave comes, the entire arrangement is destroyed. Yet a similar sequence appears elsewhere: ring, passage, terrace, chamber. It is tempting to say that something has traveled. Nothing crosses the intervening plain. No continuous body can be found. The arrangement alone departs and reappears.

Such a being would not inhabit locations. It would inhabit formation.

Its nearest equivalent to feeding may be the capture of a pressure front. Chambers gather along the path of a deep pulse, altering the walls that grow around them. Part of the pulse is diverted into filaments, crystal towers, and charged salt. Afterward the chambers are larger or more numerous. Its nearest equivalent to excretion may be the perfect black spires standing alone in broad basins: masses of hardened material from which all further responsiveness has been removed. Nothing grows on them. Fields bend around them. Even new crust avoids their bases.

Its nearest equivalent to memory lies in repetition. A six-step descent occurs again and again across the province, regardless of scale. Some examples are small enough for a hand; others descend by ledges hundreds of meters deep. Rings favor particular numbers of slabs. Passages meet at a limited family of angles. Terraces reproduce the outlines of chambers long since folded into the dark below. The forms are never copied exactly, but neither are they forgotten.

Perhaps each recurrence is an individual. Perhaps they are generations: brief geometries inheriting distortions from geometries that preceded them. A stair with six steps becomes one with six unequal drops; elsewhere that imbalance appears as six mountain belts, the third and fifth raised higher than the rest. What begins as a minute variation in a salt sac may eventually be expressed as a range.

There may also be predators, though not in any animal sense.

Some pressure waves arrive cleanly, announcing themselves far in advance. Others contain abrupt discontinuities—zones in which all alignment fails. Before these, the black crusts retract instead of spreading. Sacs flatten while still transparent. Wall-less chambers contract to points and disappear. When the wave passes, it leaves broad surfaces of smooth, unresponsive stone. Filaments caught within that stone go dark.

These dead regions expand over time, then are carried toward the plateau’s margin and folded into the depth. The inhabited formations seem to avoid them. New rooms face away from them; crystal forests lean from their borders. Whether the discontinuities are hostile organisms, diseases of stress, or merely conditions under which the local life cannot occur is impossible to determine.

At the outer precipices, the greater inhabitant appears to die most visibly.

As a district bends downward, its chambers open one after another. Their shaped absences emerge from the dissolving rock and hang briefly above the mineral haze. For the first time they can be inferred without walls: immense clear polyhedra moving through the dim atmosphere, outlined by dust and electrical veils. Some remain connected by long corridor-like distortions. Others separate and drift upward against the pull of falling stone.

Most collapse before reaching the higher currents. A few are caught.

There, among the peaks, the charged dust gathers around them. The airborne columns, arches, and hollow wheels are not always accidents of wind. At times they turn against the current. They pause above new fractures. They lower extensions toward the plain without touching it. Held together by opposed charges, clothed temporarily in mineral grains, the otherwise invisible volumes acquire something like bodies.

These are the nearest the country comes to visible animals.

They have no faces, but often a front: a narrower opening oriented toward whatever is about to rise. They have no limbs, though streamers divide and join beneath them. They do not fly by pushing against the air. Rather, they maintain a hollow pattern into which the air continually falls. Their passage is silent except when grains strike one another and produce a dry whisper like sand poured onto glass.

They travel until they encounter a young ridge. Then the dust flashes and enters the stone. Soon afterward black branching crusts appear along that ridge’s hidden stresses.

So the life of the plateau may be a circulation. It begins as a prediction written in black. It passes into white records inside rock, inflates as taut skins, clears rooms before walls exist, extends through arrangements of terraces and rings, and finally rises as enormous dust-clothed hollows. At the margin it is stripped again of substance and carried back across the province to enter new stone.

Or all these may be incidental expressions of something deeper.

The plateau itself shows several features of an organism. It maintains conditions unlike those beyond its margins. It produces repeated forms. It redirects energy into structures that aid further redirection. It preserves traces of prior states and uses them in subsequent growth. It sheds hardened districts into the depth while replacing them with responsive new ground. Its apparent architecture may be less like a city than like tissue: chambers as cells, passages as vessels, mountain chains as folds in an organ too large and too young to have a settled shape.

If so, the black crusts and translucent sacs are not residents. They are surface processes, as fleeting and local as a flush of skin. The dust forms are not creatures but gestures. The silent pauses are not peace; they are moments of integration, when changes spread across the whole lifted province before the next movement begins.

There is no builder because building is what the being does instead of breathing.

There is no witness because sensation, if present, lies in the way strain is anticipated across hundreds of kilometers. A forming ridge may be a reflex. A line of aligned chambers may be a thought that exists only until the next pressure wave revises it. The repeated empty rings may be attempts at some first stable boundary between self and world.

The country is newly arriving, and whatever lives there is newly arriving with it. It has not yet decided whether to be colony, landscape, mechanism, or mind. Its individuals do not persist long enough to acquire names. Its body is repeatedly assembled and withdrawn. It is born not into the warm darkness but from the act of giving that darkness form.

For now, it lives fastest in the instant before stone appears: in the cleared volume, the expected fracture, the room awaiting its walls.
