At first it is difficult to say where the inhabitants begin.

The pale films on the stone are alive. So are many of the stains beneath them. What looks like rust is sometimes a colony feeding on iron carried down in seepage; what looks like soot withdraws from a fingertip’s warmth. The green-black skins lining the stone cups are layered communities, their upper surfaces drinking the little light that reaches them while colorless organisms below consume whatever the water brings. During dry intervals they contract into lacquer-thin scales. At the first drop they soften, opening pores too small to see.

The chamber’s smallest animals graze these skins. Ivory beetles move over them with padded feet, leaving clean trails no wider than hairs. Glass mites crowd the grooves behind them. Minute snails rasp mineral and life together, building translucent shells that become cloudy with limestone as they age. Nearly all are pale, but not uniformly so. Their colors are those of things seen through water: diluted amber, old pearl, bruised blue, the faint pink of root flesh.

Sight is of limited use. Many creatures possess eyes only as dark flecks capable of recognizing the opening of a shaft or the passing shadow of a leaf. Others have lost them altogether. They read the hollow through pressure, heat, dissolved chemicals, and the tremors traveling through stone.

The translucent lizards beneath the leaves are among the most watchful. Their ribs and organs can be glimpsed through their skins, and the slow beat of the heart appears as a pulse of shadow. Their eyes are broad but poor at forming images. Instead, each lower jaw contains a comb of delicate bones resting against the leaf. Through these they feel beetles walking on the upper surface. They do not spring after prey. They wait beneath its path and open their mouths at the precise instant it passes overhead.

Their courtship is performed through warmth. During the wet season, males settle on opposite sides of a leaf and flush blood into branching vessels along their throats. A female below can feel the pattern as a succession of warm points. The most intricate patterns attract her without producing the slightest shake.

The moths use light. Their wings, folded against the limestone, resemble stains and mineral fans, but under the scales lie narrow organs that glow briefly when exposed. A courting moth opens one wing by a fraction, flashes a line of blue, closes it, waits, and repeats. The answering flash may come from only a hand’s breadth away. Their larvae live within the old insect turrets, feeding on root hairs that penetrate the walls. Before becoming moths, they plaster their chambers with luminous fungus. The emerging adults carry its spores in hollows between their scales.

Around the pools live the creatures most governed by the chamber’s pulse.

The thin-legged crabs are born during a wet interval and may reach maturity before the same water has vanished. Their bodies remain soft except for the claws, which are hard enough to cut chambers into drying mud. When the pools contract, each crab excavates a pocket, folds itself tightly inside, and seals the entrance with saliva and silt. Its heartbeat slows until hours pass between contractions. Some remain enclosed through several false rains, waking when moisture touches the shell of the chamber but refusing to emerge until the stone transmits the continuous patter of a true seep.

Blind fish wait farther down, in basins beneath the visible basins. They are long and narrow, with fin rays that spread like fingers over the bottom. In still water they scarcely breathe. When the channels connect, they become sudden and forceful, passing over terraces in depths barely sufficient to cover them. Their mouths are lined not with teeth but with soft inward-pointing bristles. They sweep up eggs, worms, drowned insects, fungal threads, and mineral flakes indiscriminately. For a few wet hours they are everywhere. Then they disappear into gravel so completely that the pools seem never to have contained them.

Some do not return in time. These strand themselves beneath the broad black leaves and bury their heads in silt. Their bodies secrete a clear casing that hardens against the air. Inside, the fish consumes much of its own muscle and waits as a living knot. The oldest casings have accumulated so many mineral layers that they resemble white stones. When water reaches them again, they split along a seam.

Higher in the vegetation, root-dwelling serpents lie wound around the warm amber nodules. They are thick, short-headed animals with scales fringed by moss. Algae grow in the spaces between those scales, and old individuals may carry ferns along their backs. Their tongues are divided into many fine strands. Rather than sampling the air, they lay these tongues against roots and taste whatever has disturbed them: the bitter trace of a lizard’s feet, the warmth left by a climbing crab, the stress chemicals released by a bent stem.

They strike only when they are nearly certain. A missed strike shakes leaves, and shaken leaves warn the whole garden.

Lower down are the mud-jawed hunters. They resemble salamanders only when moving. At rest they are little more than a pair of nostrils, a seam of mouth, and two dull eyes level with the silt. Their skulls are broad and thin, extending beneath the mud like buried plates. A worm passing nearby is felt across the entire jaw. The mouth opens downward as well as upward, and the disturbed mud closes over prey and predator together.

These hunters are not large, but they are old. Growth is slow in the dry intervals. Mineral deposits gather on their backs, preserving the scars of seasons as pale ridges. Moss takes root there. Tiny beetles shelter in the pits behind their eyes. One that has remained in the same wallow for years may be mistaken for a low, mossed stone until the rain begins.

Yet even these are not the most characteristic inhabitants.

Those are the animals that cannot be distinguished from the basin’s architecture until they choose to move.

They lie along the curved terraces, usually where seep lines cross. A mature one may be as long as three people lying head to foot, though no thicker than a fallen slab. Its upper surface is ridged and cream-gray, mottled with mineral deposits and pierced by openings that resemble the mouths of old insect galleries. Roots pass across it and sometimes through shallow channels in its hide. Mosses cover its back. Small animals use it as ground.

Beneath this stony surface is a soft, dark body arranged around a web of fluid-filled chambers. It has no single obvious head. Along one edge are several folds capable of opening as mouths; along another, a row of lidless pressure organs rests directly against the limestone. These organs are so sensitive that the creature feels a drop strike at the opposite side of the hollow. It knows the movement of fish through flooded gravel, the sealing of a crab’s mud cell, the fall of a moth, the footfall of a serpent descending a root.

Most of the time it does nothing.

It feeds on what settles over it: leaf rot, fungal growth, shed skins, insects careless enough to enter its pores. Cilia inside the openings carry this material inward. During drought its internal fluids thicken, and its body adheres to the terrace so completely that even a predator crossing it produces no response.

Rain changes it.

As water begins to move through the basin, the animal fills its chambers one by one. Its edges loosen. Mineral plates separate along old seams. With a slowness almost impossible to perceive, it raises part of itself and redirects the shallow flow. A channel that once entered gravel is dammed by a lifted ridge. Another opens beneath a folded margin. Water spreads over a nursery of snail eggs or drains away from a nest of mud-jawed young.

This does not appear to be deliberate in the manner of a builder tending a garden. The creature may simply be turning toward food, pressure, or dissolved minerals. But its movements have shaped the hollow for longer than any single root has lived. The curved terraces follow the positions where generations of these animals rested. Some of the chamber’s blunt “columns” contain their mineralized remains. The stream’s thirty-pace course is partly a history of their bodies.

They reproduce rarely. In the fullest seepages, two may extend pale fringes toward each other beneath the water. Where the fringes meet, they knit into a thin membrane spread across the stone. Weeks later, after the adults have withdrawn, the membrane divides into hundreds of flat young, each no larger than a fingernail. Most are eaten. A few attach themselves beside reliable drips and begin the long process of becoming indistinguishable from rock.

The smaller animals react to their stirring before any visible water arrives. Lizards flatten beneath leaves. Crabs break their seals. Fish turn in the deep pockets. Moths open their wings and show a single cold line of light. The whole basin appears to wake at the sound of the first drop, but it may be responding instead to the nearly imperceptible shifting of these buried listeners.

Nothing rules the hollow. Predators starve. Eggs dry. Roots strangle other roots, and fungi consume living tissue as readily as dead. But neither is anything entirely alone. The moth carries fungus. The serpent carries moss. The beetle cleans the luminous nodule that warms the lizard’s courtship leaf. The great terrace-creatures hold water in one season and become soil in another. Even their dead continue to alter where the pools form.

Thus the inhabitants tend toward forms that conceal themselves not merely from hunters but within the workings of the place. Shell becomes pebble. Skin becomes leaf. Egg becomes mineral blister. Sleeping fish become stones, and sleeping animals become terraces.

When the hollow seems empty, it is at its most crowded.

Every dry plate of mud contains folded legs. Every calcified wall has larvae feeding in its corridors. The black leaves shelter clear-blooded hunters. The pools that have vanished persist as damp chambers below the gravel, dense with waiting mouths. Under the mossed stone, vast thin bodies hold themselves still enough to feel the rain assembling far above.

Then the drop falls.

The stone cup receives it. The tremor passes outward through limestone, root, mud, shell, scale, and bone.

What opens throughout the basin is not one pair of eyes, but thousands—and beneath them, slower than sight, the terraces begin to breathe.
