What lives there is not a population so much as a continuous, many-bodied skin.

It begins on the basalt as a darkening. Spores settle in the mineral sweat around a crack; films of algae and bacteria take hold where the stone is warm but not hot, feeding on seepage, sulfur compounds, iron, and the little gray light that reaches the floor. From a distance these first inhabitants look like discoloration—olive smears, rust-red veils, a black gloss on black rock. Up close they are the notch’s oldest occupation: microscopic sheets dividing, dying, and sealing the raw lava beneath a film thin enough to be erased by one hard rain.

Moss follows. It grows not in delicate tufts but in dense, water-heavy pads, almost muscular in their compactness. The outer tips are green or bronze-green; the interior is brown, compressed, and sour with old growth. Each cushion is both plant and reservoir. It drinks fog directly, catches condensate, and holds it long after the exposed stone has stopped dripping. During dry intervals it closes upon itself and appears dead. With the return of cloud it swells within minutes, lifting grit and tiny basalt splinters as it rises.

The moss has no deep allegiance to any ledge. A pad may persist for years, thicken, and become a small suspended bog, only to peel loose under its own soaked weight. It falls as a single living slab. If it lodges lower down, much of it resumes growing there. In this way the inhabitant moves without walking: the notch repeatedly uproots it and teaches it to survive displacement.

Liverworts occupy the darker surfaces. They spread as slick, overlapping lobes along walls that receive no direct light, fastening themselves to mineral crusts too fragile to support anything larger. Their surfaces shine like wet skin. Fine cups and flaps catch spores and fragments, and rainfall scatters these propagules into neighboring seams. They are easily torn and almost indifferent to tearing. Every piece is a possible beginning.

Above them, wherever a joint offers a finger’s width of shelter, the ferns establish themselves. They are short, tough, and darker than lowland ferns, their fronds thickened against wind and cold. New croziers rise through the remains of fronds shredded only days before. The mature leaves are frequently split into streamers, their edges browned by sulfurous breath or bruised silver where blown grit has struck them. When downdrafts enter the notch, the whole fern layer overturns at once, exposing pale undersides crowded with spores. The gust strips those spores away and drives them into cracks, against wet walls, and sometimes upward toward the rim.

Some ferns root directly in moss. Others root in nearly nothing: a spoonful of mineral grit, a seam of softened ash, the fermented paste of old leaves. Their root masses are fibrous and communal. They bind stones, trap debris, and create the soil that later betrays them by becoming heavy enough to slide.

The shrubs and mountain bamboo form the largest bodies in the chamber, though none is permitted to become tall. They grow sideways, branch into the wind, and interlock until individual plants are difficult to distinguish. Their stems are scarred by mutual abrasion. Bark has been polished off at crossing points, exposing pale wood that hardens, splits, and is rubbed smooth again. Roots travel laterally beneath moss mats or enter cooling fractures in the lava. When a fissure warms, some roots die back from it; when it cools and begins to collect water, new roots advance.

These plants flower seldom. A flower here is small, brief, and almost private: a few dull petals held close beneath the leaves, protected from the weather. There are no insects to visit it. Fertilization, where it occurs, depends on wind, self-pollination, or chance contact between neighboring branches ground together in a storm. More often the thickets reproduce through persistence. Runners cross gaps. Stems bow against wet substrate and root. Broken branches lodge in moss and continue living. A single genetic individual may occupy several separated shelves, having been divided by rockfall and redistributed downslope.

Nothing browses these plants. Nothing nests in them. There are no bite marks, tunnels, eggs, droppings, feathers, husks, or shed skins. Leaves unfold without being eaten and age without being skeletonized. Spores rise into empty air. Seeds fall without being carried in fur or gut. The vegetation has no need for thorns, poisons meant for mouths, bright fruit, enticing scent, or conspicuous blossom. Its defenses are against pressure, abrasion, acidity, burial, cold, and sudden heat.

Because no animal crops it back, the living skin grows dense wherever the gases permit. Yet it does not become luxuriant in the ordinary sense. The notch itself performs the pruning. Wind tears leaves. Falling basalt severs stems. Sulfur burns pale lesions into fronds. Floodwater combs moss from the lower rocks. Root mats are buried, flipped, or drowned in temporary pools. Every plant is shaped by impersonal violence rather than appetite.

Fungi live beneath and within this greenery. Most reveal themselves only as threads in the cold mulch or as a faint whitening beneath bark. They penetrate the mats of fallen leaves slowly, working without assistance from worms, mites, beetles, or larvae. Decay is therefore thick and incomplete. Leaves remain recognizable for seasons, pressed into leathery black layers. Fungal cords stitch them together, draw minerals from the basalt dust, and enter into the roots of ferns and shrubs. In a terrain with almost no soil, these partnerships extend the plants’ reach into stone.

After several cool, wet days, fruiting bodies sometimes appear: minute gray fans against a dead stem, gelatinous knobs in a crevice, pale stalks no taller than moss tips. They are soft, transient things in a place of hard edges. Rain destroys many before their spores mature. Others release into a downdraft and vanish through the fissures.

The temporary pools have inhabitants too, though none can swim or crawl. Their black water clouds gradually with microbes. Green films gather near the surface; colorless colonies spread below where volcanic gases bubble through. Around warmer inlets, threads of bacteria form cream, orange, or sulfur-yellow streamers that tremble in the current. When the pool drains, these collapse against the rock in papery stains. Some cells survive in damp pores until water returns. The pool’s community is assembled and extinguished over and over, its continuity stored invisibly in slime, spore, and stone.

Nearest the vents, life becomes sparse and zoned. Bare mineral surrounds each opening. Beyond it lies a ring of heat-tolerant films, then liverwort, then moss, then fern. The rings shift as the vent pulses. A fissure that was merely warm may exhale a killing concentration overnight, bleaching the nearest plants to straw. Months later, when its breath weakens, green advances over the dead margin again. There is no fixed boundary between the living chamber and the furnace below—only a border that contracts and expands.

Carbon dioxide pools along the floor, but the plants hold most of their working surfaces above it. Fronds stand on stiff stalks. Shrubs arch over hollows. Moss grows thickest on shoulders of stone and the upper faces of fallen blocks. Low leaves yellow when still weather traps the gas too deeply; higher ones remain intact in the moving air. The vegetation’s shape is therefore also a record of invisible depth. It marks where the notch can be breathed.

Taken separately, its inhabitants are small and vulnerable. Taken together, they form something remarkably tenacious: a suspended landscape that catches water, manufactures soil, braces loose rock, and reproduces through its own destruction. It has no central body and no single lifespan. One section can be scalded, another buried, another ripped from the wall, while the whole remains alive.

It does not answer the notch’s machinery with movement or noise. It answers with coverage.

After each fracture, a film appears. After each fall, spores settle. After each flood, torn stems root in the dam they helped create. Green closes over black stone—not permanently, never peacefully, but with the patient speed of a wound repeatedly trying to heal.
