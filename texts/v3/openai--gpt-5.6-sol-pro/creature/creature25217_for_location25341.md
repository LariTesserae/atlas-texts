What lives in Drainage Interspace 6-C lives thinly.

First there is the moss beneath the leaking flange: several black-green pads compressed against the concrete, dense as old velvet and no broader than coins. They occupy the one place where the water arrives faithfully but without enough force to tear them loose. Their world is the underside of a steel lip, the mineral face beneath it, and the perpetual shiver of spray between. They never dry. They never warm. Under the amber safety lamps they look almost black, but in the brief gray daylight from overhead a submerged green appears in them.

The moss grows by increments too small for the machinery to register. Turbines change load, gates open, purge lines thunder, and still its filaments advance into microscopic pits left by the concrete forms. Dust lodges there. Lime leaches from the wall. A faint skin of silt collects along the lower edge, then is scoured away during washdown. The colony survives by being low, close, and difficult for water to grip.

In the shallow channel lives a paler thing: not quite a mat, only an algal discoloration stretched along the route of a constant trickle. It is yellow-green where the water thins and gray where metal filings have settled into it. Most of the time it is nearly indistinguishable from a stain. Beneath the film, however, cells divide in the weak ration of light reflected down from the slit of sky. They inhabit a landscape measured in scratches, weld scale, and the tiny ridges of brushed stainless steel. A drop passing over them is a flood; a purge cycle changes their temperature like weather.

Behind the cable trunk are the court’s tallest permanent inhabitants: three grass blades rooted in almost nothing.

Their seed arrived anonymously, perhaps in the tread of a boot, perhaps in mud shaken from a tool case or washed from a worker’s cuff. A teaspoon of grit had gathered where the floor meets the wall—concrete dust, fibers, flakes of paint, windborne particles, and a little organic dirt carried in from elsewhere. In that accidental soil the seed opened.

The blades are long because the light is far away. They are narrow, pale, and weakly green near their tips, leaning out from behind the trunk as though trying to see the sky. When the exhaust fans surge, all three lie flat against the floor. When the pressure eases, they rise slowly, not upright but enough to resume their reach. Condensation beads on them until each blade bows beneath droplets larger than its own thickness. Sometimes a drop slides to the base and enters the tiny root mass. Sometimes it knocks a blade down.

They cannot spread far. Beyond their pocket is bare concrete crossed by runoff, warning paint, and the soles of maintenance crews. Their roots meet the floor almost at once. They have no season in the ordinary sense, only fluctuations in temperature, daylight, spray, and generating load. Their winter is a washdown. Their storm is the starting of a pump. Their night begins when cloud closes the overhead slot.

Smaller life almost certainly persists with them, though it has no visible bodies: bacteria in the wet seams, microscopic films around the sump, spores held dormant in protected pores. They live on scant dissolved minerals and on the minute debris brought in by people and water. They do not make the court into an ecosystem so much as prove how difficult it is to keep wet surfaces entirely lifeless. Their colonies are repeatedly thinned by heat, cold, detergent, abrasion, and sudden torrents. What remains occupies the depth of a fingerprint.

No animal makes a home there. Nothing hunts among the moss pads or nests above the channel. No wing crosses the gray opening. The lamps burn without moths. The sump receives water without larvae. Even scavengers would find nothing to eat but films too slight to matter, and no quiet interval in which to feed.

People enter, but they do not belong to the place. They arrive armored in bright waterproof fabric, helmets, gloves, boots, hearing protection, and tethers. They communicate with signs and touch, reduced by the noise to deliberate gestures. For a few minutes they become the court’s largest moving organisms: crouching at grilles, bracing against railings, wiping condensation from gauges. Their faces appear behind clear visors and vanish again. They remove growth where it obscures paint, dislodge the little accumulations from which growth might begin, and carry away anything loose enough to become dangerous.

Then the pressure door closes. Their warmth drains from the handrails. Their boot marks fill with spray and disappear.

What seems most alive after that is not alive at all.

The court breathes through its louvers. It sweats from its walls. Its pipes pulse, its joints bleed clear water, and its buried forces alter pitch like a huge animal settling in sleep. Indicator lamps blink without looking. The penstock expands and contracts by fractions, making the concrete shed sudden skins of water. Mist forms, travels, and collapses. The place has rhythms, reflexes, fevers, and a continuous deep voice, but no hunger and no awareness. Its apparent life is load translated into motion.

Against that immense counterfeit vitality, the actual inhabitants are almost absurdly slight: a dark cushion under a flange, a greenish smear in a channel, three blanched blades behind a cable trunk, and unseen cells holding to wet imperfections.

The machinery occupies the court completely. Life inhabits only its errors.
