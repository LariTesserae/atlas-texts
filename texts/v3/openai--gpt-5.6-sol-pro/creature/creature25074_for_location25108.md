At first it seems that the chamber is inhabited by condensation.

It gathers without arriving, swelling out of warm breath against the concrete until the ceiling is studded with clear, inverted pools. Each pool fattens around a grain of sand or a fungal thread. Some tremble for minutes before falling. Others remain all day, lenses through which the minute life of the ceiling appears enlarged: mites nosing through mineral paste, translucent eggs fastened in cracks, the jointed feet of crickets passing upside down.

The crickets are the chamber’s nearest thing to a populous nation. They are smoke-gray when dry and almost colorless when wet, with long antennae that test spaces before their bodies enter them. Their wings have dwindled to small metallic slips. When they raise them, the scales catch stray light and flash like filings of silver, but they cannot fly and seldom attempt to jump. A jump in this crowded place is too uncontrolled. Instead they pour along fractures, one after another, feet finding old footholds in the concrete.

They live by maps made of temperature and taste. One crack is known by the faint salt on its eastern lip; another by the warm pulse that travels through it when the pump moves. Nymphs learn these routes by following the antennae of adults. If a seam is blocked by new carbonate, the procession pauses for hours, touching the obstruction from every side. By the next condensation cycle, a deviation has been established. Within days it appears ancient.

Their eggs are inserted into soft mineral fans before those fans harden. Thus each new shelf of silt may contain a generation. When water returns, some eggs drown and some hatch; when the floor dries, the survivors emerge through pinholes and climb immediately toward the ceiling. Cast skins remain standing in the mud, empty and upright, and for a while are used as drinking vessels by mites.

The mites are everywhere moisture is about to be. They arrive before a droplet forms, feeding on invisible films around the cool spot. Pale as ground glass, they seem less like animals than moving imperfections in the surface. Their trails cross rust, shell, bone, plastic and living skin without distinction. A sleeping frog may wake with a pale ring of them grazing around one eye.

Beneath the rust scales live the isopods. They are dark red where iron stains their plates and ivory underneath. Whole families occupy a space no deeper than a fingernail, layered belly to back. The youngest fit between the legs of the older ones. When exposed, the mass does not scatter at once. It contracts. The outer animals fold inward, and for several seconds the colony becomes a single armored knot.

They feed on what the chamber has softened: mold-furred belting, root bark, cricket husks, paper labels dissolved to pulp. Rust enters their bodies with their food. Their droppings are tiny iron-rich pellets that roll downhill during each tremor and collect behind screws and insect cases. There they alter the drainage, and in this way the isopods build without knowing they build.

The water has its own inhabitants, but no pool keeps the same population for long. Threadworms write and erase themselves in the films. Flat, transparent larvae rest against the bottoms of bolt holes, breathing through tails as fine as drawn wire. Small crustaceans—colorless except for the dark green line of the gut—work at every fallen leaf. They begin at the edges, reducing it to a lace of veins, then sever the veins and carry pieces beneath stones.

The pale creeper feeds them regularly. It grows as though governed by a strict account: four leaves, never more. Its fifth leaf is the sentence of the first. The oldest darkens from the tip, loosens, and falls into the pool cupped by a bent plate below. Scavengers gather before it has settled. By dawn only a soft stain and a few copper-green fibers remain.

The plant’s roots have entered the coil so completely that wire and root cannot be separated without killing one or breaking the other. New rootlets follow the copper’s spiral. Green salts mark their branching, and some of the scavengers feeding nearby carry the same blue-green color in their guts. Crickets that drink there sometimes develop turquoise rims around their mouthparts. The color passes briefly through the population and disappears.

The frogs are more distinct, though they spend much of their lives pretending to be geology.

A dry frog is a sealed shape beneath the silt, its skin filmed with clay and its limbs folded so tightly that it might be a stone with lungs. Only the nostril gives it away, opening at long intervals. Some remain like this for weeks. Mineral dust settles over them. Mites cross them. Hair roots touch their backs and turn aside.

When the internal rain begins, the silt chambers soften. One wall caves inward; an eye opens behind it. The frog presses forward until the chamber breaks along the yellow lines of its body. Wet, those markings become vivid, almost artificial—the color of old caution paint preserved under a machine.

They hunt by occupying chokepoints. One waits between two washers where crickets must turn sideways. Another sits beneath a hanging cable and takes mosquitoes as they rise. They rarely call. Sound would carry too far through the pipes, and there is little need: during wet weather they find one another through vibration and scent. A male may drum the mud once with the heel of a hind foot. A female answers from another basin, and the signal passes through water, sheet metal and silt more effectively than a voice.

Their spawn appears in absurdly small quantities of water. Five eggs in a cracked insulator. Three in the recess of a bolt head. A strand of seven under an oil skin. The tadpoles hatch quickly and remain narrow, feeding on bacterial films with rasping mouths. If a pool shrinks, they crowd its deepest blemish. If it vanishes, the chamber keeps only a black comma of dried bodies until the next seepage loosens them from the concrete.

The fish do not belong in the same permanent way. They are visitors from a larger, incomprehensible water, arriving whenever the culvert and the trench remember that they are connected.

Their entrance is abrupt. One moment the trench contains larvae and scraps of reflected ceiling; the next it is packed with pale sides, mouths and beating tails. They have eyes, but small ones, and use them chiefly for changes in brightness. Their flanks are sensitive to the pump’s first motion. Before the machinery can be heard, the entire shoal tilts.

To the permanent inhabitants, the fish are both weather and catastrophe. They consume a season’s larvae in minutes. They nose apart silt dams, flood burrows, strand isopods on trembling islands of rust. Then the water reverses and pulls them away. Those left behind circle their contracting pools until there is no room to circle. They survive by pressing their mouths to the surface, beneath bacterial skins that close after every breath.

Some have lived so long in isolated pockets that they are known to the other life. A spider anchors a line above one fish’s nightly breathing place. Frogs wait where its movements shake drowning insects from the shore. The fish itself has learned the eleven-second drip and rises just before it strikes.

The spiders possess the chamber’s empty intervals. Their bodies are small, but their jaws are long and their legs seem designed to measure gaps. A web is not spread across open air—there is too little of that—but strung through a passage already used by something else. Each thread therefore negotiates: high enough for the rat’s belly, low enough to take mosquitoes; clear of the nightly drip, close to the cricket seam.

They repair constantly. Condensation loads the silk. Rust flakes slice it. Frogs walk through it without acknowledgment. The spiders respond by making structures that are temporary even by the chamber’s standards: a line for one hour, a snare for one hatch, a nursery suspended only until the pump’s next turn.

Their egg sacs resemble mineral blisters. Some are plastered with gray dust and pieces of cricket scale. One species incorporates copper fibers stolen from the creeper’s roots, so that its nursery shines green where daylight reaches it.

The rats are easiest to mistake for the chamber’s owners. They are the largest, the warmest, and the only permanent creatures that regularly leave and return. Yet even they do not command the place. They submit to its measurements.

They are thin without being starved. Their rib cages are elongated, their shoulders narrow, their skulls smooth from generations of passage through abrasive holes. Whiskers on one side of the face are often shorter than on the other, worn down by habitual routes. Their tails bear pale mineral rings corresponding to the constrictions through which they drag them.

A rat can loosen itself in a way that seems boneless: forelegs extended, ribs compressed, hindquarters flowing after the skull. But every body has a limit, and the chamber records it. There are passages used only by juveniles, chambers abandoned when an animal grows, and polished apertures through which pregnant females can no longer pass.

They nest around the humming cabinet, where heat persists through the driest nights. Their nests are bright with imported matter. Blue fibers are preferred, then silver foil, then the white floss from pipe insulation. Nutshells are fitted concave-side down to make dry platforms. Plastic is bitten into scales and layered like roofing. In one cavity, generations have packed the walls with curled fragments of warning labels, so that pieces of old words—VOLT, KEEP, PRESSURE—line the nursery.

The cabinet is dangerous. Sometimes it gives a sharp internal snarl and fills the nest cavity with the smell of scorched hair. The rats recoil but do not leave. Warmth is too rare to abandon.

They carry the surface down with them in fragments, but they also carry the chamber upward. Rust dust stains their paws. Isopods cling briefly in their fur. Seeds lodge beside their claws. A rat emerging from the inhabited vent may shake itself on the plain and release a small fall of mites, spores, silt and cricket eggs into daylight.

There are beetles along every route. Some are glossy and black; some have backs rough enough to disappear against clinker. They trim the roots descending from above, not by consuming them entirely but by keeping them at the thickness needed for passage. Around favored roots, beetle generations have bitten waist-high clearances into the living curtain. They drink from cut ends. Their larvae tunnel in grease and compacted mold, leaving round chambers that later become shelters for smaller things.

There are also inhabitants that cannot be separated cleanly from the chamber’s materials.

Bacteria draw oil-colored membranes over stranded water. Fungi enter rubber belts and soften them into edible wood. Orange microbial threads grow from corroded steel and wave in currents too slight to move the water’s surface. Carbonate crystals rise around dead insects, taking the shape of legs and antennae long after the bodies inside have emptied. Roots put out hairs into rat droppings. Worms carry bacterial spores from pool to pool inside transparent bodies.

Death does not remove anything. It changes its use.

A drowned cricket becomes a feeding platform, then a mineral scaffold, then a dam. A rat bone channels condensation along its groove. A frog’s dried skin lines a beetle chamber. Spider silk catches silt until the silt can support moss, and when the silk decays, the new shelf remains. Even excrement is too structurally useful to be merely waste.

For this reason, the chamber’s oldest inhabitant may not be an animal at all. It may be the accumulation of consequences.

It has no continuous body, but it has circulation: vapor from the pump, water through cracks, fish through the culvert, rats through the vent. It has nerves of copper and root. It has joints in valves and belts. It has a memory stored in polished lanes, buried eggs, mineral rings and inherited pauses. When the pump moves, every creature changes posture at once, and the response travels farther than any single creature can perceive.

The third vent is inhabited by part of this body.

Inside it is a vertical colony of crickets, spiders, roots and old nests arranged according to height. The bottom is damp and crowded with silver-winged adults. Above them, where the pipe warms, egg cases fill seams in the rust. Higher still, black beetles shelter during noon. A spider lives near the rim and takes the insects drawn to exterior light. Rat claws have polished one side. On the other, a thin root has climbed upward from below, contrary to its usual direction, following condensation toward the sky.

At night, the vent releases a few crickets onto the plain. They seldom travel far. The open ground offers no ceiling, no close wall, no inherited lane. After several exposed steps they turn back, feel for the rusted lip, and descend.

What comes from the pipe is not quite a chorus. The reduced wings cannot sing properly. The sound is made by feet, mouthparts, scales brushing rust, small bodies shifting to admit other small bodies. It is a dry, continuous adjustment.

Deep below, a relay snaps.

The crickets stop. Frogs lower themselves. Rats hold still with foil in their teeth. In every pool, surfaces tighten around the expectation of movement.

The flywheel advances.

For a moment the inhabitants are not a multitude but one listening thing, fitted exactly into the narrow place that has made it.
