Nothing lives there in the manner expected of a sea.

There are no eyes reflecting the diluted light, no mouths opening in the weed, no quick muscles held ready beneath the mud. Nothing patrols a territory or flees from intrusion. If the drowned highland has inhabitants, they cannot be counted by watching for bodies.

At first, the ribbonweed seems closest to a population. Each forest appears to contain innumerable individuals: trunks rooted separately, leaves rising from distinct crowns, old stalks collapsing while young ones emerge. Yet excavation around the roots would show that the divisions are misleading. Beneath the alpine soil, pale fibers pass from plant to plant. They enter roots, leave them, knot around mineral seams, and merge with neighboring networks. Some belong to the colorless colonies that feed on the springs. Some are modified rootlets. In older groves it is no longer possible to say which is which.

A valley forest may therefore be one life wearing ten thousand crowns.

It senses slowly. Light reaching a single slope is registered as warmth, pressure, and chemical alteration. Minutes later, leaves in darkness turn their transparent surfaces toward currents that may eventually carry the light to them. Damage is recognized over hours. Seasons are recognized over years. A cold river entering one end of a valley causes distant stalks to fill with heavy sap and bend low before the descending water reaches them. The response is not swift enough to resemble alarm, but it is too consistent to be chance.

The groves do not fear destruction. They prepare to survive it.

When boulders tear through them, trunks split into long fibers rather than breaking cleanly. The fragments lodge against ruins and take root along their frayed edges. Buried crowns remain dormant in silt. A forest flattened beneath a landslide may reappear decades later as a ring of shoots around the slide’s perimeter, drawing minerals from the stone that buried it. Nothing comes to consume the dead tissue. Loss becomes storage.

Their age is difficult to determine. A particular leaf may endure only a few cold phases, a stalk perhaps a century, but the root-webs have no obvious beginning. They spread, divide, reunite, and exchange living tissue whenever seasonal currents bring their margins together. Two forests separated by bare mud can become one after a single year of heavy spores. One forest split by an avalanche may remain chemically synchronized across the divide for generations.

Individuality is a temporary condition caused by distance.

The bladdered vines are different. They are more nearly solitary, though even they gather into communities whose boundaries shift. Their clear floats lift them from the mud until they touch stone ceilings, bridge arches, or the undersides of overhanging ledges. There they spread sideways, making pale inverted fields. Their roots trail below them like rigging.

They spend the warm season distilling mineral-rich water into buoyant sap. During the cold phase, that sap thickens. The vines sink one by one from the cavern roof and lie coiled on the floor, where they remain through the violent currents. When the springs strengthen again, they rise.

An entire basin may seem to breathe as thousands of vines ascend in silence.

The red mats of the ridges live more slowly still. They are thin enough to be mistaken for mineral stains, but their internal structure is elaborate: layer beneath layer of sealed chambers, each holding a different solution. They survive exposure to the coldest descending water by allowing their outer tissues to die. The dead layers become armor. On some stones the living portion lies beneath centuries of its own remains.

These mats avoid the old cup marks.

They will cover a lintel, fill its cracks, pass over carvings worn nearly flat, yet stop at the rounded edge of each hollow. Roots turn aside. Spores that settle inside fail to open. The exposed cups remain clean enough to collect silt, but even the silt is often removed by small, regular pulses of water rising from the stone.

No organism appears to cause those pulses.

The colorless growth around the vents is the least plantlike of the inhabitants. It forms translucent sheaths around warm fissures and sends threads deep into rock. Rust, sulfur yellow, milk white, and glass-clear strains occur together. They exchange substances through contact, each processing what another cannot use. Without them, most of the highland’s vegetation would starve. With them, a mountainside can remain fertile in complete darkness.

The threads enter the ruins wherever the masonry touches bedrock. They pass under floors and through wall joints, but seldom penetrate tools or shaped standing stones. A hand axe left in a recess may be surrounded by roots without being moved. Copper hooks are given a wider berth. The colonies seem to recognize worked objects, though perhaps only through the oils, salts, or stresses left within them.

Their recognition does not fade.

A newly broken slab is colonized within a season. A slab shaped by the vanished builders may remain bare after centuries underwater.

This makes the old settlements feel occupied even when nothing stirs inside them. Their rooms are maintained by avoidance. Weeds frame the doorways. Root webs reinforce the outer walls while leaving shelves and bowls unobstructed. Spore mats grow over collapsed roofs but stop at the edges of sleeping recesses. Sediment is drawn into neat drifts along the walls, as if swept there by currents too slight to feel.

No will can be located. There is no custodian hidden beneath the floor. The effect arises from thousands of small refusals repeated over immense periods.

The drowned highland’s most developed inhabitant may be this refusal.

It inhabits boundaries rather than bodies.

It lies along the lines where old fields still divert water. It occupies the threshold that keeps warm and cold currents separate. It persists in the broken spiral pecked into a standing stone and in the strip of bare rock around two opposing combs. It has no substance that can be sampled. Remove the boundary stone and the line may remain. Break the doorway and the division still holds between its surviving posts. Grind away the markings, and for a time ribbonweed continues to avoid the places where they were.

Whatever the builders established has outlived both them and the structures that expressed it.

Perhaps the marks were not writing but instructions imposed on place. Perhaps the cups, lines, and spirals told water how to behave, and water—under pressure, in darkness, repeating the same passages for centuries—learned. The vegetation learned from the water in turn. What began as cultivation may have become instinct distributed through an ecosystem.

The great stone instrument on the saddle participates in this distributed life.

Its tones pass through root webs more effectively than light. When the lowest slabs begin to groan, forests in adjacent valleys alter the pressure inside their stalks. At the regular pulses from the drilled apertures, bladdered vines release clouds of seed-filament. The glassy vent colonies contract around their fissures. Red mats open microscopic channels and drink the mineral water shaken loose from pores in the rock.

The monument’s song is therefore not merely heard. It causes events throughout the highland.

Whether this was intended cannot be known. The stone field may once have summoned workers, marked a season, measured the force of the flood, or spoken to something no longer present. Now it coordinates growth. It is an organ without an animal: a great mineral throat through which the seasonal river tells the valleys when to open.

Not every response is useful. Some groves unfold just before freezing water reaches them and are shredded. Some spore clouds are released into currents that carry them over the abyss, where they sink beyond the range of the springs. The instrument does not command with perfect knowledge. It repeats an ancient sequence, and the living country has arranged itself around that repetition as best it can.

This is the nearest the highland comes to ritual.

The unstable chambers contain stranger forms of habitation. There, weeds rooted to opposing walls may belong to the same colony. Threads cross the room through directions that cannot be reconciled, vanishing into corners and reappearing beneath ledges too small to contain them. A root severed near the floor sometimes recoils from the ceiling. Seed-filaments circle for months without settling.

The growths in these chambers are stunted, dense, and very old. They form knots around the points where gravity changes. Their tissues contain concentric deposits of iron, recording each alteration as a dark ring. Some have survived so many shifts that their interiors resemble maps: branching lines, closed loops, abrupt breaks, and regions where no rings form at all.

They may be the only things that remember the highland before its drowning.

Not as an animal remembers an image, but as wood remembers drought and stone remembers pressure. A ring narrows when the ice thickens. A mineral seam changes color when a vent fails. Roots bend permanently toward a gravity that no longer exists. The past is held in structure.

The vanished builders persist in the same way.

There are no sleeping bodies in sealed houses. No preserved hands remain on the tools. No ghosts cross the roads. Whoever raised the huts and tuned the standing slabs has left nothing that can be met.

Yet their preferences continue to organize the living world. The low doorways determine where currents accelerate. The roads guide cold water into ravines. The stone circles collect spores. The roofed pits shelter vent colonies. Their field boundaries divide forests that would otherwise have merged. Their monuments make the seasonal signal to which whole valleys respond.

They survive as habits imposed upon matter.

Thus what lives in the drowned highland is not a hidden people, nor a great animal mistaken for landscape. It is a society of rooted processes: ribbon forests that are single lives at one scale and many at another; floating vines that rise and sink with the mineral seasons; red films older than buildings; colorless colonies passing nourishment through buried soil; and something less tangible, lodged in thresholds, carvings, tones, and remembered divisions.

None of it hungers in the animal sense. None hunts. Nothing there has learned pursuit, concealment, venom, armor, or flight. Its world contains injury but no attack, abundance but no competition for flesh. It knows burial, scouring, cold, darkness, and the failure of springs. It does not know teeth.

If it possesses anything resembling thought, that thought is too broad and slow to announce itself. It occurs when a light shaft touches one summit and a grove beyond the next ridge opens. It occurs when the stone instrument sounds and distant roots alter their chemistry. It occurs when spores stop at an absent wall.

A traveler might remain there for years and conclude that no one had noticed them.

They might be right.

Or they might simply be moving too quickly to perceive the regard of something whose briefest attention lasts a season, whose body is a drowned mountain range, and whose only answer is that, long after the traveler has passed, all the forests in a dark valley turn their pale undersides in the same direction.
