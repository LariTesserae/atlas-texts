At first it seems that only small things live there.

The moths are the easiest to notice. They are the color of old paper, nearly translucent, with bodies furred against a cold that never quite arrives. During the condenser’s long inhalation they hide behind gauge faces and in the dry hollows of electrical boxes. When the vents open, they come out all at once. The pressure drop is their evening bell. They fly through the warm white plume, feeding on mineral salts condensed around the vents, and return to shelter before the overflow begins.

There are water-striders on the cisterns, though they are not true water-striders. Their six feet end in tiny black hooks that let them stand on wet steel even when the tanks tremble. They graze on oil films, algae, and the soft metallic bacteria that collect beneath leaking rivets. A whole population can live on one pressure vessel without ever touching stone.

The pitcher plants eat them occasionally. More often they eat what the machine brings up.

Each red cup is lined with warm water from the thermal well. In that water live pale threads, finer than roots, which digest sulfur and iron. The insects are almost incidental. A pitcher cut open contains no ordinary soil and little recognizable flesh—only red membranes around a dark slurry that smells faintly of hot coins. Their flowers appear inside the operator’s booth, protected from wind, on stems that thread neatly through the cracked window seals.

The trees are stranger.

They look like a grove because their trunks rise separately, but there may be only one tree in the notch. White aerial roots descend from branch to branch, enter drains, follow cable trenches, and reappear tens of paces away. Under the moss, the roots have joined. They clasp pipe collars and warm bearings without crossing the surfaces hot enough to kill them. Where a flange leaks, they form a cup. Where runoff threatens to undermine a step, they knit themselves into a fibrous retaining wall.

The grove does not grow toward light so much as toward recurrence.

It knows where water will fall next hour.

Its leaves fold before the main vent opens. Its youngest root tips withdraw from a pipe shortly before that pipe begins to hammer. Seedlings gather beneath flanges that have not yet begun to leak but will do so within a season. Whether this is foresight or sensitivity to minute changes in heat and vibration is impossible to determine. In the notch, prophecy may be only attention practiced for a very long time.

Deeper in the machinery, the distinction between inhabitant and habitation disappears.

Roots entered the condenser generations ago through the empty bolt holes of the operator’s booth. Most were crushed, cooked, poisoned by lubricant, or cut apart by moving linkages. The survivors thickened around the slow parts. They grew beside floats and counterweights, drank from condensation traps, and laid pale callus over cracked insulation. Fine root hairs spread across brass contacts. Fungal filaments bridged the gaps between them.

The dead indicator board is dead only to eyes. Behind it, changes in voltage produce heat, and changes in heat alter the flow of sap. Relays click against root knots. Copper wires carry tiny currents through damp wood. Pressure bells flex, pressing into cushions of living tissue that have grown precisely to receive them.

There is something there that can be called the keeper, though it has no place from which it looks out.

Part of it is the grove. Part is moss and fungus. Part is the bacterial skin inside the thermal pipes. Part may be the condenser itself: its recurring pressures, its iron memory, the dispositions built into cams and valves by engineers long gone. None of these alone is the keeper. Together they make a slow, distributed animal whose organs do not share one substance.

Its heartbeat takes an hour.

Its breath is the white discharge from the upper gantry.

Its blood is not the water in the pipes but the minute exchanges around them: warmth entering roots, salts entering pitchers, current passing through wet copper, pressure bending old diaphragms, sap shifting in response. The great piston gives it rhythm. The lower line gives it purpose.

It cannot walk. It has nowhere to go.

When one of the daily signals comes up the pipe—three impacts, two, then one—the whole notch receives it. Water shivers in the cisterns. Moths lift from the gauge boxes. The pitcher cups ring too softly to hear. Root tips pressed against the lower pipe feel the sequence as three compressions, then two, then one.

The bypass valve opens because an old hydraulic actuator still functions. Yet the actuator functions because roots wick water away from its corroding spring. The spring remains free because moss catches grit before it reaches the housing. The moss survives because the valve leaks a measured warmth. There is no point in the chain where mechanism ends and tending begins.

The keeper answers.

It is not clear that it understands the settlements below. Perhaps to it the coded impacts are simply a season that occurs each noon, as dependable as light and condensation. Perhaps it imagines the lower world as an immense thirsty root attached to the other end of the pipe. Perhaps it has no image of anything. Its knowledge may consist entirely of adjustments: open this passage, close that leaf, thicken here, retreat there, wait through the gathering pressure.

Even so, it has preferences.

It dislikes loose objects. A fallen bolt will be surrounded by moss within days and fixed in place by roots within a month. It dislikes stagnant water except in the red pitchers. It keeps the central drain open. It tolerates moths in the gauge housings but seals cracks used by larger beetles, whose larvae chew insulation. It permits vines over abandoned panels and diverts them from anything that still vibrates.

Once in several years, a component fails beyond its ability to preserve.

Then the notch changes.

A pipe cools, and the vegetation withdraws from it. Ferns colonize the housing. Mineral bloom closes the seams. Elsewhere, roots explore forgotten conduits until they find another route for warmth or water. A wooden shim becomes fungus, the fungus becomes soil, and in that soil a seedling rises. The keeper cannot repair the plant as its builders did. It repairs by succession, substitution, and incorporation. What cannot be made to move is made to grow. What cannot be made to grow is entombed in lime.

There may once have been a human operator who encouraged this.

In the booth, the chair’s cushion has collapsed into brown fibers and white mycelium. One brass label has been polished more recently than the others—not by fingers, but by a root that swells and contracts across it with each thermal cycle. Behind the fused paper records lies a narrow cavity kept oddly dry. Inside are a few objects the roots have not consumed: a ceramic cup, a corroded whistle, three glass beads, and a small handwheel wrapped in cloth.

Nothing visits the cavity. Nothing uses the objects. Yet whenever seepage approaches, new rootlets appear and drink it away.

This might be accident. Much of the keeper might be accident.

At night, when the cloud fills the notch, its body becomes more apparent by vanishing. The separate trunks disappear. The walls disappear. The pipes and fern beds disappear. What remains is a field of responses.

A lamp warms the leaves nearest its cage, and they turn their pale undersides toward it. A turbine murmurs behind stone, and root membranes pulse to the vibration. A moth touches a live contact, producing a brief blue snap; nearby fronds close. Water climbs in a float chamber. Pressure gathers. Somewhere in the white dark, a valve stem rises through a collar of moss that has shaped itself around the exact limits of its motion.

Then the condenser exhales.

For several minutes the keeper is everywhere at once: flattened in leaves, streaming from drains, trembling in rails, sounding through the walls. Warm rain strikes the grove. The pitchers fill. The lower line takes its burden and carries it down through the clouds.

Afterward, everything lifts again.

The keeper resumes its long accumulation, inhabiting neither mountain nor machine alone, but the agreement between them.
