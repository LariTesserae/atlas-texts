The first inhabitants are films.

They spread where the hot water thins over stone: rust-red sheets, cream-colored threads, skins of green so dark they look black until sunlight passes through them. Different colonies occupy differences of only a few degrees. One flourishes at the lip of a spring, another begins precisely where the runoff cools enough for insect larvae to survive. They trap iron, build silica around themselves, and leave behind terraces that outlast generations of forest. Much of the basin’s ground is partly their body and partly their remains.

Above them lives an immense population of grazers too small to see at a distance. Clear mites browse fungus from wet bark. Pale crustaceans swim in bromeliad cups. Metallic beetles lay their eggs in the warm seams of mineral deposits, where the larvae scrape bacterial films with stone-hard mouthparts. Clouds of soft-bodied flies emerge after rain, mate in the few windless hours available to them, and are eaten so quickly that by morning only their transparent wings remain pasted to leaves.

The frogs divide the water among themselves with extraordinary precision. There are frogs of cold rainwater and frogs of hot seepage; frogs that breed in tree hollows, in hoofprints, in acidic pools, and in the narrow clear interval between a sulfur spring and the marsh into which it empties. Some are no larger than seeds. Others sit heavily in the reeds, mottled like wet basalt. Their blood carries oxygen with unusual efficiency, necessary in the thin air, and many spend the day almost motionless, reducing their needs until dusk.

They do not produce a continuous chorus. Calls are costly here. A male gives one or two exact notes into a leaf chamber and waits while the hollow shape of the plant carries the sound. To an animal a few body lengths away, the source is obvious. To anything farther off, it is only another drop of water.

The marsh deer are among the more visible inhabitants, though even they disappear easily. They are compact, deep-chested animals with high shoulders and broad, spreading feet. Their coats are short and nearly black, except for pale markings under the jaw and around the eyes. Both sexes bear horns: low, backswept structures with ridged surfaces on which moisture gathers and runs. Moss sometimes establishes near the bases on old animals, but never grows thickly enough to inconvenience them.

They feed on reeds, floating leaves, mineral-rich water plants, and the soft new growth around warm channels. Their trails are not fixed paths. A herd uses one passage for several days and abandons it as soon as the mud deepens or a predator begins waiting nearby. Reeds bend, water closes over the prints, and the basin removes the evidence.

During the underground discharges, the deer stop feeding before the springs visibly rise. They feel the first pressure wave through their feet. Heads lift throughout a marsh, all turned in different directions, listening through the ground. When the deep concussion comes, they step onto firmer root mats and stand there while the current rearranges the floating vegetation around their legs.

On the open shelves live broad-headed lizards with flattened ribs and long gripping toes. In sunlight they are copper, scarlet, or acid green. Under cloud their skin darkens within minutes, breaking into angular patches indistinguishable from iron-stained basalt. They bask not merely for warmth but for digestion: many eat toxic beetles and must maintain a high body temperature to dismantle the poisons. Blue electrical fire can pass over their scales during storms without entering deeply into the body. At the first prickling of charge, they flatten against the rock and close a transparent second eyelid.

The great birds belong more to the cliffs than to the forest. They are long-winged, narrow-bodied hunters whose bones contain dense internal struts, giving strength in thin air without much weight. Their plumage is gray from above and pale beneath, making them difficult to see against either stone or cloud. They can spend most of a day without flapping, crossing from one updraft to another along the escarpment.

At dusk they become sudden and physical. They fold their wings and enter the wetlands feet-first, striking at fish stranded in warming shallows. Their hind toes are long enough to close around frogs without piercing them. Some follow the pulse of the western shelf from lake to lake, arriving minutes after the rising water forces prey from submerged burrows. Their nests are built on fern-curtained ledges behind waterfalls, less to hide the chicks than to keep blood-feeding insects from reaching them.

The long-bodied cats are seldom seen entire. Usually there is a shoulder moving behind fern stems, a tail crossing a gap, or the brief oval of a face at the forest edge. Their fur is not simply black. It carries faint red bars that appear in the warm, diffuse light beneath the canopy and vanish in direct sun.

They hunt by patience and by knowledge of pressure. The cats learn which spring channels will swell after a subterranean stroke and wait where deer, lizards, and ground birds must cross to avoid the new water. Their whiskers are unusually long and sensitive. Resting with the jaw or chest against a root, they can feel footfalls traveling through the saturated ground. They do not roar. Their territorial call is a low vibration, almost below hearing, which passes farther through root mats than it would through air.

Yet the most watchful inhabitants live above them.

The dark-furred primates move through the middle canopy in small, enduring groups. They are long-armed but heavy through the chest, with large nasal chambers and slow resting hearts. The thin atmosphere has shaped them as decisively as the forest has: their blood is dark, their lungs deep, their movements economical. A troop may cross a ravine without shaking more than one branch at a time.

Their hands are broad and hairless on the palms. The last two fingers oppose the others slightly, allowing them to hold wet limbs from both sides. Their feet have the same divided grip. Adults are charcoal or black-brown, while infants are born with silver fur that darkens during their first wet season. Water beads along the adults’ guard hairs and runs toward the elbows and heels instead of soaking the undercoat.

They rarely call because the basin offers them a better medium than air.

A primate resting on a branch lays the inside of its wrist against the wood. Another, several trees away, answers with a knuckle tap, a heel press, or a short scrape of the nails. The message travels through interlocked crowns, vines, and root-connected trunks. It is not fast communication. Pauses may last half a minute while the tremor passes through living timber. But it is quiet, and its direction is clear to those who know how to feel it.

The hollow-rooted trees greatly extend this language. When vapor rises through their chambers, the trunks knock according to the pressure below. The primates do not cause the sequence, but they know it intimately. They place their own taps between the natural blows, using the grove’s rhythm as cover and measure. A hillside may appear empty while several troops exchange warnings, courtship signals, and the location of fruiting trees through its wood.

Their knowledge is carried in timing rather than objects. They know how long after one western concussion a particular marsh crossing remains safe. They remember which cloud shapes bring electrical storms over the eastern rim. Elder animals can distinguish the hollow thump of rising vapor from the sharper vibration made by a cat climbing into the canopy.

They build little. Sleeping nests are bent together each evening and collapse within days. Feeding stones are used where they lie and left there. A twig may be stripped to draw grubs from bark, then dropped into the litter, softened by fungus, and gone before the next season. They mark no boundaries and clear no ground. Their presence survives chiefly in the behavior of younger animals.

When lightning enters the wet forest, the primates descend from exposed crowns and gather against the thickest trunks. Blue fire crawls over thorns and vine tips around them. Adults close their bodies around the silver young. No one moves until the charge has passed into the mineral earth. Then, often before the rain begins, a single hand strikes wood three times. Other trees answer across the dark.

Nothing in the basin reigns over the rest. The cats take deer but lose cubs to snakes and infection. The great birds empty a pool and then carry parasites to the next one. Primates scatter seeds, beetles kill seedlings, fungi consume fallen giants, and microscopic colonies make new ground from the dissolved mountain.

Even the largest lives remain subordinate to the basin’s circulation. They wake, breed, migrate, and hunt according to movements of water they cannot see. The deep pulse passes through bone and root alike. Marsh leaves tilt. Deer step toward higher ground. Cats raise their heads. Hands become still on branches.

For a moment, innumerable bodies listen to the same stroke beneath the plateau.

Then the springs rise, the forest resumes dripping, and everything goes on.
