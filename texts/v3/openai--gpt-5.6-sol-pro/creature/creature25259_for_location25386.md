The people are difficult to distinguish from their occupations at first.

A woman stands thigh-deep in a reed bed, cutting stalks with a stone flake. A man sits beneath a raised house, coaxing fiber through his fingers. Children press their thumbs into clay cups. Someone is always carrying a jar along a causeway, though the jars are seldom seen being filled or emptied. Their bodies are brown, black, ocher, or the reddish gray of fired earth, shining continually with sweat. They wear bark cloth, plaited belts, bead nets, feather capes, and broad hats made from leaves that never dry.

They seem healthy. Their teeth are strong. Their limbs are muscled by work. Almost everyone bears a small fresh injury: a nick from an obsidian edge, a blister from a digging stick, a thorn embedded in a heel. These wounds do not worsen, but neither do they close. Blood beads brightly and remains bright. A bandage may be removed after many sleeps to reveal the cut exactly as it was, waiting without pain or infection.

Closer acquaintance reveals small inconsistencies in their construction.

The whorls on their fingertips do not always turn continuously; one ridge may end at a minute straight line and resume on the other side. The finest hairs of the forearm emerge in paired rows, as if inserted with an awl. The folds of an ear sometimes retain a faint maker’s thumbprint. Their navels differ greatly. Some are natural hollows, some tight knots, some little ceramic spirals glazed the color of flesh.

These marks are neither hidden nor considered deformities. Mothers wash the impressions in their children’s skin and memorize them. Lovers trace them. A person may be known by a seam behind the knee or by three tiny punctures beneath the tongue. Names change often, but such signs do not.

When a body is opened—by accident, hunting, violence, or the patient curiosity of healers—it proves as living as any body and as manufactured as the groves. There is blood, warm muscle, slippery membrane, and the powerful contractions of the heart. There are also woven structures where no weaving hand could reach. Tendons divide into cords finer than spider silk and pass through polished wooden pulleys. Certain bones are hollow reeds strengthened with ceramic rings. The liver has the glossy red surface of an organ but is impressed underneath with mat patterns. Inside a broken tooth is another, smaller tooth.

No two people are made in quite the same manner. One may have ordinary ribs and a basket-woven skull. Another may have clay finger bones but a wholly fleshy heart. They do not divide themselves according to these differences. Flesh, wood, fiber, and fired earth all bleed if they belong to a living body.

Birth occurs, though seldom. Pregnancy advances normally until its final days, then becomes uncertain. A woman may remain on the edge of labor for what she recalls as many years, able to walk, eat, laugh, and work while the child inside her continually turns toward birth. Another may wake with a newborn already nursing and no memory of delivery. The infant’s cord leads beneath the sleeping mat, passes through a gap in the floor, and disappears into the wet brightness under the house. When cut, it is found to contain no blood, only tightly twisted green thread.

Children grow, but growth does not accumulate evenly. A child may become taller during a journey and be small again upon returning home. Skills persist better than stature. There are children with the speech and patience of elders, and broad-shouldered adults who remember only a few arrangements of shells. No one can agree whether the very old were once young. The oldest-looking people have smooth joints, unclouded eyes, and only those wrinkles required by their expressions.

Death is known as an experience but not as a conclusion.

The dead cool. They stiffen. Families wash them, paint them, sing close to their ears, and place them in large lidded jars under the houses. Yet graves cannot be maintained. A burial jar opened later may contain woven reeds, warm rainwater, or a sleeping animal. The dead person is eventually encountered again, often far away, carrying another name and no certainty of having belonged to the mourners.

For this reason grief is intense but brief in public. The bereaved do not say that someone is gone. They say, “She has become difficult to find.”

The inhabitants understand that their country resists alteration, but they do not regard this resistance as a law. To them it is a temperament: the land is easily startled and dislikes being corrected. If watched too sternly, it restores itself. If ignored, it also restores itself. Between these two certainties lies a narrow interval in which human life takes place.

Their constant labor is an attempt to widen that interval.

Weaving is not meant chiefly to produce mats. It keeps a pattern present in the hands. Gardening is not meant to remove weeds. It maintains the distinction between weed and crop. The scraping of a boat hull tells the boat that it remains a boat. Cooking persuades food to be edible, even though the same loaf has browned in the same oven for generations of memory.

People abandon a task only with care. A weaver stopping midway places another person’s fingers on the warp before removing her own. Gardeners leave tools crossed over the beds. A dying fire is told aloud the names of nearby houses. They believe unattended things lose not their existence but their specificity. A house ceases first to be one family’s house, then to be a house, then to be noticeable. Eventually the land incorporates its shape as a low rise or a dry patch in a water court.

Memory therefore belongs less to individuals than to groups. An event remembered by one person becomes a dream. Remembered by two, it is a dispute. Remembered by three, it may endure for a while. Important happenings are distributed among many witnesses, each entrusted with a different detail.

One keeps the color of the injured man’s belt.  
One keeps the number of fish in the canal.  
One keeps the exact words spoken.  
One keeps the direction in which the silent bird was facing.

When they gather, they assemble the event by recitation. No single speaker tells the whole story. To do so would be considered dangerous, as if the world might mistake a complete account for permission to put the matter away.

Their languages contain precise words for unfinished states. They distinguish a thing newly begun from a thing that has always been beginning, a thing interrupted from a thing complete but unable to become past. Their stories avoid endings. Heroes return carrying proof of their journeys, but the proof is lost before anyone examines it. Monsters are wounded and continue being monsters. Marriages are celebrated, though husband and wife may later meet as strangers in another village and marry again.

Promises are made in the present tense.

They have little use for ownership. Objects return to accustomed locations regardless of who carries them away. Instead, people speak of “being accompanied by” a knife, a canoe, a necklace, or a house. A well-used tool acquires a reputation rather than an owner. Some stone blades have accompanied dozens of people who all remember finding them that morning.

Metal objects are exceptions. A copper hook, a corroded iron bead, or a sheet of dull gold may remain displaced longer than clay or wood. Metal is therefore handled reluctantly. It seems slow to forgive. Priests keep small pieces wrapped in wet leaves and consult them when two versions of an event cannot be reconciled. If the metal has tarnished, they conclude that something has truly happened. Since tarnish itself often vanishes, such judgments remain provisional.

There are no rulers over large territories. Authority weakens beyond the reach of a voice, and voices travel poorly. Each cluster of houses has several keepers: one for paths, one for births, one for remembered injuries, one for things found where they should not be. The most respected person is often the one able to notice an alteration before the country smooths it away.

Such people are called, in different villages, Finders of the Second Shape, Those Who See the Cut, or simply the Wakeful.

They walk at the edges of groves and inspect the undersides of bridges. They study droplets for missing reflections. They press their ears to the tiled flats. They can identify places where a new structure is trying to persist or where an old feature has returned imperfectly. Some carry bundles of short sticks, laying them in configurations that cannot be remembered once the sticks are gathered again.

The Wakeful are also the ones who listen for the distant wooden knock.

Most inhabitants hear it only rarely and accept it as part of the country, like heat or wetness. The Wakeful insist that the interval between knocks is not always the same. They say it shortens after a birth, lengthens when a village is forgotten, and pauses whenever someone reaches the top of one of the unfired towers.

They have several explanations.

One is that the country was built upon the chest of a vast sleeping person, and the knock is the heart striking against a wooden cage. The canals are not an image of ribs but the ribs themselves, seen through a covering thin enough to inhabit. The oval lakes are closed organs. The groves grow at the joints. The inhabitants are said to be mites, ornaments, thoughts, or children placed on the sleeper before it woke.

Another teaching says the enormous figure is not beneath the land. It is the land, and every village is part of its nervous system. Human journeys are sensations passing across its skin. The country prevents consequence because the creature has not yet decided whether to wake. Nothing can finish while that decision remains unmade.

A third teaching, spoken only near water, claims there is no giant at all. The shape visible from the towers is the impression left by something that has already risen. The world is the damp mold from which a being was removed. The knock comes from outside, where the finished creature walks.

None of these accounts is considered older or more authoritative than the others. Age cannot sanctify a story where nothing becomes old.

Most people do not spend their lives in dread of these possibilities. They flirt, quarrel, sing into one another’s shoulders, race boats along motionless currents, and make jokes about the stubbornness of jars. They value fragrance, cool mud, intricate beadwork, sour fruit, and the sudden isolated sound of a frog that has found its voice. They are generous with food because food cannot be depleted. They are cautious with attention because attention can.

Their greatest fear is not pain, death, or even forgetting. It is becoming an addition the country refuses.

This sometimes happens to travelers who invent too much of themselves. A person takes a new name, builds where no house stood, cuts an unfamiliar pattern into their skin, and declines every customary task. For several days they appear unusually vivid. Their voice carries farther. Their footprints remain in the wet clay. Then others begin overlooking them. Questions are answered as if no one asked. Offered food passes from hand to hand around them. At last they are visible only indirectly, as a slight interruption in the white light or a human-shaped dryness beneath a bridge.

The Wakeful say such people have not vanished. They have gone below the account of the world. They may be the ones whose movements produce the distant knock.

Thus the inhabitants persist between two makers: the immense hands that have just withdrawn from every surface, and the deeper unfinished creature whose outline the whole country repeats.

They are neither captives nor caretakers, though at times they resemble both. They are the brief freedom permitted inside a permanent first moment. They cannot leave a lasting mark, yet they spend their lives making marks. They cannot complete a story, yet they speak. They cannot cause the future to arrive.

Still, in every village, someone sits beside a water court arranging shells in a sequence never used before.

Someone else watches.

And for a little while, the country allows the new pattern to remain.
