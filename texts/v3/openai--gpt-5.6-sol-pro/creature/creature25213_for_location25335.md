What lives there is difficult to divide into individuals.

At first there seem to be ordinary distinctions: rooted things, flying things, grazers, predators. But these categories fail on closer inspection. A plant may pump a river through its body, shelter an animal colony in its shade, and depend upon that colony to operate its valves. A herd may be composed of separate beasts in summer and become a single heat-sharing organism in winter. What appears to be weather may feed, migrate, reproduce, and die across mountaintops.

Life on the plateau survives not by resisting crowding but by becoming part of it.

The first inhabitants of any new surface are the silver root threads. They arrive while basalt is still warm enough to steam. Almost invisible individually, they enter cooling fractures and branch through the stone, drinking condensed vapor and the first dissolved metals. They are root, nerve, and surveyor. Through electrical pulses they announce pressure, warmth, moisture, and fresh space to colonies many valleys away.

Dark films follow them. These are not simple lichens but layered commonwealths: light-catching skins above, insulation beneath, mineral-eaters pressed against the rock, and minute translucent grazers moving between the layers. Together they make the black-green lacquer seen on young cliffs. When conditions worsen, the film dries to something thinner than paint and waits. When water returns, it thickens within minutes, releases spores, and begins constructing soil out of its own dead.

The hand-high moss forests belong to a later, denser stage. Their wrist-thick trunks stand so close that they form a second ground suspended above the first. Beneath their crowns is a warm, humid underworld that rarely sees direct light. Flat animals live there, pale as root flesh and broad as rugs. They swim through packed stems by rippling the edges of their bodies. From above, their progress appears as a traveling swell in the moss, though the creature itself may require months to pass beneath a single grove.

Many of these under-moss animals have no fixed mouth. When they encounter food, part of the lower surface temporarily becomes digestive tissue. After feeding, it closes again without a scar. Some carry chambers of warm brine through the winter. Others contain generations of larvae that never emerge, living and breeding inside the parent until the entire lineage is released by injury or death.

Above the moss grow the mirror-fronds. Their blue metallic undersides are not merely reflective. Thousands of microscopic plates swivel independently, throwing light away from overheated tissue or directing it toward neighboring organisms. Whole slopes shimmer as the plates adjust. The effect resembles wind passing over water, even when the air is still.

The fronds are gardeners of shade. Animals gather beneath them; condensation forms on their cool undersides; parasites puncture their stems and install feeding bulbs. The oldest fronds may carry so many dependent species that their own tissue accounts for less than half their mass. When such a plant turns through the day, an entire inhabited canopy rotates with it.

Among them stand the hollow towers, jointed organisms whose frosted shells knock together in the wind. Each tower is both plant and resonating chamber. The sound it makes regulates pressure in its tissues and attracts small winged pollinators able to recognize one vibration among the plateau’s continuous roar. In severe cold, the towers fill their hollow joints with concentrated fluid and let the outer sheath freeze. Some live for centuries by repeatedly sacrificing their exterior.

The wing clouds are the plateau’s most numerous visible animals. Each grazer is small, six-winged, and nearly weightless, with a body that alternates between black absorption and glassy transparency. Their wings do not beat in pairs but in traveling sequences from front to back. A single animal makes almost no sound. Billions together produce the pressure that fills the high country like distant machinery.

They feed on spores, reflective pollen, mineral dust, airborne microbes, and the exposed films of cliffs. Their migration is too long for any one of them to comprehend or complete. Each generation inherits only a direction, altered by temperature, polarized light, and the electrical state of the stone below. Over three centuries, the population circles the upland continent. No individual returns, but the cloud does.

When supercooled rain seals them together, they become a temporary communal body. Outer animals freeze and die; inner ones pass warmth through linked limbs. During thaw, the survivors consume the dead before separating. Thus a storm reorganizes the cloud rather than dispersing it.

The cliff browsers are larger and more deliberate. They have six short, muscular legs arranged around a low central body, allowing them to turn on vertical rock without changing direction. Their fur is made of hollow transparent filaments. Sunlight enters these filaments and is scattered toward dark skin beneath, while trapped air insulates the animal from cold.

They rasp mineral films from sheer faces with rotating mouthparts. Their feet secrete a substance that hardens under pressure but softens when unloaded, so each step grips until the next has been placed. They rarely fall alone. Herd members anchor themselves to one another with braided tail tendons, and a slipping browser may hang for days before the others pull it back or let its tether decay.

They are attended by shade-dwellers: thin black animals folded into abdominal grooves, where they clean the browser’s skin and shelter from the sun. Some circulate blood through external loops and are so deeply integrated that neither host nor attendant can survive separation. The browser carries its own patch of night wherever it goes.

In the thermal basins live the pale heat herds. Their bodies are soft beneath articulated armor, and each individual has a hot side and a cold side. Around a fissure they arrange themselves in concentric rings, warm surfaces inward and insulated surfaces outward. Slowly, almost imperceptibly, they exchange positions. Those chilled at the perimeter migrate toward the crack; those near overheating are passed outward through the mass.

The arrangement may endure for years. Births occur within it. Corpses are dismantled without breaking the circle. Young animals learn no landscape at first, only pressure, temperature, and the touch-signals by which bodies make room for one another.

Their predators are infiltrators rather than chasers. A predator resembles an underfed member of the herd and enters at the cold edge. Over weeks it is passed inward. It copies the herd’s muscular signals, shares heat, and waits beside a weakened animal until its feeding tissues can be inserted unnoticed. Predation proceeds with such slowness that victim and predator may exchange warmth for an entire season. Often the predator becomes trapped in the herd after feeding and eventually nourishes the same cluster it entered.

The rivers are inhabited differently because many of them are themselves alive.

A river-organism begins in a warm fault as a web of permeable tubes. It collects condensation, then grows downhill, uphill, or sideways according to the needs of its distant tissues. Muscular collars contract in waves, pushing water through transparent channels. Rooted filters remove grit. Gilled animals regulate dissolved gases. Sponge-like reservoirs expand after rain and close against evaporation.

From above, such a river resembles a silver vein crossing the plateau. Up close, there may be almost no exposed water. The current passes through mouths, bladders, vascular cavities, and shared gut chambers. Fishlike creatures move through it not by swimming but by being swallowed in one place and expelled in another. They graze the interior lining as they travel.

When a channel freezes or a ravine closes, the river does not flood in the usual sense. It buds. A blind, wet limb begins growing toward another basin, guided by root-borne electrical signals. The abandoned branch dries into a translucent husk and is eaten by organisms that specialize in dead waterways.

Above all of this hang the buoyant colonies.

Each begins as a ribbon of linked gas cells no longer than a hand. Photosynthetic membranes produce food; gut chambers generate hydrogen; ballast animals crawl through internal passages, shifting mineral grains to control altitude. As the colony matures, it tangles with others. Ribbons become nets, nets become floating islands, and islands become pale aerial reefs whose shadows travel across several valleys at once.

Their outer membranes support entire ecologies. Hook-footed hunters patrol the ridges between gas chambers. Small nesters burrow shallow pits without puncturing the pressure layer. Parasites tap hydrogen veins and cultivate black microbial gardens around the wounds. Winged grazers rest there during migration, covering the colony so densely that it temporarily descends beneath their weight.

The colony has no central brain. Its direction emerges from local contractions, light sensitivity, the movement of ballast keepers, and the pull of organisms nesting along one side. Yet it can tack against a weak wind, avoid some storms, and lower feeding tendrils into fertile ravines. It behaves less like an animal than like a drifting country.

In age, its gas cells fail one by one. The colony sinks over weeks or years. If it catches between peaks, it becomes a bridge reef. Rooted life climbs up from both ends. Cliff browsers cross its stiffening membranes. Moss forests grow in hollows filled with windblown dust. Long after the last gas chamber has emptied, the dead colony may remain suspended as a populated valley with no stone beneath it.

Deeper down are the strain-feeders. They cannot be seen except where their anchor roots surface in silver ropes. Their true bodies occupy faults and pressured chambers within the rising plateau. They grow fibers across cracks and allow tectonic motion to pull them taut. When the tension reaches a precise threshold, internal strands break in sequence.

The resulting discharge passes into fungal conductor webs. For an instant, ridges shine blue-white and buried storage nodules begin converting charge into dense chemical reserves. Other organisms gnaw these nodules during winter. Some protect the root network from parasites; others merely steal from it. The distinction between power plant, food crop, and living body has no meaning there.

The rarest inhabitants are the eclipse feeders.

They live below the moss floor, inside the moist compression of roots, carcasses, shed skins, and mineral felt. Light harms them. Even reflected brightness can destroy their colorless tissues, so they spend most of their lives in a nearly liquid state, threaded through pores too small for other animals to enter.

They sense an eclipse long before darkness arrives. Changes in plant tension pass downward; wing pressure diminishes; the root web alters its charge. Then the feeders assemble themselves. Fluid threads pour together into soft, temporary bodies with many mouths and no permanent symmetry.

When the shadow reaches them, they rise.

For those few minutes they feed with astonishing speed, flowing over exposed surfaces, absorbing spores, eggs, weakened animals, frost, and films of mineral nutrient. They may multiply several times before the returning light forces them apart. Any body still exposed when brightness returns becomes clear and rigid, then shatters into droplets that retreat belowground.

Most plateau creatures never encounter one. Nevertheless, eclipse feeders prune every ecosystem they can reach. Their brief appearances prevent the upper layers from sealing themselves completely shut.

No species lives alone. Nearly every body contains lodgers, gardens, parasites, inherited symbionts, and partially digested ancestors. Shade is exchanged for cleaning. Warmth is exchanged for blood flow. Water is passed mouth to mouth until ownership of it becomes meaningless. Waste scarcely exists long enough to be recognized; one organism’s discharge is another’s atmosphere, armor, food, or signal.

Nor is there a privileged scale at which the inhabitants become most real. The tiny wing grazer is an animal. The cloud composed of its descendants is also, in some sense, an animal. A heat-herd is many bodies and one seasonal metabolism. A river is a colony, a habitat, and an organ. The plateau-wide fungal web carries information without thought, memory without awareness, and power without intention.

Nothing commands these systems. Nothing understands the whole.

Yet when cliffs split, roots are already entering them. When warm vapor condenses, mouths are waiting beneath the leaves. When a mountain rises, life ascends with it—not afterward, not as a visitor, but woven into the event of its emergence.

The inhabitants are therefore not merely creatures upon the high country. They are its moving water, its temporary soil, its shadows, bridges, reservoirs, electrical pathways, and weather. They cling to every new ledge and fill every old cavity. They hang in the air and labor under the stone. They eat one another, shelter one another, become one another.

Together they form a world that has never been empty enough to belong to anything.
