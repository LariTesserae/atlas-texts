What lives there is difficult to count, because counting requires things to exist at the same time.

There may be one inhabitant. There may be a colony. There may be a people whose members occur one after another in the same body.

Most who enter the summit sea notice only its lesser life: the amber-tipped veils, the translucent fans, the blind uphill swimmers, the shells full of warm water. These seem separate at first. In time, their separateness becomes doubtful.

The fans do not merely fold at vibration. They fold in sequence. A disturbance near the eastern wall closes one; several breaths later another shuts near the central ring; then a third, behind the disturbance, closes just before the sound reaches it. When mapped over weeks, these responses resemble the flexing of an enormous hand—one whose fingers are scattered across the basin and whose palm is missing.

The pale veils behave like nerves exposed on stone. Pressure runs through them as amber light, but the light sometimes branches toward rock that has not yet cracked. When that crack finally opens, new filaments are already waiting inside it.

The ribbon swimmer may be less an animal than an impulse. It threads endlessly from the lower vents toward whatever direction the sea has most recently decided is above. If cut, it does not bleed. Both ends continue uphill. In the following days the translucent fans become unusually sensitive, as if something has been warned.

Even the little shells beneath the ledges seem to belong to this scattered anatomy. The warm water inside them maintains the same temperature regardless of the surrounding current. When poured out, it crawls over the basalt in a thin sheet, hesitates at intersections, and chooses a crack. Sometimes, much later, an arch appears where it disappeared.

The true inhabitants are distributed among these things, but they are not identical to them.

They are called builders only because construction is the nearest available word. No one has seen one carry a stone. No one has watched an arch rise. Nevertheless, the summit is full of their gestures.

A builder does not occupy a place as an animal does. It occupies a sequence of changes. Its approach may be recognized by a footprint appearing in bare mineral crust, then by the brief warming of an empty shell, then by a fan folding somewhere across the basin. A shadow passes through a fissure. Gravel lifts and rattles. An arch is found where none stood before. Only after all this, sometimes hours afterward, does something resembling a body move through the water.

Seen directly, it is never complete.

A shoulder may emerge first: a sloping absence in the silt, outlined by bubbles that refuse to cross it. Farther away, black lengths bend among the vents like legs viewed through broken glass. What might be a head appears as a clear oval in muddy water, containing no features except a small revolving light. Hands are inferred from the arrangement of stones around them. The stones turn, settle, and fit while the space that holds them remains apparently empty.

At moments of red volcanic glow, more can be seen. The fissure-light reveals tall, folded silhouettes standing close to the rock. They have too many joints, though this may be because several stages of the same movement are visible together. One figure seems to kneel, stand, and lean forward all at once. Another extends an arm that divides into three possible arms, each ending at a different stone.

When the glow dies, the figures vanish. The stones remain.

They have no fixed scale. Near the vents, one may seem small enough to crouch beneath a hand-sized fan. Seen through the central ring, the same shape may surround the whole mountain, its limbs following the outer rain-wall, its bent back supporting the vaulted sea. Scale, for them, appears to be another direction—one through which they can turn.

Nor do they possess a stable number. The three arches may each contain a builder, or may be three openings through a single builder. During periods of violent pressure, silhouettes gather behind every basalt ridge, an assembly without faces. During quieter months, all signs converge upon one slow presence circling the stone ring. Its circuit takes several days. No matter where it is glimpsed, it appears to be facing inward.

They do not seem to eat. They attend to pressure, heat, and incompletion.

A fresh fissure draws them. So does a broken mineral plate, an interrupted current, or a stair ending over emptiness. They gather around unfinished forms with a stillness more intense than sleep. Then the water begins behaving as though the missing part were already present. Currents divide around an invisible wall. Bubbles crawl along nonexistent ceilings. Silt settles on steps that have not yet risen.

Eventually the stone obeys.

This is why their structures lead nowhere. The builders are not making passages for bodies. They are establishing directions the world has not learned to contain.

One arch opens into bare rock because the chamber beyond it has not happened yet. The narrow crevice is not an error but a doorway seen edge-on in time. The arch that frames sinking bubbles may lead downward in a sense unrelated to depth. The six steps above the chasm are perhaps the beginning of a road whose remaining landscape has yet to erupt.

The footprints facing several ways are accurate. A builder’s foot does not press down. It presses toward.

The upright slabs at the center are their nearest equivalent to a dwelling, though nothing rests within the ring. They enter by subtraction. As one approaches, the builder becomes less visible and the enclosing water more exact. Turbulence smooths. Noise separates into individual impacts. Every grain of silt that crosses the threshold is returned because the space inside permits no accident, no erosion, no event without intention.

Sometimes a clear vertical shape stands there, narrower than a person and much taller. Its surface resembles water under enormous tension. Within it, minute fragments of black stone move slowly into arrangements like ribs, ladders, branching trees, or hands clasped in prayer. None of these arrangements lasts.

This may be how a builder thinks.

They notice swimmers, but not as intruders. They notice them as temporary structures.

A visitor passing near the ring may feel the water fitting itself closely around the body, measuring every hollow: the sockets of the eyes, the cup of the ear, the spaces between knuckles, the chambers of the heart. Afterward, shallow human-shaped depressions sometimes appear on the steps. They do not copy the visitor’s posture. They show several postures the visitor has not yet assumed.

For days, the visitor may hear quiet stone sounds inside their own movements. A click when turning the head. A soft grinding beneath the breath. Dreams become architectural: corridors entered before they are discovered, rooms built around a sleeping body, stairways descending into earlier versions of the dreamer.

Most recover after leaving.

Some return.

Those who return repeatedly become difficult to follow. They may emerge from the rain-wall before they entered it. Their voices sometimes answer questions a moment too soon. Mineral colors gather under their nails. Eventually one crosses the boundary and is not seen again, though a new upright slab may later appear in the central ring, warm to the touch and faintly resonant with a heartbeat that has not yet stopped.

The builders themselves may be unfinished. This would explain their tireless attention to making.

Perhaps the mountain is assembling them from available laws: heat for desire, pressure for touch, stone for memory, living filaments for sensation. Perhaps every fan and ribbon is an organ placed in advance, waiting for the body that will one day connect them. The arches may be joints. The steps may be teeth. The central ring may be an empty skull around which its creature is slowly learning to exist.

Or the builders may be assembling the mountain.

In the deepest hours, when the whole ocean lifts from the basin and hangs overhead, their scattered parts draw nearest to coherence. The pale veils rise from the rock though no current moves them. The fans open simultaneously. Every shell empties itself. Blind ribbons gather along the underside of the suspended water and braid into long, trembling lines.

Below them, on the exposed summit, figures appear.

They are thin, dark, and wet, with limbs made from fitted pieces of night. They kneel around stones that have not yet fallen. Their heads are bowed, not in reverence but concentration. Each holds one edge of an absence.

Then comes the small, deliberate sound.

Stone placed carefully upon stone.

For those few breaths, it becomes possible to understand that the sound is not the conclusion of their labor. It is the seed of it. The arches, the steps, the ring, perhaps even the mountain itself, spread backward and forward from that single contact.

The sea falls.

The figures come apart into bubbles, currents, veils, swimmers, shells, and shadows. The basin fills with thunder again.

And somewhere in the newly drowned dark, one more builder begins to have been.
