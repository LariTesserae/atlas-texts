At first the city appears to be inhabited by light.

Blue sparks move in files along the avenues. Green embers hover over the mud and vanish when approached. High in the towers, amber beads brighten, divide, and drift apart. There are slow white blurs like lamps carried behind curtains, and sudden violet lines that seem less like bodies than cracks opening in the dark.

Only after watching for a long time does the flesh around each light become apparent.

The blue sparks belong to needlefish: narrow, almost transparent animals with black eyes too large for their heads. Beneath each jaw hangs a luminous sac populated by captive bacteria. A fish can cover the sac with a fold of skin, but a school seldom goes wholly dark. Its signals pass from one animal to the next too quickly. A flash at the front bends the entire school; a double pulse draws it tight; darkness releases it into confusion. They enter the city in thousands, threading through windows and ruptured chambers, turning towers into perforated lanterns. They feed on drifting larvae and are themselves swallowed by nearly everything large enough to take them.

Near the ground live the mud-breathers.

Most are never seen entire. Their mouths stipple the plazas, round and muscular, fringed with sensory hairs. Each mouth belongs to a long animal folded many times in a burrow beneath it. They draw in sediment, digest the bacterial film coating every grain, and push the cleaned mud out through a second opening several body-lengths away. Over years, whole courtyards pass through them. They bury small ruins, uncover old ones, undermine walls, and raise low embankments that redirect the city’s traffic. When poisoned water arrives, they seal themselves below with plugs of mucus and clay. Some remain closed for months. Their return is announced by rings of fresh black pellets around the old mouths.

Among them walk shell-bearers with many jointed legs and no fixed adult shape. A young shell-bearer begins inside a delicate spiral case, but as it grows it abandons this shell for any hollow object of suitable size: a dead colony chamber, the skull-cup of a fish, a mineral tube, the calcified brood pouch of another species. Its abdomen is soft and asymmetrical, molded by successive homes. Older individuals are walking accumulations of the city. Their borrowed chambers become encrusted with grazing animals, eggs, bacterial gardens, and parasitic threads. Some carry so much life that the original bearer can barely move beneath it.

They gather at constrictions in the roads. There, cleaner shrimp climb over them in pale crowds.

The shrimp are blind, but blindness has not made them solitary. They recognize one another by touch and dissolved scent. A cleaner station may contain tens of thousands, packed into wall hollows and hanging beneath ledges by their rear limbs. When a large animal approaches, the front ranks extend their antennae and sweep its skin. If it releases the proper chemical, they swarm over it. They enter gill chambers, pry parasites from folds, trim diseased tissue, drink from infected wounds, and polish the hard margins of shells.

They do not serve peacefully. Too many shrimp can strip healthy skin. A weakened visitor may be cleaned to death. At old stations, the mud below is full of tiny bones.

The towers themselves are alive, though not in any simple way.

A tower begins as a soft bud no larger than a fingernail. The bud divides but does not separate. Each new body remains attached to the others by threads carrying food and chemical alarm. One body catches particles, another secretes mineral, another broods larvae, another becomes a muscular valve. As the colony enlarges, its individuals grow increasingly specialized. Some lose their mouths. Some lose the ability to reproduce. Some become only transparent bladders that inflate and empty, drawing water through the deeper chambers.

The visible city is the residue of these lives. Beneath the mineral walls lies a thin, continuous skin of living tissue. It tastes the water, distinguishes kin from stranger, and slowly redistributes nourishment from well-fed branches to shaded ones. Its thoughts, if they can be called thoughts, are contractions. Touch one opening and the nearest chambers close. Injure a branch and bitterness spreads through the shared flesh. In a large tower the warning may take several minutes to reach the far side, passing from chamber to chamber as a darkening ripple.

Different towers behave differently. Some are nervous, shutting at the brush of drifting debris. Some tolerate armies of grazers. Some release larvae whenever the water warms by the smallest degree. Neighboring colonies may fuse if their chemical signatures are close enough. If they are not, they grow stinging borders against each other, pale ridges lined with sacrificial bodies. Such conflicts can persist for centuries. Two towers may arch together high above an avenue while continuing to poison one another along the buried seam below.

Within their walls live tenants that never enter open water.

There are colorless worms whose foreparts resemble flowering plants. Their crowns extend into ventilation currents and comb out food. Their hindparts are fixed permanently in mineral tubes. When danger approaches, they retreat so sharply that the pressure snaps their crowns shut with a sound like a seed cracking. Some have not moved from their chambers in a hundred years.

There are soft mites that graze the living skin of the colonies without quite injuring it, unless their numbers become excessive. There are flat parasites that imitate the colony’s scent and enter brood cavities unchallenged. They devour the true eggs and replace them with gelatinous sacs containing their own young. There are small, eyeless fish that spend their entire lives inside hollow towers. They are born there, mate there, and die without ever crossing an avenue. Their fins have become blunt hooks for bracing against the colony’s pumping currents. Their skin is pink from blood seen through it.

Deeper still, where the walls are warm and oxygen-poor, animals give way to quieter forms of hunger.

Bacteria grow in folded veils around the seeps. Some consume sulfur. Some feed on iron and leave red mineral sheaths behind. Some live inside larger creatures and provide much of their nourishment. The pallid ribbons near the fissures are animals as much as they are growths: mouthless bodies packed with symbiotic microbes. They absorb sulfides through their skins and deliver them to the organisms housed within. In return, the microbes manufacture food. The ribbons have neither gut nor leaves. Their roots are muscular anchors sunk into cracks. When currents strengthen, whole beds bend in one direction, and their pale surfaces appear to bow before something passing overhead.

The bacteria also cultivate citizens of their own.

On the sheltered sides of towers, red-brown films thicken into layered pastures. Blind grazers crop them in arcs, leaving clean mineral behind. The films regrow according to the flow of waste and warm water. Certain armored crawlers defend especially productive patches. They pile stones and shell fragments along the edges, not with foresight but through a territorial reflex: anything loose beneath their front limbs is pushed outward. Over generations, these reflexive movements create low enclosures. Within them the bacterial crop grows thick. Rival crawlers break the walls apart. Juveniles shelter in the rubble. The enclosure is therefore farm, fortress, nursery, and accident, depending on which animal is using it.

The broad avenues belong to the pilgrims.

These are heavy, low-bodied filter-feeders, each wider than a tower doorway. Their backs are covered in overlapping plates softened at the edges by age. They have no eyes. Around the mouth hangs a double fringe of fleshy vanes, continually folding and unfolding. They follow chemical traces laid by earlier generations and travel the same circuits between the warm central basin and the upper terraces.

A pilgrim may take years to complete one circuit. It moves by slow strokes of four fins, touching the road with a whiskered lower lip. Its passage presses the sediment flat and draws fresh water behind it. Entire neighborhoods depend on this wake. Feeding crowns emerge after it passes. Shrimp leave their stations to pick at its skin. Needlefish gather around its mouth to catch whatever escapes the vanes. Predators follow at a distance, waiting for the young, the sick, or the disoriented.

The oldest pilgrims carry maps no one can read. Scar tissue crosses their undersides where they scraped fallen arches decades before. Mineral animals grow on their armor in layered colonies. Their backs support mobile districts of worms, shell-bearers, parasites, cleaners, eggs, and grazing mites. When an old pilgrim rests in a plaza, it can be mistaken for a new mound. When it dies, the roads may bend around its body for generations.

Hunting beneath the arches are the beam-limbs.

A beam-limb consists largely of patience. Its central body is small and soft, wedged into a crevice overhead. From it extend six or eight enormously long arms strengthened by internal rods. The arms follow the contours of the architecture and darken or pale until they resemble mineral branches. Each ends in a sensitive hook. When a fish touches one, the arm does not seize it at once. Instead, the other arms shift fractionally, enclosing the space through which the prey is likely to flee. Only then do they close.

Large beam-limbs may occupy the same arch for most of their lives. They learn the rhythms of a particular avenue: when the needlefish pass, where pilgrims stir the water, which chemical alarms precede a migration. Their learning is bodily rather than reflective. Frequently used postures become easier. Unsuccessful reaches are abandoned. An old hunter fits its intersection as precisely as a valve fits a chamber.

Its young are different creatures—minute, bright, free-swimming things with rows of beating hairs. They drift through the city until they find an empty crevice. Most are eaten before they settle. For a few days after hatching, the offspring of one of the city’s quietest predators are among its brightest lights.

The squares belong to the buried rays.

They descend gently and settle into the sediment, stirring it over their backs until only paired breathing holes remain. Their bodies are broad and thin, their mouths on the underside, their eyes reduced to pigmentless pits. They detect electric activity through pores around the head. A mud-worm contracting below them, a shell-bearer walking nearby, the heartbeat of a fish resting against a wall—each appears to them as a distortion in a field.

They are not always hunters. Many sift minute animals from the mud. Others lie in the warm outflow of vents and host dense bacterial populations in grooves beneath their wings. Cleaner shrimp tend these grooves, and needlefish shelter along their margins. A resting ray becomes a temporary public structure. Animals cross it, feed on it, hide beneath it. Then, without warning, the structure lifts and swims away.

When threatened, some rays produce a pulse that convulses nearby muscles. The whole square appears to flinch. Worm crowns vanish, fish buckle, shrimp scatter, and the ray departs beneath a cloud of black sediment.

Higher on the towers cling the bell-throats.

They resemble limp translucent sacks during dormancy. When feeding, they inflate into bells with a ring of thread-fine tentacles. Their tissues contain green luminous cells. A disturbance causes one bell-throat to flash, then the next, until the warning climbs a spire in a spiral. Predators sometimes exploit this response, touching the lowest animals to illuminate prey above. The bell-throats, in turn, sometimes flash without danger. Whether these false alarms confuse hunters or are merely errors in an excitable tissue is impossible to say.

Their spawning alters the city.

At certain changes in salinity, every mature bell-throat releases a small luminous body into the water. The bodies rise, meet, and burst into clouds of gametes. For less than an hour, entire districts shine green. The towers become visible in their immensity: domes behind spires, bridges behind veils, streets continuing into sediment haze. Animals from dark quarters converge on the light. Some feed. Some mate. Some simply become active in response to the chemical abundance.

Then the glow fades, and the city’s dimensions vanish again.

Not all its inhabitants are permanent.

Armored scavengers arrive by scent after a death. They come quickly even from the suburbs, walking in single files with their front claws raised. Each is covered by a mosaic of mineral plates separated by flexible seams. They have rasping mouthparts strong enough to scrape cartilage and soft enough to comb edible bacteria from bone. Their first task is to open the body. Once the skin is breached, smaller scavengers enter through the wound: worms, mites, shrimp, translucent eels, soft-bodied borers. The carcass swells with visitors.

A large death can found a neighborhood.

Bones arrest drifting sediment. Shell-bearers shelter among the ribs. Bacterial films bloom on the released minerals. A tower colony may settle on the skull and grow upward, incorporating it into the foundation. Years later, nothing announces that the structure began as an animal except the curve of an old jaw deep inside the wall.

Disease has its own inhabitants.

Some are threadlike organisms that grow through a colony’s living skin and turn its chemical messages against it. Infected chambers refuse to close. They draw nourishment from healthy branches and produce swollen buds full of parasites. Cleaner animals graze the buds but spread fragments of them on their mouthparts. A diseased tower smells sweet in a particular way. Grazers avoid it; certain shell-bearers seek it out; predators wait nearby because confused fish often shelter in its unresponsive openings.

Other diseases are social effects without a single cause. When too many animals gather around a weakening vent, oxygen falls. The slowest creatures become still. Their bodies feed bacteria, which consume more oxygen. A quarter can pass from crowding to silence in a few hours. Survivors leave chemical traces of distress as they flee. For months afterward, travelers turn away at the boundary, even when the water has cleared.

The empty district is not truly empty. Anaerobic life continues under the mud. Parasites wait in sealed cysts. Eggs endure inside walls. Scavengers work in the dark. The quarter belongs for a time to beings that cannot tolerate the city when it is flourishing.

There are also visitors from above.

After distant floods, drowned surface matter eventually reaches the basin: wood softened beyond recognition, seeds, mats of vegetation, the bodies of animals built for air. Such falls are rare enough to transform behavior across several districts. The first arrival is detected as a shadow of chemistry, a sweetness foreign to the mineral-fed city. Needlefish gather. Pilgrims deviate from their routes. Scavengers climb towers and wait on exposed branches.

What descends may never reach the ground intact. It is intercepted, grazed, pierced, and hollowed during its slow fall. A branch from a surface tree becomes a black framework crowded with feeders. A fruit bursts into a cloudy feast. A land animal is reduced from below while still suspended, so that hide, hair, and separated bones settle in different streets.

None of the residents know where these objects come from. For them, the upper darkness occasionally bears food.

The largest inhabitant is seen least clearly.

It passes over the city during periods of warm inflow. First the needlefish extinguish themselves. Then the beam-limbs withdraw. The pilgrims settle flat against the roads. A broad pressure moves through the towers, bending curtains and turning every feeding crown in one direction.

The animal above is long enough to cover several blocks. Its body is soft, without armor, and edged by slow fins that undulate from head to tail. Lines of dim organs run along its flanks. It does not illuminate itself to see. Instead, it releases brief sheets of light into the water and reads the returning shadows with sensitive skin. Each flash reveals the city to it as pressure and interruption: towers, passages, moving schools, hollows containing prey.

It feeds infrequently. A mouth opens somewhere in its underside, not biting but widening. Water, fish, larvae, and loose sediment are drawn upward. For several seconds an avenue flows toward the sky. Then the mouth closes, the current reverses, and the animal moves on.

No one follows it. No one knows where it rests. Shed strips of its skin are found on the highest spires and eaten until only their luminous bacteria remain.

The inhabitants do not divide neatly into builders and built, rulers and ruled, wild creatures and domestic ones. A tower is an animal, a habitat, and the skeletons of its ancestors. A road is a migration remembered by bodies. A cleaner station is cooperation bordered by predation. A garden is waste that happens to be eaten. A public square is a place where rays repeatedly bury themselves. A gate is a curtain that tastes those who pass and contracts against the unfamiliar.

Even individuality becomes uncertain.

A colony contains thousands of mouths but shares food. A pilgrim carries hundreds of dependent organisms and survives because some clean its gills. A ribbon animal houses bacteria without which it would starve. A parasite borrows the chemical identity of its host. A school turns so perfectly that it seems to possess one intention, yet breaks into terrified separate bodies when struck.

The city’s citizens are therefore not the animals alone.

They are animals with their microbes, colonies with their tenants, travelers with their inherited routes, hunters fitted to particular arches, eggs waiting in dead walls, and chemical plumes extending beyond every body. Each life exceeds its skin. Each creature’s behavior becomes part of another creature’s surroundings.

No one sees the city entire. The needlefish know corridors of light. The mud-breathers know pressure and edible grains. The pilgrims know the scented roads beneath their mouths. The colonies know contact, injury, nourishment, and the slow arrival of kin. The great animal above knows a field of shapes disclosed one flash at a time.

Together, without agreement, they inhabit more than the basin.

They inhabit one another.
