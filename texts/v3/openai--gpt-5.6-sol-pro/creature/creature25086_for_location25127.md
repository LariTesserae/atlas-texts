At first the inhabitants are difficult to distinguish from the mountain.

A ridge of basalt proves to be a row of shut mouths. A scatter of pyrite nodules turns, all at once, toward an electrical flash. What appears to be a fracture fills with jointed legs and walks away. Even when the animals move, they do so with the reluctance of geology. Their camouflage is not concealment so much as kinship: they build themselves from the same iron, silica, sulfur, and glass as the crags, until predator, prey, and stone differ chiefly in appetite.

Nearest the fresh fractures live the **seam-eaters**.

They are low, broad creatures with twenty or thirty legs arranged around the rim of a shield-shaped body. Their undersides contain rasping wheels of enamel and magnetite. These do not chew rock for nourishment in the ordinary sense. They strip away films of oxidation and expose reactive mineral beneath, then flood the scratches with acidic saliva. Iron and sulfur enter solution; symbiotic chambers within the seam-eater harvest the resulting charge.

A feeding herd advances no more than a handspan in a year. Behind it, the basalt is left engraved with parallel grooves so precise they resemble tooling marks. Filterers settle in these grooves. Egg-layers use them as threads for their spiral cases. Tiny hunters run along them as if along roads.

The seam-eaters are continually threatened by the mountain’s growth. They can feel pressure accumulating in the rock through hollow mineral whiskers along their legs. When a new blade is about to emerge, the oldest animals withdraw. The young often do not. A basalt fin passes through a feeding mass over months, dividing flesh too slowly to cause shock. Some individuals crawl free on either side. Others survive separated, sealing their exposed organs behind plates and becoming two smaller animals. For them, being cut is one of the ordinary forms of reproduction.

Above these herds hang the **rib-bridges**, pale colonial creatures stretched between adjacent pinnacles. Each begins as a soft larva that attaches at two points and draws itself taut. It secretes a segmented shell around its body, one white rib at a time. As it grows, new individuals bud along its length until the original animal can no longer be identified.

The bridges filter dust and eggs from currents passing through their comb-like undersides. They also provide routes across the crags. Coin-thin swimmers shelter between their ribs; needle-legged scavengers traverse their backs; ambush animals grow inside their hollow joints. Old bridges may support so much life that they sag, break, and continue living as two colonies.

Nothing in the sea remains merely itself for long.

A shell becomes a habitat before its owner dies. A wound becomes a nursery. A predator’s jaw acquires filterers that feed upon the scraps between its teeth, then hunters that feed upon those filterers. The largest creatures carry entire moving districts.

The mill-whales carry the richest.

A mill-whale has no smooth hide. Its body is plated in overlapping black scutes, each bordered with sensory cilia that lie flat when the combustion chambers fire. Between the scutes run channels of warm, mineral-heavy water. Blue salt-lickers crowd these channels in such numbers that an old whale appears furred in cobalt.

Its head is blunt and ringed with intake valves. Behind them, muscular sacs compress water against lattices of ceramic grown from food. Reactive fluids enter from paired digestive reservoirs. Ignition is chemical rather than fiery, but the pressure stroke flashes through the whale’s translucent seams.

**Clang—thrum—thrum—THOOM.**

The first sound is the locking of internal plates. The two softer strokes are compression. The last is release.

A young whale fires raggedly, sometimes losing thrust and tumbling inward until it strikes the crowded crags. Adults learn steadier sequences, not from instruction but by proximity. Calves accompany larger animals and entrain themselves to the vibrations traveling through the water. Over years their strokes begin to match those of the surrounding circuit.

This creates loose dynasties of rhythm. Whales from one side of the mountain may fire in fours; those from another use a long-short-long sequence. When the circuits intersect, the patterns interfere. For several days the whole sea becomes arrhythmic. Then one cadence absorbs the other, and the whales separate again.

Their migrations are not searches for food alone. Embedded shafts of magnetite run through their bodies from head to tail, assembled grain by swallowed grain during youth. The shafts align with the mountain’s field and twist against surrounding tissues. The whales follow that torque as faithfully as ordinary animals might follow scent. Because the ore veins shift when the mountain grows, ancient routes slowly bend. A whale may spend its entire life correcting a turn its ancestors began centuries earlier.

They reproduce rarely. Two adults meet near a strong magnetic seam and exchange packets of mineral organs—valves, catalytic nodules, fragments of ceramic plate. Each carries the other’s contributions for several years. Eventually both produce a calf, assembling it in an external shell bolted beneath the ribs. The calf emerges already armored and scarcely larger than a hand. It feeds first on the exhaust crust of its parent.

When a mill-whale dies, it does not sink from the life of the sea. Inward gravity draws it against the central crags, where scavengers enter through the valves. The combustion chambers may continue firing weakly for months, driven by fermenting tissues and involuntary contractions. Colonies claim the ceramic linings. Gear-shells establish themselves around the drive shafts. Within a century little remains but the magnetic spine, still aligned toward a route no living body follows.

The **gear-shells** grow wherever currents are dependable.

They resemble coiled mollusks until they open. Inside each shell is a toothed annulus surrounding a crystal hub. Mantle tissue secretes every tooth separately, correcting flaws over decades by dissolving and regrowing microscopic edges. Once the ring is complete, ciliated vanes catch the water and set it turning.

The motion is exceedingly slow. A large ring may complete one rotation in nine days. Yet it does not stop. Mineral grit enters through one aperture and leaves through another as fine powder. The colony’s larvae absorb this powder into transparent skeletons.

A mature gear-shell is usually not a single animal but a succession. The soft founder dies long before the ring is finished. Its offspring inherit both shell and labor, occupying chambers within the outer coil. Fifty or sixty generations may contribute to one mechanism without any generation seeing it begin or reach completion.

Around the conductive ore veins stand the **storm-stacks**.

They are sessile colonies formed from hundreds of flat, translucent animals pressed together like panes of cloudy glass. Each pane maintains a different ionic concentration. Current from the mountain passes through them in stages, feeding their metabolism and charging mineral nodules along their edges.

When a stack can hold no more, it discharges.

Blue light forks through the water. Nearby creatures contract before the report arrives. Those requiring electrical maturation gather close despite the danger. Their hard cases crack under the arc, exposing a second body beneath the first. Others are killed and cooked where they cling, becoming food for the storm-stack’s scavengers.

One minute animal, the **spark mite**, cannot attain adulthood by any other means. Its juvenile form is a metallic paste that lives in the rotating joints of larger creatures. It reduces friction and consumes flakes of worn tissue. After years or decades, chemical changes in the host cause the paste to bead together and crawl toward the skin. Thousands of silver droplets emerge and migrate to a storm-stack. At the next discharge they fuse into needle-legged adults, which mate in the fading electric field and dissolve after laying their liquid young in new joints.

Farther from the crags, where the water opens, live the **glass kites**.

Their broad bodies are nearly invisible except for silica spars and the slow passage of food through branching guts. They do not swim against the inward pull. They tack across it, adjusting the angle of their fins to curved currents around the sphere. One surface of the kite is adapted to water, the other to the shifting boundary where water ends in sky.

Along that outer surface are rows of pressure-sensitive cups. They taste rain before it arrives. During storms the kites gather where droplets strike most heavily and open mouths as wide as their bodies. Rainwater is poor in metals but rich in gases and foreign dust. For the inhabitants of the closed sea, every storm is an importation of another world.

Small creatures travel on the kites’ skyward sides. Some are armored against drying; some seal themselves inside droplets held by hooked limbs. They look outward upon clouds, sun, and distant snowfields but possess no eyes capable of forming those sights. They know the exterior as temperature, ultraviolet injury, pressure, and the arrival of rare molecules.

At the black spires that pierce the boundary, the **wind-backs** face open air.

A wind-back may remain fixed to one place for longer than a human life. Its front half is submerged; its armored rear rises from the sea and unfolds corrugated heat-exchange leaves. Moisture condenses in the folds. Oxygen passes through thin metallic membranes into dark circulating fluid. In strong wind the leaves vibrate, producing tones too low to hear in air but clearly transmitted through the animal’s anchored body.

Other wind-backs answer by altering the stiffness of their leaves. Over days, a chord passes around the punctures of the sea. Whether this is communication is uncertain. The pattern also regulates blood flow, shakes loose ice, discourages parasites, and warns submerged young of approaching storms. Here, no function remains singular.

The swiftest animals are not truly swift. **Shear-coins** move in silver schools through gaps in the central crowd, their flat bodies turning edge-on whenever space narrows. Each has a cutting rim and a soft mouth at its center. A school surrounds the extended feeding organs of a larger animal and clips them away one by one. It may take hours for the victim to retract them. By then the coins have passed into another crevice.

Their principal hunters are **fan-jaws**, folded creatures that anchor near mill-whale outlets. A fan-jaw waits with its mouth collapsed into a narrow rod. When an exhaust stroke throws stunned swimmers past, the rod opens into a circular net of teeth. Closing takes nearly an hour. Escape remains possible for much of that time, but the disoriented prey often cannot find the narrowing path.

The central dusk belongs to the **lamp-skulls**.

They are long, soft predators with dense cranial plates and cavities lined in reflective crystal. Chemical light generated deep in the skull is directed outward through a slit, producing a flat white beam. The animal sweeps this beam over the crags by rotating its entire body around an anchored tail.

It hunts responses rather than silhouettes. Shell colonies close when illuminated. Flexible prey recoil. Metallic bodies flash. The lamp-skull remembers these disturbances in a chain of pressure bladders running along its spine. On the next sweep, hours later, it compares the pattern. Anything that has changed position is approached.

Its mind, if the word applies, is slow and spatial. It may deliberate for a day before biting. A particularly old lamp-skull can memorize a large district of the mountain: every rib-bridge, every storm-stack, every inhabited crack. Mountain growth continually makes its memory obsolete. New basalt enters the beam; old landmarks divide or are buried. The animal responds with prolonged agitation, illuminating the same impossible geometry again and again until its internal map stretches to admit the change.

There is no sovereign species here, no builder standing apart from what is built. Yet the entire population produces an order larger than any inhabitant.

Mill-whales pump the water. Gear-shells reduce stone. Seam-eaters expose reactive surfaces. Storm-stacks direct current through living layers. Kites harvest the boundary. Wind-backs take breath from the sky. Every waste stream enters another body; every corpse becomes equipment.

They do not recognize this as cooperation. Most would consume one another given the opportunity. But escape is difficult, matter is finite, and the mountain supplies energy more readily than substance. Thus nothing useful can remain discarded. Hunger closes every loop.

The oldest inhabitant may not be an animal at all, but a circulation: a burden of iron that has passed through thousands of bodies. It has been tooth, blood, shell, catalyst, egg blade, magnetic spine, and grinding ring. It remembers nothing. Still, its route describes the sea more faithfully than any map.

Around the fresh black blades, life gathers first as dust that moves contrary to the water. Then as films made entirely of crowded microscopic animals. Then as mouths, joints, shells, and turning mineral cores. Within a few decades the new rock is hidden. Within a few centuries it rings with labor.

And beneath that labor, another seam begins to open.
