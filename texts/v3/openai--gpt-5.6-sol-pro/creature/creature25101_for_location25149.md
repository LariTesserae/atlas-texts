At first the inhabitants seem to be trees.

They stand in the middle of the basin, silver-ribbed and hollow, older than anything around them. But they are not quite a grove of separate trunks. Their roots have met below the peat, their branches have grafted overhead, and wounds along their sides have sealed against neighboring bark. What appears to be a ring of elders is one interrupted body—or several bodies that no longer preserve the distinction. Each hollow bole contains its own dry chamber, its own banks of sleeping buds, yet water, minerals, and the thin products of sunlight pass among them.

They live slowly enough for weather to become anatomy. A century of fog has thickened their crowns. Old snowfalls persist as bent limbs and healed fractures. Where one trunk failed, the others drew across the opening, so the dead column remains upright inside a casing of younger wood. Their hollows exhale in thaw weather, warmed by decay. From outside, this resembles breath. It is only vapor, fungi, and the release of cold stored in the trunk—but “only” is not a useful word here.

Around these central beings grow the light-seekers: tall braids of stems that have climbed by embracing, leaning, and sometimes strangling. No single stem could withstand the ridge winds, so they rise in bundles. The outermost take the frost. The innermost remain supple and transport water. At their tips they divide into black-green needles and shallow leaves, all turned toward the few bright openings that travel across the sky.

These tower plants are the basin’s hungriest inhabitants. They climb over roofs, root into walls, and draw support from whatever they overtake. Yet they also feed what lies beneath them. Their fused tissues carry sugars downward into shaded districts where no leaf receives enough light to sustain itself. Some lower plants may not have seen direct sun for generations. They live by attachment: pale shoots pressed against vascular seams, buds opening in darkness, rootless chambers kept alive by the surplus of the canopy.

So it is difficult to say whether the towers are rulers, providers, or parasites. They are all three, changing roles from branch to branch.

Farther out live the low ones, formed by wind into another kind of life. Cushion plants grip the scree in dense colonies, each colony looking like one soft organism until frost opens it and reveals thousands of tiny stems. Their exposed surfaces are hard, dark, and close-fitted. Inside, among dead leaves and trapped dust, they preserve pockets of damp warmth. Their oldest centers have become soil. Their youngest edges advance over stone by fractions of a finger each year.

Horizontal conifers lie among them, not fallen but deliberately shaped downward. Their trunks creep behind ramparts of their own branches. Needles overlap like scales, and snow presses them more tightly together rather than breaking them. A branch that touches wet ground often roots; a rooted branch eventually forgets which trunk produced it. Whole terraces may therefore be descendants of a single tree, although no central trunk survives.

The violet reeds inhabit the chambers between.

They are not reeds in the manner of valley marshes. Their stems are short-jointed, hollow, and packed with pale insulating tissue. A solitary one would freeze. In stands, they share walls. The outer stems die first and remain in place as a palisade; the next generation grows inside that dead enclosure. Leaves meet overhead, making dim rooms in which seedlings, spores, mites, and beads of water wait out the cold.

A reed quarter is made of generations inhabiting one another. The living stand occupies a house built from its ancestors, while its young begin inside its own body. Beneath it lie crushed chambers, each still recognizable by a curved roof of stems and a floor of compacted moss. Roots descend through these former rooms as through buried streets. The reeds knock together in freezing weather, passing small dry sounds from wall to wall.

Moss lives everywhere that the larger inhabitants fail to close completely.

There are many mosses, though from above they make one continuous pelt. Some are dark and saturated, storing water among their leaves. Some are nearly white and grow over ice, their filaments scattering what little light reaches them. Some make soft floors in protected courts. Others advance over damaged roofs in tight fronts, each new shoot rising from the last as if the colony were measuring its conquest.

The red moss is the most conspicuous aggressor. It does not hurry, but it keeps a remarkably straight edge. It crosses weak bark, enters needle roofs, and seals the pores through which smaller plants breathe. Behind its frontier, old structures soften into peat. Its color deepens when wet, so during rain its occupied districts appear bruised.

Lichens inhabit the harder surfaces: blue crusts on wet stone, gray plates on bark, black grains along the margins of old ice. They are partnerships within the greater partnership, composite beings whose members would fare poorly alone. They eat the mountain with acids too weak to notice and too persistent to resist. A dark ridge fragment falling into the vegetation may disappear first beneath moss, but it is the lichens that begin opening the stone grain by grain.

Fungi live beneath nearly all of them.

Their visible caps are brief accidents. The true inhabitants are the fine pale threads passing through root, peat, feather, dung, and dead wood. They enter a fallen branch before its last living cells have ceased working. They lace needles into walls. They soften the keratin of lost feathers and dismantle the tiny bodies of midges after thaw season.

Their underground networks do more than consume. They carry phosphorus from one terrace to another, draw nitrogen out of old chambers, and connect shaded seedlings to the roots of established towers. They are roads, merchants, extortionists, and weather. A grove well joined to them may receive what it cannot reach; a grove cut off may starve while standing a handspan from abundance. The fungi favor no permanent district. Their alliances follow moisture, sugars, and damage.

Some of the plants cultivate them with secretions from their roots. Others wall them out with bitter resins. Still others permit them to enter only certain cells, holding them in intimate confinement while taking what they bring. Beneath the apparent communal peace, these negotiations never stop.

Water is not alive, but the inhabitants treat it almost as if it were livestock being passed from household to household. Upper leaves hold it. Cups store it. Bark channels lead it down. Moss releases it slowly. Root lattices divide it according to use and accident. Tiny aquatic films line the narrow streets, inhabited by organisms too small to give the water color: transparent grazers, dormant cysts, threads of algae that green only when briefly exposed.

Among them move the black snails. They are scarcely larger than seeds and polished by constant wet. Their shells are dark enough to warm in weak light, though direct light rarely reaches them. They graze films from the channels and retreat into fissures when the water rises. In winter they seal themselves against bark or stone and remain there, indistinguishable from droplets of pitch. Moss sometimes grows over their shells. Those that wake must push through a roof formed during their sleep.

White midges belong to the brief season. For most of the year they exist as pale larvae in saturated fibers or as pupae fixed beneath leaves. Then a thaw releases them almost together. Adults gather under the vaults, where the trapped air may be a degree warmer, and form weak, wavering clouds. They do not rise above the canopy. Their whole sky is the underside of leaves.

They mate, lay eggs in water seams, and die within days. Their bodies adhere to wet hairs and are absorbed. The basin spends nothing extravagantly, not even a wing.

Birds are visitors rather than citizens. A mountain bird may descend after seeing what looks like a sheltered grove, only to find no branch clear enough for landing. The canopy accepts weight but not intrusion. Twigs bend around the bird’s feet; hooked shoots catch at feathers; moss gives way without providing ground. Most birds leave quickly. A few do not. Their bones lie deep in the peat, distributed by fungi and roots until flight becomes bark, needle, spore, and leaf.

The most distinct inhabitant—the one that preserves a private interior and moves by its own decision—is the ash-colored burrower.

It is a small animal, though the narrowness of its world makes it seem larger. Its coat is dense and without shine, matching dry root fiber and old frost. Its ears are reduced to furred folds. The forefeet are broad, with dark nails suited less to excavating soil than to separating woven roots. Its eyes are usable but small. It navigates chiefly by whiskers, scent, and the changing pressure of air in connected chambers.

It does not travel through the city as a person travels streets. It moves inside the architecture.

The animal finds old reed rooms, hollow trunks, gaps above buried ice, and spaces left where dead wood has collapsed. It enlarges these with its teeth, clipping young roots before they can seal the way. Root tips are its principal food: tender, mineral-rich, and constantly renewed. It also eats fungi, dormant buds, snail eggs, and the softened remains of midges. At certain seasons it gnaws the inner bark of the towers, an injury the plants answer by flooding the wound with resin and sending fine roots toward the disturbance.

Its nests are pockets of dry fiber in the warmest ruins. There it gathers shed pith, fungal felt, fur pulled from its own flanks, and the translucent hairs of alpine leaves. A nest may be used by many generations until roots penetrate it. Then it becomes another compact layer in the wall.

The burrower is the basin’s maker of vacancies. Everything else fills, binds, thickens, and closes. The animal cuts openings. Its passages admit air into peat, drain trapped water, and allow warmth to move between chambers. Seedlings sometimes germinate in the soil it disturbs. Fungal bodies rise from its abandoned nests. For a little while after it passes, there is room.

The city resists this gift.

Roots sense the broken surfaces and branch. Moss creeps across exposed fiber. Reeds angle new shoots into the tunnel. Some passages remain open because the animal uses them often; others narrow between visits. It knows the settlement as a shifting set of pressures: this corridor easy, that one damp, another newly closed. It must continually revise its memory.

Sometimes the revision comes too late. A thaw swells roots around a sleeping chamber. A tunnel freezes at both ends. A wall that yielded yesterday is rigid today. The animal scratches, chews, waits, and listens. In spring there may be a muted sound within the living masonry. If it stops, the city opens the small body and takes it inward. Fungi enter the mouth and eyes. Roots gather around the bones. No grave remains, only a patch of unusual growth.

There may be very few of these animals—perhaps only several family lines distributed through the basin, meeting by scent more often than by sight. Their territories overlap vertically: one among the recent reed chambers, another deep in the warm black peat, another following the underside of the ice courts. They announce themselves with tooth marks, clipped roots, pellets left in dry corners, and sudden tremors felt through walls.

To the plants, the burrower is injury, pruning, transport, and heat. Seeds cling to its coat. Spores pass unharmed through its gut. Its tunnels redirect water. Its breath condenses on chamber roofs. Even its hungers become part of the basin’s architecture.

No inhabitant, however, belongs wholly to itself.

The old boles contain fungi. The fungi contain bacteria. Lichen bodies house two or three separate lineages in one skin. Moss holds sleeping eggs. Snail shells bear algae. The burrower carries spores in its fur and a private wilderness in its gut. Dead reeds shelter living seedlings; living roots travel through dead rooms. Individual bodies overlap until the city’s true resident begins to seem neither plant nor animal nor colony.

It is the exchange among them.

It lives in the sugar descending from the towers, in minerals lifted from freshly split stone, in a feather becoming fungal tissue, in a tunnel becoming a watercourse and then a root. It has no place where it thinks, no organ from which it commands. It can starve one quarter while feeding another. It can crush its mobile inhabitants and then build from them. It competes with itself, consumes itself, shelters itself, and passes the results inward.

The basin’s inhabitants are therefore countless, but their way of living is singular: each survives by becoming part of the conditions under which something else can survive.

They are not peaceful. They are not purposeful. They do not know they resemble a city.

But together they behave like one—an inward-growing creature with towers for leaves, streets for veins, fungi for commerce, buried chambers for memory, and the young mountain in its mouth.
