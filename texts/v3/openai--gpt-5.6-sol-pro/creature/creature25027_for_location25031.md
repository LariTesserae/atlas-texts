At first, the inhabitants of the Stillworks appear to be the maintenance families.

They are narrow people—not always in body, but in habit. They turn sideways before reaching constrictions, duck beneath pipes that cleared their grandparents’ heads, and place each foot as though the floor might remember an old injury. Even above, in open rooms, they seldom spread their arms. Children born to the families learn early that empty space is usually reserved for something that moves, leaks, or falls.

They dress in layers of thin fabric beneath waxed aprons. Thick clothing catches on bolt heads and drinks too much condensation. Their gloves have bare fingertips. Their boots are soft-soled and wrapped at the ankle so no loose lace can be taken by a chain. Each carries a lamp with shutters, wax chalk in several colors, a brass listening rod, wedges, cord, and a small hammer whose handle is marked with generations of teeth.

The hammer is used sparingly.

There are fewer of them than the records imply. Family names persist after bloodlines fail because tools, duties, and routes are inherited along with names. A young worker apprenticed to the Vale rack may become Vale regardless of birth. Someone entrusted with the western pumps takes the surname Sigh. Those who watch the Receiver are called Mere, though they do not watch it often and never alone.

They speak quietly, but not from fear of waking anything. Loud speech conceals small sounds. A shouted warning can be useful once; the missed crack beneath it may matter for thirty years.

Their hearing is unusual. They distinguish water striking copper from water striking iron, a cooling seam from a tightening brace, an empty pipe from one whose contents have merely stopped moving. They listen through rods pressed to their jawbones and describe what they hear in terms that sound almost social: a bearing is lonely, a tank evasive, a wall troubled in its sleep. These are not superstitions to them. They are categories as exact as pressure or temperature.

A good keeper can identify a chamber in darkness by the taste left on the teeth.

They do not repair the Stillworks so much as negotiate with it. A leak is moved before it is sealed. A weight is persuaded into a new path. A failing support receives a brace, and the brace receives another brace when the first begins to bend. Nothing is forced back to an earlier shape. Earlier shapes are considered dangerous abstractions.

Among them, impatience is treated as a kind of deafness.

Their children are not brought below until they can stand still for one hundred drops. On the first visit, each child is shown a safe pool, a live cable, a false floor, and a thing whose purpose no one knows. The order varies, but the unknown thing is always last. They are told to look closely and never invent a use merely because ignorance is uncomfortable.

Some become keepers. Others refuse the heat, the stooping, and the inherited obligation to grease machines that may no longer serve anyone. Those who leave often find the inhabited levels offensively loud. They complain that ordinary buildings do not tell you when they are failing.

The oldest keepers acquire pale scars across the shoulders and forearms where warm metal has kissed them. Their hands become stippled by rust that no washing removes. Many lose high tones in one ear while retaining, with uncanny clarity, the ability to hear the low shudder preceding a pressure change. In retirement they sit near the upper hatch and identify events below from vibrations in the kettle.

They rarely die in the Stillworks. When one does, the body is carried out by the route it knew best. No memorial plaque is mounted below; plaques become edges, edges catch sleeves, and sentiment is not permitted to create a hazard. Instead, the dead person’s most reliable route is added to the maps under their name.

Thus a keeper may continue for centuries as a sequence of instructions:

*Take Ossa’s turn beneath the red line. Use Bren where the grating dips. Do not follow Elian after warm rain.*

But the families are only the most visible inhabitants.

The pale moths live deeper in the district’s fabric. Their larvae graze on old insulation, carving galleries through felted fiber and mineral wool. They emerge without apparent season. For months there may be none; then a hot casing will open along a rusted seam and release hundreds, soft as ash, each with wings too weak for sustained flight.

They do not seek flame. They seek difference. A lamp in darkness is difference enough, so they gather around it, striking the hood with faint dry taps. Their bodies carry fibers from one nest to another until they seem less like insects than small pieces of the Stillworks coming loose.

Where moths gather, the pipe lizards wake.

No keeper claims to have seen an entire pipe lizard at once. The Stillworks does not offer enough open distance. One sees toes flattening against a vertical tank, the folded translucence beneath a jaw, a tail drawn into a collar of pipes. Their skin is white only at first glance. Held in lamplight it shows faint maps of blue vessels and dark interior organs, with metallic flecks gathered beneath the scales.

They grow slowly and shed rarely. Their cast skins remain glued to warm surfaces, so delicate that they are mistaken for mineral film. Some are no longer than a hand. Others, judging by the sections glimpsed passing between obstructions, may be longer than a person.

They have no voices, but occupied pipes sometimes produce a soft, rapid ticking unlike contraction. The families call this “lizard rain.” It is considered rude to investigate.

A pipe lizard can remain motionless for days with its mouth open beside a moth seam. Its lidless eyes are covered by a clear secondary skin that clouds before warm rain. For this reason the keepers watch them when they can. A lizard moving upward foretells flooding below. A lizard crossing cold metal means heat is returning. A lizard drinking means the pool is safe, though no one has seen the instant when its tongue touches water.

There are smaller lives as well.

Transparent mites inhabit the rust moss and turn orange as they feed. Threadworms knot themselves inside condensation drops and uncoil when shadow passes over them. Blind silver things, neither fish nor insect, flick through the shallow channels during district-wide rain and vanish when the channels dry. The black algae beneath old leaks withdraw from heat, leaving thin bare crescents that can be read like tide marks.

The Orchard has its own population. Tiny gray aphids cling to the undersides of its five or six leaves. They are born there, feed there, and fall eventually onto the hot machinery below. No winged form has ever been found. How they reached the vine is one of the few questions the keepers allow themselves to discuss at length.

Then there are the sealed tanks.

Officially, nothing lives in them.

The keepers repeat this exactly, often enough that it has become less a statement than a boundary. The knocks are transferred pressure. The intervals are mechanical. Three equal blows signify nothing beyond valves, trapped condensate, and old lines answering one another through the walls.

Nevertheless, no keeper answers.

Some tanks knock only after being passed. Some wait until a worker has set down a tool, then reproduce the sound—one strike for each contact of metal on metal. One tank in the eastern tangle has knocked in groups of four since before the current maps. Another answered three for decades, fell silent when Keeper Arven died, and has not sounded since.

No one cuts them open. Cutting creates heat, sparks, and obligations.

At the Receiver, life becomes harder to name.

The clear water supports no visible algae. Moths that fall into it reach the edge despite making no movement. Condensation gathers on the surrounding rods in beads that descend, hesitate near the bottom, and sometimes climb again. Keepers who remain too long report hearing breathing, but never in the room. It seems to come through their own chests, slightly out of time with them.

The square in the basin is not discussed as an opening. Nor is it called a machine. The Mere keepers record its apparent dimensions once each year without crossing the water. The measurements vary by fractions. Whether the square changes or the room shifts around it is considered an unhelpful distinction.

There may be something beneath it. There may be nothing beneath it and something within the water. There may only be the final behavior of a system so old and entangled that its processes resemble appetite.

For the greatest inhabitant of the Stillworks is the Stillworks itself—not because it thinks, necessarily, nor because it possesses a hidden heart, but because everything living there has learned to treat it as a body.

It has warm regions and cold ones. It swallows water and redistributes weight. It heals badly, incorporating splints into bone. It sheds rust, grows mineral shells, carries colonies in its insulation, and speaks through contractions too slow for ordinary hearing. Its dead parts support its living parts. Its failures migrate. Its smallest motions can take a human lifetime to complete.

The keepers tend it as parasites might tend a host, or as cells might tend a body whose whole shape they cannot know. The moths digest its lining. The algae color its dampness. The lizards move through its casings like pale impulses along nerves. Even the knocks, whatever their cause, travel from chamber to chamber as if the sealed vessels were testing whether they remain connected.

Nothing there lives independently. Not the vine with roots in a manufactured shaft. Not the lizard drinking from a pool made by a warped floor. Not the families whose surnames are routes through machinery. Every life in the Stillworks is attached to some obsolete function, feeding upon its residue or preserving it without understanding.

And perhaps that is what the district gathers now.

Not water alone, but dependence. Habit. Small continuities.

Drop by drop, name by name, it collects them.
