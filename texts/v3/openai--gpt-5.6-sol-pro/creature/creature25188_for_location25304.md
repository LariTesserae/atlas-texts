What lives in the Low Pocket is not best understood as a collection of species.

The names given to its animals—runner, shieldback, threader, clamper—describe temporary solutions to local problems. They are names for occupations and silhouettes, not necessarily bloodlines. A brood taken from beneath one mineral scale may mature into three or four different forms depending on what reaches it first: heat, current, fresh metal, powdered insulation, or the chemical trace of a predator. Two creatures that appear identical may have no common ancestry within a hundred generations. Two that appear unrelated may have unfolded from capsules shed by the same parent.

The Pocket does not divide life cleanly.

Its commonest inhabitant is the slate runner. From standing height, the runners are a dark granular motion, broadening and narrowing like smoke laid flat against the ground. Close up, each is a low chain of interlocking plates with between eight and twenty legs. The number changes over its lifetime. Legs wear down, detach, and are replaced from dormant buds along the underside. Old runners often have mismatched limbs: hook feet for climbing on one side, flat pads for crossing glass on the other, a scavenged ceramic spur where a softer appendage once was.

They possess no obvious head. Either end may lead. A runner encountering an obstacle pauses only long enough for its plates to ripple, reversing the direction of all its joints. Its feeding combs lie along both flanks and rake the mineral pavement as it travels. These combs collect carbon dust, salts, conductive films, fragments of wax, and the reflective specks that feed at charged edges. What cannot be digested is pressed into the outer plates. Thus the oldest runners resemble the ground over which they have traveled. Some carry softened lettering on their backs: half an **R**, the foot of a **7**, a white line that once belonged to a warning symbol.

Runners gather into streams not from sociability but because each animal prefers to place its feet in the faintly warmed impressions of the one ahead. A single runner leaves almost no trail. Ten thousand create a road. Their migrations braid, divide, and recombine around obstacles, and the road itself slowly migrates as heat moves beneath the floor.

At intervals, one stops.

The others pass over it until it vanishes under the stream. Beneath them, the stopped runner unlocks its plates. The body comes apart without dying. Each segment pulls free on tiny provisional legs and disappears into the traffic. Some segments become young runners. Others harden into shieldbacks or climb upward and hang beneath ledges, beginning the pale elongation that produces threaders. What appears to be birth is often disassembly. What appears to be predation is sometimes a change of form.

The shieldbacks occupy the world under the paving scales.

They are scarcely larger than grains, but their front plates are disproportionately wide and shaped to match the curvature of the cracks they inhabit. When threatened, they drive themselves farther beneath the stone, levering upward with their backs. Large colonies can make a whole patch of floor whisper as thousands lift and release the scales in sequence.

The space under a single hand-sized fragment may contain generations of them arranged in layers. The warmest, most active individuals cling to the underside of the scale. Beneath these are the feeders, scraping at salts and trapped conductive dust. Lower still are sealed capsules and rigid old bodies incorporated into the colony wall. At the bottom lie the flat, dark founders, alive only during strong pulses of current. Some may not move for years. When awakened, they chew upward through their descendants.

Shieldbacks are among the few inhabitants that defend fixed ground. Their colonies recognize neither kin nor individual, but they recognize the chemical polish of their own traffic. An intruding shieldback is pinned, stripped of its joint wax, and built into the floor of the chamber. Colony boundaries are therefore marked by bands of immobilized bodies. Under magnification these borders resemble black masonry.

Above the pavement hang the threaders.

A threader begins as a compact animal and becomes less substantial as it grows. Its central body remains a small pale hinge, while its limbs lengthen into cords many times the body’s size. These cords contain almost no muscle. They move through differential contraction of wax-filled chambers, slowly curling and uncurling with changes in temperature.

Threaders suspend themselves from beams, pipe undersides, rustless bolts, and the hardened towers of old nest material. Several limbs serve as anchors. The others descend into the traffic. At their tips are paired mouthparts shaped like folding pliers.

They do not see prey. They detect interruptions in the minute static discharge between the hanging limb and the floor. When something passes beneath, the threader closes along its entire length. The capture travels upward as a rapid sequence of bends, the way a loose chain might leap when pulled from one end. Small prey reaches the mouth intact. Larger prey is held against the ceiling and grazed gradually by several threaders at once.

Old individuals can span an entire passage. Their limbs cross those of their neighbors and become impossible to assign. In some chambers the ceiling appears draped with pale fibers, all trembling independently. There may be hundreds of threaders there, or only one.

The copper mites live where current is strongest.

Each has a many-faceted shell of conductive plates separated by dark insulating seams. Alone, a mite is erratic, continually turning and tapping the surface with a forked forelimb. In a colony, the mites lock edge to edge until they form a flexible sheet. Charge moves across this sheet faster than any individual can travel. The colony responds to the electrical field as a whole: contracting around faults, spreading across warm conductors, or lifting its outer ranks into ridges when the potential changes.

These colonies are the nearest thing the Pocket has to pasture.

Reflective specks collect along their margins. Runners graze over their backs. Pale attendants probe between their plates for residues. Clampers settle nearby. The mites tolerate this traffic until too much of the colony’s charge is drained. Then the sheet convulses. Thousands release their grip at once and leap outward in a metallic spray.

A person touching such a colony would not receive a dangerous shock. The sensation would be smaller and more intimate: innumerable dry pinches followed by numbness, while mites arranged themselves along the ridges of the fingerprints.

In the ceramic hollows are the barrel grazers.

They move with deliberate slowness, rolling their ribbed bodies through accumulations of powder. Their mouths form continuous rings around one end, but feeding usually occurs at the other. Dust enters through pores between the ribs, passes through stacked electrochemical membranes, and is sorted. Useful compounds are retained. Waste is compacted into a hard central rod.

When this rod grows too long, the grazer divides around it. The two halves pull apart, leaving the rod exposed between them like an axle. Smaller animals descend immediately to strip it. The grazer halves seal, each becoming a complete, shorter animal.

Barrel grazers are followed by bead-creatures that collect what leaks from their pores. The beads are glossy, black, and nearly spherical. They possess retractable legs but often prefer to travel by rolling. At the chain trench they gather in large herds and wait for a pressure wave. An instant before the chain moves, every bead folds its legs inward. The vibration sends them tumbling along the trench among grit and shed armor. They unfold at the next warm patch, frequently several body lengths from where they began.

Their apparent playfulness is deceptive. A bead unable to find movement will roll itself against a slope for hours, spending more energy than walking would require. Motion through impact is part of its development. Without repeated shocks, its inner layers fail to separate and it matures into a sterile, heavy form that other beads eventually hollow out for shelter.

The clampers wait where all these paths narrow.

A closed clamper resembles two or three fragments of dark machine casing wedged together. Its limbs fit into grooves along its armor. Dust settles on it. Runners cross its back. Shieldbacks establish temporary chambers under its edges. Only the jaw seam betrays it, and even that may be mistaken for a crack in stone.

It waits by feeling the traffic through braced legs. It ignores isolated movement. A single animal is not worth the expenditure. What opens the clamper is density: the compound vibration of a migration compressed between obstacles.

Then its plates lift.

The strike is less like a bite than the closing of a tool. The jaws meet with a sound that travels through the pavement. Several creatures may be shattered at once. The clamper remains locked while attendants arrive to extract edible material from the broken shells and push it toward the narrow feeding slots behind the jaws.

The attendants are tolerated because a clamper cannot clean its own hinge recesses. Their pale bodies fit precisely between its plates. They remove grit, salts, and parasites, coating the joints with oils gathered from prey. They also eat the clamper’s sensory hairs when other food is scarce. A neglected clamper jams; an overattended one becomes blind to vibration. It regulates them by occasionally closing one secondary jaw. The crushed attendant is consumed by the others.

Clampers grow by replacing themselves from the inside. Beneath the visible armor is a smaller set of folded plates. When the outer body becomes too worn, the animal anchors itself and splits along its sides. The inner clamper pulls free, soft-jointed and dangerously bright. Attendants immediately dismantle the abandoned shell. During the few minutes before the new plates darken, every hunter in the area can detect it.

For this reason, clampers molt during machine starts.

The impact and confusion conceal them. Fresh industrial surfaces appear at the same time, drawing scavengers elsewhere. A chain movement or relay strike that kills thousands of smaller animals may permit nearly every adult clamper in a district to renew its armor.

Higher up live the hook-legged rafter animals.

They travel inverted, their bodies held close to pipes by six or more recurved limbs. They are narrow enough to disappear behind a cable. Their backs are matte gray, but their undersides are striped with polished plates that flicker as they move. During the dim part of the power cycle, these flashes are the only sign of them.

A rafter animal produces no brood chamber. Instead, one of its hooks swells, seals at the base, and drops away. The animal continues with one fewer leg. Inside the fallen hook is a folded juvenile wrapped around a packet of salts and stable oil.

Most hooks collect in cold cracks and never open. A heat front wakes them all at once. Their outer shells spring apart, releasing juveniles on long temporary filaments. The young are almost weightless, more sail than body. Air stirred by the warm pulse carries them through the chamber.

For a short time, the Low Pocket’s ecology turns upward. Threaders draw in their limbs and seize the drifting young. Runners rear against stones to rake them from the air. Copper mite colonies lift conductive spines that attract the filaments. Clampers, unable to follow, strike at shadows passing over them.

Those juveniles that reach the ceiling consume their own filaments and harden into new hooks. Many attach not to pipe or stone but to adult rafter animals. The adult may tolerate several until they compete for the same grip. Then a slow contest begins, each animal attempting to pry the other loose without releasing the ceiling itself. The loser falls into the floor traffic and is ordinarily gone before it can right itself.

When the western fan turns, sailbacks emerge.

They have been folded beneath overhangs, their broad dorsal plates collapsed into pleats. In the wind they climb exposed bolts and face the grille. Their backs open into porous fans, mineral-gray at first, then darkening as solvent traces and reactive powder collect within them.

A sailback does not eat while the wind blows. It stores.

Afterward it retreats to shelter and closes its plates around the captured material. Its internal surfaces react slowly, producing heat and usable compounds for many hours. Other creatures gather around it. Some scrape harmless excess from the outer pores. Others drill through the armor to reach the concentrated interior.

The predators of sailbacks are long, narrow animals called seamers. A seamer’s body consists of two parallel rails joined by flexible crosspieces. It lays itself along the edge of a sailback’s plate and advances by drawing its rails alternately forward. Its mouth is not at either end but runs as a shallow groove down its full length. It cuts a line through armor and drinks the wax that rises into the wound.

A sailback attacked by seamers cannot easily flee. Instead it closes with the predators still attached, crushing some and sealing others into its own body. Old sailbacks are ridged with these inclusions. Occasionally an embedded seamer survives and continues feeding inside the closed plates. Such a sailback gradually changes shape, becoming narrower, more mobile, and predatory. Eventually it ceases to open in the wind.

Whether this is parasitism, injury, or another route of maturation is unknown.

The reflective specks are harder to call animals.

At rest, each is only a bright point on an edge. Under magnification it has no stable outline. It spreads into a thin patch, contracts into a bead, or divides across adjacent grains. It moves by discharging from one surface and assembling on another. A speck can cross a crack without touching the floor, but only where an electrical difference exists.

Specks feed on films too thin for the larger fauna: transient oxides, adsorbed gases, reactive layers produced in the instant after a surface is scratched. They gather at fresh damage before any visible scavenger arrives. Their presence gives newly exposed metal a brief glittering border.

Nothing hatches from a speck. Nothing visibly mates with one. Yet containers brought into the Pocket and electrically isolated remain free of them, while charged containers develop populations within hours. If the current is removed, the specks collapse into inert dark grains. Some revive when the charge returns. Others are eaten by sweepers and later appear, apparently unchanged, along the sweeper’s shell.

It may be that the specks are organisms. It may be that they are a mobile stage shared by many organisms. It may be that much of the Pocket’s life passes through them between recognizable bodies.

The deeper chambers of the motor dome contain stranger forms.

There, between copper windings and mica partitions, movement becomes orderly. Flat animals arrange themselves in repeated rows along the wires. They are almost transparent except for branching dark structures inside them. When the magnetic field strengthens, every animal turns at once. Their internal branches rotate a fraction later.

These are called compass young, though no adult has been identified.

They feed by fastening across neighboring windings and allowing minute currents to pass through their bodies. As they grow, they add plates asymmetrically, always on the side facing the stronger field. Reversals deform them. Frequent reversals produce coiled individuals that crawl in circles. Stable current produces long straight forms that eventually leave the dome in files.

Outside, they become difficult to follow. Their transparent plates cloud. Their bodies shorten or divide. Some join runner streams. Some climb. Some vanish beneath copper mite colonies. Marked individuals have never been recovered, but after the straight forms emerge, unfamiliar variants appear throughout the Pocket: runners with magnetic grains in their joints, threaders whose limbs point toward the dome, shieldbacks that pulse in synchrony with the hum.

In the warmest interior space is something that may not move at all.

It covers the buried portion of the motor housing as a thin, layered skin, alternating between conductive black material and pale insulation. Every accessible seam leads toward it. The animals that enter carrying metal dust tend to leave stripped of it. Those entering damaged may emerge with their wounds sealed by unfamiliar plates. Some do not emerge. During strong current, waves pass across the skin and the entire dome’s population aligns.

It has no mouth, no limbs, and no definable edge. It might be a mineral deposit maintained by animal traffic. It might be the fused remains of countless generations. It might be a colony whose mobile creatures are merely feeding parts sent out into the Pocket.

If it is alive, it is the largest inhabitant there.

If it is not, then the distinction may have ceased to matter.

What all the Pocket’s inhabitants share is an aversion to unused difference. A gradient of heat summons legs. A fresh scratch summons mouths. A charge summons specks. A hollow gathers capsules. A current organizes bodies. A shadow causes armor to close, and the shelter created by that closure becomes another creature’s opportunity.

They do not merely occupy the Low Pocket. They complete it.

Leave a tool on the floor and runners first map its warmth. Shieldbacks enter its joints. Specks silver its charged edges. Copper mites gather where metal touches stone. Threaders adjust their hanging lines to the new obstacle. Clampers settle beside the traffic it diverts. Within a cycle, the tool has acquired roads, ambush points, feeding grounds, nurseries, and a weather side.

Given longer, its name and purpose disappear.

Only its surfaces remain, and everything in the Low Pocket knows what to do with a surface.
