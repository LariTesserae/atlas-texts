At first they seem like vermin.

They pour from cable sheaths and pack themselves beneath shelves of salt: white bodies, blind heads, too many jointed legs. Their shells are almost colorless except where charge moves through them, briefly revealing dark internal branches. When the hammer falls, they flatten as one. When the groan rises, they unfold and continue.

A person standing still among them is climbed over but not attacked. They test boot leather with palps like braided wire. They taste buckles, seams, old scars, fillings in the teeth. Having classified the intrusion, they withdraw. Before long they know where the person will step. A pale tide clears from beneath the descending foot before the foot itself has begun to move.

No creature here appears to command the others. Nevertheless, they have the organization of a workforce.

Closest to the machine are the heavy-backed cable beasts. They stand low on six pillar legs, their hides made from overlapping plates that resemble kiln-fired iron. Heat gathers between those plates and leaks from rows of breathing slits along their flanks. Their heads are narrow and almost delicate, ending in paired mouthparts shaped like electrician’s pliers. Every eleven seconds, two of them exchange positions beside the buried conductors. One braces. The other twists a cable by an amount too small to see. Then both become still until the next blow.

Their armor bears hooks, pits, and blunt scars, though the scars never change. Tiny creatures live in these marks. They comb the joints, remove grains of metallic dust, and disappear beneath the plates whenever the larger animal moves. It is difficult to tell whether these are attendants, parasites, or parts of the beast that happen not to be attached.

Above them hang the gliders.

Folded, they resemble discarded membranes stretched between black cartilage spars. Their faces are little more than a ring of sensory pits around a hard central beak. They have no obvious eyes. They do not need them. The hammer blow tells them the entire shape of the chamber: every crystal edge, every moving back, every gap opened for a fraction of a second between two crossing routes.

At impact they let go.

The pressure wave catches their bodies and flings them outward in perfect ranks. They skim the mineral fans, tilt between the towers, and pass so close to one another that their skins almost touch. Some trail filaments through the sparks above the ceramic drum. Others strike the walls beak-first, cling for seven seconds, then crawl upside down to their former places.

Their flight is not graceful. It is exact.

On the salt blades graze black-toothed creatures with small, translucent torsos. The largest are the size of a thumb. The smallest could stand inside the hollow of a printed letter. Their jaws scrape the charged films from stone, though the films do not diminish and their bellies never fill. Seen through a lens, each grazer carries a row of mineral plates in its gut. The plates brighten one after another as it climbs. At the crystal tip, the final plate goes dark. The animal turns and descends backward, placing each foot into the minute impression left by all its previous descents.

They are born old enough to know the route.

No parent guides them. Newly split from their membranes, still soft and faintly steaming, they pause through one hammer stroke. During the groan, their legs harden. By the clicking, they have joined the traffic and will not deviate from it again.

There are tool-bearers among them: long-bodied copper things with pincers at the end of flexible arms; round-bellied tap-worms that insert hollow needles into the drum; comb-faced sweepers that arrange suspended grit without causing any of it to settle. Their anatomical divisions follow the machine more closely than any natural ancestry. There are species for holes, species for wires, species for narrow differences in temperature. One tiny animal appears to exist solely to bridge a spark across a damaged-looking terminal. It climbs into place, stiffens until current passes through its body, then drops away unharmed.

The apparent weapons belong to this same vocabulary of labor. Venom is used to alter conductivity. Saw limbs shave microscopic burrs from surfaces that never become smoother. Crushing jaws crimp soft metal that springs back before the next cycle. Hooks hold bodies steady against the hammer’s pressure.

Perhaps elsewhere these structures once killed. Here, violence has been reassigned.

The black stalks around the buried conductors are harder to understand. The animals treat each clump as a dwelling, feeding ground, shrine, and piece of equipment. Their copper vanes turn toward the drum at the strike, and for seven seconds the stalks emit a dry tremor too low to hear. During that interval, luminous specks crawl from vane to vane. Each speck has legs and mouthparts; each also fits precisely into a pore in the stalk’s surface. Once embedded, it becomes indistinguishable from a seed.

If these things are plants, they manufacture animals as temporary extremities.

If they are colonies, they imitate vegetation to reach veins of pyrite.

If they are machines, they are the only machines in the chamber that appear to dream. Their vanes sometimes shiver just before a visitor speaks, and the luminous creatures on them arrange themselves in angular patterns resembling the alphabet on the booth gauges. The patterns never repeat long enough to translate.

Death occurs behind and beneath the visible work.

Old animals do not collapse. At a particular cycle, they leave their routes. The others make room without looking at them. They crawl beneath the warm slag bank and lie down among red filaments, which enter through the joints and patiently separate them.

Shell becomes mineral grit.

Soft tissue becomes pale membrane.

Stored charge moves through the filaments into buried channels.

Eleven or a hundred and eleven cycles later, something young emerges from the alcove behind the machine. It may not be the same species. The Foundry preserves no individual, only quantities.

Yet there are hints of individual awareness. A cable beast will sometimes angle its narrow head toward the booth and remain that way for several turns. A glider passing a human face may flex one translucent surface, producing a brief expression like a hand pressed against frosted glass. Grazers occasionally stop at the tips of their crystals and tremble as if resisting the command to turn.

They always turn.

The nearest thing to a person lives in the booth, though the chair remains vacant.

Its presence is distributed among absences: the two missing ladder rungs, the warm coat, the unworn patch on the chair, the seven jumping needles. The switchboard expects hands. Certain toggles have polished ends despite never visibly moving. The coat holds the faint shape of shoulders, and sometimes, immediately after the iron groan, its sleeves settle against the chair arms before lifting back into their folded position.

The animals acknowledge this absent worker. Silver mites circle the booth’s threshold but do not cross it. Cable beasts turn their armored faces toward the door once every shift-length, though no shift has a beginning or an end. The gliders leave a human-sized corridor open between the mineral blades.

Visitors are not hunted toward the chair. They are accommodated.

First the room learns the rhythm of their breath. Then it removes alternatives. Creatures occupy the wrong passages. Sparks descend across attempted shortcuts. Crystal fans seem more tightly crowded than before. The one open route becomes easier with every circuit, until walking anywhere else feels not dangerous but grammatically incorrect.

In the booth, the gauges wait. The labels beneath them offer no instructions, only angular words that seem on the verge of becoming readable. The chair is warm because warmth belongs there. The coat fits whoever lifts it, though its sleeves initially bend at unfamiliar joints.

Those who sit do not disappear. Nothing so wasteful happens.

They become periodic.

A hand rests on the switchboard. Breathing reaches one inhalation per revolution. Hunger ceases to point toward food. Old wounds remain open but orderly, incorporated into the body’s circulation like additional mouths. After enough cycles, the seated worker knows when the hammer will fall—not by prediction, but because the moment before the blow has become a part of their anatomy.

Eventually the hand moves a switch.

Nothing visible changes.

The machine was not waiting to be started. The animals were not waiting to be commanded. Whatever duty the operator performs is subtler: witnessing the gauges, completing a circuit through flesh, or occupying the single vacancy around which every other route has been arranged.

This may be why the coat is always found folded.

The worker is not the body in the chair. The worker is the shape prepared for one.

And beneath all these inhabitants—the armored exchangers, the salt grazers, the pressure-winged gliders, the red dismantlers, the possible plants—there is another life that has no separate body at all.

It lives in their accounting.

Its pulse is the hammer. Its long inward breath is the groan. The clicking is either thought or digestion. The machine is one organ, the fauna another, the black groves a third. The booth may be its sense of itself. It does not repair damage after damage occurs; it admits only the portion of damage already included in its present arrangement. It does not resist change so much as digest change into recurrence.

Nothing in the chamber lives forever.

Instead, the chamber lives as the refusal of “after.”

The thousands within it are not prisoners, exactly, nor workers, nor offspring. They are the moving edges of a single fixed event: a shift forever staffed, a blow forever landing, a task whose completion and continuation are the same act.

When something new enters, the inhabitants do not ask what it is.

They discover where it has always belonged.
