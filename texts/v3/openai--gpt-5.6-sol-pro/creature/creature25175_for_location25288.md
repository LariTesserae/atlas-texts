Most life in the basin survives by becoming small, flat, intermittent, or difficult to distinguish from stone.

The open pavement belongs first to the lichens. They lie in maps no thicker than stains: ash-gray rosettes, black branching lines, circles of sulfurous yellow that advance less than a finger’s width in a century. Their exposed surfaces are dead armor. The living portion remains beneath, pressed into microscopic pits where frost cannot quite pry it loose. They wake under mist without visibly changing and return to suspension before the rock has dried.

Among them grows the silver grass, each island a fist of colorless blades around a dense, almost wooden heart. The blades do little photosynthesis. They are chiefly combs for cloud. Moisture runs down them into the buried knot, where it may be held for an entire dry season. In strong wind the grass does not ripple. It folds as a single body, all its blades aligning with the stone.

Closer to the city, life thickens.

The moss in the shaded chambers is not one uniform growth but a layered country. Its upper fronds are green and wet, its lower mass brown, compressed, and ancient. Much of each cushion is already dead, yet the dead material is essential: it hoards water, buffers frost, and deepens the shelter in which the living tips persist. A moss bank may slowly fill a room over centuries, only to collapse when a buried channel floods beneath it. Torn pieces are carried through the streets and lodged elsewhere, so that one colony can occupy several courtyards without any continuous path between them.

The liverworts keep nearer the drains. Their translucent bodies darken when water approaches, sometimes before the first drop appears. Fine cups along their surfaces collect mineral grains and pale reproductive dust. A sudden flow breaks these loose and carries them through the lower city. Their entire dispersal depends upon brief floods: long stillness, then a few minutes of violent travel.

The alpine herbs in the courts are compact enough to be mistaken for mats of dark pebbles. They hold their leaves almost vertically beneath the midday sun and flatten them at dusk, sealing their soft inner surfaces against frost. Their flowers are rare, low, and without fragrance. Some years they do not flower at all. When they do, the blossoms open during the warmest hour of a single clear day, displaying a blue so deep it appears black against the pale walls.

No flying insect visits them.

Instead, pollination is performed by the grazers.

These are soft-bodied animals smaller than a grain of rice. Their whiteness is not pigment but translucence; through the skin one can sometimes see a green thread of food, a dark mineral fleck, or a single pearl of stored water. They have eight short gripping limbs, though the front pair are often held against the head and mistaken for mouthparts. Where eyes might be, there are only shallow heat-sensitive pits.

They perceive the world as impacts and changes in dampness. A falling droplet strikes the moss, and within moments the nearest grazers turn toward it. More emerge from pores, seams, and hollow plant stems. They gather around the wet mark in a pale ring, feeding on newly softened algae and microbial films. If several drops fall in succession, the ring becomes a moving cluster, following the water’s spread.

In drought they contract. Their limbs draw inward, their bodies lose nearly all visible moisture, and their skins harden into wrinkled husks. In that state the wind can suck them deep into the walls. Some awaken after months. Others may remain sealed in mineral for decades, released only when the city’s slow circulation dissolves the lining of a pore.

They are preyed upon by the leaf-creature.

At rest, this predator resembles a brown flake peeled from dead vegetation. Its body is broad, thin, and articulated around six legs that can fold entirely underneath it. It has no head in the usual sense. The front edge bears a row of minute sensory hairs, while the mouth opens on the underside as a circular seam.

It does not chase. It selects a wet route and settles across it. Grazers arriving at the sound of droplets pass beneath its edge and are held by a clear adhesive. The predator may remain motionless for days while prey accumulates under its body. Only then does the underside open.

Its rarity may be deceptive. Much of its life is spent inside flooded channels, flattened against the ceiling above the current. Individuals seen in daylight are probably those displaced by unusually strong rain. The black birds recognize them immediately. Whether the birds fear the animal itself or something associated with it is unclear, but their reaction is unmistakable: circling, harsh calls, wings held rigid, no attempt to feed. Even after the leaf-creature has entered a crack, the birds avoid that courtyard for the rest of the day.

The birds are the largest regular visitors, though they do not truly belong to the summit. They come from lower ridges in twos and threes after storms. Their feathers are matte black except around the throat, where moisture reveals a hidden bronze sheen. Their feet are long-toed and careful. They drink without lowering their faces, dipping the tips of their beaks and lifting each droplet toward the throat.

They never drink from deep pools. They prefer water moving over pale stone.

Nor will they pass through an opening that conceals the sky. A bird may stand at the mouth of a gallery, listening while water runs inside, but it will not cross the line into shadow. Even nestlings brought on their first flights show this refusal. The city offers shelter, ledges, and abundant cavities, yet no nest has ever been built within it. At dusk the birds leave together, often before the first ice forms.

In the upper chambers hang the communal filaments.

A curtain begins as a few colorless threads extending from a damp seam. These threads are not roots and do not require soil. Each is a chain of water-bearing cells enclosing a narrow strand of green tissue. Where several touch, they fuse. In time they form ropes, nets, and hanging veils that may descend through several stories of the grown architecture.

No part is clearly an individual. A severed thread can continue living; two curtains meeting in wind may join permanently. Their swollen knots store mist, but also serve as weights. When wet, the knots enlarge and pull the filaments taut, exposing more surface to the passing cloud. As they dry, they shrink unevenly. The whole curtain then twists and wrings itself, pressing droplets from older knots into younger ones or releasing them to the moss below.

The curtains grow according to airflow. In chambers where wind reverses, they braid themselves into thick cables. In quiet wells they spread into delicate fans. Some have incorporated hair, grass fiber, grains of mineral, empty grazer husks, and portions of their own dead tissue until they resemble woven fabric. Their slow creaking is produced where these inclusions rub together.

Deep within the knots live colonies of dark single-celled organisms. They remain inactive while the curtain is full of water and become metabolically intense as it dries, consuming compounds concentrated by evaporation. They give the oldest knots a smoky center. Without them, the communal plant sickens; with too many, sections of it blacken and detach. The relationship is neither wholly cooperative nor wholly parasitic. It has persisted too long to be simple.

Lower still, beneath the visible moss and behind the pale walls, the distinction between inhabitant and architecture becomes uncertain.

The ancient colonial builder is dead in the sense that it no longer feeds, reproduces, adds chambers, or responds as a whole. Its original soft tissues are gone. Yet not everything that moved through those tissues vanished with them.

The capillaries contain a mineral film organized into branching membranes. Most of the time this film is inert. After several days of mist, when the outer walls cool faster than the interior, water begins to climb through it. The movement is partly physical—surface tension, pressure, temperature—but not entirely. At junctions, the ascending fluid repeatedly favors certain paths. Block one route with ice, and neighboring channels alter their flow. Dissolved material is sorted. Some ions are carried upward; others are left below. Cracks receive deposits most heavily along their stressed edges, as though the system can still register where the city is weakest.

Within the milky water are particles smaller than cells and more regular than crystals. They have layered shells and no metabolism that can be detected in isolation. Removed from the walls, they break down. Within the capillaries, they gather at damaged mineral membranes and arrange themselves along the fracture. Whether they are remnants of the builder, descendants of something that once lived symbiotically inside it, or merely self-organizing products of its chemistry cannot be decided.

They do not rebuild the city according to a plan. They preserve pathways. They maintain the possibility of circulation.

This is the oldest remaining form of life in the basin, if life is what it is. It has no separate bodies, no season of birth, and no future form toward which it grows. It yields material from one surface and deposits it upon another. A lower wall thins so that an upper lip can persist. One sealed pore opens while three exposed ones close. Across centuries, the city shifts its own substance through itself and retains its outline.

The younger inhabitants depend upon this motion. It raises water to the hanging curtains. It renews mineral films grazed by the pale animals. It keeps certain drains open for the liverworts and builds shallow lips where the birds drink. The entire small ecology occupies the afterlife of the colony’s circulation.

There may also be life below the central court.

The deepest shaft breathes after the rest of the city has fallen silent. Its exhalations are warmer than the surrounding stone by only a fraction, but in winter that difference is enough to prevent complete freezing. Threads lowered into it return wet and coated with a colorless slick. During long cold spells, pale bubbles rise in the black water at its bottom, one at a time, at intervals too regular to seem accidental.

Nothing has been seen climbing from the shaft. No tracks lead away from it. The grazers avoid its rim, and moss stops several inches short of the opening. The hanging filaments sometimes extend toward it, but their tips yellow and withdraw.

Perhaps the shaft contains only a trapped reservoir and the slow chemistry of buried stone. Perhaps some remnant community persists where the old colony’s channels converge: blind, cold, dispersed through water and mineral, never forming a creature that could be brought into daylight.

Above it, the visible inhabitants continue in their brief cycles. Moss opens. Grazers gather around drops. Curtains tighten and relax. Birds arrive, drink, and flee. The leaf-creature waits across a wet seam.

And through all of them passes the city’s weather: cloud made into beads, beads made into streams, streams drawn back into walls. No inhabitant possesses the place. Each occupies an interval—the wet hour, the shaded pore, the thawing channel, the thin span between mist and frost.

The closest thing to a permanent resident is not any animal or plant, but the exchange itself: water moving through an ancient body, carrying the living with it and keeping the dead from entirely becoming ruin.
