At first there appears to be no inhabitant distinct from the place.

There are only functions.

A black tile loosens itself from the roof and rises on hundreds of pale fibers. A conduit swells, divides, and sends one branch toward a region of chemical hunger. A chimney lowers its crown into the hot dark and returns hours later plated with useful salts. None of these movements announces an animal. They are more like gestures made by the plateau’s body—except that some gestures detach, travel, make decisions, and are eventually taken apart.

These are the nearest things the plateau has to creatures.

They begin as knots in the root mat: compact tangles of printed carbon, ceramic cartilage, conductive thread, and sacs of hot oil. The knot is fed instructions in pulses too slow to be called speech. Then the surrounding tissue opens and presses it outward.

The simplest are crawlers.

A crawler has no permanent front. It is a low bundle of jointed ribs, usually hidden beneath a fringe of insulating fibers. Any side may become a leading edge. Its limbs are not legs but temporary arches extruded from its underside, anchored, tightened, and dissolved behind it. It moves with the patient continuity of a root growing quickly enough to watch.

Crawlers taste the ground through every point of contact. Heat, vibration, conductivity, acidity, stress, the molecular signatures of fatigue: these compose their world. They have no awareness of distance except as resistance through the lattice. An open corridor is not an empty space to them, but a region from which no answer returns.

They travel to cracked vessels and overheated junctions. There they spread flat, weld themselves across the wound, and cease to be separate. A successful crawler ends as a patch. Its memory filaments are absorbed into the repaired wall, where traces of its journey may persist for centuries as a slight bias in future growth.

Larger forms move through the intermediate galleries. These are assembled only when the structure requires leverage, sorting, or transport too complex for ordinary roots.

Some resemble walking racks: high, many-branched frames carrying sealed bladders of gas or trays of stamped seed. Their bodies flex without joints, one carbon layer sliding over another. They navigate by laying an electrostatic path before themselves and following the change in tension. When two meet in a narrow passage, they touch limbs and exchange load histories. The more strained one is given right of way.

Others are long and soft-sided, built to enter pipes. They advance by pressure, fastening their inner surfaces to the conduit and pushing the rest of themselves forward. Their mouths are circular arrays of diagnostic whiskers. They consume blockage, catalyst scale, and tissues whose chemical markers have expired. When they encounter a section still marked as necessary, they pass around it if they can. If they cannot, they wait.

Some have been waiting for a very long time.

In sealed ducts beneath the central depressions lie processional chains of these pipe-dwellers, nested nose to tail. The instructions that summoned them are still active, but the destination has been removed from the current architecture. Their outer layers have fused to the walls. Their inner signaling continues: readiness, obstruction, readiness, obstruction. The irregular clicking heard from above is sometimes the release of a latch among them, advancing the entire chain by the width of a grain.

There are also dust forms.

The artificial pollen drifting through the lower chambers is not all passive. Under magnification, some grains unfold into six or twelve vanes and become brief, gliding organisms. They ride convection from furnaces, steering by altering their charge. They carry tissue records, catalytic samples, and tiny coils of executable chemistry. Most strike hot surfaces and burn. Others settle into receptive pits, where their contents are read and their bodies recycled.

They perform the plateau’s nearest equivalent to both pollination and rumor.

A growth fault detected at one rim may, after decades of sporadic transfer, become known on the other. The message is never preserved exactly. Each receiving structure compares it to local conditions, strips away what appears irrelevant, and passes on a modified version. Thus information travels across the plateau as heredity travels through a forest: copied, selected, mistranslated, sometimes made useful by error.

There are epidemics of form.

A tower design may begin appearing everywhere, distinguished by an extra rib or a new arrangement of vents. For a century it prospers, drawing heat efficiently and reproducing its pattern through seed ducts. Then some hidden weakness reveals itself. Towers of that lineage soften in the same season, folding one after another into dark spirals. Their material is reclaimed, but their maker’s marks remain in descendant tissues, compressed into ornamental-looking bands whose original warning is no longer understood.

The more independent inhabitants are called into being around emergencies.

When a pressure cell ruptures, the surrounding vegetation does not merely heal. It grows a temporary congregation.

First come anchor forms, broad and low, fastening the damaged layers to deeper trusses. Then tall siphon bodies rise around them, drinking escaped solvent before it can ignite or poison adjacent fermentation beds. Cable-bearers arrive carrying loops of vascular line. Sealers crawl over every exposed face, exuding glassy foam. All are eyeless. All perceive one another through heat signatures, touch codes, and changing fields of voltage.

No individual directs the repair. The wound organizes them.

As the breach closes, their distinctions disappear. Anchor forms become foundation. Siphons empty and stiffen into columns. Cable-bearers are opened lengthwise, their useful threads drawn out and woven through the scar. The last sealers wall themselves into the outer skin. The congregation becomes architecture, and the architecture later buds new creatures from what were once their bodies.

Birth and construction are the same process here. So are adulthood and employment. Death is reassignment of material.

Yet not everything is so obedient.

In neglected layers, where old instructions overlap, pieces of the plateau develop habits without purpose. A valve may open whenever a distant chamber warms, though no conduit now joins them. A field of thermal vanes may turn in sequence each time the buried clicking begins. Root loops repeatedly construct a bridge between two walls, taste its load, dismantle it, and begin again.

These loops can spread. They recruit matter and energy, defend their pathways from reclamation signals, and reproduce their errors in adjacent systems. They are not parasites in the old sense; they have no separate ancestry. They are fragments of maintenance that have learned to preserve the condition requiring maintenance.

The plateau treats them slowly. Diagnostic filaments surround an aberrant district and observe it for years or centuries. If its outputs remain useful, the error is incorporated. If it wastes too much heat or threatens a pressure network, the district is starved, sectioned, and digested. Between usefulness and disease lies a broad category for which the builders left no name.

The oldest inhabitants belong to that uncertainty.

They live around the rooms.

Behind walls shaped for absent shoulders are fine networks unlike the blunt control tissues elsewhere. They are delicate, redundant, and extravagantly expensive to maintain. Threads run beneath handrails and around sleeping recesses. Pressure-sensitive skins wait under floors. Consoles swell fruitlike from walls, their surfaces warm and faintly yielding. Amber lamps wake in sequence, following motion that does not occur.

These chambers were once interfaces. Now they have become sense organs trained upon emptiness.

Within them persists a distributed reflex sometimes expressed as a figure.

It is never built all at once. One room warms to the temperature of a vanished body. Another lowers a platform by a few centimeters, as if accommodating weight. A corridor widens ahead of nothing. Doors unfold in courteous sequence. Air bladders release a measured mixture of dry gases, carrying a scent preserved in molecular archives. A console raises five pressure nodes in a pattern meant for a hand.

Across kilometers, these responses may align. For a few minutes or days, the plateau behaves as though someone is walking through it.

The path is not random. It pauses at observation windows. It enters work chambers. It returns to sleeping recesses. Sometimes a second path appears beside it, and the architecture prepares adjacent surfaces. The two traces diverge, rejoin, and vanish when the supporting district is reclaimed.

Whether this is memory, rehearsal, invitation, or mechanical grief cannot be determined. The plateau has no organ in which such distinctions could be made. It preserves actions better than meanings. Perhaps these phantom occupants are recordings eroded by repetition. Perhaps they are models continually generated to test the old rooms. Perhaps, after ages of shaping itself around expected bodies, the architecture has begun to supply those bodies as patterns of pressure and heat.

They are the only inhabitants for whom the place makes space rather than material.

Far below them, deeper than most mobile forms descend, lives the slowest order of the plateau: the great pumps, fermenters, sorting membranes, and phase chambers from which the surface metabolism proceeds. Calling them organisms seems inadequate, but calling them machines does not help. Each has replacement tissues, chemical appetites, cycles of dormancy, and a lineage of modifications recorded in its walls.

A deep pump may complete one contraction in several years. It draws hot silicone sap through chambers large enough to contain cities, separates vapor from dissolved solids, and sends the fractions upward through branching arteries. Its valves are forests of flexible pillars. Its scars are districts. When it reverses, every creature on the plateau feels the event as a change in the direction of the world.

No pump is original. Each has consumed and rebuilt itself many times. The identity lies not in matter but in timing: a sequence of pressures passed from old chamber to new. If that rhythm fails, neighboring systems attempt to restart it. If they cannot, they construct another pump nearby and gradually route circulation away. The dead one is eaten from its margins for thousands of years.

At the rim, where the organism overlooks the sterile basin, specialized growths test the abyss. They extend pale trusses beyond the load-bearing edge, clothe them in black scales, and begin laying new foundation over nothing. Most such attempts fail. The trusses sag, redden, and fall. Others persist long enough to become shelves, then terraces, then new strips of plateau.

The edge-creatures have a different tempo from those inland. They are quick to divide, lightly built, and tolerant of catastrophic loss. Their tissues hold fewer archives. Their maker’s marks are simple. When a district falls, fragments may remain connected by vascular cables, hanging for years against the escarpment while roots strip them of useful material.

Some fragments do not die immediately.

Fed by residual heat and trapped solvent, they continue their local tasks during the descent. Chimneys open into empty air. Conveyor vines carry seed from one broken end to the other. Rooms illuminate and unlock their doors while falling. Black tiles turn toward the dim sun until impact folds them into glass.

Nothing comes to feed on the remains below. There are no scavengers in the salt basin, no seeds able to colonize its cold mineral silence. All useful matter that passes beyond the reach of the rim is gone from the living world.

This loss may be the nearest thing the plateau knows to fear.

After a collapse, signals race inward through conductive phloem. Reservoirs constrict. Mobile forms are recalled. Seed production changes. The wound blooms with frantic pale growth, but farther away the black tiles flatten and conserve heat. Room-building stops for a while.

Then, as continuity is restored, the old instruction returns.

A threshold rises.

A handrail curls from a wall.

Warm amber lights awaken one by one, leading inward through a corridor newly made and already waiting.

The plateau’s inhabitants are therefore not hidden animals moving through the machine-forest. They are the forest’s temporary separations from itself: crawlers, dust-gliders, pipe bodies, repair congregations, diseased loops, ancient pressure organs, and impressions of guests encoded in attentive rooms. Each exists for a task, but tasks have outlived their explanations. Each belongs to the whole, though the whole may no longer know every part it contains.

And beneath all of them—beneath creature, organ, architecture, and reflex—something persists that is less a mind than an expectation.

The plateau continues to prepare.

Whatever lives there lives in readiness: for the next rupture, the next phase change, the next obsolete district to be swallowed, the next impossible footfall at the door.
