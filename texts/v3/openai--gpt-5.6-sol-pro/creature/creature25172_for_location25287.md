No single kind of creature can be called the inhabitant of the Amber Ward. The first life encountered there is seldom an animal at all. It is the dark seam inside a wall, the pale scale turning toward the light, the amber pressure slowly filling a bladder behind a translucent skin. Much of the Ward is alive only a few finger-widths beneath what appears to be dead architecture.

The towers are colonies rather than individuals. Their rigid exteriors consist of generations of abandoned tissue, each layer sealed before the living growth withdrew inward. Within, narrow bands of black pith climb through the old structure like buried vines. They divide at chambers, surround foreign objects, and reconnect where cracks permit. A tower may contain several lineages that have grown through one another for centuries. They exchange solvents where their tissues meet, compete for light above, and digest one another at their borders. There is no clear moment when two colonies become one or one colony splits into several.

Their reproductive vanes open high above the lanes. They do not scatter soft pollen. They release hard grains enclosed in wax, each grain faceted to catch against scales, shell joints, and adhesive feet. A grain may remain dormant for years while its carrier moves through the Ward. It begins to grow only after being pressed into a warm mineral fissure and exposed to a particular mixture of stone salts and animal oil.

Thus the towers depend upon the creatures that wound them.

The largest regular visitors are the rib-backed drinkers. An adult stands as high as the lower galleries, though its body is so narrow that it can vanish into a passage scarcely wider than its skull. Its limbs fold tightly along its flanks when not in use. Each ends in two hard hooks and a fleshy central pad, allowing it to brace between opposing walls without placing its full weight on the floor.

Its hide is not continuous. Over the back lie overlapping hollow ribs filled with trapped gas and fibrous insulation. Between them, softer bands expand and contract as the animal warms. At night it turns its ribbed surface toward the sky; by midday it presents one narrow flank to the sun and keeps the other against a cool interior wall.

The drinker’s mouth is normally fused shut by a reusable waxy seal. When it finds a swollen bladder, sensory grooves around its snout test the tension of the membrane and the chemistry beneath it. Only then does a single hollow tooth unfold. The animal punctures, draws, withdraws, and presses the wound closed with a secretion from its chin.

It rarely takes much. A drinker that empties a healthy bladder gains more solvent than it can safely store and risks rupturing its own organs in the heat. Its restraint is physiological rather than moral. Sick, breeding, or injured animals are less measured. They may open bladder after bladder, leaving a trail of collapsed tissues. Such an animal can kill an ancient tower in a few days.

Around the mouths of old drinkers, reproductive grains accumulate in permanent grooves. Some germinate there. Elder animals often carry pale branching growths across the jaw and cheeks, giving their heads the appearance of carved masks. These growths do not penetrate deeply, but they draw minerals from saliva and release compounds that keep the mouth seal flexible. When the animal dies, its face may root to the nearest wall before the rest of the body has cooled.

The drinkers do not form herds. They know one another through the walls.

A resting animal presses its breastbone against hardened rootwork and produces sequences of muscular tremors. These pass farther through the ceramic foundations than any airborne sound. The signals are slow: location, condition, a full bladder discovered, a passage closed. An answer may come minutes later from a body several towers away. During the breeding season, the lower Ward can be filled with these exchanges, though a standing listener hears nothing. A hand against the floor would feel only occasional, deliberate pulses, like distant doors being shut.

Other creatures eavesdrop.

The flat grazers beneath the copper fans possess vibration-sensitive fringes along their legs. They cannot interpret the whole pattern, but they recognize the approach signal of a drinker. When it passes, they crowd toward its feet. The drinker cracks old scales and exposes fresh growth; the grazers feed on the torn edges and on the tiny organisms shaken loose from its pads.

They are broad, thin animals, sometimes no thicker than a folded strip of bark. Their many legs move in traveling sequences, so that the body seems to slide without effort. Most of the time they remain attached upside down beneath a blade, scraping away its outermost mineralized layer. They feed for only a few breaths, then seal their mouthparts and spend hours redistributing what they have taken.

A grazer’s young develop within shallow pockets along its back. At first they resemble extra plates. When mature, they detach one leg at a time, each remaining fixed to the parent until the next has found purchase on the fan above. The young do not enter the open air if they can avoid it. Entire generations may live and die beneath a single blade.

In the courts dwell the turning shells. Their resemblance to broken stone is more than camouflage. As they grow, they cement actual mineral fragments to their backs, selecting pieces by texture rather than color. A shell from black-red ground becomes dark and ridged; one raised beneath an amber blade is thin-edged and luminous. When the Ward changes around them, they slowly alter themselves, dissolving one attached fragment and replacing it with another.

Only their eyes move quickly. Each eye sits beneath the shell and looks outward through a line of transparent pits. Instead of lifting its head, the animal rotates an internal band of lenses. What appears from outside to be a single eye turning is often a row of twenty or thirty small organs passing behind the same opening.

Turning shells gather where light warms the floor. They feed on fallen grains, hardened droplets, shed mouth seals, and the bodies of very small gliders. In danger they draw their limbs inward and fuse temporarily to the ground. Some remain sealed too long and are built over. Living shells have been found inside walls, moving through chambers formed around their ancestors.

Above them hang the thread flocks.

A thread animal is nearly weightless: a central knot of sensory and digestive tissue with six long limbs, each limb finer than hair and branching at its end. Alone, it is a poor flier and an inefficient feeder. It can only cast itself from a height, spread its threads, and hope to catch against something before reaching the hot ground.

A flock is a different creature in all but ancestry.

Hundreds join limb to limb beneath an arch. The outermost individuals anchor to the structure; those within adjust their tension until the whole colony forms a suspended sheet. Tiny changes travel across it. A captured spore causes the nearest threads to contract, passing the object inward. A dangerous glider sends a widening slackness through the net, opening a hole before impact. When one edge enters sunlight, pigments migrate through the linked limbs and darken the exposed side.

No individual commands these acts. Most do not receive food from a capture, and many spend their lives as anchors. Yet the arrangement persists because the flock continually exchanges members. Fed individuals move outward. Starved anchors are released and carried toward the center. During still periods the net turns itself inside out with such slowness that its shape appears unchanged.

When plateau winds enter the cleft, the flock dissolves. Knots separate, trail from their anchors, and glide through the lanes in pale clouds. They gather again wherever the air becomes quiet. In this way they carry reproductive dust between distant parts of the Ward and occasionally beyond it. Most that cross the rim die on the bare upland, their threads stuck to glassy stone. A few reach the sheltered black fans and begin temporary nets there, outposts that seldom survive a season.

The tower slits shelter long-bodied climbers. Their limbs are too small to support them on level ground; they live vertically, suspended by rows of adhesive pads along the belly. The pads do not rely on moisture. Each bears thousands of dry mineral hooks that interlock with the tower’s porous surface.

Climbers feed on things living inside the walls. They press a narrow head into a seam and wait until heat or vibration betrays movement. Then the jaw opens sideways and draws out burrowing shells, tower parasites, or the pale larvae of gliders. Their own eggs are inserted into empty chambers and sealed with a plug of regurgitated fiber. Because the plug may remain after hatching, old climber nurseries become new partitions in the architecture.

Some climbers never emerge. In deep towers, pale populations have lost their gliding membranes, pigmentation, and functional eyes. They navigate by tapping the wall with rigid whiskers. A tower collapse can expose thousands of them at once, packed into internal seams and recoiling from direct light. Most are eaten or desiccate. A few enter neighboring ruins, founding new interior lineages.

Smaller lives occupy nearly every sealed reserve. There are bladder mites that pierce resin vessels with mouthparts too fine to trigger the tower’s closing response. There are grain mimics that wait among reproductive particles until a grazer swallows them, then scrape oils from its gut before being sealed in waste. There are slow infections that turn black pith amber and transparent, leaving the tower alive but unable to sense damage. There are scavengers whose entire life cycle takes place inside the hollow dorsal rib of a drinker.

Death does not release these creatures from the Ward so much as change their tenancy.

A dead drinker dries from the outside inward. Turning shells remove the eyes and mouth seal. Grazers shave the softer joints. Thread animals anchor to the ribs and trap dust across them. Tower growth enters through the puncture grooves of the face, while root discs rise around the feet. For decades the skeleton may remain standing in a narrow lane, slowly becoming a bridge support or the core of a new wall.

The Ward contains many such bodies, though they cease to look like remains. A row of pale arches may be the ribs of an animal long enclosed. A polished threshold may be a shell worn flat by generations of passage. Some round chambers began as egg cases. Some chimneys are the mineralized digestive tracts of creatures for which no living counterpart remains.

Nothing here possesses a map of the city. Nothing knows the Ward whole. A shell knows the court in which light returns. A grazer knows the underside of one fan and the vibrations that mean danger. A drinker knows a chain of passages, remembered through pressure and scent. A thread flock knows tensions, openings, and air. The towers know only light, contact, chemistry, and the slow subtraction of whatever blocks their growth.

Together these partial senses produce something that resembles memory.

A frequently used lane remains open because moving bodies abrade new growth. A useful bladder is spared because animals that drink lightly survive to revisit it. A breach becomes a doorway because reproductive grains fall from those passing through. A dead creature hardens into a foundation because every nearby organism treats its body as shelter, mineral, or boundary.

The Amber Ward’s inhabitants do not live inside architecture in the usual sense. They live inside one another’s accumulated refusals to waste. Every wall is a former skin. Every chamber is something once protected. Every bridge began as a reach toward light, food, anchorage, or escape.

From the rim they are invisible, and the Ward looks deserted.

Below, countless sealed mouths are feeding. Threads tighten. Eyes rotate under stone. A buried animal sends a pulse through the floor, and far away another presses closer to the wall to answer.
