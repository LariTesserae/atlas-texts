What lives in the Warm Works is not one creature but a layered citizenry, divided by height, heat, wetness, and tolerance for machinery.

The uppermost inhabitants are the bats. They live beyond the reach of ordinary attention, in the narrow country between the visible ceiling and the city’s weight above it. Individually they are small, fine-boned things, their folded wings no thicker than damp cloth. Together they become architecture. They pleat themselves into expansion joints, upholster abandoned ducts, and blacken whole sections of roof. A colony can resemble a single sleeping organ attached to the concrete, breathing in thousands of minute contractions.

They know the Works not as rooms but as currents. A stopped fan, an opened door, a cooling motor: each alters the invisible map by which they move. At the approach of evening, cooler air draws them upward. They pour into the shafts, first by dozens, then by thousands, a black fluid climbing against gravity. Those too old, too young, or unwilling to leave remain behind, muttering and shifting above the pumps. Their fallen young sometimes cling to cable mesh below the roost. Their droppings feed pale, busy nations that never see the sky.

Lower down are the heat-followers.

Cockroaches inhabit the pipe trenches in such numbers that the insulation sometimes seems to move. They prosper nearest steam valves and transformers, where warmth persists through every season. Their trails pass through rust flakes, tea leaves, guano, grease, cardboard, and the softened labels of chemical drums. They eat what the larger inhabitants overlook and what the Works themselves shed. When a trench cover is lifted, they vanish in coordinated waves, pouring into gaps too narrow to admit a coin.

Cave crickets keep to rougher walls. They stand on long, uncertain legs, seeming assembled from wire and husk. A sudden lamp sends them springing without visible destination. Moths settle over warning labels and instrument faces, obscuring voltage marks with powdery wings. Mosquitoes rise from hidden water in slow clouds, breeding wherever a cup, pipe elbow, footprint, or sagging sheet of plastic holds enough stillness.

The rats belong to the middle levels: the ledges, cabinets, corridors, wall cavities, and undersides of raised roads. They are the most accomplished inhabitants because they understand both the animals and the humans.

They know shift changes. They know which lunch tins close badly, which storeroom doors fail to catch, which workers throw scraps into the same gutter. They can distinguish a pump starting from a cart approaching and a harmless relay snap from the metallic preparation of a trap. Their routes shine with use. Generations have pressed their bodies along the same panel edges until dust and rust have been polished away.

They are large in the Warm Works, but not monstrous. Their success is more unsettling than monstrosity would be. They drink delicately from sweating flanges. They carry their young through cable openings. They pause beneath the houses and listen to feet overhead. When they fight in the ducts, the sound travels far from its source, so that their brief screams seem to issue from the walls themselves.

Water has its own tenants.

Minnows flicker in the grates, feeding in the thin illuminated layer near the surface. Below them, pale fish occupy the sumps. Some still bear faint traces of color; others have the washed, unfinished appearance of creatures molded from candle wax. They have learned the pulse of the pumps. When machinery engages, they retreat to pockets behind ladders and intake screens. When the water quiets, they drift out again beneath floating skins of oil and mineral foam.

Frogs live in the settling tanks and flooded recesses. Most are small enough to fit in a worker’s palm, but the pipes enlarge their calls and send them booming through distant chambers. They call without regard for the human day. A frog beneath a grate may answer another three rooms away, each voice traveling through water, steel, and hollow concrete until the Works seem occupied by hidden animals of enormous size.

After storms, eels arrive like messages from another underground. They appear in gutters where no eel should be, black-backed and shining, carrying the smell of open drains and rain-swollen canals. They do not panic when stranded. They bend, test, rest, and continue, working through a depth of water scarcely sufficient to cover them. Workers who find one may guide it with a broom toward a sump. Rats watch from the walkway. Frogs stop calling until it passes.

There are quieter lives behind all these.

Fungi inhabit timber braces, old sacking, paper labels, and the dark sides of crates. White fans spread where wood touches stone. Orange pinheads emerge from damp insulation. Translucent filaments stitch together dust, hair, guano, and cloth. Their growth is slow but nearly universal. Whatever remains unused long enough begins to soften at the edges and enter their keeping.

The few plants are temporary exiles. Seedlings descend through grilles or germinate beneath sidewalk vents, encouraged by rain and a narrow blade of daylight. They raise two pale leaves, sometimes three. Their stems lengthen toward the opening, too weak to support themselves. Dust settles on them; warm air dries their tips; insects chew their softer parts. They rarely die all at once. They lean, yellow, and disappear into the films that fed them.

And then there are the humans.

They do not exactly inhabit the whole Works. They maintain islands within it.

They keep the workshop bench bright and dry. They hang boots upside down, clip tins shut, mesh the tea stall, and raise anything valuable above the remembered height of water. Their houses stand on the platform like three defended boxes. Inside are calendars, folded bedding, steel cups, religious pictures dulled by moisture, medicine packets sealed in jars, and fans that move the hot air without cooling it. The walls sweat behind their possessions.

The workers have acquired the local caution. Before opening a cabinet, they kick its base. Before lifting a grate, they look for movement below. Before starting a motor, they strike it and wait for wings to erupt. They recognize individual pumps by sound and know when water is rising from changes too slight for visitors to notice. They can hear a blocked intake in the throat of an engine. Some can tell, from the bats’ unease, that airflow has failed in another chamber.

They speak loudly near machinery and softly near the roosts. Their clothes smell of metal, mildew, soap, and the smoke that enters from the surface. They bring news of weather down on their shoulders: rain stippled across a jacket, summer dust in hair, cold trapped briefly in the folds of a coat. By the middle of a shift, the Works have absorbed these signs. Everyone becomes damp and warm.

No human there mistakes ownership for control. A room may be locked and still belong to rats behind its walls, moths inside its light fittings, frogs beneath its floor, and bats in the seam above its door. If people cease using it for a week, droppings accumulate. If they cease for a month, paths form through the dust. Cardboard collapses, labels peel, nests appear in drawers. Surrender is not dramatic. It begins at the edges and proceeds inward.

What truly lives in the Warm Works, then, is a community of users of unfinished things. Each species takes advantage of something meant for another purpose. Bats turn ventilation into migration. Rats turn conduits into streets. Frogs turn settling tanks into resonant ponds. Fish turn sumps into depths. Fungi turn storage into soil. Humans turn maintenance platforms into homes.

Even the machinery participates in this life without being alive. Pumps breathe and change their voices. Pipes sweat. Fans create seasons. Motors provide hearths. The whole place has a metabolism assembled from electricity, runoff, waste, and bodies.

None of its inhabitants waits for completion. The painted future doors are already crossed by insects. The rail ending in clay is already a rat path. Bats roost above capped pipes intended for chambers not yet dug. Along the raw excavation face, roots die slowly while fungal threads enter them from below.

The city imagines the Warm Works as a space under construction. Its inhabitants know better. To them, nothing is pending. The place is already complete in the only way a habitat must be complete: it offers warmth, passage, water, food, darkness, and somewhere to bear young.
