The first inhabitants one notices are not the ones that move.

They occupy the black pores of the stone in crowded, laminated nations: skins beneath crusts beneath translucent veils. A blue-green filament will spend a century advancing the width of a fingernail, only to be buried by rust-colored cells, flooded with metal, and pressed into a stain. It does not die at once. Nothing there dies at once. The buried layer continues to breathe through the lives above it. Spores pass upward. Minerals pass down. What appears to be a patch of lichen is often a vertical history of predation, shelter, inheritance, and sleep.

The pale reeds are part of this traffic. Their circles are not colonies but listening organs distributed across the plain. Each stalk bends toward a different portion of the roar. When thunder approaches from the future, the whole ring inclines toward the hour from which it will come. Beneath them there are no roots, only thin white knots embedded in separate cracks. Yet if one reed is torn away, circles many miles distant recoil.

The bladder-plants are their brief, feverish offspring. They inflate when a sound is too large to pass cleanly through the world. For a few minutes they shine in the warm pools, full of spores and captured noise. Then they burst. Their spores drift away; the sound remains inside the water and may be released years later beneath a stranger’s foot.

The glassy animals that pulse in condensation feed on these remnants. They have no mouths. They press themselves against droplets containing old reflections and clear them from within, leaving the water ordinary. A colony can erase a cloud that has haunted a pool for centuries. During rare lulls they gather into branching forms like tiny transparent hands. When the hammering resumes, the hands scatter into beads.

Larger creatures are more difficult to distinguish from the country.

The jointless channel-rollers have several names in the surviving arrangements of stone, though none can be spoken above the weather. Their bodies are long, soft underneath, and armored with scales they grow from whatever rock they cross. A creature passing over red mineral becomes red-backed. One crossing black stone darkens and shines. If it rests too long, lichens claim it, water cuts channels across it, and smaller lives begin to inhabit its scales. It may remain motionless for generations, waking only when erosion exposes its belly.

They travel against currents because they eat destinations. At the fork of a stream, one will raise its blunt head and taste both courses. It chooses the branch that arrives least completely at the sea—if there is a sea—and rolls slowly upward, swallowing the tendency of the water to go elsewhere. Behind it, streams hesitate. Some turn back. Some divide into finer and finer threads until direction has been exhausted.

Their young resemble loose pebbles, the only loose pebbles on the plain. They migrate by being carried away.

The shadows beneath the shallow floods are not bodies in the usual sense. They are the surviving depth of animals whose bodies have been worn out.

Once, perhaps, they swam in the inland seas between mountains. As the seas became rivers and the rivers became films, the animals flattened with them. Flesh vanished; depth remained. Now they pass under water a finger’s breadth deep, immense and unhurried. At times the curve of a back crosses fifty miles of pools without disturbing their surfaces. At times an eye opens beneath a wet footprint.

They feed on drowning. Not on the drowned, but on the downward passage itself: the surrender from air to water, from sound to muffling, from struggle to stillness. There is little true depth left to them, and so they are always hungry. During the blackest rain they gather under the stone rings, waiting for someone to kneel above the unrippled pools.

The cries from below belong to smaller descendants that live in the shadows’ wake. They have fins as thin as cracks and white, hooked faces like birds seen through fog. They swim through submerged distances rather than water. A pool may contain only a cupful of rain and still hold a migration thousands of miles long.

But there are inhabitants who made—or inherited—the arrangements.

They are seldom seen whole.

A traveler might first mistake one for a cluster of leaning pillars. Later, after walking for hours, they may notice that the pillars have changed their inclination to remain facing them. On another day there will be only three low stones where the figure stood, warm water threading between them. Elsewhere a dark shape may be glimpsed crawling flat against the plain, with too many broad limbs and a burden of reeds tied along its back. In a still pool, however, the same being appears upright and narrow, taller than the vanished mountains, its head lost in cloud.

All of these impressions are accurate.

The inhabitants have been reduced by the same process that reduced the land. They call themselves, insofar as their gestures can be translated, the Remaining Inclinations. They have no single enduring anatomy. Height was the first thing the plain took from them. Then symmetry. Then the dependable order of childhood, maturity, and age.

What survives is posture.

One may consist of a bent spine of red slabs, two cavities that retain warmth, a hand-shaped pressure moving through puddles, and the habit of turning toward certain storms. Another is carried in six widely separated bodies that never meet: a black mat of vegetation, a walking ridge, a cry under water, a polished chamber, a sequence of footprints, and a person not yet born. Together these constitute one citizen. Damage any part and the others alter. Break the ridge and the cry becomes hoarse. Drain the chamber and the future person wakes with a hollow in the chest.

They do not speak into the air. Air is too turbulent and too immediate. Their language is made of resistance.

A stone that should lie flat is tilted by a finger’s width. A channel is narrowed so that it produces one pitch during warm rain and another during cold. Three pillars are leaned together to delay their collapse. A pool is enclosed, not to keep water in, but to keep one reflection from escaping into adjacent years. Statements may take centuries to weather into legibility. Replies are made by redirecting the water that wears them away.

Their shortest word is a threshold opening onto nothing. It means approximately:

*I was prevented from vanishing here.*

The roofless chambers are shelters, though not for bodies. The Remaining Inclinations go there to keep one event in the correct order. Inside, a birth may reliably precede a death. A wound can be made to follow the blow that caused it. Someone entering with a memory of tomorrow may emerge remembering yesterday instead. Such order is exhausting to maintain. The polished walls are worn by generations leaning against them while they struggle to remain consecutive.

The fitted rings are places of assembly. Their pools do not ripple because every ripple is answered before it forms. Around them the inhabitants gather in whatever portions of themselves are locally available: a row of dark humps; reeds bent inward; warm hollows; elongated reflections with no visible owners. They exchange losses.

One brings the recollection of a mountain no pool has retained. Another brings the extinct taste of snow. Another has preserved a predator’s fear, though not the predator. These are placed in the still water and divided among those present. Memory is not sacred to them because it is accurate. It is sacred because it is weight, and weight helps them remain where they are.

They do not build upward. To raise anything is to offer it to the weather. Instead they build down into persistence: polishing a hollow, sealing a crack around a sound, teaching moss to repeat a contour. Their greatest works are almost indistinguishable from flaws in the stone.

The dark arches are not a city, though they may once have been one. They are the oldest of the Remaining Inclinations.

Long ago, when the ranges still stood, some inhabitants tried to survive by becoming architecture. They joined their bodies across valleys and made themselves load-bearing. They expected stone to endure longer than flesh. They were correct, but not in the way they hoped. The roofs were erased, the towers planed away, the foundations opened sideways by the folding of distance. Only the acts of spanning remained.

Thus each arch is a being, or the remainder of one: a tremendous instinct to hold two separated places in relation. Weather systems pass through their ribs. Horizontal rivers run along their nerves. Moss covers their inward faces because inwardness is all they have left. Lightning at their roots is not random. It is the slow conversation of the arches, one impulse requiring decades to cross the horizon.

When the arches appear close, they are reaching.

When they appear distant, they are remembering their original size.

They shelter something beyond them, but not a population or a treasure. They shelter an interval—possibly the last intact interval in the plain—during which nothing happens. The seabird-things cannot enter it. Rain cannot begin there. Stone does not wear. Cause does not hurry after effect. The Remaining Inclinations make pilgrimages toward this stillness, though few arrive. Distance dislikes being crossed and folds them back into their own tracks.

Those who reach it do not stay. Perfect preservation is unbearable to creatures assembled from loss. They stand for a moment in the unworn silence, hear the small sounds of their remaining bodies, and return to the roar.

There are young among them, though youth is not a matter of age. A new inhabitant begins whenever several remnants develop a shared reluctance.

A reflection refuses to leave a pool. A ridge refuses one particular current. A cluster of reeds continues bending toward a thunderclap that has already passed. After years or millennia these refusals align. Footprints appear between them. A warm cavity opens in the rock. The new one acquires a posture, then a range of motions, then perhaps a name encoded in drainage.

The elders recognize it by altering the water around it so that, briefly, all streams flow away. In that small, impossible dryness, the young inhabitant learns the outline of itself.

Death is the opposite process, though equally gradual. An inhabitant loses agreement among its parts. Its shadow migrates without it. Its chamber begins preserving someone else’s sequence. Its hand-pressure loosens and becomes weather. Eventually nothing answers to the same resistance.

Yet it does not disappear. One posture remains in the reeds. One pause survives in an animal’s gait. Certain pools grow warmer when its name should have been used. The plain receives it, not as a body, but as a new habit.

This is why the Remaining Inclinations do not fear the grinding beneath the water. They grieve it, avoid it, brace stones against it, and sometimes spend whole epochs repairing what one storm has unmade. But they know endurance is not the preservation of form. Form is merely what erosion has not yet asked for.

They endure as biases in the ruin.

A preference for circles. A tendency of loose reflections to gather. A route taken by thunder. A black growth that clings more fiercely where one of them once rested. The peculiar lean of pillars toward a traveler who believes themselves alone.

During the continental shudder, when every pool shows the mountains falling, all the inhabitants become briefly visible.

The layered colonies flare in their buried colors. Channel-rollers lift their stone-scaled heads. Vast depthless animals arch under the flooded plain. Reeds bow toward the silent collapse. The arches tense along the horizon. And among the remnants stand the Remaining Inclinations in the shapes they possessed before time and weather pared them down: multitudes of tall, rain-dark figures, each carrying pieces of landscape within its body, each holding the others upright.

Then the mountains vanish from the pools.

The figures collapse back into ridges, cavities, stains, pressures, and arrangements. The roar returns. Water strikes stone hard enough to erase any certainty that someone was there.

But here and there, against flood and gravity, a stone continues to lean.
