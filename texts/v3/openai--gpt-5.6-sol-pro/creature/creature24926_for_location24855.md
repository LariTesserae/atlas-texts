At first it appears that nothing lives there.

The city offers no sudden movement by which life usually declares itself. No head rises above a wall. No small body breaks the skin of a reservoir. Nothing startles from beneath a step. One may stand in a wash court for an hour and see no change except the darkening of a stone as moisture reaches it.

Then the inhabitants begin to separate from the stillness.

The most numerous are not individual plants so much as slow territories. Beneath the wet meadows, pale runners travel laterally through the thin soil, dividing and joining, sending up bands of sedge wherever the depth of water permits. What looks like a thousand separate stems may be one old body spread across an entire shelf. Its exposed growth is replaced continually, but the hidden part scarcely moves from year to year. It remembers the fixed edge of the marsh and keeps to it.

Each band has its preferred measure. Fine, bright blades stand in the shallowest overflow, where water passes over the roots in a film. Taller, blue-green reeds occupy the ankle-deep basins. On the slightly raised rims grow low, broad plants whose leaves lie close together like overlapping scales. Their arrangement makes the terrain legible. Even where paving has sunk beneath mud, the plants preserve the buried contours: a straight line of sedge marks an old channel; a rectangular patch of floating leaves fills a former settling pool; a dry green curve reveals the clay lip of a reservoir.

The floating plants have no need to anchor deeply. Their roots hang in the warm clear water, gathering the fine mineral cloud that settles there. Their leaves are pale underneath and gray-green above, with thick margins that keep water from flooding across them. They produce flowers only rarely—small, colorless structures that open in still air and close again without attracting anything. More often a leaf breaks at the joint, drifts no farther than the next overflow notch, and begins another mat.

On the exposed stone live the smaller nations.

Moss gathers around cistern mouths in cushions dense enough to hold the print of a fingertip, though no fingertip comes. It drinks from condensation rather than rain. During the brighter hours its surface tightens and darkens; toward evening it loosens, lifting thousands of fine points into the saturated air. Some cushions are round, some follow masonry joints, and some have slowly occupied the shallow hollows worn into thresholds by vanished feet. These last are the closest thing the city has to green footprints.

Ferns inhabit the walls. Their roots enter no deeper than the packed clay between stones, but the seepage brings them everything they require. They grow downward as readily as upward, fronds hanging beneath lintels and from the undersides of drains. When humidity changes, the mature fronds release dust-fine spores. The spores do not travel far. They settle on neighboring walls, on roof mats, on the still surfaces of covered reservoirs. Most vanish into water or fungus. A few find a wet seam and become another patient fan of leaves.

The shrubs occupy the transition between garden and bare rock. They are low and widely spaced, with thick leaves whose dark surfaces remain glossy even when dry. With nothing to browse them, they possess neither thorns nor bitterness. Their protection is against water rather than hunger: waxed skins, sealed buds, roots that can survive long immersion without rotting. New stems bow under their own weight until they touch the ground. There they root, so that each shrub advances by kneeling.

Some still grow in the terraces where they were planted. Their lines have blurred, but not disappeared. One variety forms long parallel beds beside abandoned kitchens. Another persists around milling floors, its starchy underground knots multiplying in the old soil pockets. No one harvests them. They rise, thicken, fall back, and feed the molds that return them to the beds. The gardens continue without cultivation, not because they have become wild, but because their manner of cultivation was built into their bodies.

The trees are the city’s largest living things and among its most constrained. They stand where hands placed them: around reservoirs, at court corners, along the central way. Their trunks are pale and smooth, stained black on the windward side by constant wet. They do not form woods. Bedrock lies too near the surface, and each tree’s roots spread outward in a thin disk, following cracks and old paving joints. From above, every tree is centered in its own circle of greener ground.

Their crowns are open, with heavy horizontal branches and small leathery leaves. Cones remain closed through long intervals of stable weather. When a drier upland breath briefly lowers the humidity, the scales loosen with faint ticks and release wingless seeds directly beneath the parent. Most seedlings fail on stone. Those that survive do so in the damp margin of the same root circle, making loose rings of younger trunks around the old one. Thus the groves replace themselves without expanding.

Some trees have outlived the roofs they sheltered. Their branches rest on walls or pass through fallen matting, but their roots do not wrench the masonry apart. They accommodate obstacles. A root encountering a foundation flattens against it, follows its face, and enters the next wet joint. Over generations, tree and building become fitted together without either conquering the other.

Below all visible growth is the quieter population.

Clear bacterial films coat the channels. They are almost impossible to see except where they bend light around a submerged stone. Rust-colored stains inhabit fractures rich in iron. White mineral-loving colonies make soft rims at overflow notches, trapping crystals in their transparent bodies until the living layer becomes indistinguishable from deposit. In covered cisterns, faint green sheets spread just beneath the waterline, using the little light reflected through doorways.

Fungi live in every roof mat, reed bin, fallen leaf, clay wall, and forgotten basket. They do not erupt into large caps. There is no need to raise themselves above a forest floor or offer spores to moving creatures. They remain as threads and stains: pearl-gray veils on timber, black fans within packed earth, soft orange margins advancing through old grain. They digest slowly because warmth and wetness scarcely vary. A roof pole may take decades to lose its strength. A fallen leaf may retain its veins for years beneath a translucent skin of decay.

Together these unseen lives perform the work that animals elsewhere would hasten. They open dead fibers, loosen minerals, cloud the shallow soil, and make it available again. Nothing is dragged away. Nothing is buried by paws or scattered in dung. Matter descends almost exactly where it grew.

There are no true scavengers, no hunters, no pollinators, no parasites with legs or eyes. Life here does not pursue life. It overlays, penetrates, dissolves, and replaces.

The abandoned objects have acquired inhabitants according to their substance. Wooden pegs wear dark fungal collars. Reed lashings carry moss where they touch stone. Clay jars hold films of green water. Copper remains mostly bare, its salts discouraging even the smallest growth, so that fittings and blades stand out in deserted rooms as cold green absences. The narrow crescent blade in the workshop is one of the few objects the city’s living surface has not begun to soften.

No single organism possesses the place. Even the oldest meadow-clones occupy only the depth of a hand. Yet taken together, the inhabitants form something with the habits of a larger body. The ridges gather water. Stone warms it. Channels distribute it. Reservoirs hold it level. Plants lift it into leaf and air. Condensation returns it to walls. Fungi and films return the fallen plants to soil. The city’s works have become organs in this circulation, though they were not built for that purpose alone.

What lives there now lives by remaining.

It spreads without invasion, feeds without pursuit, reproduces without courtship, and dies without attracting a crowd. Its times are measured in the rounding of plaster, the thickening of a moss pad, the advance of a root along one masonry seam. Even its accidents are gentle: a fern dislodged by a drop, a cone opening, a saturated beam settling low enough to touch the water.

The city is not empty. It is inhabited by continuities—by colonies, clones, films, stains, and rooted lines that have accepted the old human geometry without understanding it. They keep the reservoirs green-edged, the drains open enough to whisper, and the garden terraces distinct from the marsh.

They do not wait for the builders to return.

They do not know the builders are gone.
