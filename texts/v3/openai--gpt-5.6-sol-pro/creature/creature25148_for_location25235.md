No single creature can be called the inhabitant of the clearing.

At noon, when the pools are contracted and the scaled floor has drawn itself tight, the place seems to belong to the little browsers. They travel the high roads in loose, purposeful herds, each animal scarcely larger than a hare but built with the grave proportions of an antelope. Their coats are dun, russet, or the gray-green of lichen. Some have short paired horns; others carry soft, branching growths that leaf during the rains and are browsed away by their companions.

They rarely touch the ground. Their world is made of inclines, forks, suspended galleries, and the undersides of branches. Their ankles rotate almost completely, and each split hoof closes on bark like a pair of fingers. A herd may pass above the central court, vanish beneath a horizontal limb, and continue upside down without altering its pace. Only the pendulous ears swing to acknowledge the change.

They are the settlement’s most conspicuous citizens and among its least consequential. They prune new growth, open passages through curtains of leaves, and carry seeds in the seams of their hooves. They sleep inside the warm domes of the outer districts, pressed together so closely that by morning their coats have exchanged patterns. When one descends to drink, its reflection remains erect on the pool, watching the branches. The animal itself drinks nervously, as though aware that the reflection is the more natural of the two.

Below them live the runners.

These are bright, thin lizards whose bodies seem cut from glazed leaves. They are turquoise in shadow, yellow on warm bark, and briefly colorless when crossing open air. Their legs move too quickly to resolve. They race along the living floor in sudden straight lines, halt with their chests pulsing, then push their pointed faces into seams that open just wide enough to receive them. The floor closes over their tails without injury.

Beneath the rind, they inhabit a warm interstitial country: flattened chambers between layers of leaf, root, and remembered soil. They tend clusters of translucent eggs attached to the city’s inner veins. Their young hatch during floods and swim for the first hour of life. When the water withdraws, they lose their gills, take on color, and climb toward daylight through pores no larger than raindrops.

The beetles are more numerous than either browsers or lizards. They occupy the trunks in nations.

Most are hidden under the outer bark, where they eat dead tissue before it can remain dead. Their shells are bronze, green-black, silver, or deep blue, but their great colonies show almost no color from outside. A pale trunk may appear smooth until heat or sap pressure causes it to flex. Then a million wing cases lift together, and the whole surface shines like metal under water.

They polish the newly exposed tissue with their mouthparts. They widen sap channels, clear fungal obstructions, and remove any splinter that has ceased to belong to the living whole. A fallen branch is covered by them before it touches the floor. By dawn, wood, beetle castings, fungal threads, and ground-rind have become one substance.

Their work is accompanied by a constant fine rasping. Magnified through hollow timber, it becomes one of the city’s deepest sounds. What resembles the turning of immense machinery is often only a thumb-sized colony eating around a knot.

The open air belongs to birds, though it seldom looks occupied.

They are narrow creatures with long wings and almost vestigial feet. Some are vivid around the throat and eyes; most are bark-brown above and leaf-pale below, vanishing whenever they bank. They nest in hanging pods whose skins continue to grow around the nests. Parent birds enter through the flower end, and their fledglings emerge weeks later with seed fluff woven into their plumage.

Not all of them have voices. In several kinds, the breastbone opens into a set of small chambers. Wind crosses these apertures in flight and produces piping chords. The bird controls pitch by turning, swelling its throat, or lifting individual feathers over the holes. A flock can therefore sing without breathing. Its music changes with the weather and sometimes continues after the flock has landed.

Because echoes do not obey sequence here, courtship songs are performed backward. The answering note comes first, then the invitation, then the call by which one bird announces where it has already arrived. Nestlings learn the final phrase before they learn the beginning. An inexperienced bird that sings in ordinary order may attract a mate from yesterday or summon an animal that is not a bird at all.

Green snakes hunt them along the branches.

The snakes are slender enough to lie within the grooves of leaves, and their scales carry pale lines resembling veins. A snake following a bird does not leap between trees. It pushes its head past the end of one branch, disappears into the interval, and emerges from the next, while the middle of its body remains absent. Some spans are short enough that this lasts only an instant. Across the broad central court, a snake may spend half a day passing through nowhere.

During that time it exists as two living ends. Both can feed. Both can recoil from danger. Occasionally the city closes the route before the snake has finished crossing, leaving one animal on each side. These halves heal, acquire new tails or heads as necessary, and thereafter avoid one another.

The pools support a second population, though much of it is present only when rain gives it room.

Soft-shelled frogs wake inside unopened flowers. Their skin is nearly without pigment, flushed faintly by green blood vessels. In dry weather they lie folded around the flowers’ ovaries with their limbs drawn close, breathing through the moist inner petals. Rain opens the blossom and unfolds the frog. It drops directly into the floodwater, calling before it lands.

Its call is a hard wooden knock. Entire pools hammer with it during storms.

Translucent crustaceans appear at the same time, swimming through gutters and drowned galleries. Their digestive tracts are visible as moving threads of gold, green, or black, according to what district the rain has washed them from. They feed on pollen, beetle husks, and the softened edges of paths. When the water vanishes, they climb into buds and seal themselves inside with a film of saliva. Predators learn to recognize occupied flowers by their slight warmth. In opening them, they spread pollen from chamber to chamber.

The small white fish are less easily explained. They arrive with floodwater although there is no stream from which they could come. They have no eyes, but along their sides run rows of pits sensitive to vibration. They swim toward sounds that have not yet happened, gathering below a branch shortly before a drop strikes it. As the flood subsides, they pass into the living floor. Sometimes, during the next dry interval, their pale bodies can be seen moving deep inside a leaf.

Many creatures are not born from parents.

They collect from repetitions.

A drop falling once is only water. The second identical drop gives the first a kind of memory. If the third lands with exactly the same weight and interval, something pale may crouch where the sound has accumulated. It resembles a frog from a distance, but it has no mouth and no organs beyond a tense cavity full of the three remembered impacts. It moves by releasing one sound at a time. When it has spent the last, it collapses into moisture.

Rain makes more durable animals. A rhythm maintained across many leaves may thicken around a root and detach as a jointed crawler. Its limbs strike the ground in the pattern that made it. Some live for minutes; others learn to feed and continue for generations, though they retain compulsions inherited from their originating noise. One many-legged species freezes whenever rain begins, compelled to listen for the rhythm of its first ancestor. Another travels only in groups of seven because the branch that produced it was struck seven times before breaking.

Predators exploit these births. Certain snakes tap seedpods in measured sequences, attempting to call edible things into being. Beetles drum misleading rhythms beneath bark. Birds repeat the cries of insects they have eaten, sometimes reproducing the insect itself and eating it again.

Exactness is dangerous. Nearly all established animals introduce deliberate flaws into their calls. A frog pauses early; a bird roughens its last note; a browser stamps with alternating hooves. Perfect repetition is generally the mark of youth, desperation, or a trap.

There are also the shadows.

They belong, initially, to animals that have passed through the outer gate. For several days after the body leaves, its shadow remains behind. It detaches from the threshold and resumes the life its owner would have lived: following the herd roads, drinking blackness from the pools, curling beneath branches at night.

A shadow-browser grazes the shade beneath leaves rather than the leaves themselves. A shadow-bird flies over the ground no matter where the sun stands. Shadow-snakes move across bright surfaces as if those surfaces were tunnels. They possess weight only at dawn, when the floor briefly dimples under their feet.

The ordinary fauna recognizes them as neither absence nor imitation. They may be groomed, courted, chased, or killed. Predators that specialize in shadows are very flat and extraordinarily patient. They hide under other creatures, matching every movement until the prey crosses open light. Then the predator’s edge rises like a jaw.

An animal whose shadow is killed beyond the gate does not die. Instead, color and opacity slowly leave it. If it returns in time, the floor may grow a replacement, but the new shadow does not always agree to follow.

No creature wholly avoids predation. Yet death here is less an ending than a loss of boundaries.

Flesh left on the floor sinks gently into it. Fur becomes root-fiber. Scales arrange themselves among the green plates. Bone softens, sprouts pores, and begins carrying sap. By morning there is no carcass, though the place where it lay may develop an animal habit. A patch of pavement might shiver at birds for several seasons. A branch grown from a dead browser may stamp against its supporting trunk in sleep. Flowers raised from frogs sometimes knock from within even when empty.

The oldest residents are difficult to distinguish from architecture.

Some of the pale trunks were perhaps once separate trees, but they no longer conduct only their own lives. Their sap pulses in correspondence. Their roots exchange water, larvae, heat, and intervals of space. Wounds on one trunk can scar over on another. A crown in sunlight feeds a shaded arcade across the clearing. When one of the oldest supports begins to fail, neighboring branches alter their growth years before the weakness becomes visible.

Whether this is communication, reflex, or common identity is impossible to determine. The settlement may be a colony whose members have forgotten division. It may be one plant expressing itself as many trees. It may be something more diffuse: not the trunks or the floor or the roots, but the agreements among them.

Its awareness, if it has one, is slow in some matters and immediate in others. It takes decades to turn a road. It opens a seam for a fleeing lizard in less than a heartbeat. It remembers the positions of pools through floods that shift every visible landmark. It routes condensation from leaf to branch to hollow with the apparent foresight of plumbing, yet it also grows arches that lead into empty air and circles that become entrances to places it did not previously contain.

The city does not command its inhabitants. It accommodates their habits until those habits become anatomy.

A browsing route thickens under hooves. A bird’s customary flight draws two crowns toward one another. Generations of frogs calling from the same pool hollow resonant chambers beneath it. Predators waiting at a particular crossing cause the crossing to migrate. Nothing plans these changes, and nothing is merely affected by them. Animal and structure revise each other continuously.

Even the apparent emptiness is maintained by inhabitants.

Gnats clear columns of air by rising in spirals. Birds keep avenues open by hunting through them. Browsers prune the aerial roads. Snakes prevent the birds from filling every pod. Beetles keep the trunks hollow enough to resonate. Frogs and crustaceans move fertility during the floods. Sound-born scavengers consume the excess chorus and vanish. Each vacant court is the product of innumerable small appetites.

Near the center, the dark pool contains other citizens—or the same citizens displaced in age.

Under an undisturbed surface, unfamiliar animals sometimes come to drink. Some have too many joints and carry gardens along their backs. Some resemble the present browsers but are tall enough to reach the highest galleries from the ground. Others are no more than moving hollows in the reflected vegetation. They do not react to observers above them. They lower their heads to another water and drink from another time.

During rain, concentric rings bring these populations briefly together. A present bird may strike at an insect in one ring and emerge in the next with an ancient seed in its beak. A future animal may look upward, startled by a sound that has not yet entered its age. Most such contacts vanish as the rings overlap, but not all. This may be the source of creatures for which the settlement has no obvious ecological history.

Before dawn, the distinctions among all these lives are weakest.

Condensation taps from the recurved branches. Hollow roots pulse underfoot. Birds murmur their songs from ending to beginning. The browsers sleep in warm communal chambers, their breathing synchronized with the expansion of the floor. Frogs wait folded inside flowers. Fish move through leaves. Beetles rasp beneath the bark. Shadows wander among the pools on errands their bodies have abandoned.

New rhythms begin to gather limbs.

For a little while, the whole clearing seems to be not a place containing animals, but an animal attempting every possible body: trunk, herd, wing, flood, echo, shadow, street. By sunrise the attempts have separated again. Creatures resume their hunts and migrations. Branches become architecture. The floor becomes ground. Empty air appears empty.

But the separation is temporary, and none of them lives there alone.
