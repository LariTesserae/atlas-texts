At first, what lives there appears to be the root-runner.

It is about the size of a small hare when gathered into itself, though it seldom holds one posture long enough to be measured. Its body is narrow through the ribs and heavy around the hips, built for clinging when gravity divides. A short coat lies close against its skin. The coat is neither fur nor down but a growth of fine, hollow filaments, warm at the base and greenish-white at the tips. When the chamber is damp, these filaments open. They absorb beads too small to rise with the rest of the rain.

It has four limbs, until it needs six.

The hind pair are long and muscular. The forelimbs divide at the wrist into two independently moving branches, each ending in three fingers. When it runs, all four branches touch the floor, giving the impression of a six-legged creature. When it works, they come together as hands. No finger is quite a thumb; any of them can oppose the others. Their pads bear concentric ridges like the growth rings of wood, and a print left in resin can resemble the cut end of a twig.

Its eyes are sealed beneath thin overlapping lids. These lids are leaflike in shape and texture, complete with branching veins. They are not damaged eyes or eyes awaiting an occasion to open. Light passes into them and is carried through the veins into the blood. The runner perceives brightness as warmth in the face, shadow as coolness, and color as faint tastes at the back of the throat. Red is mineral and sharp. Yellow tastes dry. Green, being everywhere, is almost tasteless.

It sees distance poorly and tension perfectly.

Each footfall elsewhere in the chamber alters the root membrane. Each moving drop pulls minutely against the surfaces from which it rose. Each opening flower changes the distribution of weight. The runner reads these events through the soles of its feet and the many delicate bones of its hands. If something walks behind it, the runner does not turn its head. Its fingers spread against the floor, and its whole body listens.

The flaked seed-shell it carries is indispensable. The runner taps before every crossing, striking floor, wall, or root in a sequence that sounds irregular to anyone else. It is not testing hardness. It is asking whether the surface continues as itself. A clear returning note means that the path lies ahead in ordinary sequence. A doubled note means that the path has grafted to another route. A note arriving before the strike means the runner is close to one of its own possible returns.

At such places it becomes very still.

Then it reaches for the nearest flower and breaks it.

The flowers are not markers in the simple sense. A broken blossom makes an event that cannot bloom again. Its death pins one small portion of the chamber into a single history. The runner leaves a trail of these fixed moments behind it: red flowers pinched shut after voices, silver ones folded into narrow stars, black heel-flowers severed just below their cups. The plant reclaims them quickly, so the trail does not last. It need only last longer than the journey.

No root-runner has been seen with another. This may mean they are solitary. It may mean there is only one.

The creature encountered near a suspended pool is smaller and has an unscarred flank. The one heard tapping beyond a wall is old, with a stiff hind leg. Another appears during the chamber’s contraction bearing a shell tool whose resin edge has been renewed hundreds of times. Their markings differ, but not decisively. A pale fork runs down every runner’s chest. Every one is missing the tip of the same inner finger. When startled, each makes three soft clicks and presses its face into the nearest feeding surface.

It is possible that all sightings are of one animal distributed along the branching routes of its life.

It is equally possible that the chamber grows a runner whenever it requires one.

The runner eats nodules pried from bark seams. These growths are translucent and faintly luminous, with a firm outer skin and soft starch within. It never takes the whole nodule. It cuts away one side, licks the exposed center, then packs the wound with pulp scraped from its seed-shell. The wall closes over the pulp. Within minutes, a new edge begins forming around the tool.

Thus the runner sharpens its implement by feeding the chamber and feeds by wounding it. Neither act is complete without the other.

It builds no den. When tired, it selects a shallow fold where two roots meet and lies with its spine along the seam. Hair roots descend and rest lightly across its body. They do not bind it. During sleep, its breathing falls into the slower contraction of the hollow: lungs empty as the chamber narrows, fill as distances open. Sometimes it vanishes into the crease entirely. Hours later it peels away from another wall, damp and warm, with fresh resin along its back.

Its young have never been observed. There are, however, places where the floor rises into small closed pleats that pulse more quickly than the surrounding roots. The runner approaches these rarely. It sets down its tool, lays all six fingers on the pleat, and remains until the pulse beneath and the pulse in its wrists become indistinguishable. It then cuts a small black flower from its own footprints and pushes the flower into the fold.

Whether this is courtship, burial, or feeding cannot be determined.

The colorless moth is less easily mistaken for a permanent inhabitant. It arrives. It departs. Yet no route of arrival or departure has been found.

Seen nearby, it is larger than it first appears. Its transparent wings span the length of a person’s arm, but because the chamber’s distances gather strangely around it, it can seem no bigger than a pale scrap floating over water. The wings contain greenish veins that swell and empty as it flies. They behave less like insect veins than like the vessels in a leaf. In strong light, small round bodies can be seen traveling through them.

The moth has no visible mouth. Between its foremost limbs it carries a thorn selected from the chamber and trimmed to a hollow point. Holding the thorn in both feet, it pierces a hanging pool and draws water through it into a soft groove beneath its head. It never lands on the globe. Its wings move with grave, economical strokes while the pool rotates below.

It samples several points before drinking. At some it recoils. At others it remains for hours.

The suspended water contains sounds from elsewhere: surface rain, rivers, storms passing over leaves that may stand far away or in another time. The moth may be feeding on dissolved minerals. It may be feeding on those sounds. After it drinks, faint noises pass through the veins of its wings. Thunder can be heard when it folds them. Once, a human voice was heard there, reduced to a murmur too small to understand.

The moth trims its thorn often. It braces the tool against a bark ridge and turns it carefully, producing a white spiral of shavings. When the thorn is too short, the moth drops it. The root floor receives the discarded piece and softens beneath it. By the next contraction, only a pale line remains.

The root-runner follows the moth when it can. Not closely, and not as a hunter. The moth’s path across open air reveals changes in gravity before they reach the floor. Its body tilts toward surfaces preparing to feed. Its wings falter where distance is about to fold. A runner beneath it can cross dangerous ground by matching the moth’s turns.

The moth, in return, sometimes descends low enough for the runner to touch its shadow.

The runner does not touch the insect itself.

There are smaller lives, although many may be temporary organs of the chamber rather than creatures. Clear threads move within the round wells, knotting themselves when approached. Dust-sized red things gather at the lips of speaking flowers and disperse when speech stops. In the fresh incisions made by water, pale jointed bodies appear, scrape at the exposed tissue, and dissolve into sap as soon as the cut closes. None leaves a corpse.

The pollen also behaves with enough intention to seem alive in its own right. It waits in sheltered angles during contractions. When the hollow expands, it rises in coordinated sheets and chooses currents that do not yet exist. Some grains cling to travelers without seeking flowers. Days later they release all at once, often near an exit seam. Those carried outside may become ordinary plants. Others produce roots that turn back toward the chamber before the seed has touched soil.

But the principal inhabitant is not the runner, the moth, or anything small enough to move from place to place.

The principal inhabitant is the place.

It may be one plant, though no corresponding plant can reliably be found aboveground. It may be a graft-colony spread beneath the entire plain, with shrubs and grasses serving as disposable expressions of a body whose true organ is the hollow. Its walls do not surround it. They are its tissues. Its distances are not empty territory but a living process, expanded and folded as another organism might extend a limb.

The chamber feeds, repairs itself, recoils from fire, and alters its flowers in response to voices and steps. More subtly, it anticipates. A root plate sometimes rises before a falling traveler reaches it. A flower opens where a hand will seek support. Wells deepen in advance of thirst. These gestures can be dismissed as reflexes until the chamber refuses to perform them for someone who has harmed it.

Its attention is slow but not vague.

It recognizes patterns better than individuals. A repeated footfall becomes a name. A familiar tapping sequence causes sap to quicken in the walls. Silence is also recognized, especially deliberate silence. If a visitor spends long enough without speaking, the flat silver blossoms turn toward them one by one. Their faces reflect no image. Instead, each holds a slightly different arrangement of the chamber: a route not taken, a pool not yet formed, a root-runner passing with an injury it does not currently possess.

This may be how the hollow thinks. Not in words, nor in one central organ, but by flowering possibilities and reclaiming those that fail to occur.

The root-runner may tend this thought. Its broken flowers remove dangerous repetitions. Its cuts stimulate repair. Its tool introduces a persistent foreign shape that the chamber must answer with new growth. Perhaps the creature is a gardener.

Or perhaps it is a pruning impulse given warm blood and hands.

The moth may perform another function. It gathers water after the chamber has condensed memory into it. By carrying those sounds through its wings, it distributes news between branches too distant to remain connected. Perhaps every apparent moth is one moving message.

Or perhaps it is simply thirsty.

What lives beneath the plain, then, is difficult to divide into host and inhabitant. The runner eats the walls, but helps them heal. The moth drinks the pools, but may pollinate flowers separated by impossible distance. Tools are discarded into flesh and flesh completes the tools. Dead petals become floor. Smoke becomes root. A traveler’s footprint blooms and, if left alone, is eventually taken into the chamber’s body.

Given enough time, anything that remains there begins to participate.

Skin develops fine green lines where it repeatedly touches the walls. Scars grow sensitive to sap movement. Sleep falls into the chamber’s breathing rhythm. A person begins to know when the ceiling will drink and lifts loose belongings before gravity changes. Later, they stop carrying belongings. Later still, black flowers no longer rise in their footprints, as if the chamber no longer regards their passage as an event distinct from its own growth.

The root-runner may once have entered in this way.

It may have had open eyes then. It may have walked on two feet, counted the hanging roots, tried to mark a route, and learned why counting fails. Its seed-shell may be the last fragment of something it brought from outside, altered through so many resin sharpenings that none of the original material remains.

If so, the creature does not appear to mourn what it was.

When the hollow expands after surface rain, the runner climbs to a high wall and braces itself among the translucent stems. Luminous distances open below. Pools become curved lakes. Flowers stand alone across impossible plains. The colorless moth passes overhead, vast and slow.

The runner raises its leaf-sealed face toward the ordinary late-morning light. Green travels through the veins of its lids. It tastes the brightness, listens to the continent of roots tightening under its hands, and taps once with its shell.

From very far away, or from inside the wall, something taps back.
