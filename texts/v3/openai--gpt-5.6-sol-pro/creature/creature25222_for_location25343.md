From the rim, the basin appears inhabited by a few pale, solitary animals.

The most conspicuous are the waders. They stand higher than the low vegetation but lower than the trees, long-legged and narrow-bodied, with hides the color of wet limestone. At rest they resemble pieces of the exposed seam that have risen on jointed stilts. Their legs divide near the ankle into spreading pads, each pad softening until it meets the water without a splash. Underneath are concentric ridges that grip polished rock and shelter minute symbionts. Wherever a wader steps, these symbionts are pressed into the warm film and begin feeding. Thus every footprint becomes, for a few hours, a garden.

The waders browse with their heads submerged. What they eat is not obvious. They pass over visible moss and leaf, drawing instead on the nearly invisible skin of organisms covering the stone. Their mouths are round, toothless, and surrounded by fine sensory threads. These test the water’s temperature, mineral content, and recent history before the animal settles to feed.

They are not truly white. Beneath their translucent hide, slow tides of green, rose, and smoky gold move through branching vessels. The colors come from colonies they carry within themselves: photosynthetic organisms near the back, heat-storing cells along the flanks, and dark digestive cultures in the belly. A wader is less an individual animal than a procession of climates enclosed in one skin.

They seldom approach one another. Courtship occurs through reflections. Two animals may stand on opposite sides of the basin and tilt their bodies until sunlight travels from one pale flank to the other by way of several pools. They exchange patterns in the reflected glare for hours, never closing the distance. Months later, each lays a single flexible egg beneath the same horizontal tree.

The eggs are flat and root-colored. They resemble wet patches until they begin to crawl.

Elsewhere, broad reptiles lie on the bone-pale shelves. Their backs are almost perfectly level and collect shallow puddles when it rains. Moss, swimmers, and even young bladder-plants grow in these temporary pools. An old reptile may support a complete food web without seeming to notice. Its eyes are buried beneath clear plates in the skull. It watches upward through the water on its own back, seeing the sky as a trembling green lens.

Most of the time it basks. Then its feeding organ unfolds.

This is called a tongue only by analogy. It is a transparent sheet stored in layers behind the jaw, and when released it slides over water, moss, and stone without disturbing them. Reflective insects adhere to it by the dozens. The sheet retracts, folds, and is absorbed into the mouth. Afterward the reptile remains still for days, while the empty skin of the tongue grows cloudy and is shed as a film upon which microscopic grazers feed.

In the trees hang the gliders. Folded, they look like narrow leaves that have browned at the margins. Open, each is a hand-sized lattice of ribs with almost no membrane between them. They do not fly by pushing air downward. They alter the warmth above and below their bodies, rising on differences too slight for a larger creature to feel. In consequence their passage is silent. A glider can cross the basin while scarcely moving anything but its eyes.

Their young are raised in droplets.

Condensation hangs beneath the trees in beads large enough to hold a curled larva. The larva breathes through the surface of its droplet and feeds on whatever the living chains deliver from the canopy. As the water diminishes, it grows lighter. Just before the drop would otherwise fall, the juvenile opens its ribs and takes the droplet’s place in the air.

The chains themselves are neither vines nor social insects, though at different hours they resemble both. Each link is a soft six-winged body attached mouth-to-tail to the next by a filament that can be dissolved instantly. A chain may reach from the crown of a tree to the dark web of roots beneath it. Condensation passes along the bodies in bright pulses. Nutrients travel upward; water and chemical instructions travel down.

No member retains one form for long. Those nearest the leaves are thin, reflective, and nearly sexless. Farther down they become dark carriers packed with moisture. At the root, some thicken into brood keepers. The oldest detach, unfurl broad membranes, and descend to graze in the moss. After feeding they climb the trunk as another sex entirely and join a different chain. Whether the chain is a colony of animals or a single body continually replacing its organs depends on where one chooses to draw the boundary.

Such choices become increasingly difficult closer to the water.

There, the transparent swimmers live in layers. Surface grazers are no thicker than fingernail parings. They travel belly-up beneath the water film and scrape brightness from it. Below them move narrow hunters shaped like clear splinters. Their jaws are visible only when blood or green digestive fluid colors them. Deeper still, inside the rounded grooves, blind colonies strain the slow runnels through forests of cilia. Some have occupied the same channels for generations beyond counting. Their dead become the channel walls; their offspring feed through those walls and inherit traces of every dissolved season.

Around the pillars dwell creatures adapted to conditions that exist nowhere else.

When rain enters a pillar and gravity weakens in the adjacent water, soft animals emerge from pores in the stone. They possess no stable upper or lower surface. Their organs drift within them, rearranging around each floating droplet. In ordinary weight they collapse into translucent stains and wait. In the pillar’s altered field they inflate into bells, cups, branching stars, or forms without useful comparison. They filter mist, mate by intersecting, and seed the stone with durable knots of tissue before weight returns.

Some of those knots will not wake for centuries.

The red bladder-plants are less patient. They listen through heat.

Their swollen traps cling to wet ledges in glistening colonies. Hair-fine roots spread outward, touching mineral, moss, fallen skin, and the metal threads of the old works. When a warm-blooded grazer exhales nearby, the colony receives the heat as a moving pressure. Bladders farther along the ledge fill in sequence, calculating speed and distance. Hooks form only after the animal has passed several times by the same route.

When they strike, they do so slowly. A hook catches a tuft of symbiotic fur or the soft edge of a footpad. It rarely wounds. Instead it plants a seed, takes a little salt, and releases a compound that causes the host to return to that pool. The grazer may carry the plant for years as a red fringe along one leg. If it dies, the fringe digests it. If it lives, the plant travels.

The smallest flyers occupy the basin’s glare in uncountable numbers. Their bodies are minute hinged mirrors with living tissue hidden behind the reflective plates. Each continually turns to negotiate between warmth, concealment, and communication. Alone, one is no more than a spark. Together they produce weatherlike events.

At the passage of a cloud, they rotate in succession. Silver becomes gray, gray becomes transparent, and a moving darkness crosses the basin against the direction of the sun. Messages travel inside this darkness: warnings, mating permissions, maps of new mist, news of a reptile’s unfolding tongue. No sound accompanies it. The entire bright air changes its mind without buzzing.

Soundlessness is common to nearly everything here. It is not gentleness.

There is a hunter associated with vibration, though no complete specimen has been seen. Its presence is inferred from disappearances, from the basin’s ancient abandonment of calls, and from certain moments when even the water seems to brace. A creature that scrapes stone may be missing at the next observation. A wing that beats audibly once does not beat twice.

Some believe the hunter lives beneath the central platform and extends itself through the rock as a net of pressure-sensitive filaments. Others believe it has no body except the seven pillars and feeds when they swallow vibration. A third possibility is that it is not a predator at all but a maintenance process, removing organisms whose noise interferes with the basin’s operations.

Whatever it is, prey detect it before instruments do. The waders freeze with their mouths underwater. Gliders clamp themselves into leaf shapes. The reflective flyers turn edge-on and vanish. Even the moss flattens as burrowing bodies draw downward together.

Then something passes—not visibly, but as a sequence of tiny failures of movement. Suspended droplets deform. A patch of swimmers goes still. One of the reptile’s back-pools loses its ripples. Afterward there may be a hollow skin on the stone, intact except that its interior has been removed.

At dusk, armored animals scrape out from beneath the black platform. They are low, many-jointed, and dark enough to appear as openings in the luminous haze. Each carries fragments beneath overlapping plates: seeds, mineral flakes, eggshells, cast membranes, pieces of translucent panel, occasionally a bone polished into a precise curve. They sort these objects into grooves around the platform. Their arrangements persist until morning, when water or some subtle change in weight disperses them.

They may be scavengers. They may be technicians.

Several have metal running through their bodies, not as implants but as inherited anatomy. Their young hatch with soft conductive threads that harden as they feed on mineral films. When an armored animal dies, the thread continues growing through nearby roots. In time it may join one of the old buried lines. Signals then pass through it that resemble neither nerve impulses nor machine current, but something intermediate.

At night almost everything living in the basin becomes part of a larger creature.

The animals gather around the pillars in radiating lines: waders at the outer margins, reptiles low against the stone, gliders hanging open from the trees, armored forms nearest the bases. The flyers occupy the air between them. Swimmers crowd the pools along invisible spokes. Roots alter their internal flow. Chains detach from the branches and stretch toward the gray surfaces.

No one calls. No leader arrives.

The pillars darken, and synchronization begins.

A tremor moves through skin, water, metal, root, and nerve. Waders digest at the same instant. Reptilian hearts delay the same beat. Billions of flyers change angle in patterns too subtle to make light. Creatures with no brains participate through contractions of their membranes. The bladder-plants open their traps. Old dormant knots inside the stone briefly become electrically active.

What exists during those hours cannot be found in any single body.

It may remember storms that occurred before the present species evolved. It may compare the mineral taste of stored rain with patterns carried in migratory flesh. It may dream through the fauna, using each nervous system as one small, temporary chamber. Perhaps it calculates repairs. Perhaps it rehearses vanished landscapes. Perhaps the entire living population is an organ by which the old works sense what they have become.

Before dawn, the shared thing loosens. Memory fragments into instinct, appetite, altered routes, eggs laid in unusual places, a root turning toward metal. The creatures separate without seeming to know they were ever joined.

By daylight there are once again only solitary grazers, basking reptiles, folded leaves, a few red plants, and specks flashing in brilliant air.

Yet none of them lives there alone. Each inhabits another, carries another, becomes another, or waits to be assembled with thousands of others into something whose lifetime is measured not in bodies but in nights. The basin’s true inhabitant may be this recurring assembly: an animal dispersed across water, weather, machinery, and flesh, waking after sunset and dying harmlessly every morning.

Or it may be only the basin itself, having learned over ages to move by borrowing legs.
