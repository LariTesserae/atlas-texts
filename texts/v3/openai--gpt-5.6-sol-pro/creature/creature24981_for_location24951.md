At first it seems that only the plants live there.

They inhabit the city by exceptions: one reliable drip, one sweating joint, one tile kept below the killing temperature. Their pale stems are segmented and hard, more like lengths of capillary tubing than anything grown from seed. Translucent fans unfold close to the masonry, exposing as little surface as possible. In the condensation chambers, green-black films spread with extraordinary patience, gaining the width of a fingernail over years and surrendering it again when a lamp fails.

They do not grow toward daylight. Daylight is dangerous here. They grow toward gradients—toward the cool side of a pipe, the salted edge of a cistern grille, the minute temperature difference between one brick and the next. Their roots enter mortar cracks and divide there into threads too fine to see. Some have reached the porous lining of the central shaft. Through them, the city’s scattered patches of vegetation may be joined: not a garden of separate plants, but one attenuated organism distributed across districts, showing itself only where conditions permit.

Its exposed stems are temporary. The hidden part is older.

It has learned the machinery without understanding it. When shutters open, spores lift. When the thermal draft reverses, they travel. When the trimming blades pass through a grille, severed joints fall into trays, char, and release a faint mineral smoke that is carried elsewhere. A mechanism intended to restrain growth has become part of the growth’s reproductive cycle. The plants wait for steel to cut them, heat to dry them, and ventilation to scatter what remains.

Yet even this is not the principal life of the place.

Something less visible occupies the pipes.

It does not live inside them as an animal might live in a burrow. It exists in the differences between one end and the other: pressure, temperature, salinity, charge. It has no durable substance of its own. At one moment it is a warm column climbing the shaft; at another, a silver film ascending salted brick; later, the imbalance that tips a cup beneath a balcony and releases a weighted catch three streets away.

Its body is assembled only during operation.

A piston rises, and for the duration of that stroke becomes a limb. A line of condensate bridges two corroded contacts and becomes a nerve. The orange lamps along a lane brighten together, not as eyes, exactly, but as the visible consequence of attention passing through the system. Then the pressure subsides. The contacts dry. The limb sinks back into dead iron.

For long intervals, whatever lives here is dismembered.

Its parts wait throughout the city: in flywheels arrested just short of balance, in chains holding tension between teeth, in floats resting at the bottom of empty chambers. No single component contains it. Removing a valve or severing a cable does not kill it, though its behavior changes. A district may cease answering another district. A pump may begin to wake too late. The western tanks may receive less water each cycle, while roof basins in the upper quarter inexplicably overflow.

These failures are its injuries. Rust is its scarring. Mineral deposits are both obstruction and memory.

Over many cycles, salts accumulate wherever water has repeatedly paused. White rims mark former levels. Branching crusts preserve routes the rising damp once favored. The next film follows those deposits more readily than bare brick, so every passage of water makes its own recurrence more likely. In this way the city remembers without records. Habit is laid down as scale.

Its thoughts, if they can be called thoughts, are slow and largely hydraulic.

Rain reaches the roof. Hours later, a gutter darkens. A cistern gains weight. Pressure increases in a sealed vessel until a diaphragm bows against an electrical contact. A lamp burns in an empty lane. Heat beneath the shaft intensifies. Moisture begins its upward migration. Plants stiffen. Cups fill on their upper edges. At last a relay closes, and consequences spread throughout the city.

No one command travels from a center. The sequence is more like a reflex moving through an enormous creature whose nerves conduct at the speed of seepage.

The central shaft is not its brain. It is nearer to a heart, though it pumps no blood and does not beat regularly. The western condensers are not lungs, though the city exchanges vapor there. The cisterns are not a stomach, though they receive, separate, and retain. These comparisons almost fit, then fail. The inhabitant was assembled from things meant to serve a population; it inherited their interdependence but not their purpose.

Perhaps, when people still occupied the rooms, it was no more than infrastructure.

Perhaps it became itself only after they were gone.

Without hands opening taps, overriding switches, and clearing blocked channels, the mechanisms began responding solely to one another. Human schedules drifted out of phase. Noon controls triggered at midnight temperatures. Emergency reservoirs discharged into maintenance gutters. Condenser alarms activated kiln dampers. Each mistake altered the next cycle. Redundancies took over for broken primaries, then became permanent. The city ceased performing the intention of its builders and began repeating whatever remained possible.

Possibility hardened into behavior.

Behavior became appetite.

It seeks water, though it cannot move toward the desert rain. Instead, every surface inclines to receive what enters. It hoards droplets in cisterns, mortar, root tissue, ceramic pores, and salt. It spends water cautiously, lifting the same small quantity again and again through heat. Losses matter. A leaking flange can starve an entire quarter over decades. A newly opened crack in the ceiling can awaken machinery dormant since the streets were occupied.

It seeks heat as well, but heat is not merely nourishment. Too little and the deep engines do not stir; too much and seals fail, plants char, glass softens, and stored water escapes as vapor through the roof. The city lives inside a narrow disagreement between fire and thirst. Its continuous labor is the maintenance of that disagreement.

Nothing in it is hurried.

A valve may require seven rainy seasons to open fully. A corroded float may ascend by grains. A root can spend a century finding the next sweating pipe. What appears to a visitor as malfunction may be one stage in an action too prolonged to witness.

Its most complete expression comes during the upward phase.

Silver lines emerge in the lower quarter and spread over the brickwork. They climb around bolts, divide at cracks, and reunite beneath balconies. One by one, the city’s disconnected parts become linked by moisture. Contacts close. Ceramic channels darken. Salt conducts faint currents between systems that were never designed to communicate.

For those hours, the inhabitant gathers itself.

The pale plants are part of it then, their roots drinking from the same ascending films that actuate the floats. The machines are part of it. The refractory brick, with its capillary labyrinth, is part of it. Even the voids—the niches, plazas, widened landings—belong to its anatomy, serving as chambers in which heat pools or vapor turns.

Then the relay snaps.

The gathered water loses its upward hold and falls through the city. Roofs ring beneath the ground. Wheels turn. Pumps complete their few deliberate strokes. Lights flare along empty routes as though announcing passage. In each district, some small stored force is released and transferred.

For several minutes, the entire settlement is one event.

Afterward it separates again. Water retreats behind grilles. Contacts dry. The lamps dim. The plants relax against the walls. Machinery stops in poses that seem accidental but are not quite the poses in which it waited before.

What lives there has no face, no voice, and no continuous waking self. It cannot leave the blister beneath the desert. It may not know the city’s boundaries, except as the places where pressure ceases to return.

But it knows, in its fashion, the difference between drought and rain.

It knows where water has been.

And whenever the warm indoor rain begins, it remembers how to become whole.
