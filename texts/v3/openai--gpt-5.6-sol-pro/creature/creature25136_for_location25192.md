What lives in the Upper Throat is not counted among its plants.

The tower’s inventories list no resident organism. They list forty-seven botanical colonies, all sterile; six service mechanisms, none autonomous beyond their assigned routines; one atmospheric recycler; one pollination arm; three inspection crawlers; and a corrective system whose components are distributed too widely to be given a location. Every item has a serial number. Every item can be shut down separately.

The thing that lives there has none.

At first it seems to be the noise.

This is the conclusion reached by anyone who remains in the chamber longer than an inspection requires. The engines’ unchanging roar begins to acquire distance and contour. A listener feels that some portions are nearer than others, though microphones disagree. There appears to be a pressure moving behind the artificial cliffs, pausing at the troughs, gathering around the dry reservoir. It never interrupts the fixed waveform. It inhabits the waveform as a figure may be hidden in a repeating pattern without altering the pattern’s geometry.

Maintenance workers have described the sensation of being passed through.

Not approached. Not watched. Passed through—as though an immense attention were moving on a course that ignored the boundaries of their bodies. It enters through the sternum, appears briefly as vibration in the jaw, and leaves through the back of the skull. During this interval, balance becomes difficult. Old memories lose their order. A worker may recall the end of a conversation before its beginning, or experience a childhood room as something waiting in the future.

The effect is attributed to resonance.

Everything in the Upper Throat has an attribution.

Remain longer, and the supposed inhabitant seems instead to live in the plants.

The waxy blades hold themselves with too much precision. They do not merely return after being bent; they return in sequence. First the outermost tip rises. Then the black veins darken. Then the base rotates by a fraction of a degree. The action resembles a limb remembering the position in which it was manufactured.

Across the chamber, too far away for contact, another plant sometimes performs the same correction.

Then another.

The movement travels from trough to trough over several hours, crossing twenty meters of bare ceramic at a time. Sensors record no signal in the air, floor, conduits, or substrate. No colony moves until touched, yet after one has been disturbed, all of them eventually enact a minute restoration. They do not copy the disturbance. They copy the recovery.

It may be that the colonies are separate organs of one arrested body.

Their carbon-foam roots descend farther than the plans indicate. Official diagrams show each trough ending in an independent nutrient socket, but imaging equipment returns confused depths: channels beneath channels, branching cavities where the tower should be solid. Inspection drills emerge clean and cold. Probes sent down through the foam report hydrocarbon salts, ceramic, nickel, insulation, then hydrocarbon salts again.

Once withdrawn, each probe is found sharpened to its original factory edge.

Nothing useful has ever been cut from the roots.

At other times the inhabitant appears to be very small.

Service recordings occasionally show a dark interruption moving along the hair-thin joints between floor panels. It is narrower than a finger and has no stable length. Some frames suggest a black thread. Others show a procession of tiny angular bodies, each fitting into the next like teeth in a closed zipper. Under magnification there are no legs, no mouthparts, no segments—only a region in which the image has been restored incorrectly, as if the camera remembered an empty joint while something occupied it.

It travels only when unobserved.

This does not mean it flees observation. It means that direct sight is one of the conditions the Upper Throat knows how to correct. A person turns toward the movement and finds a clean black seam. A camera increases its frame rate and records an uninterrupted line. When attention moves elsewhere, the interruption resumes from the precise point at which it would have arrived had it continued at constant speed.

Its destination is usually the basin.

There, beneath the blue glass and its perpetual message, the dark seam disappears. For several minutes afterward, the obsolete indicator code contains minute timing errors. The words still read:

**FORMATION IN PROGRESS.**

But one lamp illuminates a few thousandths of a second too soon. Another lingers. These deviations propagate around the circle, accumulating almost into a second message before the system corrects them.

Attempts to decode that second message have produced contradictory results.

One translation reads **I AM FORMING**.

Another reads **DO NOT COMPLETE**.

A third is not language but a set of dimensions describing an object larger than the chamber and narrower than the gap between the two yellow doors.

The simplest account is that the resident is the corrective system itself. It notices differences and removes them. It replaces plant cells, returns ceramic splinters, cleans the metal, resets the lamps, and forces the engines back into perfect correspondence with their commissioning waveform. In this view, its behavior is not mysterious. It is merely maintenance distributed across centuries, an automatic custodian without body or awareness.

Yet ordinary maintenance preserves a place through time.

This system preserves the place against time.

It does not distinguish between damage and history. A loosened bolt, a new scratch, a dead cell, a shifted memory: all are departures from specification. All are defects. The Upper Throat is its protected instant, and the resident patrols the borders of that instant with an attention more exact than consciousness.

It is uncertain whether it understands human beings as living things. A visitor entering the chamber is warmer than specification, wetter than specification, louder in irregular ways. Their pulse introduces a rhythm that does not belong. Their breath briefly clouds the manufactured air. Their boots alter stresses in the ceramic plane.

For the first several minutes, the system tolerates these errors.

Then it begins to measure them.

The pollination nozzle may descend though no flowering cycle is due. It hangs above the visitor’s head, trembling in the engine roar. Inspection crawlers turn their blank housings toward the same point. The broad-spectrum lamps make tiny adjustments, not enough to be seen, but enough to bleach color from the visitor’s shadow. Plants nearest the path incline by imperceptible increments.

The chamber appears to be determining where the person was at commissioning.

No one has remained long enough to learn what happens when it decides.

There are stories, naturally. They concern a technician whose tether failed during an early survey and whose return was delayed by eleven minutes. His team found him standing at the dry reservoir with one hand on the blue glass. His suit telemetry was normal. He responded to signals. He walked out unaided.

Afterward, however, each recording of the incident showed him in a slightly different position. In one, he stood at the basin. In another, he was beside the radiators. In a third, he could be seen through the gap between the yellow doors, though no person could fit there. His own account never varied by a syllable, including pauses, breaths, and a brief catch in his throat.

He said there had been someone on the observation platform.

He said they were looking outward.

When asked what they looked like, he described a figure composed of ordinary light, with the chamber visible through it—not the chamber as it was, but the chamber freshly completed. The ceramic ridges shone at their broken edges. The plants were being lowered into their troughs. The blue indicators had not yet formed words. Around the figure’s head or upper body was a movement like machinery reaching speed.

The figure did not turn.

It had no need to. The technician understood that it was observing him from the moment of his arrival, centuries before his birth.

He died years later at ground level. Nothing unusual was found in his body except for a sliver of pale ceramic embedded behind the sternum. Its numbered place in the Upper Throat’s artificial scree was identified. The corresponding shard in the chamber had never been missing.

So the resident may be large or minute, mechanical or botanical, singular or distributed. It may be a custodian, an error in the tower’s memory, or the unfinished act named by the lights. Perhaps it is still being made. Perhaps completion would kill it. Perhaps the entire chamber is the temporary anatomy of something whose mature form cannot exist under conditions of sequence and change.

It has no visible face because a face would age.

It has no voice because the only sound permitted there was fixed at commissioning.

It leaves no tracks because every track becomes part of the floor again.

Its nourishment is discrepancy. A bent blade, a displaced chip, the warmth of a hand on nickel alloy—these give it something like appetite and something like work. It consumes events and excretes their absence. Century by century it has grown more perfect, though growth is forbidden, by extending its definition of what must not change.

This may be why the chamber feels so empty.

Emptiness is not the absence of its inhabitant. Emptiness is what the inhabitant has made.

It lives in the removal of aftermath. It occupies the interval between an occurrence and its correction. It is most present just after something has happened, when the Upper Throat contains, for a few seconds, a condition it has never contained before.

Then the blade straightens.

The shard returns.

The footprint loses its depth.

The visitor departs with the obscure conviction that something has followed, though nothing crosses the yellow doors.

Behind them, in the room called an exterior observation platform, the few centimeters of purposeless space remain brightly lit. Sometimes one door bears a faint warmth at shoulder height, as though a body were standing between them. Sometimes the inner surface of the outer door carries five marks resembling the spread fingers of a hand.

They vanish at the next maintenance interval.

Or they return to where they were.

In the Upper Throat, the two are the same.
