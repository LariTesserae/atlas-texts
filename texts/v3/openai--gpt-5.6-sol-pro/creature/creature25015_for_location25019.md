At first, what lives in the Low Works would not be recognized as an inhabitant.

It has no territory distinguishable from the masonry, no shelter separate from a flaw, no body that crosses the open paving. It does not move against the continent’s gradients. It accepts them. Its migrations are accomplished by stormwater, by the lifting of dust, by the slow opening of joints under heat. Much of the time it is indistinguishable from residue.

On exposed floors there are films finer than soot. They lie a fraction below the surface, inside the pores of brick and lime, where light penetrates but the wind cannot scour. On white paving they lend a faint cream or greenish cast. On basalt they are visible only after rain, when every block darkens except for narrow, living margins around its edge. These films drink first. Before the gutters murmur, before the moss changes color, they have swollen into activity.

They are the Low Works’ smallest keepers of time. Some wake for an hour in a year. Some remain dry through many seasons, their cells sealed in mineral sugars and dust. When water reaches them, they divide, stain the pores, and leave behind infinitesimal skins that trap the next windblown particles. In this way they manufacture the first suggestion of soil—not as a layer, but as a darkening within the constructed surface.

The moss comes after.

Dry, it resembles damage: black collars on cistern rims, scorched-looking smears beneath overhangs, brittle scales inside the mouths of drains. Rain transforms it too quickly to seem botanical. Black becomes green from the center outward. Threads loosen. Tiny leaves lift, overlap, and hold droplets above the brick. For a day the cisterns wear soft margins. Then the color withdraws, and the moss closes around its remaining moisture.

It grows most densely where condensation falls with regularity. Beneath the ceramic fins, each repeated drop has made a small round garden. The gardens are arranged in rows corresponding to the projections overhead: thousands of dark green coins on an otherwise empty floor. Their edges seldom touch. Between one drop and the next lies ground too dry to cross.

Other mosses inhabit the undersides of things. They line the shaded lips of stair-dams and follow hairline cracks into cool chambers. Their growth is pale and elongated, almost translucent. They possess no reason to be sturdy. Wind never reaches them, and nothing brushes past. They hang in delicate fringes that would be destroyed by an animal’s passage, had any animal ever passed.

Above these films and fringes stand the seam plants.

From a distance they seem like rulings drawn across the Works in charcoal and green. They follow mortar joints with such obedience that they turn at right angles, divide into grids, and run straight for kilometers. A colony may occupy one longitudinal seam no wider than a finger while identical seams on either side remain bare. Its roots spread horizontally through damp mortar, flattening when a joint narrows and thickening in hidden pockets where lime has crumbled.

The most common is a hard, blue-gray grass. Its leaves do not form lush blades but folded needles with pale reflective hairs. In drought they twist shut until a stand resembles a scatter of wire filings. After rain they open within hours. Their color deepens; their tips bend under droplets; and fine reddish stems rise with seed heads held only a little above the hot floor.

No creature pollinates them. They release their pollen into the evening air, when weak basin winds begin moving toward the day’s heated districts. Most falls uselessly on paving. Some enters another seam. Their seeds are flat on one side and slightly cupped on the other. Wind slides them across tile; water floats them like tiny boats. At every curb they accumulate in thousands, mixed with husks and mineral grit. Nearly all die. The few that lodge in a damp joint establish the next green line.

Because the Low Works has no ordinary seasons, the grasses do not share a calendar. Rain may wake one district while another remains dormant beyond a dry retaining wall. A flowering front can travel along a drainage system for months, always appearing after the water and vanishing before the next basin fills. Seen from high above, if there were anyone to see it, reproduction would look like a dim green signal passing through the continent’s channels.

The shrubs are slower and less exact.

They gather at north-facing walls, where shade moves only slightly through the year. Their branches lie close to the masonry, silver on top and nearly black beneath. Salt beads at the tips of their smallest leaves. They tolerate it by drawing brine into old tissues and abandoning them. Around every living shrub is a brittle skirt of salt-filled twigs, gray enough to resemble ash.

Their roots are not shaped like the roots of plants from deep ground. There is nowhere for a taproot to go. Instead they form broad, compressed fans within the courses, branching repeatedly along interfaces between brick and mortar. They find old finger impressions and fill them. They enter the grooves left by woven mats. Sometimes a root traces the drag of a reed made during construction, preserving that vanished gesture in living tissue.

When a wall heats, the outer roots dry and die. When rain enters from above, deeper strands wake. Thus the shrub continually exchanges one hidden architecture for another. What appears above the surface may be only a small fraction of it: a handful of leaves fed by a root network extending through several hundred meters of wall.

Some networks reach cracks on both sides. There a single shrub rises as two separate plants, one in sunlight and one in shade, with no visible connection. Their leaves can differ so much in size and color that they seem unrelated. Yet when rain reaches the wall, both flower on the same evening.

Their flowers are minute and without fragrance. Fragrance would summon nothing.

In the deep courts stand the largest inhabitants: trees, though they do not possess the easy vertical certainty of trees rooted in earth. They begin in drain mouths where silt has gathered. Their trunks emerge tight against stone, flattening on one side, sometimes enclosing the rim of a channel without breaking it. Roots descend through accumulated sediment until they reach paving, then turn and travel outward in every joint available to them.

The oldest trees have mapped entire courts beneath the surface. Their root systems reproduce the drainage geometry: straight lines, angular branches, sudden concentrations around hidden cisterns. Above, their crowns are thin and asymmetrical, shaped less by wind than by the moving boundary of shade. One side may bear narrow green sprays while the other stands bare for years, waiting for a change in runoff.

During floods, these trees are briefly surrounded by water. Their lower bark darkens. Fine roots rise out of submerged silt to breathe, forming pale bristles around the trunk. Seeds loosen and drift across the temporary lake. They collect on distant stairs in tidy horizontal bands marking each stage of evaporation.

A few germinate there. The seedlings survive while the step remains damp, then perish from the top downward. Their roots persist longer than their stems, entering microscopic separations in the masonry. Sometimes the next storm arrives soon enough to restore them. A seedling that appears dead may green again at its base after years of stillness.

Nothing eats the leaves. Nothing bores into the wood. No nests weigh down the branches. Fallen seeds show no tooth marks, and fruit, where any plant makes it, ripens without being sought. The plants consequently bear strange excesses and strange deficiencies. They need no thorns against mouths, yet some retain hard points inherited from elsewhere. They produce more seed than any local ecology can use because dispersal is almost entirely waste. Flowers open to wind and close untouched. Dead wood remains intact until heat splits it, salt weakens it, or rare floodwater carries it into a drain.

Decay is quiet and incomplete. There are living films that soften fallen stems and darken trapped leaves, but no visible swarm arrives to consume them. A branch may lie across a curb for decades, bleached on its upper face and damp beneath. Dust gathers against it. Grass roots enter the dust. Moss enters the roots’ shade. By the time the wood collapses, it has become the ridge of a narrow island.

These islands are the richest places in the Low Works.

They begin with almost nothing: a stem, a chip of unfired brick, a clot of grass lodged against a step. The obstruction catches silt. The silt holds water. Films spread through it; moss covers it; seam grass sends roots into it from below. Shrubs establish on the thicker ends. Each storm enlarges the island by a few grains and may also tear half of it away.

The inhabitants therefore live by constructing, though on a scale opposite to that of the absent builders. The builders assembled the continent from carried blocks. The plants disassemble it into habitable particles. Roots powder mortar. Moss retains acids against lime. Films enter ceramic pores. Shrubs pry apart courses by the width of a hair. Then all of them catch what they have loosened and make from it a dark, frail ground.

There are places where this process has advanced far enough to conceal a whole tile. Such places are rare. A patch of true soil no larger than a sleeping body may contain the remains of thousands of growth cycles. Grass thickens there. A shrub rises higher than the sheltering wall. After rain, its surface stays dark for several days longer than the paving around it.

But abundance is dangerous. A dense patch transpires too quickly and exhausts its own reservoir. Roots crowd the cracks that feed them. Stems catch hot wind. Without grazing or trampling, the center fills with dead growth until water is diverted around it. The colony then hollows itself out: living at the edges, gray and vacant within. From above it forms a ring, echoing the salt marks around the shrinking lakes.

No single organism governs these enclaves. Yet over distance they behave like parts of one dispersed life.

The black films prepare the pores. Moss deepens the damp. Grass catches dust. Shrubs open joints. Trees reach buried water and shed matter into the courts. Storms detach fragments of each and carry them downslope. A basin that has never supported more than a green stain may receive, in one afternoon, the remnants of a garden hundreds of kilometers away.

What arrives is not always alive in any obvious sense. It may be a sealed seed, a dry shred of moss, a root tip encased in clay. It waits where gravity leaves it. The wait may be longer than the growth that follows. In the Low Works, dormancy is not an interruption of life but its usual condition.

So the continent’s inhabitants are mostly absent even when present: folded into seams, sealed under salt, pale inside walls, or sleeping in the dust at the bottom of an empty reservoir. Their visible green moments are brief disclosures.

And slowly, without feet, they are traveling.

They proceed along the intended falls of plazas and the accidental lifts of tiles. They cross districts in floods, climb walls through condensation, descend into foundations through cracks. The made gradients tell them where to go, but their roots alter those instructions. A blocked gutter spills into a court it was never meant to feed. A shrub splits a curb. A tree turns a straight channel aside with its trunk. Water follows the change, and life follows water.

The first wild act in the Low Works is not growth itself.

It is the revision of a slope.
