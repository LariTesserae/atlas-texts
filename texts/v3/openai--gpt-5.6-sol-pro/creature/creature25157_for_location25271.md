What lives there first appears to be innumerable.

There are the pavement leaves, each broad enough to hold a pool of rain and thick enough to outlast the tower that shaded it. They lie in overlapping colonies, their margins fitted together, lifting and settling as pressure travels through the ground. Each possesses a pale sensory seam along its underside. Where the seam touches another leaf, the two exchange moisture, sugars, and minute electrical disturbances. A footfall would not alarm them—nothing has feet—but a change in weight is carried outward through the avenue until distant roots begin reinforcing themselves.

There are the tower growths: pale, urgent trunks that rise in bundles and constrict one another into stability. Their hollows are not chambers in the human sense. They are lungs for pressure, resonators, cool wells for storing air, pollen, and weather. Their windows regulate heat. Their balconies expose spore tissue to the wind. When a tower leans, vines thicken on the far side, drawing it upright. When it breaks, the remaining columns seal themselves with resin and continue growing around the absence.

The reed districts seem to be another kind of life altogether. Slender, jointed, and nearly leafless, they respond to changes in the atmosphere by producing sound. A falling barometer lowers their pitch. Heat sharpens it. Approaching rain passes through them as a long chord, too coordinated to be accidental and too vast to resemble any call made by a throat. Their screaming may loosen pollen, warn distant tissues, or simply be the unavoidable voice of their structure. Roots beneath them vibrate in answer.

In the wet courts live the bulbs: blind, bladderlike growths packed beneath flexible floors. They drink runoff, swell, divide, and fuse. Some hold clear water. Others hold dark nutrient syrups or dense suspensions of dormant cells. During drought they collapse one by one, sending their contents into the deepest roots. A court can seem dead for years and then inflate before dawn, lifting an entire plaza on the pressure of water gathered in some distant quarter.

The flowers have their own brief lives. They emerge without beauty intended for witnesses. Some are warm as blood, though no blood exists here. Some are black discs charged with static. Some are colorless membranes stretched between branches, visible only when pollen strikes them. They court one another through thunder, vibration, and underground exchange. One may open in the east while its receptive half opens simultaneously inside a western wall. To call these halves separate flowers is convenient, but perhaps incorrect.

Then there are the dismantlers.

They resemble fungi because they have become what fungi would have been needed for. They bloom from fresh fractures in forests of wax-white threads and soft antlered stalks. They enter fallen wood through its vessels, reverse its growth, and make it soluble. Their sheaths become hot enough to steam after rain. For a day or a week they possess whole ruins, turning walls into amber slurry. Once their work is done, they liquefy too. Nothing remains to distinguish the eater from what was eaten.

Each of these lives has its own rhythm and apparent purpose. Each can be separated from the others by shape. Yet separation becomes difficult below the surface.

A tower root descends, branches, and joins the mat beneath a canal. The canal lining becomes the root of a thorn gate. The gate’s vascular strands pass into a reservoir court, where they divide among bulbs that feed a flower several kilometers away. Tissue grafts to tissue without rejection. Signals cross every boundary. Nutrients taken from a collapsed northern district appear in the sap of southern towers before any continuous route can be traced between them.

Even death does not establish an individual. A fallen structure may be dead along its exposed face, alive in its core, digesting itself at one end and budding at the other. It may separate from the substrate, drift with a sliding garden, and root elsewhere. It may later reconnect to the same system it left, though “the same” and “left” are uncertain words in a place where a root can cross distance without occupying the ground between.

What lives there may therefore be a colony.

If so, its members are more thoroughly joined than any forest elsewhere. They share water, memory, wounds, and reproductive tissue. A drought endured by one district changes the shape of leaves grown years later in another. A root front encountering hard clay sends a slow electrical pattern beneath the basin; new towers then favor wide foundations, even where the clay lies deep and untouched. The colony remembers conditions through architecture.

But a colony suggests that its members could once have lived apart. There is no evidence they could.

A seed released from one of the armored pods does not contain a miniature version of the whole. It contains a proposal: a knot of tissue able to become root, reservoir, blossom, reed, or wall according to what receives it. Most seeds do not sprout in soil. They strike living surfaces and are admitted. Their shells open only after the surrounding plant sends the correct chemical sequence. They are less offspring than packets in which the growth moves pieces of itself from one part of itself to another.

Nor is reproduction clearly distinct from construction. When a bud the size of a town unfolds, it may be a new district, a reproductive organ, an experiment in climate control, or all three. It may release seed into the high air and then become a lake. It may close around its own pollen and generate unfamiliar tissue inside. Occasionally such a bud disappears through one of the basin’s folded adjacencies. The root mat shudders for days afterward, as though something enormous has detached—or as though the organism has extended into a place for which the basin has no direction.

It is tempting to call the whole growth intelligent. It senses light, weight, water, chemical injury, vibration, heat, and spatial strain. It anticipates storms. It redirects resources. It repairs damage and repeats useful forms. It retains the consequences of events after their physical traces are gone.

Yet it does not behave like a mind housed in a body.

There is no center where its perceptions meet. No ruling trunk, no buried brain, no oldest heartwood from which decisions descend. A shadow causes leaves nearby to turn; their turning changes air pressure; reeds translate that change into vibration; vibration alters the opening of distant pores; the changed flow of water swells a root gate elsewhere. What looks like a decision emerges from the passage of disturbance through millions of responsive surfaces.

Its thoughts, if they are thoughts, are districts.

A covered arcade might be a remembered drought. A spiral stair might be the working-out of tension between two root masses. A plaza opening to the sun may be attention made physical. When a neighborhood folds, migrates, and is absorbed into another, that may be closer to forgetting than to demolition. The impossible geometry may not be a power the organism uses so much as one of its senses: it feels nearness according to need, and its growth makes that nearness true.

It has no reason to distinguish architecture from anatomy or anatomy from landscape. A bridge is a branch under tension. A reservoir is a swollen cell repeated at civic scale. Streets are the spaces required for crowns to breathe. Towers are vascular bundles enlarged until weather can pass through them. The basin is not occupied by its body. The basin is a temporary shape its body is taking.

Its apparent imitations of a city are not imitations. The amphitheaters do not await audiences. The niches do not anticipate figures. The avenues were not made for traffic. These forms arise because circulation, support, storage, exchange, and exposure produce similar geometries whether they serve citizens or sap. The resemblance is strongest at the moment before function diverges. Then the growth continues, and a palace becomes a flower, a marketplace becomes a seed case, a throne roots downward and drinks.

There may once have been many ancestral plants, though nothing in the living basin preserves the distinction. Grafting, parasitism, fusion, and digestion may have continued until no lineage remained pure. The present being could be an alliance so old that cooperation has passed beyond dependence into identity. It could also be a single organism that learned to manufacture diversity within itself, producing temporary species as another plant produces leaves.

No animal lives upon it. That absence may be a defense, an incompatibility, or simply the condition under which it came into being. Its nectars are not food. Its cavities are not shelter. Its flowers offer no invitations. Even the moss-lined silent chambers, so perfectly shaped for refuge, regulate pressure and humidity for tissues in the walls. Nothing is meant to enter them.

The city is not lonely. Loneliness requires recognition of an absent other.

It is nevertheless full of encounters. Root meets root. Pollen finds receptive flesh. Water arrives from a folded canal. A migrating garden presses against a tower district, and both alter themselves along the contact. New hybrids of structure spread outward from the collision. For a few days the boundary produces arches, chambers, lattices, and unfamiliar blooms. Then the two growths are inseparable, if they were ever two.

At any given moment, portions of the being are young, mature, collapsing, dormant, digesting, reproducing, and not yet spatially present. It has no single age. Dawn is happening somewhere inside it even at noon: condensation gathering in a courtyard whose opening leads to an earlier hour of the same ordinary day. Elsewhere, tissues respond to rainfall that has not crossed the visible sky.

So what lives in the basin cannot be pointed to as one object among others.

It is the green density and every interval within it. It is the towers and the forces that topple them, the cisterns and the thirst that empties them, the flowers and the underground exchanges between flowers. It is also the changing adjacency that lets one root touch another across impossible distance. Its body includes not only matter but arrangement.

Perhaps it is one plant attempting every possible landscape.

Perhaps “plant” is only the nearest surviving word for it.

And perhaps the city is not the shape of the creature at all, but the shape of its ongoing act: drinking the air, folding distance, breaking itself down, and growing itself again before the map can harden around it.
