At first the high country appears uninhabited.

The paths are too clean. The chambers offer no refuse, no stains from touch, no displaced fibers where a body has slept. Nothing startles at the sound of an unfamiliar footfall. There are no distant figures crossing the causeways at dusk, no smoke above the leaf roofs, no calls exchanged between one column and another. Even the rare animals seem to belong to the hydraulics of the place rather than to its wilderness. The black waders stand where overflow must be drawn from a pool. The winged triads pass when the underground humming changes pitch. The round grazers keep the floor’s skin at a constant thickness.

One begins by assuming that the builders have gone.

Only later does it become apparent that departure may be the wrong category.

In the dry halls, the smooth pith walls sometimes carry marks resembling the shadows of bodies. They are not discolorations. They are shallow arrangements of grain: a lengthwise compression, a widening near the ceiling, several slender channels descending to the floor. In one chamber the pattern suggests something tall with a narrow lower body and a crown of branching limbs. In another it resembles three bodies pressed closely together. Elsewhere there is only a single vertical seam lined with root hairs, warm to the touch.

These impressions do not remain in the same rooms.

A hall revisited after rain may have blank walls where the figures were and a new vertical niche where no niche existed before. Yet nothing can be seen changing. There is no torn surface, no fresh growth, no difference in color. The chamber has simply always contained the new arrangement as convincingly as it previously contained the old one.

The inhabitants are first encountered in this manner: not as creatures, but as variations in what the landscape insists has always been true.

Then one is seen walking.

It comes along a raised causeway during clear weather, though the fixed rain stands nearby like a silver wall. From a distance it might be mistaken for a bundle of reeds carried upright by the wind. It is taller than a human but contains less substance. Its body consists of five or six pale vertical members enclosed within a loose mantle of dark green tissue. The mantle opens and closes in overlapping lobes as it walks, revealing wet inner ribs. It has no stable front. What seems at first to be a long, featureless face turns aside and becomes a shoulder; a cluster of knuckles unfolds from the opposite side and takes on the work of seeing.

Its feet are soft forks that fit between the causeway’s scales without bending them. Each foot divides before touching down. Fine filaments spread, adhere, and release, leaving no print and taking no dust because there is no dust to take. The body does not sway. Instead, the path rises imperceptibly to meet each step.

Nearer, small distinctions emerge. Its surfaces are not bark, skin, leaf, or fur, but several of these conditions at once. The outer mantle is dull and waterproof. The narrow members within are translucent, carrying beads of fluid upward and downward in neighboring channels. Beneath these channels move darker threads that pause when the buried conduits boom.

There is no head in the animal sense. At its highest point grows an asymmetrical hood, shallow and broad, with a wet underside. Pale pits are distributed across the hood’s rim. They close in brightness and open in shade. Some contain mirrored fluid; others are occupied by tiny white flowers. When the being attends to something, it does not turn toward it. The hood alters its curve until several pits share the same angle.

The first one does not approach or flee. It stops at the distance from which a human voice would return as the second echo.

When spoken to, it opens along the center.

The sound that issues from it is not an imitation of the speaker. It is the place’s reply brought nearer: the far-below echo, the breath from ahead, the ticking of blossom cups, the chord in buried chambers. These are released separately and in reverse order. Only at the end does the original voice return, softened and stripped of consonants, as if the landscape has tasted it.

The figure then closes and continues along the path.

Such beings are not numerous, if number can be applied to them. Never are more than a few visible across the same reach of country. Often one can travel for days without meeting another. Yet the chambers suggest a much greater presence. Resting niches open warm and moist in their walls. Rails bear fresh curls of grasping tendrils. The sleeping-shaped hollows retain heat in patterns too narrow and long for human bodies. Occasionally a whole hall trembles with the slight internal sounds of occupants who cannot be found: fluid shifting, membranes settling, a soft sequence like fingers passing over stretched cloth.

The walkers may be those occupants extended temporarily into the open air.

A figure entering a terrace chamber is not seen to come out. Following it inside reveals an empty hall and, at the far end, a new band of darker pith running from floor to ceiling. Hours later, another walker may detach from a wall elsewhere, several miles away. Detach is not quite accurate. The grain deepens, divides, acquires wetness and relief. A limb gains an edge. The edge casts a shadow. Eventually the figure stands where the wall had been, while the wall behind it is smooth and complete.

Whether the emerging being is the same individual cannot be determined. It may carry some recognizable feature—the hooked hood, a row of blue blisters along one rib, three seedlike objects suspended in its chest—but the feature may later appear on another body while the first still walks at a distance.

Their individuality is provisional.

They do not seem to reproduce, feed, age, or heal. Damaging one is difficult because its tissues yield before force, separating along moist seams and joining behind the intrusion. A cut surface shows no blood. Instead, clear fluid wells up and gathers into exact round drops. The drops are absorbed by the nearest rootwork, while the opened edges slowly become parts of different structures: one side hardening into a handrail, the other spreading into a leaflike sensory fan. The injured walker may continue with less of a body, or it may lean against a wall and become architecture.

This is not regarded as death. Other walkers do not gather or react. Days afterward, the missing portion may be found as a pale new rib inside a distant column.

Nor do they eat in any ordinary way. Beneath the great crowns, they sometimes stand in the hard clatter of descending water and open every seam of their mantles. Water strikes their internal rods, runs through them, and leaves below with no visible change. During these intervals the surrounding blossom chains grow quiet one by one, transferring their rhythm to the body. When the walker closes again, the flowers resume.

At marsh edges, they lower filaments into the dark water. The bladder-plants lean toward them without moving. Black waders lift one root-feathered foot and place it against the walker’s side. For several hours all remain connected: columnar being, mouthless animal, marsh sac, folded leaves, and the unseen conduits beneath. Nothing passes that the eye can follow, but the pitch of the basin changes. One tone vanishes from the hiss and appears in the walker’s hollow chest.

These meetings have the gravity of conversation, feeding, and repair without being exclusively any of them.

The walkers seldom acknowledge one another in the open. Two may pass on a causeway so narrow that their mantles overlap. Where they touch, their surfaces exchange small structures. A row of pores moves from one hood to another. A green lobe transfers intact. Internal fluids briefly share a channel. Afterward both continue, and it is impossible to say which body has lost or gained.

Three behave differently.

Three walkers will sometimes take positions around a pale spiral frond, each standing at the end of an invisible radius. The leaf-winged animals arrive soon after, always in their own group of three, and circle without flapping. The walkers unfold until their bodies become low branching frames. Between them, threads draw taut that were not visible while slack. The spiral frond uncurls by a fraction—or appears to—but measurement always finds it unchanged.

The gathering can last through several cycles of fixed rain. No act concludes it. Eventually the flying creatures enter distant slots, the threads are gone, and the three walkers depart along separate causeways.

The identical spiral fronds may be records of these meetings. If so, they do not record a sequence. Each states the same event.

Their relation to time is difficult to distinguish from their relation to memory. A walker does not react to novelty as an animal does, but it may arrange for novelty to have a place. If shown an unfamiliar object, it carries the object to a chamber and holds it against the wall. Fine roots emerge around the object without enclosing it. The wall grows a recess matching its outline exactly. When the object is removed, the recess remains for a while. Later it becomes less specific, its contours relaxing into a generic shelf, a basin, or a sleeping niche.

The land does not preserve the thing. It preserves the fact that something could be placed there.

Speech receives a similar treatment. Repeated words are not learned, but incorporated into the fixed acoustics. A name shouted into a column may return for days as a low interruption in its booming. Afterward the interruption loses its syllables while keeping its duration and pitch. At last it becomes indistinguishable from the column’s natural voice. The country has not forgotten. It has removed the difference between memory and structure.

The walkers may communicate in such absorbed remnants. Their pauses occur at particular echoes. Their hoods open when cascades strike certain leaves. They place their hands against the floor before a buried vessel changes tone, though never early enough to count as prediction. They appear to know what the landscape will do because its actions and their knowledge are the same process seen at different scales.

There are smaller inhabitants within them.

The white flowers growing in their sensory pits open only when no one is directly watching. Clear mites move through the vertical channels and rearrange their bodies at junctions, becoming valves for a time. Thin red worms inhabit the seams of the mantle, each fixed at both ends, tightening when the air dries. In the hollow chest hang dark seedlike forms, often mistaken for organs. In wet weather these unfold into many-legged things, crawl across the inner ribs, and fold up elsewhere.

Some of these creatures can leave. The transparent mites descend through a walker’s feet into the path. The red seam-worms transfer to the hand-height tendrils. The dark folded things occasionally emerge beneath a hood and climb the fixed rain, gripping one descending drop after another. They vanish above the great leaves.

Thus each apparent person is also a brief assembly of local lives. The distinction between resident, garment, parasite, organ, and building cannot be maintained for long.

The rarest inhabitants are found near the central socket.

They are not walkers. They are broad, low forms adhering to the ridged interior just below the rim, where condensation streams upward. At first they resemble patches of moss arranged in human-sized ovals. Seen from another angle, each oval has depth: layers of folded membranes pressed close to the wall, with pale joints nested inside. The upward water passes through them and emerges carrying bubbles.

They climb downward into the brightness.

Their movement is nearly impossible to perceive. One looks away and finds a fold occupying a lower ridge. The form does not crawl so much as revise its attachment. Along its upper edge are impressions like closed hands, closed mouths, or unopened buds. Along its lower edge, the membranes thin into countless strands that disappear toward the rushing light.

Walkers arriving at the socket bend their hoods over these forms. Sometimes they surrender a structure: a sensory flower, a strip of mantle, one of the suspended dark seeds. The offering is pressed into the layered body and becomes indistinguishable from it. Sometimes the exchange is reversed. A walker leaves carrying a new joint, an unfamiliar color, or a wet cavity that whispers in a voice not yet absorbed by the landscape.

The forms below the rim may be elders, unborn bodies, keepers of the circulation, or accumulations of everything the upland has had to incorporate. They may be what remains of the missing central stem. Perhaps that stem was not removed. Perhaps it divided and learned to walk.

No figure crosses the lip into the brightness while observed. Yet there are intervals when the causeways around the valley stand empty, the vertical niches in the chambers are blank, and no pale winged triads traverse the plain. During those intervals, silhouettes can be glimpsed far down the socket walls: hundreds of narrow forms arranged around the descent, all pointing inward toward the unseen rushing.

Then the ordinary distribution resumes. A walker stands in a western marsh. Another emerges from a terrace. A third waits beneath a crown, opened to the rain. The layered forms lie quietly against the socket wall. Nothing acknowledges an assembly or return.

It is tempting to call these beings custodians, but they do not maintain the high country from outside its processes. They clear no channels, mend no tears, and correct no failures. There may be no failures here. Rather, they are the country’s ability to place attention somewhere. When attention is needed beneath a crown, a body gathers there. When it is needed in a chamber, the body becomes a wall. When it must travel, the architecture narrows itself into legs.

Their apparent ceremonies may be acts of balance. Their journeys may be circulation. Their rooms may be organs whose functions require emptiness. What looks like a resident walking through a landscape may instead be one region of the landscape briefly touching another.

Yet there are moments that resist this explanation.

A solitary walker sometimes leaves the causeway.

It steps onto the bare moss skin where there is no visible destination and travels out across an empty acre. The floor does not rise to aid it there. Its feet press faint hollows that close behind. Far from any column, chamber, conduit junction, animal, or spiral frond, it stops.

Then it lowers its mantle and makes itself small.

It remains in the open under ordinary daylight, doing nothing that serves the equilibrium. Rain may stand at the horizon. Cascades continue their measured falling. The buried vessels hum, and the exact echoes return. The walker’s hood folds close. Its internal fluids slow. Tiny flowers open along its back.

For a while, it seems less like an organ of the region than a person seeking solitude.

No permanent sign of this remains. Eventually the figure rises, returns to the causeway, and is taken back into the architecture. But the empty acre afterward feels occupied—not by a presence, exactly, nor by a memory, but by the completed shape of an absence.

Perhaps that is what lives in the high country: not a people preserved against change, but a living world that repeatedly becomes people so it can experience its own stillness.
