At first, the inhabitants do not resolve into animals. They are warmth disturbed into motion.

A hand placed near the entrance feels them before the eye finds them: little impacts in the rising air, pressure changes from wings turning in confined space, currents broken and rejoined by thousands of bodies. Their voices overlap until individuality disappears. The Cleft seems to breathe coarsely through fur, membrane, feather, shell, and naked skin.

The broad-eared bats occupy its hottest folds. They hang wherever stone can be gripped, packing themselves into seams until the rock appears to have grown a dark, trembling pelt. Their ears are almost too large for their heads: thin, veined leaves continually swiveling toward cries and echoes. At rest, they are not truly still. Pups push through the curtain in search of milk. Adults quarrel over fractions of a ledge. Claws release and catch again. Wings open briefly like black hands and close around the body.

No bat keeps a permanent place. Warmth, damp, pregnancy, parasites, and the pressure of neighbors rearrange the colony from hour to hour. Yet among all this exchange there are recognitions. Mothers returning from the valleys enter a wall of sound and odor and find their own young. Mates groom the mineral dust from one another’s faces. Injured animals are not tended in any organized way, but familiar bodies tolerate their weakness, allowing them to remain inside the warm press when a stranger might be bitten loose.

The largest bats are deliberate creatures with blunt muzzles and heavy thumb-claws. They know the Cleft not as an open volume but as a web of remembered approaches: three wingbeats from a basalt rib to a hanging crust, a drop and turn into a gallery, a crawl sideways through pillars that will not admit spread wings. Old individuals carry maps made of echoes and scars. Missing fragments of ear, healed tears in the membrane, and pale scratches along the muzzle record collisions, fights, snakes, and narrow escapes from owls.

These bats sometimes descend to shelves where carcasses collect. There they worry stiff quills free from mountain rodents, testing them with tongue and claw. A chosen quill is held awkwardly in the wing’s small hook and inserted into grub-holes. The act is patient, almost dainty: probe, withdraw, lick, turn, probe again. Once the larva is hooked or driven within reach, the quill is forgotten. It falls among splinters of limestone and beetle cases, indistinguishable from debris. The knowledge persists only in living hands.

The cliff-swifts keep nearer the upper fractures, though “nearer” may still mean far below daylight. They cling vertically to stone, narrow bodies pressed upright, wing tips crossing behind them. Their feet are weak for walking and excellent for holding. A swift that lands on a broad shelf looks misplaced and anxious; against a rib of basalt it becomes exact.

Each pair chooses a natural cup no deeper than a thumb joint. Saliva, down, and the faintest rim of windblown fiber fasten a single pale egg to the rock. There is no nest beyond this adhesion. Hatchlings begin life above a drop they cannot comprehend, gripping the mineral hollow while adults arrive out of darkness at speed. Their mouths open wide and bright in the gloom, making them briefly more visible than their parents.

Swifts are less tolerant of touch than bats. Their colonies are close without being soft. Beaks jab. Wings slap. Each bird maintains a small, vehement circumference around its egg or chick. But when danger enters, their separateness becomes coordinated. Alarm passes from rib to rib faster than flight: one sharpened cry, then another, until the upper Cleft releases them in a torrent.

Beneath the birds sleep the great moths. By daylight measures they ought to be conspicuous, some broad enough to cover a human palm, but their wings repeat the stone’s stains: ash gray, lichen black, faded rust, and the cream of mineral salts. They settle head-down in overlapping rows. Condensation beads on the fine scales of their backs. Occasionally one wakes and shudders the water off, revealing eyes that return almost no light.

Their larvae live elsewhere—some in the pale vine, some in moss, some perhaps outside among summit plants—and the adults enter the Cleft for shelter, sweetness, mates, or reasons embedded in instincts older than the present shape of the mountain. Bats eat many of them. Spiders take others. Still they come, and in certain weeks their shed scales coat the shelves like smoke made solid.

The smallest winged inhabitants form no stable shapes at all. Flies gather above the cups of water, tightening and loosening into clouds. Their larvae occupy bacterial films, wet guano, and the thin margins where a pool is becoming mud. Midges breed in water that may exist for only a few days. Their lives are timed not to seasons visible outside but to drips, seep-strength, and the cooling of particular stones.

On the ledges below, beetles make roads through dung. Some are round and glossy, varnished-looking things that shove crumbs larger than their heads. Others are long, soft-bodied, and nearly colorless. They consume waste, fungus, eggs, molted skin, dead flies, dead beetles, and one another. Pale mites ride the bats, inhabit the guano, graze mold from feathers, and mass along warm cracks in numbers so great that the rock itself seems to crawl. Nothing edible remains merely discarded. A fallen swift becomes first a carcass, then a congregation, then a dark articulation in the ledge, then heat and odor returning to the air.

The salamanders belong to the thin water.

They lie lengthwise in seep channels with their translucent bellies against warm stone. Blood shadows their bodies from within. Their external gills stand out in dark red sprays, moving gently when the water appears motionless. The largest are no longer than a hand, and many are much smaller: delicate heads, threadlike toes, tails flattened for swimming in channels scarcely deep enough to cover them.

Their eyes are reduced but not absent. They distinguish the dimming of a fracture, the sudden passage of a wing-shadow, perhaps the pale bloom that precedes a heavier drip. More important are tremors. A fly larva writhing upstream, a cricket losing its footing, a crab scraping mineral film—each sends information through the film of water. The salamander waits with its jaw slightly parted. Its stillness is not sleep but a form of listening.

When a seep weakens, some retreat into wet hairline cracks. Others remain in shrinking cups and become slick with protective mucus, enduring against stone that is only damp. Generations may spend their entire lives along a few meters of wall, migrating vertically by the width of a finger as moisture shifts.

The blind crickets live behind that wall.

They are all antennae and bent limbs, pale enough to look freshly formed. Packed into fissures, they face outward, each sensing the movements of those around it. Louder dripping draws them forth. They drink from beads before the beads can run away. They browse fungus, guano, moth scales, and softened plant fragments. They also eat the weak of their own kind. Their long hind legs, extravagant in the tight galleries, are useful less for open leaps than for bracing across gaps and springing free when touched.

A cricket entering exposed stone risks many mouths. Salamanders take the small ones. Spiders take the large. Bats sometimes snap them from ledges while crawling between roosts. Yet their hidden population is immense, extending into cracks inaccessible to every hunter larger than a mite.

The spiders make dense things in narrow places. Their webs are not the broad, geometric wheels of open air but white-gray plugs, hammocks, funnels, and short curtains stretched across routes that insects must use. Moisture gathers on the threads. Mineral dust thickens them. Some old webs become permanent-looking felt, even after the spider has died and beetles have eaten what remained of it.

The spiders themselves are heavy and low, with strong front legs adapted to the abrupt violence of close quarters. A moth strikes a web and has no room to wheel away. A cricket touches three lines at once. The spider rushes only a short distance, but in the Cleft a short distance is enough.

The snakes are quieter still.

They are narrow-bodied, dark-backed, and faintly iridescent where condensation wets the scales. They spend hours inserted into crevices with only the head exposed, or lie along mineral shelves so exactly matched to the stone that bats crawl over them. Their ribs compress to enter spaces that appear too small to hold a vertebrate. There they wait beside the colony’s edges, where pups wander, sick adults isolate themselves, and returning bats land badly from exhaustion.

A strike is brief and often hidden by the general commotion. The snake seizes a bat by the muzzle, shoulder, or folded wing and braces itself against rock while the colony erupts around it. It cannot always swallow what it catches. A large bat may tear loose, leaving membrane behind; a snake may be battered from the ledge by dozens of panicked bodies. Successful hunters withdraw into cracks to feed, their outlines made blunt by the shape inside them.

Predation here is less a sequence of chases than a tax levied on crowding. Anything weakened is touched sooner or later by a waiting mouth.

The cliff-owl is not a permanent resident, though its presence has weight beyond its numbers. It comes alone through the summit seam, dark and compact, with wings that can shorten themselves in the air. Its feathers are soft enough that the Cleft hears not the owl but the reactions ahead of it. Colonies alter before it appears: calls rise in pitch, then stop; roosting curtains tighten; insects rain from disturbed ledges.

The owl drops through passages by memory or rapid judgment, banking where there seems no room to bank. Sometimes it plucks a swift cleanly from stone. Sometimes it takes a bat in midflight and strikes a wall with enough force to shed dust. It may perch afterward on a basalt blade, prey clenched beneath it, while the nearest chambers hold themselves in a silence not found anywhere else in the hollow.

At the bottom, the pale crabs seem at first like loose pieces of the basin moving.

Their bodies are flattened for cracks, their legs disproportionately long and jointed, their shells the color of old wax. They have eyes, but the stalks are shortened and the eyes themselves clouded. Their claws are unequal. One is fine enough to pick growth from a wet groove; the other can break a snail or crush a beetle softened by damp.

They graze continuously. The polished walls owe something to them: countless small scrapings over countless generations. They climb wherever a film of water permits adhesion, even ascending nearly vertical stone to reach fresh bacterial bloom after condensation. When the basin is dry, they spread over its gravel in a restless carpet. When floodwater arrives, they retreat upward together until every hold is occupied. They stack without ceremony, shell upon shell, the lower animals enduring the weight and the upper ones probing for higher purchase.

Some crabs carry stones.

Not every flat fragment will do. A crab tests one, turns it, abandons it, tests another. With a suitable piece gripped against the body, it approaches a snail and strikes or presses until the shell fails. A useful stone may be dragged between pools, hidden beneath the crab when rivals come near, and recovered after feeding. Yet no cache develops. Flood, jostling, or an unlucky crack eventually takes it. Another stone is chosen. The behavior creates tradition without architecture.

The fish appear only when the mountain briefly reveals them.

After heavy rains, as the bottom basin fills, small blind fish emerge with the rising water or are left behind as it drains. They are colorless except for the faint pink of blood and organs. Their heads are broad, their fins delicate, their eyes sealed beneath skin. They move close to the gravel, touching it with barbels, tasting currents that lead toward outlets too narrow for any observer to follow.

For a few days they inhabit the same visible world as the crabs. They feed on tiny crustaceans, larvae, organic fragments washed down from above, perhaps even the eggs of the animals climbing out of the flood. As the water falls, they gather in the deepest remnant pools. Then one morning they are absent. Whether they retreat through wet gravel, are drawn downward by the last drainage, or perish and are consumed cannot always be known.

Their true population lives below: in warm fractures without weather, in reservoirs pressed between ancient strata, in water whose ceiling may never have reflected open sky. The fish stranded in the Cleft are not colonists but messages from that lower country.

No single species rules this place. The bats command the air only until the owl enters it. The crabs cover the floor only until water rises. The snakes possess their ambush cracks but cannot cross the exposed colonies without danger. Even beetles and mites depend upon arrivals from valleys they will never see.

What lives in the Warm Cleft is therefore more than its inventory of bodies. It is a traffic of substances and senses.

Lowland insects become bat flesh, then milk, guano, beetle, fungus, and salamander. Pollen caught in fur falls into a pool where a larva eats it. A swift carries valley blood in its muscles to feed a snake that may never leave the summit. Mineral water grows bacteria; crabs graze the bacteria; an owl takes a crab when flood drives it high enough. Moth scales become food for mites. Feathers become web anchors. Bones vanish beneath droppings and later emerge from hardened terraces polished smooth.

The inhabitants know no whole Cleft. Each possesses a different one.

For a bat, it is a resonant architecture measured in fractions of returning sound. For a swift, it is a set of launch points and clinging cups. For a salamander, it is connected wetness. For a cricket, it is vibration passing through hidden stone. For a crab, it is a map of edible films interrupted by lethal dryness. For the fish below, the entire roaring hollow may be nothing more than a periodic brightness in the water overhead.

Together they make something that resembles an organ without becoming a single organism. The winged colonies are its pulse, leaving at evening and returning before dawn. The hidden aquifer is its buried circulation. Guano and mineral crust are its slow-growing tissue. Cracks open, seal, and open elsewhere like pores. Its memory is held not in marks or structures but in routes repeatedly flown, ledges repeatedly chosen, tools repeatedly rediscovered, and instincts adjusted to warmth that has persisted longer than any lineage remembers.

It is sleepless only from a distance. Within it, sleep occurs everywhere: bats wrapped in themselves, moths flattened under stone, crickets motionless in fissures, fish hovering in black water. But they do not sleep together. Some population is always leaving, feeding, hatching, hunting, molting, climbing, or listening.

Thus the Cleft’s apparent restlessness is made from innumerable private stillnesses, and its continuity from innumerable brief lives. No inhabitant understands the exchange that sustains them. None needs to. The bats return burdened with the valleys; the water rises burdened with the mountain’s interior; the warm stone receives them both.

Everything that enters is occupied. Everything that dies is entered. Nothing remains empty long enough to be called empty.
