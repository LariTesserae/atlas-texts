The inhabitants of the White Bell are creatures of intervals.

Almost none of them can endure abundance for long. A basin kept full would drown the sleepers in its sediment. A lasting stream would sweep away the moss and the transparent threads. A heap of food would sour before the rats could use it. Even unbroken brightness would be dangerous: on the fiercest afternoons, the lizards retreat beneath ledges and the red ceiling-growths press themselves so flat that they resemble stains in the limestone.

They live by appearing when one condition briefly meets another.

The pale gnats are the most visible expression of this. They hatch in water but spend their adult lives in the open upper chamber, poised in the diffuse radiance. Each maintains a small emptiness around itself. When another drifts too close, both alter course without touching, as though repelled by the same faint pressure. Their larvae graze the mineral-forming films in newly filled cups; their winged forms eat almost nothing. They exist chiefly to rise, find one another in the white air, and descend as eggs before their borrowed moisture is gone.

Sudden dimness disturbs them. A cloud crossing the plateau is softened by the stone roof, but they detect it. One gnat begins to tremble, then those nearest answer, until the dome acquires a high, glassy tone. The note is not loud enough to seem made by so many bodies. It sounds instead as if a wet finger has been drawn around the rim of the chamber.

The moss mites hear it through their feet.

They are dark only because their stomachs show through them. Cleaned of food, they are nearly colorless. They move around the rims of dependable cups in slow grazing fronts, consuming velvet moss, shed cells, snail slime, pollen sifted through the roof, and the pale residue of anything dead. Their populations expand after rain and diminish without migration. The missing mites have not gone elsewhere. They have been eaten, dried into specks, or absorbed into the green-black margin on which their offspring continue feeding.

Among them move the glass snails. Their shells are scarcely shells at all, only delicate whorls of carbonate laid over living tissue. In a chamber where dissolved stone is always arriving, armor is inexpensive; weight is not. The snails grow large enough to span a fingertip, but their shells remain thin enough to deform slightly when handled. A pulse passes visibly through the animal. Its dark mouth works beneath the clear head.

They follow condensation lines with extraordinary fidelity. Some trails may be older than any individual snail, renewed generation after generation along the same mineral veins. Yet no trail is a road. Once the wall dries, the slime contracts into invisible flakes and the snails seal themselves into cracks, leaving no durable sign that they ever traveled there.

The long-legged water insects hunt them when they are small. These insects have feet fringed with microscopic hairs, so they stand not upon water but upon the skin the water makes against the air. They step across the bowls without dimpling them. Each limb is placed, tested, and only then trusted with weight. When two meet, they lift their front legs and pass without contact. Their eggs are set singly in damp pits in the stone, never close enough for one hatching brood to consume another.

Below their feet, whenever a basin has filled, the buried populations wake.

The comma-creatures are not truly born at the arrival of water. They have lain folded in rust-colored sediment, sometimes for years, their bodies reduced to waxy hooks around a few protected organs. Rain loosens them. Within minutes they uncurl; within hours the basins cloud with their feeding. Their entire active existence may fit between two drips—the first that completes the pool and the last that fails to replace what evaporation takes.

They are extravagant only in speed. They shed skins, change shape, pair, divide, and deposit dark-shelled eggs along the basin floor. As the water warms and dwindles, the adults become sluggish. The final generation often dies around its own eggs, and the mites descend to dismantle it. By the time the bowl dries, its mud contains another sleeping multitude.

The three deep hollows belong to the flat fish.

They are the largest animals that never leave the water and perhaps the oldest lineage in the Bell. Broad-headed and almost without pigment, they lie against the bottoms like scraps of soaked parchment. Their fins have thickened tips with which they brace against stone. They hunt by sensing disturbances in the walls of the basin: the beat of a swimming comma-creature, a gnat larva rasping film, the careful touch of an insect foot overhead.

When water fails, a fish does not struggle in the exposed bowl. It seeks one of the memorized fissures beneath the rim, presses out much of the water held in its gut, and works itself inward. Its heartbeat slows. Blood is diverted toward the skin. Fine vessels flush faintly rose beneath the white body, allowing it to breathe from the dampness trapped in the crack.

Several fish may enter the same crevice, but they do not lie together. Each pushes until stone separates it from the next. On the surface of the plateau, drought scatters animals across a landscape. Here drought scatters them within the width of a hand.

The wall fern also keeps its distance. There is only one mature crown, though tiny unsuccessful crowns sometimes form higher in the seam and vanish. Its fronds are fleshy and grey-green, divided into a few blunt fingers. They hold water rather than shed it. When disturbed, they release the bitter smell that lies faintly throughout the chamber. Lizards dislike the taste; mites feeding on a fallen frond become unpalatable in turn.

The fern’s spores do not ride wind, because the Bell has almost none. Instead they adhere to snail shells and the bellies of rats. Most are carried to places where they cannot grow. A few reach wet cracks, germinate, and remain no larger than a stain. The solitary fern is therefore not solitary for lack of offspring. Its descendants are scattered all around it in arrested beginnings.

The transparent filaments share those cracks, though they seem less like plants than like exposed senses. Each consists of a clear tube surrounding a thread of silver granules. Touched by a snail’s mouth or the foot of an insect, the granules contract toward the anchoring end and draw the filament into a coil. Reopening takes hours. They receive light, trap mineral dust, and absorb dissolved remains, but no one function wholly explains them. In places they merge with one another beneath the stone. What look like hundreds of separate threads may be the extremities of a single, ancient organism spread through the wet seams of the chamber.

If so, it possesses no center. A dry season can kill one region without harming another. Fragments left in separate cracks continue independently. It is one creature only when water briefly joins it to itself.

The red rosettes of the dome are even harder to classify. Closed, they are hard beads no larger than lentils. In bright hours they unfold into crimson discs and turn their centers toward no particular point, since the radiance comes from everywhere. Their color is not merely pigment. It belongs to colonies of tiny symbiotic cells held under a translucent skin. The cells use light; the rosette uses what they release.

Mites cannot reach them, and snails rarely climb so high. Their chief grazers are the wingless creatures hanging at the apex of the Bell.

These are called batlike only because their resting membranes resemble folded wings. They do not fly. Each has a shallow, flexible body spread around a fine internal lattice, with a small gripping mouth at one end and several hooked limbs at the other. Between them runs a web of mucus cords fixed to points of limestone. The animals hang separately along it, never touching except when one passes another on a strand.

The web is not a communal construction in any deliberate sense. Each animal lays a line for itself. Other lines intersect it. Old strands dry, tighten, snap, and are replaced. Over time the congregation suspends itself within a structure made by no member and possessed by none.

They feed first on brightness. Cells embedded in their membranes turn the chamber’s radiance into a slow supply of sugars, enough to sustain stillness but not growth. For minerals they scrape the red rosettes from the ceiling. For water they descend.

A descending membrane-animal is among the few things in the White Bell that seems large while moving. It releases its hooks, flattens itself, and falls so slowly that falling ceases to look like an accident. Folds along its body alter the air passing around it. It slides sideways, turns, stalls beneath the luminous roof, and settles at last over a water cup.

There it becomes almost invisible. Its tissue spreads across the surface like a clear cloth. Water passes through its skin. Minute animals trapped beneath it are digested where they touch. When it has drunk, the creature thickens, gathers itself, and begins the wallward journey—sometimes upward toward the web, sometimes only to a shaded ledge, where it may remain for a day as a patch of apparent damp.

The white lizards wait for these descents, though they rarely catch one.

They are dense, warm-bodied animals, more birdlike in their inner workings than the lizards of the surface. The light alone does not warm them enough; microorganisms in deep chambers of the gut ferment moss, insects, fern spores, and whatever else can be swallowed, producing a steady internal heat. They bask not merely for warmth but to feed pale symbiotic scales along their backs. Those scales contain living cells related, perhaps, to the crimson cells in the rosettes.

Only the skin around the lizards’ eyes is blue. The color deepens when they are excited and fades almost to grey during sleep. Since the chamber gives few strong shadows, their vision is poor at boundaries and excellent at motion. They can detect a gnat shifting high in the dome but stumble over a motionless pebble at their feet. Before walking, they often sway their heads from side to side, making stationary objects seem to move against the background.

They do not defend territories. No patch remains productive enough to own. A lizard occupies the bright center until hunger sends it to the moss rims, then retreats beneath a stone to digest. When two encounter one another, they stand side by side rather than face to face. The blue markings pulse darker and paler. Usually one leaves. Bites are costly in a place where flesh repairs slowly.

Their eggs are laid singly in separate warm cracks. A mother may spend an entire bright period crossing the chamber between one egg and the next. Thus no failed seep, hungry rat, or predatory lizard can take a whole brood at once.

The long-eared rats are the Bell’s most restless inhabitants and the least suited, in appearance, to its stillness. Their hind legs are long, their ribs narrow, their fur the color of old ivory. The ears are nearly naked and furrowed with vessels. Pressed to the floor, they detect drips landing in remote basins. Turned toward another rat, they read breaths, whisker movements, and the soft impacts of pebble against stone.

They have voices, but use them rarely. Sound carries too well beneath the dome. A squeal would announce fear everywhere at once and continue after its cause had passed. Instead they greet by crossing whiskers. Warning is given with one tap. Two taps may mean water, food, obstruction, or simply attention; meaning depends on which stone is struck, how long the pause lasts, and whether the listening rat can smell what the tapping rat has found.

Their cleverness is practical but refuses permanence.

A rat selecting a tool will handle several flakes before choosing one whose edge fits a snail crack. It may carry that flake halfway around the chamber, brace it with both forepaws, and work with evident patience. When the snail comes free, the rat eats and releases the stone. It does not hide the useful edge or return it to a customary place. Later it may pass the same flake without recognition.

Seed husks and hard pods sometimes fall through roof fissures. The rats bite away one end, drink from the shell to remove dust, and use it as a vessel. Water is carried to a drying fish crack, to a juvenile rat, or simply to a shaded place where the bearer prefers to drink. Then the pod is abandoned. Another rat may use it, alter it, or chew it to pieces.

This lack of keeping is not a failure of memory. The rats remember the sequence in which basins fill after different kinds of rain. They remember where a lizard sleeps and which wall vein condenses first during a cold night. What they do not seem to recognize is possession. A useful thing belongs to the present difficulty. When the difficulty ends, so does the relationship.

They make no fixed nests. A sleeping rat gathers loose moss, shed fern fiber, and perhaps a membrane-animal’s fallen strand into a shallow bed. On waking, it leaves the bedding where it is. Mites consume the organic pieces; condensation fixes the remainder to the floor; another rat may sleep there once before the arrangement disappears.

Young rats learn through interruption. An adult prying at a shell will tolerate a juvenile’s paws on the tool, but does not demonstrate deliberately. If the flake slips away, the young animal retrieves it. If it chooses the wrong edge, the adult waits. Their traditions persist not as objects or marked places but as gestures renewed in each generation: test the shard with the whiskers; turn the pod before lifting; strike the stone only once unless the first tap goes unanswered.

Nothing in the White Bell consistently hunts the rats. Their dead are taken by smaller mouths.

A body first attracts mites and snails, then clear worms that emerge from joints and soft openings. The worms are almost invisible except when filled with pale tissue. Mineral films spread beneath them, feeding upon what dissolves. Within days the fur loosens. Within weeks the skeleton separates. The bones do not remain white for long; they take on the same carbonate coating as the floor until bone and stone cannot be distinguished.

For a while there is an outline: a finer powder, a concentration of salts, the curve where a rib interrupted a trickle. Then rain enters. The outline divides among several basins. Fern tissue absorbs a portion. A rosette reddens. A fish wakes in water carrying the thinnest remnant of a rat that died before it hatched.

There may be no single creature that rules the White Bell. There may not even be a true boundary between its inhabitants. The rosette lives with cells inside it. The lizard’s warmth comes from its gut colony. The hanging membranes feed on light through borrowed bodies. The clear filaments are many when dry and one when wet. The dormant basin-creatures alternate between animal and sediment. Minerals pass through shells, bones, eggs, floor, and roof.

What lives there is less a community than a continual act of distribution.

No great swarm forms. No growth closes the open floor. No enduring nest establishes a center. Even the highest congregation leaves spaces between each suspended body. Life is everywhere in the Bell, but it avoids becoming a mass. It persists by remaining divisible—into eggs, spores, droplets, separate sleeping fish, solitary red beads, tools without owners, gestures without monuments.

Seen during the chamber’s brightest hour, the inhabitants can appear incidental: gnats like dust, lizards like chips of chalk, membrane-creatures like scraps of wet glass. Yet the emptiness around them is not lifeless space. It is what permits them to continue. Each creature requires room for the next rain to spread, the next light to arrive without obstruction, the next brief gathering to come apart.

The White Bell contains no people in the ordinary sense. But it has a way of life resembling a custom observed by every being within it:

Take what reaches you. Change it just enough to live. Let it go before it becomes yours.
