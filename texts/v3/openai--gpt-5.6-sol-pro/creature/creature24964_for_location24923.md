At first they are mistaken for the pale swimmers.

This is partly because no one in the cistern presents a complete outline for long. The water cuts every body into wavering sections, and the brightness gives no reliable measure of scale. A narrow head seen beyond a mineral tower may belong to something finger-long or to something whose tail remains folded in a fissure several paces away. Four fins spread at the crossing of two currents. A colorless back vibrates without advancing. Then one fin closes around a stone with the unmistakable deliberation of a hand.

The cistern’s people are long-bodied and soft-jointed. They have no single posture that could be called upright. In open water they extend like ribbons, scarcely thicker through the chest than through the hips; in cracks they flatten until the paired bones of their bodies lie almost side by side. When braced against stone, however, they gather themselves into a compact shape: the lower body looped around a peg or ridge, the upper body raised, four slender limbs working independently.

Those limbs account for the reports of four-finned animals. Each bears a membrane along its outer edge, useful for trimming position in a current, and a hand at its tip. The hands have three long fingers and a short opposing hook. At rest the fingers close together so perfectly that the whole appendage resembles a pointed fin. When opened, they are pale blue within and crossed by dark pressure-sensitive lines.

Their skin is nearly transparent in the young. Muscle shows through it as faint smoke, the spine as a chain of clear knots. Adults become less visible rather than more colorful. Mineral grains lodge in the outer layers of their skin, taking on the tone of whatever stone they inhabit. An old one lying against basalt can be detected only when its throat moves. One that works among fresh silica gleams as though made from wet glass.

They have no eyes in the ordinary place. Some possess two shallow dimples above the mouth, but these are flow-sensing pits, not organs of sight. Light is perceived over the whole surface of the body. A passing shadow tightens the skin along one side. Noon makes them blanch. They know the summit’s brightness as warmthless pressure and can distinguish the shape of a nearby object by the darkness it sends across them.

Most of their knowing comes through sound.

Behind the jaw lies a fan of fine bones embedded in fluid. Each bone answers to a different part of the cistern’s roar. They hear not merely the noise of a jet but the fissure that produces it, the thickness of stone behind it, the grains carried inside it and the likelihood that it will change course. A wall newly forming has one voice; a wall about to split has another. They can tell, through several body-lengths of rock, when a shell-creature lets go.

The hammering that overwhelms other animals leaves them calm. Silence, if they could experience it, would be disabling.

Their own speech is made of taps, throat pulses and brief interruptions of current. Much of it cannot be separated from the chamber’s ordinary ringing. One presses a wrist to a ledge and sends a sequence through the stone. Another, on the far side of the cistern, replies by spreading its body across a resonant rib. A whole group in conversation may appear motionless while the mineral tower between them hums.

Names are chords rather than sounds. Because the chamber continually alters, a name cannot depend upon a fixed echo. It must remain recognizable when returned by a new wall or divided by a newly grown pillar. The young have simple, hard pulses. Older names are long arrangements of overtones, and some can be spoken only at certain crossings of the current.

They do not breathe water as fish do. Along the flanks are rows of soft cavities lined with dark tissue. Water is forced through them by the movement of the cistern itself. In a strong jet they need make no effort; in still seams their bodies weaken. This is why they rest where currents oppose one another. What looks like a pale creature suspended idly for hours may be sleeping, listening or allowing a wound to clear.

Still water is dangerous to them. A sealed vessel is a symbol of death.

They live chiefly inside the walls.

The visible chamber is their road, workshop and field, not their shelter. Their sleeping places are narrow mineral-lined pockets reached through seams that appear too small to admit a hand. They enter headfirst, dislocating the girdles of their limbs and emptying water from sacs within the body until they can be drawn through by the pressure. Several may occupy one pocket, braided together around a warm seep. The innermost sleeper listens to the mountain. The outermost keeps a hand in the current.

Their dwellings are not excavated. They are persuaded into existence.

A family wedges fibers into a fertile crack, scratches a pattern into the soft stone and places shell fragments where the desired walls should begin. For months or years, the mountain deposits a chamber around this framework. If the currents have been correctly read, the result is a smooth hollow with several narrow mouths. If they have been misread, it may become a solid block containing only the fossil impression of the intended room.

Such failures are not considered empty. The shape of the lost chamber remains in the stone, and descendants often tap greetings to it as they pass.

Their young begin in the translucent bladders mistaken for plants.

Not every bladder belongs to them; most are genuinely simple growths feeding on mineral and light. But among certain clusters are larger sacs fixed beneath overhangs, each attended by an adult whose body may be hidden deep in the supporting crack. For a season the young inside has neither limbs nor mouth. It feeds from the black-green filaments woven through the sac and learns the rhythms of the cistern as vibration.

At noon the sacs collapse with the others. This compression forms the young animal’s joints. Many collapses are needed before the limbs divide properly.

When ready, the juvenile tears free during a pressure surge and is carried into the central water. It must find, without guidance, the small equilibrium where colliding jets hold it. Those that succeed remain there, trembling and clear, while their flank cavities open. Those that do not are taken into the walls. The adults do not pursue them. No swimmer can rescue another from the central current without losing the same contest.

For this reason their tenderness appears, to an outsider, severe. They handle eggs gently, preserve broken tools and spend decades shaping a nursery ledge, yet they watch newly opened young vanish without movement. Grief is expressed later, by altering the current that took them. A splinter, a hooked stick or a single scored shell may be placed upstream. Years afterward the addition becomes a ridge, and the ridge changes the chamber’s note. Thus a lost child may be given a voice far larger than its body ever was.

The wooden ladder is theirs, though they do not understand it as a ladder.

It is a row of holds marking a seam that periodically opens at noon. When the chamber overturns, several people fasten themselves there in sequence. Their lower bodies stream sideways while their hands test the crack. The highest position is not one of rank but danger: that listener is nearest the crust and receives the first warning of a fracture.

The pegs are replaced one at a time. Their wood comes from fibrous growths in lower, warmer channels. No individual can travel all the way to those growths. The material passes from group to group through submerged passages, sometimes over generations. A particular length of wood may be older than anyone who touches it. Its route is remembered as a sequence of echoes, not places.

The seven pierced stones form both instrument and census. Each stone represents a current that can support a sleeping chamber. As crystal narrows a hole and raises its whistle, the corresponding dwelling approaches closure. When a tone disappears, the stone is removed, whether or not the inhabitants of that pocket have emerged. A new pierced stone is added only when a new stable seam begins to sound.

There are not always seven.

The bowls are used for making tools, but also for thought. Grains placed in different crossings sort themselves by weight, hardness and response to tremor. From their revolutions the cistern folk judge when a ledge will grow, when the summit will ring and where loose matter will travel during the next upheaval. Copper is especially valued because it answers the water differently from stone. The hook near the bowls is less a hook than a question left in the current. Its slow turning measures a pressure too slight for the body to feel.

They make almost nothing in a finished state. To finish an object entirely by hand would be to deprive it of the mountain’s contribution. A frame is built, seeded and surrendered. Years later another person—often one with no relation to the maker—finds what the water has made of it. Use is discovered afterward.

Consequently, they have no clean distinction between artifact, organism and mineral formation. A shell-creature that settles on a basket may become its hinge. A dead filament trapped along a blade may become the blade’s strongest edge. A tool that grows shut around its user’s hand may be left in place as a handle for others.

They do not own objects. They claim predictions. To say *this vessel is mine* would make little sense. One says, instead, *I knew where this vessel would occur.*

The most honored among them are those who can foresee not merely growth but repurpose: where a failed grate will become a nursery, where a buried scraper will emerge as part of a resonant wall, where the impression of a body will return in another chamber. Their records are arrangements of bone wedges and scored mineral ribs. Read by touch and sound, they describe transformations rather than events.

Death is understood in the same way.

When one of them can no longer hold a current, the others carry the body to the rim of the central hollow. They do not release it immediately. Each mourner taps the dead one’s name against its spine, so the chamber hears the complete chord. Then the body is let go.

It is seized at once.

Sometimes an arm remains visible for several breaths, stretched impossibly thin toward a crack. Sometimes the whole body folds and is gone without touching any opening large enough to receive it. Years later, a profile may appear in new mineral on a distant wall: three fingers, a line of vertebrae, the fan of listening bones behind a jaw. The cistern folk do not say this is the same person. Neither do they say it is not.

They place tools in niches near such reliefs, especially broken ones. The dead are thought to be skilled at finding new uses.

The parallel scrapes on certain ledges do not belong to them.

When those marks are fresh, the cistern people leave the visible chamber. Pegs stand empty; nursery bladders are cut loose and hidden in cracks; even the shell-creatures seem to withdraw. The slow pulse beneath the floor then becomes stronger. Grains rise upstream in dense streams, and the mineral towers bend before they break.

What comes up has never been seen whole.

One account might make it a vast animal enclosed in the young mountain, with the cistern only one cavity of its body. Another suggests a colony of soft, interlinked things inhabiting the deepest porous stone. The folk themselves do not distinguish these possibilities. They call it by a chord produced when every resonant surface is struck at once.

At long intervals, part of it enters the chamber: a ridged surface passing beneath the floor’s clear mineral skin, a row of gripping structures drawn along a shelf, a pale fold that closes over a tower and leaves the space empty when it withdraws. It does not hunt the cistern folk. It may not perceive them individually. Yet after its passage, old sealed dwellings are often opened and new channels lead toward the summit.

Some believe it is what raises the mountain.

Others believe the mountain is growing around it to keep it contained.

At noon the people fasten themselves wherever they can. Adults take the outer holds. The newly opened young are pushed into quiet seams. Tools meant to be transformed are placed in the strongest crossings, and tools meant to survive are tucked behind old stone. Then the light intensifies, the shadows turn upward and the chamber exchanges its shapes.

During that upheaval, the cistern folk sing.

The song cannot be heard as a separate thing. It inhabits the hammering, borrows the shriek of fissures and uses breaking towers as percussion. Each singer holds one portion of the pattern, adjusting it as walls vanish and new resonances appear. No performance can be repeated because the chamber that played it no longer exists when the hour is over.

Afterward they emerge slowly. They touch the new steps and openings. They count the whistles in the cairn. They retrieve whoever remains on the ladder. They listen for names in the fresh stone.

Then they begin setting frames into the altered currents—not to restore what was lost, but to give the water something it has not made before.
