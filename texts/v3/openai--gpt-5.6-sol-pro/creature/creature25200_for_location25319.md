What lives there is not one people but a multitude held together by thirst.

Nearest the ceiling are the black rivers: bats in numbers too great to count, hanging from the vault in layered colonies. They cling wherever the stone offers a seam, packed so closely that the rock seems to have grown fur. Most are narrow-bodied, with long wings suited to traveling the basin rather than turning sharply inside chambers. Their ears are broad and creased; their eyes, where they retain them, are small and filmed against dust. They sleep through the faint warmth of the day, lowering their pulse until they are scarcely warmer than the stone.

They do not drink. They take moisture from beetles, fruit pulp, blood, and one another’s milk. Their kidneys leave behind hard white grains instead of liquid waste. Mothers bear few young and carry them for a long time, because the basin cannot afford swarms that are not already able to survive. Orphaned pups crawl by scent into neighboring clusters and are sometimes fed by females that did not bear them. In the deepest colonies, kinship has become less important than preserving the warm, breathing mass.

When they wake, the ceiling appears to come loose.

The first bats drop silently, falling far enough to open their wings. Those beneath follow because the bodies above them have moved; those farther down follow the changing pressure of air. Within moments, migration becomes weather. They stream toward the groves and salt flats, some hunting insects above the trees, others passing into chimneys that lead to higher caverns. Their calls strike the city and return transformed by corridors, pillars, empty jars, and half-buried rooms. Each animal knows the basin not as a view but as an immense architecture of echoes.

Among them fly the cave swifts. They are paler than surface birds, soot-gray beneath and almost colorless around the face. Their feathers have stiff, fine edges that shed dust. Their claws are hooked for clinging to vertical stone, and their nests are small cups cemented into light-shaft walls with saliva, husks, hair, and powdered clay. The swifts live closer to incoming light than the bats do. They wheel around the shafts in screaming congregations, catching insects drawn upward by brightness they will never reach.

Some birds do reach the surface openings. Many do not return. The colonies below respond without mourning that can be recognized: another pair occupies the vacant nest, another chick opens its mouth into the gray air.

The trees shelter a quieter population.

Their crawling branches are crowded with pale crickets whose shells resemble chips of salt. They feed on lichen, leaf edges, bat droppings, old wood, and the shed skins of their own kind. Males call by striking hardened wing cases together. In open country the sound would be small. Here every wall repeats it. Colonies in neighboring ruins answer at slightly different intervals until whole districts pulse in overlapping metallic waves.

There are beetles in the bark, beetles beneath stones, beetles inside dung, beetles that live only in the dry remains of nests. Some are glossy black, conserving warmth beneath sealed wing cases. Others are white and soft-bodied, spending their entire lives in timber that died before their grandparents hatched. A fallen branch can support dozens of generations. They tunnel without destroying its outer form, reducing its heart to meal and husks. When at last the branch collapses under a grazer’s hoof, it breaks not like wood but like pottery filled with flour.

Dung beetles are among the basin’s swiftest creatures. They arrive almost before an animal has finished passing waste. They divide it, bury it, seal it under courtyards, and lay their eggs within. What appears to be scavenging is also agriculture: their chambers concentrate moisture and feed pale fungal threads that could not survive exposed to the draft. Several species of cricket and mite depend upon these buried gardens. So do the roots that later split the chambers open.

In cracks too small for paws live translucent things: mites, springtails, silverfish, larval forms without common names. They gather wherever breath has briefly darkened a surface. A sleeping herd can create, beneath its bodies, a humid country no thicker than a fingernail. By the time the animals rise, that country has been inhabited, grazed, hunted through, and abandoned.

The rodents live in the walls.

They are not blind, though their eyes are modest and black. Their whiskers are nearly the width of their bodies, allowing them to read doorways and passages at speed. Their fur lies in close scales rather than loose fluff, keeping dust from reaching the skin. They hoard seeds in the old granaries, not knowing those chambers once had the same purpose. Generations have packed cracks with husks and dung until some storehouses contain more rodent work than masonry.

Their caches are communal only by accident. One family seals a hole; another enters from behind. Seeds are stolen, lost, buried, rediscovered, or forgotten long enough to germinate. A courtyard grove may begin with a single overlooked kernel beneath a collapsed jar.

Warnings travel through the walls as percussion. A rodent stamps in one chamber; another answers three houses away. The sound runs faster than scent and farther than sight. Predators hear it too, but cannot always tell whether the drumming means danger has been seen or merely remembered. At times an entire quarter trembles with alarms while the hunter responsible has already moved elsewhere.

The lizards hunt those walls and the warm western cliffs. They are thin, long-toed, and dust-colored, with loose folds of skin along their flanks. At rest they flatten themselves against masonry and become another patch of mineral bloom. Most lay only one or two eggs at a time, burying them near roots where the temperature changes least. The young hatch fully equipped for drought, carrying within their yolk the greatest drink they may ever receive.

Snakes follow them into cracks.

The snakes are slow even when striking. Speed wastes too much. They wait along warmed ledges, at nest mouths, or beside the narrow trails between groves. Some can remain motionless through many cycles of the deep draft. Dust settles over their coils until only the nostrils and the clear surface of an eye remain exposed. A passing lizard may step onto what it takes for stone.

Their venom is not extravagant. It is made sparingly and used to prevent struggle. A bitten animal runs only a short distance before its limbs fail. The snake follows by scent and vibration, then swallows every recoverable part: meat, organs, fur, small bones, and the moisture held in all of them.

On the terraces move the grazers.

They are narrow animals, high-shouldered and light-boned, with long ears that turn independently toward the basin’s ceaseless noise. Their coats vary from the color of old clay to almost white. Dust adheres to the outer hairs, so individuals gradually take on the shade of the district where they feed. Only around the eyes and nostrils does their true darker coloring remain visible.

Their mouths are small and hard, made for taking single leaves without damaging the branch that bears them. They browse thorn tips, waxy foliage, seed cases, lichen crusts, and the dry sheaths of grass. When leaves fold against them, they do not force the plant open. Herds move on. Those that stripped a grove bare would leave no descendants.

Their migrations look aimless but are exact. Each herd follows a circuit of courtyards, roof groves, fern gullies, and lichen walls. The full route may take years. Calves learn it by walking behind their mothers. Old females remember which collapsed rooms contain edible roots, which salt polygons conceal sinkholes, and which streets become dangerous when the northern draft begins. A herd without an old animal may survive for a season and then vanish at the wrong end of the basin.

They rest in abandoned halls where thick walls slow the loss of body heat. During sleep they gather flank to flank, and small creatures enter the warmth beneath them. Beetles clean their hooves. Pale birds pick mites from their ears. Rodents sometimes clip loose hair for nests. Nothing about the gathering is tame, but each species has learned the limits of the others’ tolerance.

The grazers’ young are born rarely. They stand quickly and make little sound. A loud newborn draws cats.

The cats are small, long-backed, and almost impossibly patient. They live among rooflines and colonnades, using the city above ground as the rodents use it within. Their paws are broad and furred beneath, muffling movement on grit. Their tails are heavy with stored fat. They drink blood before eating flesh and leave little behind except pieces too hard to crack.

They do not defend large territories; there is not enough food for constant fighting. Instead, their ranges overlap in time. One cat hunts a grove while another sleeps beneath it. Scent marks persist for months in the dry air, creating a record of passage more useful than a boundary. Individuals can tell who crossed a threshold, how long ago, whether they were fed, whether they carried young.

During ceiling flights the cats crouch low. Bats strike them by accident, wings slapping fur, and the air becomes too confused to hunt. Afterward, stunned animals sometimes lie in the streets. The cats descend then, but so do snakes, beetles, rodents, and the blind scavengers from below.

Those scavengers are among the basin’s strangest inhabitants.

They live in buried cellars and sealed rooms reached through holes opened by roots. Hairless and heavy-snouted, they move on short limbs with wide digging claws. Skin covers their eyes. Their faces are ringed with sensitive bristles that detect disturbed dust and the minute warmth of recent death. They can smell a cracked bone through a wall.

They eat anything that once lived. Their jaws open wide, and the rear teeth are blunt enough to crush joints. Their stomachs digest leather, feathers, dry tendon, insect shell, and wood softened by fungus. When food is plentiful they bury portions in mineral dust, where it may remain for years. During scarcity they recover these stores by scent, often finding older stores made by animals long dead.

They are not fearless. A scavenger encountering another at a carcass first drums the ground with its foreclaws. Size and hunger are negotiated through vibration. Actual battles are uncommon. An injury that opens the body wastes water, and in the basin waste is a kind of suicide.

Even the predators are prey in the end.

Nothing remains sovereign for long. A dead cat feeds scavengers; its bones feed rodents; its fur lines swift nests; its dung nourishes beetle gardens; minerals from all of these enter a root and become a leaf. The leaf is bitten by a grazer. The grazer is bitten by a snake. The snake dies unnoticed in a ventilation passage, where crickets consume it from within while its skin keeps the shape of a sleeping coil.

There may also be inhabitants that never move enough to be recognized as animals.

In the lowest salt country, thin dark films lie beneath translucent crusts. They wake only when a body shades them or when a rare breath of humid air crosses the plain. For a few hours they soften, feed, divide, and seal themselves again. Colonies may be older than the ruined districts around them. A hoofprint can destroy centuries of growth; it can also carry living grains to a new depression.

Within the trees, fungi survive as threads deep in wood and root tissue. They do not make the soft fruiting bodies of wet places. Their structures emerge as hard fans, dry cups, or black mineral-like knobs that release spores only into still air. Some trees cannot draw enough from the rock without them. Some beetles cannot digest the trees without carrying them. Root, fungus, and insect have been exchanging the same scant nourishment for so long that none is wholly understandable alone.

No inhabitant comprehends the city, yet nearly all are shaped by it.

The swifts know its shafts. The bats know its invisible dimensions. Rodents know the thickness of its walls. Grazers know its avenues as inherited distance. Cats know the roof plan. Scavengers know what lies beneath the floors. Trees know where foundations crack and buried clay retains the faintest trace of vapor. Even the draft is interpreted through living bodies: by folded leaves, turned ears, banked wings, sealed burrows, and animals lowering themselves into stillness until it passes.

If anything has replaced the builders, it is not a species but this accumulated knowledge without thought. No single creature could survive the whole basin alone. Together they occupy it completely: ceiling, air, bark, wall, street, cellar, salt crust, bone.

They are miserly with movement, birth, blood, shade, and breath. Yet when the vault erupts with wings, when the swifts scream in the gray shafts and the crickets drive their iron rhythm through the ruins, they seem anything but diminished.

They seem excessive—an enormous life made from almost nothing, spending sound because sound is the one resource the basin cannot exhaust.
