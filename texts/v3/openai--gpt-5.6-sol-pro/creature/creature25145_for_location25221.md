At first, it seems that only the small things live there.

The visible ground is inhabited by bead-drinkers: flat, soft-bodied animals no broader than a leaf, with six gripping limbs arranged around a translucent belly. They spend their lives upside down. A bead-drinker settles beneath a folded fan before the drizzle, fastening itself along the leaf’s veins. As moisture condenses, grooves in its back guide the droplets toward a fringed mouth. It does not bite or wound its host. When the leaf closes against frost, the animal closes with it, becoming part of the fold.

Some remain attached for years. Their bodies slowly take on the plant’s hidden colors, first green, then violet, then a red so dark it appears black. Older individuals carry gardens of spores along their flanks. Plant and grazer exchange heat, salts, and reproductive dust until it becomes difficult to say which is feeding the other.

Beneath them are the mat-runners.

They are compact, six-legged grazers with blunt heads and velvety hides. Their forelimbs part the moss while the rear limbs press it closed again, so that they travel without making open tunnels. From above, their passing looks like a crease moving through heavy cloth. They crop only the innermost layers of the vegetation, where the tissues are soft and warm, and leave the light-facing surfaces untouched.

A mat-runner seldom sees open air. Its eyes can distinguish only pressure and electrical brightness. Blue root pulses are dawn to it; the closing of a million leaf-fans is night. Families travel nose to tail along thermal boundaries, following narrow regions in which the ground is neither frozen nor dangerously hot. When a boundary divides, the family divides with it. They may not meet again, though they continue to recognize one another through changes in the current beneath their feet.

Their predators are scarcely larger. Long, jointed things lie pressed into the underside of the moss, matching its fibers so exactly that mat-runners pass beneath their mouths. They do not pursue. They alter the local flow of heat by opening cold bladders along their abdomens. The prey, believing frost has entered the tunnel, turns toward warmth and walks directly between their jaws.

Deeper still live the hearth-tenders.

These pale burrowers are warm enough to steam when exposed. Hollow quills cover their bodies, not as armor but as insulation and storage. Each quill contains a different cultivated microbe. Some digest sulfurous stone, some bind poisonous metals, and some produce heat. A hearth-tender selects among them with combs on its forefeet, planting the necessary colony around a root chamber and scraping away strains that have become too vigorous.

It carries pellets of reactive basalt in pouches along its throat. The pellets click together as it moves—the source of one of the country’s few persistent sounds. In warm chambers it stacks them into loose mounds, smears them with secretions, and waits for the heat organisms to flower across their surfaces.

The burrower does not understand the furnace it tends. No elder teaches it, and no colony belongs to it. The sequence is held in its muscles: choose, carry, score, moisten, withdraw. Yet the combined labor of uncountable hearth-tenders keeps whole groves from freezing. They patrol blockages in the root valves, remove dead tissue, and bury their own eggs where the chemical temperature will remain exact.

If a heat colony fails, the tenders arrive from many directions. None summons the others. The failing colony changes the charge in the surrounding stone, and that absence draws them as surely as scent.

There are also animals of the pools, although they are rarely visible in the water.

Black basins connect through capillaries finer than roots, and through these passages travel ribbonlike swimmers with mineral skeletons. They browse on dissolved salts and on the faint films that form at the boundary between water and rock. During electrical storms they align head to tail in chains stretching from one basin to another. Charge passes through their bodies, and for a few moments rings of green light appear at the shores.

The largest pool-dwellers never surface. Their presence is known when a reflection bends after the reflected thing has moved away. They occupy not the open basin but the thin, cold depth immediately beneath its level surface—a depth that does not always correspond to the dimensions of the rock. Their feeding limbs extend upward without breaking the water. They touch the branching mouths of the tall walkers and exchange something too fine to be seen.

The walkers are the most conspicuous inhabitants and perhaps the least numerous.

Each stands higher than the low vegetation by many body lengths. Its torso is a narrow suspended vessel surrounded by long, many-jointed legs. Broad pads flex around every leaf-fan without crushing it. The animal seems delicate until wind crosses the plateau; then its legs spread and lock into a geometry capable of resisting forces that bend the solitary trees.

No two walkers have quite the same number of joints. New segments form throughout life, usually after long visits to the pools. Old individuals may move on twelve or fifteen angles per leg, stepping with such slowness that moss grows on their feet between strides.

Their branching mouthparts are not used solely for drinking. Lowered above a basin, they read the water’s electrical state. Minute membranes open along each branch, sampling dissolved metal, root charge, distant heat and the chemical signature of vapor descending from the cliffs. A walker may stand this way for hours, learning where the plateau will be warm, where the next condensation will gather, and perhaps how far away the next tree presently is.

Then it raises its head and waits.

Walkers do not begin a journey until the ground permits it. When a blue pulse shortens the interval between two places, every nearby walker steps at once. A herd scattered across hundreds of kilometers may arrive together at the same fissure, though none has crossed the intervening distance in the ordinary way.

They congregate only during the great moisture cycles. Adults arrange themselves around warm cracks while smaller, softer individuals emerge from slits in their torsos. These young possess few legs and no hard internal frame. They cling beneath their parents until mineral drizzle seals their skins. Afterward, each departs in a different direction. There is no guarding and no recognition that can be observed, but decades later, when the same root circuit brightens, related walkers often stop moving at the same instant.

Above them live the still hunters.

They descend from the low vapor ceiling on ribbed membranes that are neither wings nor sails. Their bodies are little more than a mouth, a cluster of sensory pits, and a frame supporting broad conductive skin. Rather than pushing against air, they fasten themselves to charged updrafts. Where the field is strong, a hunter may remain absolutely motionless for days, hanging in darkness over a migration path.

Its strike begins below. The hunter changes the charge of a patch of vegetation, causing leaf-fans to fold and tunnel animals to hesitate. Then the field collapses. For an instant the creature has weight.

After it feeds, it releases digestive droplets that travel uphill along leaf edges, carrying whatever cannot be used toward a root sink. It leaves no bones or dung. A well-fed hunter becomes lighter, rises into the ash-colored vapor, and may not touch the plateau again for years.

The long cables below the cliffs have their own populations.

Hook-footed grazers descend headfirst through the cloud sea, scraping growth from the cable skins. At swollen knots they gather in hundreds, forming living rings that rotate slowly around the strands. Predators wait inside the knots, where warm fluids pulse downward. Luminous cup-colonies open in the warmer depths and close when a cable tightens.

Some cable animals never encounter rock. They are born in the gray below, feed and mate upon a hanging strand, and die there. Their recovered matter enters the cable and is pumped upward. In this way, distant life reaches the plateaus as heat and salt without ever seeing them.

Other creatures cross the gulf.

Thread-fliers hatch in cliff gardens when the descending vents open. Each consists of a filament surrounding a bladder of charged gas. They have no means of choosing a direction, but neighboring fliers attract and repel one another until the swarm assumes a broad, branching shape. The shape catches currents no individual could catch. It drifts toward another mesa, breaks apart against its electrical field, and settles into fissures.

Many are reproductive bodies of plants. Some are animals in their entirety. Others are only temporary organs produced by things that remain embedded deep in the cliff.

During the great migrations, seed-sized black animals pour from the stone in braided columns. They are not a single species. Several unrelated forms travel together because each supplies what another lacks. One detects newly condensed water. One cuts paths through closed vegetation. One carries dormant heat organisms. One consumes the predators that follow the column. Taken separately, none could cross even a hundred paces of exposed ground; together they move like a dark liquid from fissure to basin.

Their columns divide, merge, reverse and occasionally climb straight over the plateau edge. By the next interval of darkness they are gone. What remains are minute chambers laid inside hollow stems, each sealed with wax and holding an egg, a spore, a crystal of salt, or all three.

Yet these are not the largest inhabitants.

The solitary trees appear to be individuals because each has one trunk and occupies its own horizon. This is misleading. Their roots descend through the basalt and mingle with fungal cords, storage organs and muscular vines belonging to the plateau-wide mat. A tree is less a separate organism than a place where the buried life rises into the air.

Its curtains of black foliage harvest mist, but they also sense movement in the electrical field. When a low vibration passes through the rock, the tree lowers its leaves before any animal becomes still. During storms, branching light reveals conduits within the trunk: fluid channels, conductive tissues, cavities full of warm microbes, and narrow passageways through which pale tenders crawl.

A tree can survive the death of its visible body. After a trunk freezes or splits, the roots withdraw useful matter and seal the socket. For centuries there may be only low vegetation there. Then, when subterranean pressures change, ribs rise again from the same place.

Nor is the moss merely groundcover. Its overlapping fans, creepers and rosettes are the exposed organs of many interdependent beings, some local and brief-lived, others spread across entire mesas. Beneath every step they join fungal exchanges, mineral membranes, water vessels and charged roots. No clean border separates one life from the next. Tissue is lent, absorbed, abandoned and grown again elsewhere.

This buried consortium regulates warmth, stores water, plates metals onto its inner surfaces and opens the vents that seed the cloud sea. It responds to damage. It anticipates frost. It remembers repeated paths as altered conductivity in the stone. But it has no central organ, no viewpoint, and perhaps no awareness that resembles thought.

Its moments of greatest coordination might nonetheless be called attention.

When the deep vibration comes, valves stop in sequence. Animals freeze. Pools dimple from below. Trees lower their curtains. Across mesa after mesa, the living mat holds its chemical breath.

For a while, the country is not a multitude of creatures but one act of listening.

Then the vibration passes. Root valves begin clicking. Heat colonies brighten. Walkers lift their feet. Hunters fasten themselves again to the charged air. Millions of hidden mouths resume feeding.

Nothing commands the resumption.

Everyone does.
