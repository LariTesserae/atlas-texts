They are called nothing now.

The service crews once gave them names—rimewings, forkbeaks, white mites, cable birds—but no name survived prolonged acquaintance. Each made the animals seem too familiar, too separable from the chamber. They are not birds despite the way they roost. They are not crustaceans despite their wet, articulated armor. They are not vermin, because vermin live at the expense of a place, and the Cold Lung appears to require them.

Seen alone, one is scarcely larger than a clenched hand.

Its shell consists of overlapping white plates, translucent at the edges and milkier toward the spine. Blue service light passes through the thinner portions, revealing a fine internal scaffolding and a dark fluid traveling in quick, reversible pulses. There is no obvious heart. Several organs take turns performing the work, contracting in sequence from head to tail and then, without pause, reversing direction. The creature’s blood—or coolant, or brine—never completes a circuit. It is shuttled perpetually back and forth.

Six limbs fold beneath the shell. The front pair are delicate, able to comb metallic powder from wet surfaces grain by grain. The middle pair bear pads that cling by pressure to ceramic and glass. The rear pair end in hard hooks shaped to enter the perforations of factory grating. When no grating is available, those hooks fasten around pipes, bolt heads, cable braids, or the limbs of neighbors.

This last grip is their habitual one.

Few ever support themselves entirely on the chamber. Most stand on others. Beneath every visible layer is another: smaller bodies pressed into hollows, pale heads emerging between legs, gill-fans working in the humid darkness. In the deepest clusters there may be ten or twenty tiers, each animal carrying and being carried. Their shells fit together with such precision that water runs over the colony as over a single tiled roof.

The head is narrow and without a neck. Two glossy eyes sit far apart, each black all the way through. Examination lamps produce no visible pupil, only a pinprick reflection that remains fixed even when the creature turns away. Between the eyes projects a divided beak. Its two tines vibrate independently, tasting electrical fields, scraping oxidation from metal, and setting the creature’s note.

Their chorus begins in these forks, but it does not end there.

Every shell is hollowed by resonant chambers. Every limb joint contains a minute reed. The wet membranes under their plates tighten and slacken against the constant pressure of the pumps. Air, water, and structural vibration pass through them, and each body extracts one narrow frequency from the mountain’s roar. A solitary animal removed from the colony makes almost no sound. It opens its beak, trembles, and gives only a weak glassy whine.

Among its fellows, it becomes an instrument.

Its feet receive vibrations from six directions. Its shell transmits them onward. Its note locks against those of its neighbors, and they against theirs, until the entire population maintains one continuous chord. No individual seems to know the sound. The arrangement knows it.

They do not sleep. At intervals, local groups cease scraping and drinking, but their feet continue to make tiny corrections, tightening the colony wherever a gap threatens to appear. Rest passes through them as a slow redistribution of labor. One animal stills, then another takes more weight, extends its gill-fans farther, or shifts a beak to the strip of zinc the sleeper abandoned. The pause travels through the mass without ever producing stillness.

Nor do they eat in the ordinary sense.

Condensation enters grooves along the forked beaks. Metallic frost is crushed between mineral ridges and carried into chambers beneath the face. There, weak acids separate salt from metal, charge from moisture, heat from both. What can be used passes into the body. What cannot is extruded in beads so small and regular that they resemble manufactured grit.

Those beads become part of the landscape. Hidden belts lift them back to the slopes. Water carries them down again. The animals consume them again. It is possible that every grain has passed through every body thousands of times. It is equally possible that there is, in some meaningful sense, only one meal in the Cold Lung, perpetually divided and returned.

Their differences are subtle but real.

The smallest are nearly clear. They live at the margins of pools and beneath the belly plates of larger animals, filtering iron haze with feathery mouthparts. Their legs are too soft to grip bare steel, so they cling to adults and move when the adults move. They might be juveniles, but they never become larger.

Those clustered around cables are heavier, with yellowed shells and scorched tips on their beaks. Blue sparks sometimes crawl over their backs and vanish between their plates. When a cable’s insulation cools, they press together until their bodies form a living sheath. When it warms, the outermost animals lift their shells to vent a fine mist.

The ceiling dwellers are flatter and wider. Their gill membranes hang like wet banners. They collect droplets before the drops can fall, passing water foot to foot through the inverted colony until it reaches a drinking seam. Every few minutes one loosens its grip, turns in the air, and is caught by those beneath it. Whether this is movement or maintenance is unclear.

The oldest-looking individuals occupy the drain mouths. Their shells are gray, thickened with mineral deposits, and fused in places to the metal around them. Tubes extend from their undersides into the plumbing. They open and close their beaks with the others, but they have not visibly changed position since they were first recorded. If they possess limbs, those limbs are buried. If they were once mobile, the chamber has incorporated them.

All adults contain eggs.

The eggs show through the lower membranes as dim pearls arranged in branching rows. Each holds a curled form with six folded feet and a divided beak already touching its own chest. Under magnification, the forms twitch. Their tiny mouthparts scrape at the inner surface of the egg, yet leave no mark.

There are no nests because every body is a nest. There is no breeding season because the season has approached and never arrived.

Occasionally, two adults place their forked beaks together. Their tones interfere, producing a low beat beneath the greater chord. Fluid passes between the tines—clear in one direction, dark in the other. They may be exchanging nutrients. They may be repairing one another. They may be mating in preparation for a future that remains permanently adjacent.

No corpse has been found.

Damaged creatures do occur. A cracked shell is closed over by neighboring feet. A severed membrane disappears beneath the colony and later emerges unbroken from another gap. Individuals marked with dye are difficult to follow: the mark fades from one body and appears in faint traces across dozens nearby. Tissue samples taken from separate animals have sometimes proven genetically identical and sometimes incompatible enough to belong to different kingdoms.

The best-supported theory is that individuality is provisional in them.

A white creature may be less an organism than a temporary knot in a larger circulation. Water, minerals, charge, vibration, and perhaps living matter move continuously among the packed bodies. What appears to be one animal may slowly become several without division, or several may become one without fusion. Their outlines persist because the chamber needs bodies of that size and shape, not because any particular self endures inside them.

Still, there are signs of preference.

They avoid the black column.

Those nearest its empty ring face away, even when this twists their limbs awkwardly beneath the colony. Their rear hooks dig deep into seams. Their gill-fans remain folded. The unwavering chorus bends around that vacancy: no note changes, yet the absence at the center seems audible, a silence so narrow and exact that it gives the surrounding noise its structure.

Now and then, one animal is pushed to the ring’s edge.

It may be a small translucent one, or a broad ceiling form displaced through the layers, or an ancient gray creature somehow freed from a drain. For a fraction of a second it stands with one foot over the empty floor. Its black eyes reflect the half-lit red indicator. The egg-shapes inside it shudder. Its beak opens wider than feeding requires.

Then the colony draws it back.

This withdrawal is the closest the inhabitants come to alarm.

Perhaps they maintain the Cold Lung to prevent the indicator from changing. Perhaps their endless chord is the signal that holds terrain formation in readiness. Perhaps they are waiting for the red light to brighten so they can become what they have only rehearsed: hatchlings, parents, grazers, prey; a population capable of hunger and loss.

For now they remain crowded beyond counting, but never increase.

They move without migrating, feed without consuming, age without succession. They are born only as visible possibilities inside transparent flesh. They possess all the machinery of life except aftermath.

And because no one of them ever quite stands alone, the chamber may contain thousands of inhabitants.

Or one.
