At first, the high country seems inhabited by giants.

They move at dawn, when the marsh is white with low vapor: long-backed animals advancing through the reeds in family lines, each step placed slowly enough that the water closes without splashing. Adults stand twice the height of a person at the shoulder. Their legs are narrow, jointed high, and sheathed in overlapping scales where they enter the water. Their heads hang low on muscular necks. The mouth opens sideways into two combs of flexible teeth, which they draw through submerged meadows to strain out bulbs, soft stems, shell-creatures, and mineral-rich mud.

Their backs are broad and shallowly furrowed. Moss establishes itself there first, then small ferns, cup-shaped flowers, and colonies of threadlike blue plants. Older individuals carry shrubs. One may be recognized for generations by the particular garden it bears: red blossoms along the spine, silver moss over one hip, a wind-bent sapling rooted behind the shoulders. The animal’s skin feeds this growth with heat and salts, while the plants conceal its outline and cool its blood.

The gardens are not accidental. Mothers transfer pieces of their own moss to newborns, pressing flank to flank until the growth takes hold. Family identity is horticultural. When two herds meet, they smell one another’s plantings before they touch noses.

They are difficult to alarm. At danger they fold their legs and settle until water covers the body. Nostrils close. Eyes draw beneath transparent lids. What remains above the surface is a planted hump indistinguishable from the many true islands drifting nearby. Calves fit between adults and disappear under overhanging gardens.

Some marsh islands, watched long enough, breathe.

The reed walls contain smaller, more restless lives. Six-limbed climbers run along the vertical stems with the speed of sparks. They are bright only in motion. At rest, their skin clouds to the green, brown, or mineral white of whatever supports them. Four limbs grip; the other pair comb the reeds for mites, eggs, and condensed water. Their hands have no claws. Each finger ends in a damp ring that seals briefly against a surface and releases with a soft click.

The throat pouch is the most conspicuous part of them. It hangs translucent beneath the jaw, lined with rigid resonating plates. Inflated, it can be struck by the short forelimbs like a drum. Different colonies develop different rhythms, but the basic calls are older than any colony: warnings for falling branches, announcements of fruiting, directions toward open water, changes in the pressure field.

The sound does not travel chiefly through air. A climber braces against a reed and drums into the stem. The pulse descends through the rhizomes and reappears kilometers away as a tremor in another stand. Whole marshes speak this way. At dawn, thousands join until the plateau seems to possess a single enormous wooden voice.

They build no separate nests. Instead they reshape living reeds, biting small channels into young stems and guiding their growth into suspended chambers. The plant closes around them. Eggs are laid inside warm nodes and tended communally. A chamber may serve twenty generations before the reed dies, and the colony will continue returning to its hollow shell, drumming old signals into a structure no longer connected to the roots.

Their longest calls are addressed not to their own kind but to the mountain.

When a hidden pylon changes pitch, the nearest colonies answer. The response passes outward through reed beds and forest roots, altered at each relay. Sometimes the pylons quiet afterward. Sometimes rain begins. Whether the climbers understand this exchange is impossible to determine. They may be petitioning a machine, warning one another about it, or simply occupying an acoustic niche that the buried system has learned to use.

Above them live the sailers.

They appear at first to be broad leaves carried on updrafts, though no wind moves them away. Their bodies are small compared with their wings, which stretch between long branching spars and can become almost transparent against the sky. Every vein bears thousands of sensory filaments. They feel temperature, pressure, electrical charge, and distortions in distance too slight for other animals to notice.

A sailer does not navigate by landmarks alone. It follows the shape of the enclosure. It knows where warm air has been folded inward, where a ravine contains more depth than its mouth should permit, where an invisible temporal boundary will make a wingbeat too slow. Young sailers are taught these routes. For months they follow beneath an elder’s shadow, matching every bank and turn until they can cross the basin without flapping.

Some never land after their first ascent. They feed by opening fringed mouths into clouds of spores and aerial organisms. They sleep by locking half the brain at a time while circling a stable updraft. Moss and pale parasites gather at the bases of their wings. In extreme age, the wing membranes become ragged but broader, patched by translucent tissue that grows around every wound.

Only reproduction brings them down. They descend to the warm pools hollowed in old boulders and lay strings of glassy eggs along the mineral rims. The young begin as swimmers. They graze the pool’s walls, develop six temporary legs, and climb out one wet morning to unfold wings much larger than the bodies that contained them. Their empty larval skins remain attached to the stone and are slowly covered in pearl deposit. Some pools have accumulated rings of these pale forms for thousands of years.

The lakes support animals too large to be seen clearly.

They surface in parts: a dark curve among lily pads, a round breathing aperture, a line of sensory pits opening and closing like small mouths. Their bodies are heavy, soft-sided, and wider than their wakes suggest. Much of each animal may occupy a pressure fold beneath the lake, so that its visible length and actual volume do not agree. A creature that seems to pass under a canoe for several minutes may rise afterward in a pool too small to hold it.

They feed on the anchored filter colonies of the deep shelves, tearing off mature fans while leaving the basal disks to regrow. Their teeth are mineral plates replaced throughout life. Old plates fall in regular beds and become nesting shelters for silver swimmers.

These lake animals breathe seldom. On still nights they come up one after another, and the openings along their backs exhale warm mist. The breaths may continue from one end of a lake to the other, suggesting a herd. Or one animal. No dissection has settled the question. The water between apparent bodies sometimes transmits the same pulse at exactly the same moment.

Around pylons gather the silver swimmers. Individually they are slight, nearly featureless fish with mirrored sides and vibrating internal bladders. Collectively they display a severe precision unlike anything else in the basin. At certain tones they leave their feeding grounds and take fixed positions in the water, arranging themselves into grids, spirals, nested rings, and shapes that extend from one lake through flooded channels into the next.

Each fish holds a point. The school becomes a diagram.

While they are aligned, the black pylons hum more strongly. Fine ripples cross the lakes despite the absence of wind. Pressure changes in distant hollows. Springs reverse or resume their ordinary direction. Sometimes a dead pylon, silent for centuries, gives one low note after a school forms around it.

The fish appear to be both animal and instrument. Their swim bladders contain mineral threads of the same silver substance embedded in the ancient cliff loops. Yet they hatch from eggs, compete for mates, flee predators, and die. Whether the builders designed them or merely taught the machinery to make use of an existing species no longer matters. Evolution has completed the arrangement.

The same is true everywhere.

Trees sense the buried tone through their roots. Their mineral bark is not inert armor but a slow record of pressure conditions. Each new layer preserves the field under which it formed. A core taken from an ancient trunk would contain a history not only of rainfall and heat but of local gravity, elapsed time, and the changing dimensions of nearby valleys.

The oldest trees react before the pylons audibly alter. Leaves turn edge-on. Flowers close. Roots tighten around streambanks. Seeds that have waited two hundred years split open within minutes of the proper note. The forest may be listening to the machines, but it may also be advising them. Pylon changes often follow mass root movement during floods.

Even decay has inhabitants. Pale films spread over fallen wood, neither fungi nor ordinary mineral. They digest slowly in the central wetlands and furiously near the rim. A single colony crossing a temporal seam develops two distinct fronts: one scarcely advancing, the other consuming years of growth in an afternoon. The fronts exchange nutrients across the boundary. Many organisms exploit this. They root on the slow side and feed on the fast.

Predators do the same.

A marsh hunter is long, low, and draped in filamentous skin that breaks up its outline. It does not chase. It waits beside an invisible boundary and startles prey across it. If the animal crosses into slower time, the hunter reaches through from the faster side and strikes with what appears to the victim as impossible speed. Older prey learn the seams and reverse the trick. Their migrations are not maps of land but maps of permitted motion.

There are parasites that spend only their reproductive hour in fast zones. Flowers place their nectar just beyond pressure boundaries so that visiting animals carry pollen farther than they intend. Small burrowing creatures make homes in uphill streams, letting the current lift waste away toward higher ponds. Every peculiarity that might once have been a hazard has acquired mouths, roots, eggs, and habits.

Of the people who made the bridges, rooms, markers, and pylons, there is no unambiguous living trace.

There are, however, figures occasionally seen in the forest after long rain.

They stand beside old structures, narrow and gray, with low heads and arms that seem jointed more times than they should be. They do not flee when noticed. They simply become difficult to keep in sight. A watcher glances aside and finds that the figure has moved several meters without crossing the intervening ground. Following its prints is no help. Some are fresh and filling with water; others in the same trail have mineralized hard enough to be centuries old.

At close range—on the rare occasions when close range remains consistent—they resemble neither machines nor animals. Their surfaces are layered like the bark of the ancient trees. Moss occupies hollows along their shoulders. Tiny silver swimmers have been seen moving through clear channels beneath the skin. They may be constructs overgrown by the ecology, descendants altered to survive the field, or maintenance forms assembled temporarily from local life.

They touch pylons but carry no tools. They enter the doorless ridge rooms and remain there for years. Once, after a lightning seam failed and burned a straight black wound across the marsh, three such figures appeared. They stood with their hands against the stone until vines began growing over their feet. The next morning the seam conducted normally. The figures remained for another season, gradually collecting mineral crust, until they could no longer be distinguished from knee-high markers.

Perhaps they had repaired the seam. Perhaps the seam had repaired them.

The more closely the high country is studied, the less convincing the distinction becomes. No central keeper has ever been found. There is no hidden population directing the system from a buried city, no sovereign intelligence issuing commands through the pylons. Instead, maintenance is distributed across lives that need not comprehend it.

Fish tune the lakes. Reeds carry messages. Climbers amplify them. Trees archive local conditions. Sailer migrations trace faults in the pressure enclosure. Grazer herds move seeds between temporal zones and preserve marsh islands on their backs. Mineral films seal dead wood into streambanks. Lake animals keep deep conduits open as they feed. Predators patrol the dangerous boundaries simply because prey gathers there.

The mountain’s machinery has entered their bodies, but their bodies have also entered its machinery.

It is possible that the original makers intended this. It is equally possible that they abandoned a failing system and life, patient and opportunistic, grew into every vacancy until ecology became operation. The basin does not preserve a clean line between designed and evolved. Nothing there is wholly wild, and nothing remains merely artificial.

The oldest inhabitant may therefore be the enclosure itself.

It breathes through marshes and cavern mouths. Its circulation is rain, springwater, animal migration, and the slow lateral pouring of air. Its nerves are conductive seams, silver loops, roots, reed beds, and schools of fish. Its memory is stored in bark, mineral terraces, persistent reflections, and pockets where time has not yet finished passing. Its thoughts, if they deserve the word, are minute corrections made over centuries.

The plateau does not wake. It does not sleep. It has no face hidden in the stone.

But when pressure falls in one distant basin, climbers begin to drum. Sailers turn along routes invisible from the ground. Silver bodies leave the shallows and assemble around black pylons. Trees shift their leaves. Water hesitates, then chooses another direction. Across the cloud-filled chasms, the ribbed structures answer one another with distortions of light.

Something has noticed.

All the living country adjusts around the change, slowly and exactly, and keeps what the mountain was built to keep.
