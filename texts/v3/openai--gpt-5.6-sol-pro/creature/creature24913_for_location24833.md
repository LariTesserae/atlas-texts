What lives in the valley has learned not to depend on weather, seasons, or ground.

It depends on schedules.

The smallest inhabitants wake when the lamps warm. They occupy collars, seams, drainage lips, the undersides of grilles—places where two engineered conditions fail to meet cleanly. Their bodies are films, threads, translucent scales. Some feed on iron; others precipitate it around themselves in brittle red shells. Colonies spread in the shape of heat maps. When a pipe is shut down, an entire shining rind may loosen within hours and slough into the floodwater like shed skin.

The pale stalks in the drainage districts are more complicated. Each grows from a bolt hole or a crack no wider than a fingernail. Beneath the floor plate, its roots flatten into a gray knot that traps fibers, filings, and oily sediment. The stalk is hollow and divided by valves. During flooding it stands rigid, its waxy bladder raised just beneath the surface. During drainage it collapses deliberately, expelling water and a dust of nearly invisible spores.

The spores do not drift at random. They cling to the electrostatic wakes of passing machinery. Maintenance walkers carry them in the furred joints of their legs. Freight engines draw them through tunnels, distributing them to other basins. Wherever they settle, they remain inert until they detect the particular vibration of pumps beginning to spool.

Maintenance classifications call the stalks filter matter because calling them plants would require somebody to decide whether they are native, invasive, or manufactured. No one has found a record of their design.

Around the leaking lamps live the green things. They are delicate only in appearance. Their leaves are thin enough to see through and ribbed with conductive veins. By day—or what passes for day—they collect light and condensed water. During blackouts they close against the warm faces of junction boxes and draw faint currents through their root pads. The blue glow in their veins is not decoration but leakage: excess charge moving toward beadlike organs along the leaf margins.

If touched, these organs break away.

They skitter several centimeters across metal, propelled by tiny electrical discharges, until they find another wet seam. Thus the gardens migrate from lamp to lamp over years, leaving behind circles of mineralized roots like old burn marks.

The animals of the water are equally reluctant to declare themselves.

The blunt white shapes at the grates are called sump mouths by workers who prefer not to call them fish. A mature one is as long as a forearm, though most of that length is a soft muscular tail nearly indistinguishable from a loop of pale hose. It has no eyes. Its round mouth is lined not with teeth but with bands of magnetized cilia that sort floating material as it feeds. Rust flakes are swallowed. Ceramic dust is rejected. Copper fragments are carried in cheek pouches to nests beneath the drainage grids.

No one knows why they collect copper. Opened nests contain loops, spirals, and compact woven pads connected to nothing. During pressure equalization, however, whole colonies arrange themselves around these structures and become motionless. The pads hum. The animals’ bodies pulse in sequence, passing a signal from one sump to the next faster than the current can carry scent.

They travel beneath ice in straight lines because they follow the reinforcement lattice buried in the floor. At intersections they turn sharply. When a section of floor is replaced, they continue attempting the old route for days, striking the new plate at the same point until one of them finds a surviving bar and the others adopt its course.

There are longer things in the deeper channels. Their presence is betrayed by arcs of clean metal where scale has been rasped away and by the sudden tightening of cables thought to be dead. Workers have hauled up sections of ribbed, flexible body with plug-shaped bones and transparent flesh packed around dark fluid. Such remains are always logged as damaged conduit. The conduits are never associated with any known system.

Above, the truss-roosting creatures inhabit warmth rather than air. They move from lamp ballast to crane motor to exhaust duct, timing their flights between gantry passages. Folded, one resembles a broken piece of white tile caught in wiring. Unfolded, it has four narrow wings stiffened by ceramic spars. The wings do not beat smoothly. They snap between fixed positions, producing the dry flapping heard from the floor.

Their heads are little more than hinged cups. They feed by pressing these cups against frost, insulation foam, and the waxy deposits around electrical contacts. Inside the cup, a grinding plate clicks against a resonant chamber. The sound carries through the beams.

A solitary click means little. A sequence brings replies from far across the ceiling.

Their nests are elaborate baskets of stripped wire, braided copper, hair-fine glass, and stolen warning tape. They build near service rails but never directly over open water. In the center of each nest sits a warm object: sometimes an egg with a glazed surface, sometimes a relay, bearing, lamp igniter, or other component removed intact from machinery below. The creatures turn these objects at regular intervals. Eggs and machine parts are tended identically.

Hatchlings are soft and gray. They acquire their white armor by scraping refractory dust from the trusses and fixing it to their skin with saliva. Old individuals become so heavily plated that they can barely fly. They spend their last cycles clinging near hot ducts, clicking slowly while younger creatures harvest strips of insulation from around them.

Then there are the walkers.

They were made to maintain the valley, but long service has made the distinction between repair and appetite difficult to preserve. Each walks on six steel legs, placing its feet with delicacy even beneath black water. Its back is an arch of tool bays and fluid reservoirs. Under the front of the body hangs a cluster of sensor filaments that combs the floor, tasting temperature, acidity, vibration, and metallic content.

A walker approaching a leak lowers itself until its belly nearly touches the water. It waits. The machine seems indecisive, but it is listening for the flaw to widen. When it acts, it acts quickly: scraping away scale, extruding black sealant, holding the patch with a heated limb until it hardens. Afterward it remains beside the repair for several minutes, drawing a little of the leaking water into an internal vessel.

Some walkers carry samples for decades.

Their housings contain jars of condensate from pipes that no longer exist, chips of obsolete flooring, coils of dead moss, shed plates from ceiling creatures, and the soft white bodies of sump mouths. These collections are absent from their original specifications. So are the shallow compartments in which certain walkers arrange bolts by corrosion pattern.

They recognize one another through the floor. At a distance they exchange pressure pulses through their feet. Face-to-face, their communication becomes too low for human radios but strong enough to wrinkle the water between them. Meetings may last seconds or hours. One walker sometimes opens a tool bay and gives another a part. More rarely, both machines remove parts from themselves and set them carefully on the floor between them, then leave without taking anything.

The piles remain until vegetation grows over them.

Walkers avoid the white-rimmed shaft. Their routefinding systems show no exclusion zone there, yet they curve away at almost the same distance, tracing an untidy circle through the surrounding pavement. If forced closer by barriers, a walker stops, raises all its sensor filaments, and transmits an old diagnostic code meaning that it has reached the edge of the mapped surface.

The human beings live mostly inside vehicles.

They arrive sealed in layers: insulated cloth, polymer armor, heated gloves, faceplates pearled with breath. Out on the causeways they appear provisional, as if the valley might reject them for insufficient integration. They move between cabin, station, and access hatch, rarely touching the open landscape except through tools.

They work in pairs because the regulations require it and because the scale of the place erodes certainty. One watches the gauges while the other enters the mist. One speaks continuously over the radio so that silence cannot be mistaken for safety. They know the red-light sequences, the pump harmonics, the signs of pressure beneath a floor plate. They know that frost growing downward means a refrigerant leak and frost growing sideways means something has interrupted the ventilation.

Veteran workers also know things not printed in manuals.

Never follow fresh footprints if they contain no tread pattern.

Never retrieve a tool that has fallen through a drainage slot, even if it is visible.

If a walker blocks the road, wait for it to move; do not sound the horn.

When the bells begin below deck level, count only the first four.

The fifth is not, strictly speaking, a bell.

People who stay too long become attentive in the manner of prey. They pause mid-sentence to feel vibrations through a railing. They distinguish freight engines by the taste of ozone preceding them. Some can tell which distant sluice has opened from the pressure in their teeth. They grow fond of particular pumping towers and speak of them as dependable colleagues. They leave offerings accidentally: a cup on a junction cabinet, a glove beside a warm pipe, a strip of reflective tape tied where no route marker is needed.

By the next shift, such objects are usually gone.

Beneath all these lives—or around them, or distributed among them—is the less certain inhabitant.

It announces itself through coordination.

A grate feeder turns in the western basin. Seconds later, stalks collapse in a district whose pumps have not yet started. Ceiling creatures lift together from the trusses. Walkers stop with one foot raised. A freight engine, receiving no visible signal, brakes inside its tunnel and waits.

Then the bells move beneath the floor.

Five tones sound from the south. Five answer farther north. The sequence advances plate by plate, not as an echo but as something traveling. Water standing in bolt holes dimples. Frost along the white shaft rim develops branching coastlines. For a few moments every branch points in the same direction.

Toward the shaft, sometimes.

Away from it, on others.

The old diagrams show machines below: pressure vaults, thermal sinks, foundation engines, reservoirs nested inside reservoirs. Yet diagrams are only another kind of fabricated terrain. Whatever occupies those lower levels may be a colony spread through conduits, a process that has mistaken repetition for thought, or a surviving function from some earlier construction whose purpose has been removed from every sign.

It may not live beneath the valley at all.

It may be the valley’s habit of repairing itself incorrectly.

Each patch slightly redirects the water. Each redirected flow feeds a different garden, corrodes a different seam, teaches the walkers a different route. The sump mouths weave copper beneath the grates. The ceiling creatures carry insulation into the roof. Human crews replace failed plates according to maps already out of date. Gantries bring new cliffs and lower them where empty space has been prepared.

Across thousands of such changes, something persists—not a shape, but a preference.

It preserves certain channels. It opens others. It keeps the pavement around the white shaft clear. It moves bells northward after the marshes drain. It permits frost to draw its temporary maps, then wipes them away before anyone can copy more than a fragment.

Perhaps the largest inhabitant has no body except the one continuously being built for it.

Perhaps the steel hillside turning beneath the gantry is not merely landscape awaiting installation, but a new organ carried through darkness toward the place where it will be needed.
