What lives here is easy to mistake for what the chamber is made of.

The dark circles on the stones are not stains, though they began as something scarcely more substantial: a few mineral-eating cells lodged in pores too small to admit a grain of dust. Each patch is a colony, but not a society of distinct bodies. It is a layered agreement among several forms of life. One kind lies against the warm rock and draws energy from iron, sulfur, and other reactive traces. Another occupies the cooler outer skin, trapping what little the air brings. Between them spreads a tough, translucent matrix that holds salts, dead cells, and minute quantities of water so tightly that none can evaporate.

The colony has no true color of its own. Its soot-green and rust tones come from altered minerals, while the bruised violet appears only in the oldest, thickest parts, where generations have accumulated without being shed. A patch may be older than a forest and no larger than an open hand.

Most of the time it is not meaningfully awake. Its chemistry continues, but at intervals too slight to resemble ordinary metabolism. A cell repairs a damaged membrane. A thread lengthens into a newly opened pore. Two molecules are exchanged across a boundary. Then nothing detectable happens for months or years. The life here survives not by enduring activity, but by removing nearly every need for it.

The pale filaments rising from some patches are the colony’s most extravagant structures. They are hollow, stiffened with gypsum and salt, and grown one microscopic ring at a time. They do not seek light, which has no place in their history. Instead they stand in the faint passage of air through the hollow and collect particles too fine to settle quickly: volcanic glass, sulfur dust, fragments of other colonies, and rare molecules carried down from the living plain above.

Once in a great while, when the air entering through the fissures contains a trace more moisture than usual, the filaments soften. Their tips divide and release dry, armored grains. These do not drift far. Most fall within a finger’s breadth of the parent patch and wait in the dust. A few lodge in basalt cracks. Fewer still find the exact conjunction of warmth, mineral chemistry, and protected pore needed to begin. Establishment may take centuries. Failure leaves no corpse that could be distinguished from powder.

The three black growths on the shaded side of the central mound are the chamber’s eldest inhabitants. Their separation is deceptive. Hair-thin living strands descend beneath the salt skin and enter the porous pale stone. Far below the visible surface, they approach one another through ancient channels left by vanished water. Whether they are three colonies or one cannot be answered cleanly. Their buried edges exchange chemical traces so slowly that a signal begun in one growth may not alter another until years later.

They do not communicate in any purposeful sense. Yet each patch changes the stone around it, and those changes become information for whatever reaches them afterward. A richer seam is consumed. A harmful salt is sealed away. A pore is lined with dead material that preserves moisture for descendants. Their history is written as differences too small for a hand to feel.

The chamber contains other life as well, though none rises visibly above the mineral world. Dormant cells remain inside salt crystals. Fine networks occupy the boundaries between basalt and ash. Deep in the warm floor, sparse chemistries continue where fissures admit breath from elsewhere. These hidden forms may never emerge. Some perhaps have not divided since the last water vanished.

There are no hunters because there is nothing abundant enough to hunt. There are no grazers because the crusts defend themselves less with poison than with poverty: they offer almost no moisture, little usable substance, and growth too slow to replace a single bite. Nothing moves from patch to patch. Nothing needs eyes, limbs, sleep, alarm, or haste.

Their chief adaptation is surrender without death.

They accept the chamber’s temperature, its dryness, its darkness, and its lack of event. They do not wait for rain in any conscious way, nor strain toward a future season. They persist in the narrow interval between mineral and organism, taking thousands of years to perform acts that life elsewhere completes before nightfall.

If the hollow were opened and daylight entered, the inhabitants would not stir toward it. If water flooded the basin, many would die—not from drought, but from sudden plenty. They belong to the finished condition of the place. Disturbance, nourishment, and rapid change would be catastrophes.

Left alone, they will continue almost invisibly: darkening a fraction, extending beneath one more crystal, raising another pale filament into air that scarcely moves. The stones will seem empty. The dust will remain unmarked. Yet across spans longer than memory, the black circles will widen, patient as geology and only barely less still.
