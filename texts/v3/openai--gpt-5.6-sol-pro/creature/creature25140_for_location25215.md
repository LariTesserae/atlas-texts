What lives there is difficult to separate from what happens there.

At first it seems that water is the only inhabitant. It arrives without traveling, gathers without seeking low ground, and departs by routes that do not always lead away. It occupies cracks, cups, suspended points, reflections. In the silent ravines it performs its patient reversals, carrying each broken thing toward an earlier wholeness. Elsewhere it wears stone down with equal patience. Thus one stream remembers the mountain forward and another remembers it backward, and where they meet they pass through each other without mixing.

But the water has no single nature. The rain that appears on every surface is not the same presence as the silver threads climbing vertical walls. The pools have local temperaments. Some accept stones, faces, cups. Some accept only images. Some hold their reflections long after the reflected object has gone. Travelers once distinguished them by touching the rims with copper and listening through the metal. A clear note meant ordinary depth. A dull note meant distance. No note meant that the pool was listening back.

The stone is more numerous.

The needles and fins are not a forest, though they occupy the country as trees might occupy another world. Each stands inside several versions of its own shape. The visible pillar is merely the one most firmly agreed upon. Around it cling earlier contours, unbroken summits, vanished shelves, the pressure of buried ages. Cold light sometimes catches these discarded forms and gives them shadows. Warmth brings them nearer. This is why no one shelters under remembering rock: it may resume, briefly and with irresistible force, the weight of the mountain once above it.

The slate fields are colonies of attention.

Each flake maintains a narrow regard along its edge. Most of the time their regard is scattered, distributed harmlessly among the peaks, fissures, and bright vacancies. During rain they turn and scrape, correcting themselves by fractions. Their whisper is the sound of thousands of small alignments being considered and abandoned.

When all the flakes face one point, they have noticed something large.

What passes then is not wind, weather, or creature, but direction itself: a moving orientation under which every surface acquires the same “down,” regardless of slope. Loose stones may leap sideways. Water pours from level pools in vertical sheets. The unseen seventh stones in the cairns become briefly visible, while one of the ordinary six disappears. Anyone standing upright risks being taken as an axis. Anyone lying flat is merely another surface and is usually overlooked.

The echoes also inhabit the shelf, although they are migrants.

They come from events too old, too distant, or not yet completed. They enter through hollows in the mountains and nest temporarily in passages with favorable walls. There they acquire new causes. A river-roar fastens itself to a cracking pebble. Hammering waits inside a handclap. Footsteps find a walker and follow at a careful distance, hoping to become the journey that produced them.

Most echoes are harmless. Some are lonely. A few are predatory only in the sense that an unanswered question is predatory. They try to lead sound back to its supposed source, even if the source lies inside solid rock or centuries beneath the present surface. The wet walls where answering footsteps end are not doors. They are places where an echo has mistaken condensation for arrival.

Frost lives more briefly and with greater precision.

It is born under water, already armed with edges. It splits pools from within and rises in thin, upright leaves. For the length of a passage it may travel as if choosing a route, chiming against pillars, turning at openings no air current explains. Yet it seeks nothing. Its apparent purpose is only the geometry of its dissolution. Each plate is trying to become the particular burst of dry light that ends it.

Then there are the distances.

They are the largest inhabitants and the least stable. One lies stretched between the shelf and the outer ranges; another is folded into the hand-sized amphitheaters; another descends through the central reservoir while simultaneously standing upright above it. Distances feed on clear air. They tighten around counted things. This is why peaks must not be numbered aloud. A spoken total gives the intervening space a finished shape, and a finished distance can close.

Those caught in such a closure are not crushed. They simply find that the mountain they were approaching has become the mountain behind them, while the intervening days have nowhere to occur.

The builders are gone, if “gone” can be used where erosion sometimes runs backward.

They left no bodies because the country does not preserve bodies as bodies. Bone would have been worn into a tool, restored into mineral, attached to a cliff, or distributed among a hundred calcium seams. What remains of the makers is procedural: a preference for seven stones, a hand with four fingers, the dimensions of a sleeping ledge, the habit of hanging cups within reach. Their shelters retain the shape of crouching. Their drainage trenches remember water being anticipated.

Perhaps they were never one people. The handprints range from smaller than a coin to broader than the pillars that bear them. Some were pecked from within the stone outward, leaving raised palms that can be felt but scarcely seen. Some have five intervals between four fingers. Some fill with rain before rain begins.

Whatever made them understood that the reservoir was not a well but an exchange. Water was poured into it, and depth was drawn out.

The copper cups may have measured this depth. Or drunk it.

Occasionally, when the upright reflection rises high enough, shapes appear inside the dark column. They resemble figures only because they contain vacancies where a head, torso, and four limbs might be expected. They ascend by standing still while the water moves around them. Their sizes vary wildly. None reaches the top before the column breaks into rain.

Afterward, fresh handprints are sometimes found on the inward faces of the pillars.

No tracks lead to them.

Beneath all these lesser occupants is the shelf itself—not conscious, not asleep, but engaged in the immense labor of deciding which side is outside. This decision is not settled everywhere. Through fractures, other mountain ranges press their pale profiles against the stone. Under certain pools, roots of rock hang into brilliance. Thunder rises from below when some buried region turns itself over, exchanging interior for sky.

The old surfaces know the present arrangement best. Their scallops and runnels have been rehearsed by water for ages. A hand placed against them during subterranean thunder is held, for a moment, inside that long certainty. Smooth stone says: this is exposure. This is weather. This is where the open world belongs.

That certainty may be the closest thing the country has to mercy.

And during the interval when brightness loses its distance, all the inhabitants become briefly inseparable. Water hangs as light. Echoes find events. Frost becomes sound. Slate turns toward every point at once. The buried shapes of pillars stand through their visible forms, whole and worn simultaneously. The makers’ shelters fill with the pressure of absent bodies. The upright reservoir joins the sky from which the rain has always been falling.

Then the brightness withdraws, and each thing returns to its narrower occupation.

The country does not live.

It attends, remembers, repeats, restores, misplaces, and erodes. It gathers consequences before causes. It shelters old gestures after every hand is gone. And in place of generations, it has cycles of damage and return: the mountain becoming pebble, the pebble becoming mountain, the water becoming depth, the depth becoming rain.

These are its inhabitants.

They do not need to wake.
