What lives there is difficult to separate from what merely remains.

The black lichen is alive, certainly, though not in the ordinary sense of feeding, spreading, and dying back. Each patch is a single slow reflex distributed through the stone. Beneath its scorched surface run blue filaments fine enough to enter mineral grain. They do not grow along cracks; they occupy the memory of cracks, including seams that have sealed and fractures that never opened. Touch causes the visible surface to contract, but the contraction does not pass. It has always just been touched. The pale blue margin is not new growth. It is the instant of withdrawal made permanent.

The moss around the pools is more animal than plant. Its rust-colored mats have a nap that lies in concentric directions, as though something curled there and slept until sleeper and bedding became indistinguishable. Put a hand near it and the fibers lean toward the warmth without crossing the intervening air. Beneath each patch is a shallow cavity in the rock, smooth inside and faintly resonant. The universal roar enters these hollows and emerges softened into a pulse almost like breathing.

Nothing can be found beneath the moss. Nevertheless, there is a sense that it covers something.

The leafless stalks are colonies of another kind. Their true bodies branch below the gravel: white, jointed tubes reaching outward for great distances without increasing in number. A stem on one side of the plateau may connect to another beyond three ridges, though the plateau permits “connect” only approximately. Their hollow mouths point toward regions where nothing visible differs from the rest of the sky. The flute tones passing through them are not made by wind. They are the sounds of those empty regions being sampled.

If a stalk is broken, both halves continue sounding. The severed piece remains rooted. The standing stem remains whole.

The small white creature seen beside the pools is called nothing, for there are no mouths here capable of naming it. It has been mistaken for quartz, for a bird, for a goat whose horns have not yet appeared. All are accurate from the proper distance.

Seen nearby—though nearness cannot be reliably achieved—it stands no higher than a person’s knee. Its body is covered not in fur or feathers but in minute overlapping scales like flakes of milky mineral. Four narrow limbs descend into a blur where feet should be. The head has neither beak nor muzzle. It tapers to a smooth white plane interrupted by one dark pit.

The pit is not an eye. It is the appearance of great depth on a small surface.

The creature does not drink. It stands at the margins of pools and holds its blank face above them. In the water, its reflection bends downward on a long neck and drinks continuously. Sometimes the reflected animal has wings. Sometimes antlers. Sometimes a human face worn smooth by water. The body on shore remains unchanged.

There may be only one such creature. There may be one for every pool, with distance enforcing the illusion of singularity. When it appears elsewhere after the observer looks away, it has not traveled. Instead, for the interval in which it was unwatched, all the pools were the same pool.

The nests under the overhangs belong to something less visible.

Their gray fibers are gathered from no identifiable source. Under magnification, each strand proves to be hollow and filled with a line of unmoving droplets. The translucent shells nestled among them are warm at one point and cold everywhere else. The speck inside each shell resembles an embryo only because the eye expects beginnings to have that shape.

Nothing hatches because hatching has already occurred without making the shell empty.

The hatchlings occupy the roar.

They are long, pressure-shaped beings with no exterior surface. Their bodies exist as standing disturbances in stone, air, water, and bone. One passes through the plateau whenever the metallic bellow deepens, yet its passage has no beginning or end. The grinding undertone is the contact of their bodies with one another. The avalanche-crack is a joint flexing somewhere beneath the tableland. They move immensely and never change position.

Only their shadows can occasionally be seen: vast dim forms crossing the underside of the clouds from below. A shadow may show the suggestion of ribs, or vanes, or fingers spread wider than valleys. These are not anatomical features so much as different ways the mind divides a pressure too large to comprehend whole.

They are not flying beneath the plateau. They carry it.

Or perhaps they are what the plateau sounds like from within.

The low stone enclosures have inhabitants as well, though no body could fit through their entrances. Something resides in each by occupying its proportions rather than its interior. It is present in the exact narrowness of the doorway, the angle between the wet wall and the dry floor, the inverse fitting of stone to stone. Remove a block and the inhabitant remains complete; the gap takes on the missing block’s function. Rebuild the wall elsewhere and the room does not follow.

Near an enclosure, one may notice a figure kneeling inside—a compact shape with its back turned, shoulders draped in what looks like damp gray cloth. It is always seen through an opening too small to admit the head of the observer. From another angle there is only a boulder, or a fold of cloud beyond the absent fourth wall.

The kneeling figures have no faces. They have hollows where faces would be held.

Each attends to a tiny event: a drip descending from the roof slab, a grain of white gravel on the verge of overturning, a thread of condensation climbing stone. Their task appears devotional, but it is closer to restraint. They keep these events from arriving at consequences. The drip must descend but never strike. The grain must tip but never fall. The moisture must climb but never reach the ledge.

Whether the figures preserve the plateau or are imprisoned by its preservation is impossible to tell.

The faint lines crossing the ground are associated with them. On the side where the lines appear sunken, they resemble paths made by repeated journeys from cliff to ravine. On the side where they rise, they resemble long seams closing behind such journeys. Occasionally, at the edge of vision, processions can be seen upon them: many low figures moving beneath burdens shaped like roof slabs.

Look directly, and there are only balanced stones on a ridge.

The columns themselves are inhabited by tension. Between every two edge-balanced slabs lies an exceedingly thin black shape, upright and compressed. These narrow beings hold no stone in place. Rather, they are what would be released if the stones fell. Frost marks the side facing their past; moisture marks the side facing their future. Since neither direction can be entered, the columns remain balanced and the dark occupants remain without width.

At the northern summit, where the rooms multiply by being approached, the inhabitants become more intimate.

There is only one room. There is also one occupant for every doorway.

It sits just beyond the edge of direct sight, where the wet wall meets the dry floor. Its presence can be inferred from small asymmetries: a crescent of moisture interrupted at knee height, pale grit pressed into the shape of fingers, the niche’s shadow leaning toward an absent body. Turn quickly and the room is empty. Turn slowly and the certainty of company increases until it becomes almost unbearable, though nothing appears.

The rounded stone is its organ.

Warmth is the sole action the occupant has retained. Not heat, which would pass into the hand and alter frost, but warmth without transfer: the complete condition of having once been held. The stone is smooth because a thumb is always moving across it. It remains in the niche because the hand is always taking it up. Every visitor who holds it supplies that hand without displacing the stone or becoming the occupant.

For a while afterward, the visitor may feel an extra set of fingers folded inside their own.

The water above the summit contains the largest life in the high country, if “contains” is the right relation. At times something appears far within the vertical-dark surface: not swimming, not submerged, but standing at an impossible depth. Its outline is vaguely human only because it is upright. The shoulders are mountain-broad. The head bends as though listening through the water to the stone below.

The vowel forms heard near the lower pools belong to it. Each pool is a small opening of its mouth. The great sound never reaches the end required to become a word.

It may be speaking the plateau into stillness.

It may be trying to pronounce a single name and finding that the name has no final sound.

Travelers do live there, though they do not arrive often and cannot properly leave. They are not immortal. Immortality requires duration. Instead they become held at the exact depth of their intrusion.

At first they remain themselves. Their hunger begins. Their wounds stay open without bleeding further. Their first footprint accompanies them, recurring beneath every step like a seal pressed repeatedly into the same moment. Breath hangs before their faces, yet every exhalation is the first cloud.

Later—if there can be a later—the plateau begins distributing them.

One version stands at the edge of a ravine. Another is visible on the opposite cliff, facing back. Another kneels outside a shelter listening to the drip. Another has reached the summit room and is touching the warm stone. None can approach the others because approach is the act that divides them. Each believes the distant figure to be someone already present when they came.

Their abandoned intentions persist more clearly than their bodies. A wish to rest may become a patch of rust-colored moss. A lifted hand may remain as five pale stalks in gravel. The thought of shelter may weather stones into reciprocal shapes. Fear may narrow into one of the dark beings between the balanced slabs.

The travelers themselves grow less distinct, not by fading but by becoming possible from too many directions. Eventually they may be seen only as the motionless figure across a valley: the one that seems to approach whenever approached, although neither body moves.

Those figures are perhaps the plateau’s true population.

They stand beside pools, beneath clouds, on inverted cliffs, within the dark water above the northern summit. Some are shaped like people. Some are white and small. Some are miles long and audible rather than visible. All are caught not between life and death, but between occurrence and result.

They are alive in the sense that something is happening to them.

They are still in the sense that it never finishes.
