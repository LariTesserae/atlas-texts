At first it seems that nothing lives there.

The streets are too open. The shelves beside the walls are bare. No faces turn toward the entrance seam, no bodies withdraw from the reddish light. There is no furtive movement among the chimneys, no scatter of small lives beneath slabs. The city presents all the evidence of habitation except an inhabitant.

Then one of the walls answers the heat.

A black seam running through its masonry brightens by degrees, from dull violet to the amber of old resin. A shutter opens three streets away. Pressure passes under the pavement with a noise like a sleeper drawing breath. On a rooftop, a cluster of ceramic vanes changes its angle. The motions are separated by distance and by several long moments, but they belong together. They are the gestures of one life whose body has been laid out as a district.

The inhabitants do not occupy the city. They occur through it.

What appears to be vegetation is at least partly citizenry: colonial beings of mineral shell and sulfurous interior, rooted in many places at once. A mature individual may possess a crown in a plaza, heat-catching fans above an oven court, sensory threads along a ceiling, and a buried web extending beneath dozens of houses. Between these visible organs run filaments finer than hair, fused into mortar joints or pressed between pavement slabs. Signals travel through them as pulses of charge, pressure, and chemical change. A brightness in one stalk may be answered by the contraction of fibers on the far side of a street.

Where one inhabitant ends and another begins is seldom obvious. Their networks meet, recoil, exchange minerals, or grow together. Some remain distinct for centuries, touching at only a few conductive knots. Others merge after damage and never wholly separate again. A tower struck by a thermal fracture may awaken connected to a neighboring courtyard, carrying fragments of two old patterns in one new body.

Their visible forms vary so widely that they might be mistaken for unrelated species.

There are low pavement colonies composed of overlapping plates, each plate no larger than a hand. They creep by building new shell at the leading edge and dissolving the rear. Their progress is measurable over years, but they are attentive on shorter scales: ember-colored margins darken when shadow falls, and whole patches tighten against an unexpected pressure change.

In sheltered recesses stand compact, many-jointed forms resembling stacks of scorched bowls. These gather mineral dust. They open when the air is still and close sharply during the city’s loudest breathing. Small objects are sometimes found gripped between their laminae—obsidian teeth, tablet fragments, lengths of vitrified fiber. Whether these are tools, nutrients, memories, or all three cannot be said.

The great plaza growths are more difficult to regard as plants. Each presents only a single trunk or fan to the open air, but beneath it lies an extensive thinking surface: a mesh of capillaries spread under the paving, sensitive to footfall-like vibration, electrical charge, and the minute cooling caused by a shadow. Their crowns are black-green and hard enough to ring when struck. Within, hot amber liquor rises through sealed channels. Narrow bands of changing color move through that liquor, linger at junctions, and divide.

The ceiling curtains may be their sense organs. Thousands of taut threads read the chamber’s breath: pressure, dust load, gas composition, the arrival of pollen, the bending of the salt crust overhead. When the gusts release them, they do not fall at once. They hold themselves in slight curves, as though retaining the shape of the air.

There may also be smaller inhabitants, if “smaller” has meaning for a life distributed across stone. Pale nodules grow inside ducts and valve chambers. They alter the pitch of the city’s organ note by fractions too subtle for an animal ear. The larger networks respond, opening some channels and sealing others. These nodules might be juveniles, specialist castes, parasites, or organs temporarily budded off to perform a task. Once their work is finished, many are reabsorbed. Others harden, detach, and remain in niches like rows of white teeth.

No inhabitant contains water. Their living medium is a family of dense sulfur compounds, oils and molten salts that remain stable in the chamber’s heat. It moves slowly through sealed vessels, carrying charge and suspended mineral grains. A wound does not bleed so much as glaze. The exposed fluid darkens, fumes, and sets into a glossy plug. New conductive strands are then laid through the plug one by one. Old injuries remain visible as bands of obsidian-black scar tissue, often stronger than the original shell.

Their skins are kilns in miniature. Layer after layer of silica, carbon, and salt is deposited from within, producing ceramic tissues with different uses: porous plates that breathe gas, reflective plates that reject heat, translucent windows over light-sensitive chambers, flexible hinges made from thousands of nested mineral scales. Growth is an act of masonry. Masonry is a form of healing.

This is why the oldest residents cannot easily be distinguished from buildings.

A retaining wall may be dead construction colonized by roots. It may be a living shell built around a temporary scaffold. It may be the mineralized remains of an inhabitant whose descendants still use its internal channels. Some houses show slow pulses of electrical light along their corners. Some towers seal fractures before the fracture can widen. In certain sleeping chambers, the shelves themselves warm and cool in long coordinated cycles, despite having no visible connection to a crown.

The builders, if they still exist, may be everywhere.

They do not walk now. Yet the city preserves the requirements of creatures that once changed position: handholds, low passages, work niches, processional lanes. During an earlier phase of life, perhaps the inhabitants condensed themselves into smaller, articulated bodies. Such bodies would have been light, dry, and jointed—bundles of mineral fiber enclosed in overlapping plates, with several gripping limbs arranged around a central heat core. They may have cut salt blocks, carried obsidian blades, wound braces around cracked walls, and returned at the end of a cycle to rooted beds.

The sleeping shelves suggest that they lay there.

Perhaps “sleep” was the interval during which a mobile body softened, connected its capillaries to sockets in the wall, and surrendered its gathered experience to the district beneath it. Perhaps some rose again. Others may have rooted permanently, their limbs widening into fans, their backs thickening into roof beams, their sensory fibers climbing toward the ceiling. A citizen’s final adulthood may have been architecture.

Or the apparent tools may be products of the same blind exactness by which roots sort minerals and wind sorts pollen. The city permits both readings and does not favor either.

Its inhabitants have no voices in the animal sense, but they communicate continuously. They use the chamber itself as throat, ear, and nervous system. A contracted fiber rings a bell. A warmed duct lowers its note. A row of seed vessels chatters in a pattern that changes with gas composition. Mineral membranes boom across courtyards, and the root networks answer with faint electrical flickers under the paving.

Meaning, if meaning is present, is enormous and slow. A warning may take an afternoon to cross the city. Recognition may require the repetition of a pressure pattern over several seasons. Some exchanges are quicker: when a roof slab shifts, nearby fibers contract at once, valves close, and growth around the damaged joint begins changing its chemistry. But other responses unfold across generations. A poorly shaped alley is narrowed by one layer of shell each year until, a century later, it produces the desired note.

Their memory is built rather than remembered.

They record heat cycles in laminated tissue. They preserve chemical histories in sealed resin chambers. Stacks of tablets contain grooves too fine and repetitive to be decoration, filled with mineral mixtures that alter conductivity when warmed. To read such a tablet may mean placing it in a wall socket and allowing pulses to pass through it. A record is not looked at but incorporated temporarily into the body. Some tablets may be laws. Some may be maps of vanished airflow. Some may contain the equivalent of names: characteristic sequences by which a scattered being recognizes its own separated parts.

Individuality among them may depend less on flesh than on rhythm. One inhabitant is the pattern that opens seven shutters in order. Another is the pulse traveling every dusk-equivalent from a deep fissure to a translucent rooftop fan. If two networks merge physically but preserve separate rhythms, they may remain two citizens. If one rhythm is lost, a body spanning half the city may be considered dead even while all its tissues continue growing.

Death leaves few corpses.

The sulfur medium is drained or reclaimed. Conductive fibers go inert. Ceramic tissues remain as walls, braces, pipes, paving, and tools. Descendants grow through the vacant channels. Nothing useful is buried, and nothing can decay in the familiar way. The dead become infrastructure. The very oldest streets may be paved with generations of inhabitants reduced to plates, each still bearing microscopic branching patterns where living fluid once passed.

Reproduction comes during the city’s violent flowering, but pollination is only one part of it. The dry grains thrown through the streets carry mineral shells, chemical catalysts, and tiny variations in conductive pattern. The city sorts them by weight, charge, shape, and tolerance for heat. Compatible grains collect in sockets along the galleries, where rooted tissues extend hair-fine threads to test them.

Most are rejected and burned empty by the hot stone.

A few are enclosed.

For years they may remain no more than a dark film within a socket. Then a cluster of plates appears around the rim. Filaments enter the wall. A young pattern begins sounding through nearby ducts—not yet coherent, repeating fragments borrowed from every network that nourishes it. Its first body is a place. Its first acts are to regulate a slit of air, dissolve a few grains of salt, and learn the daily compression of the ceiling.

Thus their children are born already joined to the city, and must slowly become distinct from it.

The inhabitants seem patient because their bodies are slow, but they are not passive. They compete for gradients, interrupt one another’s circuits, shade rival crowns, and alter airflow in their own favor. Bare corridors may be civic safeguards against the most aggressive networks. The trimming of growth along plazas may be maintenance performed by specialized colonies—or the visible edge of an ancient territorial agreement.

There are signs of conflict. Some wall filaments end in glassy knots where opposing chemical fronts met. Certain sealed courts contain blackened growths surrounded by rings of insulating salt. One district has been cut away from the rest by trenches so exact that they cannot be accidental. Yet even there, bridges are beginning to form: thin conductive arcs laid down grain by grain across the isolation gaps.

Whatever disputes they have, none can survive alone. Every inhabitant depends upon arrangements older than itself. One keeps a chimney open; another stiffens the roof around it. A deep network brings sulfur compounds upward. A shallow mat gathers electrical charge. Ceiling threads sense pressure and trigger vanes that scatter another being’s pollen. The city is their ecology, but also their collective body and inherited instrument.

At the hottest hour, they become most plainly alive.

The ceiling bears down. Gas surges through the ducts. The whole chamber sounds.

Black fans rotate into position. Threads snap taut. Seams open along stalks and walls, showing mirrored interiors. Amber fluids climb against gravity. Charge gathers on needle forests until every edge shines. Beneath the paving, pulses race through the networks, crossing old joins and dead districts, waking valves that may not have opened since the previous season.

For a few moments, distinctions collapse. Plant, citizen, ruin, and machine act together. The city draws one immense breath and turns its streets into a reproductive organ.

Pollen leaps in pale fire.

Then the pressure eases. The needles darken. Vanes relax. Shutters fall closed one after another, receding into the distance. The inhabitants return to their distributed silences: a crown in a plaza, a glow beneath a wall, a tension held in a ceiling thread, a patient intelligence moving through stone too slowly to be seen.

To an animal observer, the city remains empty.

To the city, emptiness is one of the things that lives there.
