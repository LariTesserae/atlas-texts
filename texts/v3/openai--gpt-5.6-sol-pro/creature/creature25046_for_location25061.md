The first inhabitants one notices are not necessarily the smallest, but the ones that make the mistake of moving.

They are low, many-legged things with backs like river stones: umber, rust-red, smoke-gray, sometimes crazed with pale mineral lines. Their armor grows asymmetrically, conforming to the hollows in which they spend their early years. An adult lifted from its resting place—something almost never achieved without injury—has the shape of that place impressed upon it. One side may be flattened against a wall, another cupped around an ancient pebble. Its underside is soft and cool, crowded with short legs, drinking folds, sensory threads, and the tiny scavengers that live among them.

These plated grazers form much of what appears to be gravel. They feed without visibly lowering their heads. Moisture drawn beneath the shell loosens a film of lichen and mineral-fed growth; rasping mouthparts then travel slowly over the stone. A feeding animal may require a day to clear an area no larger than a thumbnail. Behind it, the rock is left faintly brighter, marked with parallel grooves that will darken again over years.

They rarely touch bare water. Before dawn they extend pale brushes from under their armor and sweep dew toward hidden grooves along the belly. Each droplet passes through several mouths. Large adults drink first only in the sense that water lands on them first; much of it is carried by channels to their young, clustered beneath the rear plates. What remains is taken by mites, thread-worms, and colorless, jointed cleaners living in the damp shelter of the parent’s body.

The cleaners are abundant enough to have populations of their own. Some eat shed tissue and fungal growth. Some consume the eggs of their competitors. Some have ceased cleaning altogether and pierce the thin skin between plates, but even these parasites are tolerated in small numbers because their movements keep the breathing folds from sealing with dust. A healthy grazer is therefore not an individual so much as a slow-moving refuge, bearing several generations of other lives.

Among the gravel herds move the hinge-beaks.

They stand on six stiff limbs and resemble arrangements of dry thorn until the front half opens. Their heads consist of two narrow shields hinged along the crown. When spread, the inner surfaces expose a damp, dark membrane scented like rain. Minute grazers approach to drink from it or investigate the mineral odor. The shields then close—not quickly, but with such inevitability that speed is unnecessary.

A hinge-beak may feed only a few times in a season. Between meals it anchors its feet in cracks and lets itself become part of the local pattern. Lichen grows on its outer shields. Insects shelter between its legs. Some individuals remain in one posture so long that the stone beneath them preserves a six-pointed stain.

Their young are less patient. Newly divided from translucent capsules, they move over the rock on hair-fine legs and hunt among lichen islands. At this age they have no hinged head, only a narrow probing tube. With each molt the tube broadens and splits, until the restless hunter becomes the stationary mouth.

The translucent-winged insects belong to many unrelated kinds, though from a distance they look like one pale haze. Most spend almost their entire lives sealed in cracks as waxy, eyeless grubs. There they graze on moisture-fed membranes or drink from the roots of succulents. Wings appear only in the final days. A whole basin may hatch together when rain cools the stone past a precise temperature.

The adults rise, circle once or twice, exchange packets of eggs and mineral-rich fluid, and descend. Many have no working digestive tract. Their brief flight is powered by fat accumulated over years underground. Afterward the wings detach along prepared seams and continue blowing across the plateau, flashing in sunlight long after the insects themselves have crawled beneath stones to die.

Certain smaller creatures wait specifically for this fall. Wing-shearers, no bigger than grit, climb the reeds after rain and catch drifting wings in combed forelimbs. They do not eat the tissue. They carry it underground and laminate it with saliva into watertight cells, each lined with hundreds of eggs. Their colonies form the hollow towers on exposed shelves.

No single wing-shearer understands a tower. Each responds to dampness, heat, the scent of brood, and the angle of light at the chamber mouth. One brings clay. Another removes it. A third coats the resulting edge with chewed shell. Their conflicting labors produce walls, vents, shaded galleries, and deep condensation shafts. In older towers, air passing through the chambers draws dew down into internal cisterns.

The colony has no permanent queen. Reproduction passes through the population as a slow physiological weather. Individuals nearest the cool center become egg-bearers; those warmed along the outer wall become builders and foragers. If one side of a tower collapses, the altered temperature changes the bodies of its occupants before repairs begin. The architecture reshapes the colony, and the colony reshapes the architecture.

Many towers outlive the species that raised them. Their deeper chambers contain squat descendants that no longer possess wings or eyes and cannot survive outside the regulated damp. Nearer the surface live unrelated tenants: shell-cleaners, needle predators, sleeping rain-grubs, and small red succulents whose roots penetrate the walls and drink from the nursery cisterns. The oldest structures are less like nests than vertical ecosystems inherited by strangers.

Along the pale migration roads travel the ribbon herds.

A ribbon is composed of animals each about the length of a finger, soft beneath overlapping dorsal tiles. They lock nose to tail so closely that a line of thousands appears continuous. The foremost animal follows old chemical traces and subtle differences in the polish of the stone. Those behind follow warmth and pressure. When the leader dies, the next feeds on its softened tissues without breaking formation and assumes the exposed position.

At midday the ribbon coils into layered mats. The outer animals dry and harden, protecting those within; by dusk they exchange places through slow internal rearrangements. Eggs pass inward, toward the coolest part of the coil. Hatchlings emerge there and may spend years moving through the living braid before reaching its surface.

A migrating ribbon advances little more than a body length on a favorable night. It can take generations to cross one terrace. Routes fork, merge, and sometimes loop back on themselves. A herd trapped on a circular road may travel for centuries without reaching new water, sustained by dew and by the dead incorporated into its own length.

The pool chains have their own inhabitants. Blind paddlers fill the water column, each with a broad head-shield and dozens of leaflike limbs. They swim by turning slowly end over end, straining microbes, eggs, and dissolved organic matter from the water. Their backs carry bubbles held beneath transparent skin. As a pool shrinks, these bubbles enlarge, making the paddlers buoyant until the surface seems paved with silver blisters.

Beneath them are mud-borers, translucent and nearly liquid, which maintain tunnels through the saturated sediment. Above them stand the reed mimics: long-necked hunters balanced on three or five narrow legs. Their bodies are ringed like dry stalks and often support actual films of algae. A reed mimic can remain fixed through an entire wet season. It feeds by lowering a tubular mouth into the crowd and drawing up whatever fits, without striking or chasing.

Their courtship is conducted by shadow. One animal shifts by a fraction, laying its silhouette across another. The recipient darkens the corresponding bands of its own body. Over several days the two exchange slowly changing patterns as the sun crosses them. Eggs are then placed inside hollow dead reeds, one per segment.

Under pool ledges hang the breathing colonies. Each is a cluster of thin-walled sacs sharing a rootlike base. At cool hours the sacs inflate with oxygen released by symbiotic growths inside them. Small swimmers enter through valves and graze those inner films. Some are digested. Others clean the chamber and leave. At noon the sacs collapse, forcing stored air down through the base into oxygen-poor mud, where a second community lives around the buried tissues.

When the water vanishes, most pool creatures do not die in the ordinary sense. Paddlers fold their limbs into the hollows of their shields. Mud-borers knot themselves around their eggs. Breathing sacs drain into hard beads. All secrete films that bind them to one another, and the pool becomes a dry mosaic of sealed bodies.

Within this pavement metabolism falls almost to nothing. A creature may wake after one year or fifty. Some wake into a world populated by their remote descendants. Some never wake but remain useful: their sealed chambers hold moisture, their tissues feed hatchlings, and their shells preserve pockets of coolness beneath the mud.

In the clefts live paler beings.

The ceiling drinkers resemble loose bundles of thread. They hang from hooked plates, extending filaments into the path of condensation. Each filament is grooved, carrying droplets upward by capillary force to a central stomach. They have no eyes and little distinction between limb and gut. If a filament is severed, it may crawl away, attach elsewhere, and slowly become a complete animal.

Curtains of shell overlap below them. Between the shells pulse colonies of thumb-sized sacs, each connected to its neighbors by buried cords. A colony tightens when touched, passing the contraction outward until an entire wall draws inward. This is how alarm moves through the ravines. Predators feel the pressure change and close their mouths; prey flatten themselves; ceiling drinkers reel in their threads. Silence spreads physically.

The predators are called mouths only because little else can be seen. They occupy cracks and line the openings with flexible lips patterned like stone. Their bodies extend deep into the rock as branching digestive cords. Different openings several paces apart may belong to the same animal. Anything brushing one lip is held until neighboring mouths can bend toward it. Digestion occurs externally, in the shared hollow between them.

Some of these crevice predators grow old enough to lose track of their own extent. Parts become isolated by collapse or dryness and continue as separate creatures. Others merge where their buried branches meet. In the deepest damp places there may be no meaningful way to count them.

The great domed animals of the open plateau are related, distantly, to the small plated grazers. Their many armor plates have fused into broad, irregular shields. Dust fills the seams. Lichen covers the oldest surfaces, then succulents, then whole communities of insects and shell-mites. Their eyes are simple light-sensitive pits hidden around the lower rim, where the body meets the ground.

A dome feeds downward. Beneath its body is a circular field of soft grazing tissue that dissolves films from the rock and drinks moisture trapped in pores. Once an area is exhausted, the animal waits. New growth accumulates beneath it, nourished by its waste and protected from the sun. It may remain over the same patch for years, cultivating what it will later consume.

Exceptional rain wakes the deeper muscles. The rim lifts. Hundreds of short legs unfold in sequence, each broad and pale from disuse. The dome advances so slowly that animals living on its back may complete a breeding cycle without recognizing the movement. Beneath it, organisms adapted to permanent darkness are exposed. Some die. Others follow in the moving shade. The bare oval left behind quickly becomes a nursery for sun-loving species that cannot colonize weathered stone.

The domes reproduce rarely. Two may converge over decades, guided by mineral scents washed along the migration roads. They do not mate directly. Instead each leaves a mass of living tissue in the same sheltered basin. The masses fuse, exchange cells, and harden into dozens of small, wrinkled hatchlings. These remain clustered for years, indistinguishable from stones except during the coolest hour before dawn, when they lift together to drink.

Nothing on the plateau lives entirely alone. The smallest creatures occupy films on larger creatures; the largest depend upon growths too thin to see. Predators shelter their prey from drought. Parasites keep breathing surfaces clear. The dead become walls, roads, cisterns, and new stone. Even the apparently solitary reed mimic carries mites in its joints, algae in its skin, eggs in its hollow legs, and a thread-worm curled around its heart.

Their lives are slow, but not peaceful. Eggs are stolen. Bodies are pierced. Colonies smother one another by increments. Whole populations perish when a dew channel shifts the width of a finger. Yet violence here is seldom sudden. Hunger takes days to close. Pursuit lasts generations. One species may defeat another by shading it for a century.

They are restrained because restraint is the condition of abundance. Water is passed from shell to mouth, from mouth to nursery, from nursery to parasite, from corpse to stone. Heat is stored in armor and shared by contact. Information travels through pressure, scent, and imperceptible color. The plateau’s multitudes survive not by escaping its austerity, but by fitting themselves into every remaining interval.

Seen closely, each inhabitant appears ancient, deliberate, and complete.

Seen longer, each is only a temporary chamber through which the plateau’s scant moisture moves.
