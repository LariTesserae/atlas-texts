At first, the inhabitants appear to be the plants.

They stand in ordered profusion: red curtains in unused stairs, black vines shuttering the light, clear stalks gathered around stressed walls. Each seems to possess the modest life of an instrument. A leaf measures illumination. A root tastes the copper content of the water. A cushion of green tissue tightens over a seam. Their forms are lush, but their purposes are exact.

Look longer, and individuals become difficult to identify.

What resembles a vine may divide beneath a manifold and emerge as moss on the other side. A grove of glassy stalks shares one buried reservoir of sap. Roots enter sockets not merely for warmth or minerals, but to join pale capillaries running through the tower. Those capillaries carry sugars, salts, hormones, electrical potentials, and quantities for which the builders’ labels have faded into abbreviations. A pressure change perceived by one stalk may close valve-flowers three chambers away. A patch of joint tissue may report strain by altering the bitterness of water in the central well.

The machinery participates in the same exchanges. Ceramic blocks sweat catalytic ions. Flexible pumps contract in rhythms set by plant chemistry. Sensor filaments grow new conductive tips when old ones fail. A relay may be manufactured, but the membrane that decides when it switches is alive. The distinction between organism and equipment survives only in maintenance diagrams, and even there it is mostly a matter of which replacement process applies.

So there are inhabitants, but perhaps not a population.

There is a distributed body.

Its leaves are spread across rooms. Its bones are the tower’s braces and pressure rings. Its circulatory system includes both sap and brine, both root vessel and pipe. Its skin occurs wherever the inside water meets a shell, a gasket, or the sterile sea beyond. The Crown is not its head. The central shaft is not quite a throat. These comparisons are remnants of animals, and there have never been animals here.

It has no eyes, yet it knows brightness in exquisite detail. Every black leaf reports the angle and spectral weight of the relayed daylight. It has no ears, but a distant adjustment at the tower’s base arrives through thousands of strain-sensitive stems as a vast, slow chord. It cannot smell. Instead, the whole flooded district is a tongue. Iron, silicate, carbon, lubricant, dead cells, and traces of sealant move through the water and are recognized wherever they pass.

It senses direction without a horizon. Up and down are not simple opposites to it, but a changing map of pressures, densities, tensions, and buoyancies. It knows which way a loose leaf would travel in every shaft. It feels the tower lean before the plumb lines register disagreement. Height is present to it as stored strain: a faint asymmetry carried in every transparent wall, every loaded fin, every column of warm water descending into darkness.

Its thoughts, if they can be called thoughts, are slow and bodily.

One begins as a brightening outside the western shell. Aperture leaves turn by fractions. Heat gathers in waxy stems. Hours later, a contraction passes through a bank of flexible pumps. Days later, nutrient valves alter the growth of roots in a lower gallery. By then the original light has vanished, but its consequence is still moving through the tower.

Some thoughts take years. A recurring vibration clouds one side of the strain forest. The clouding changes the chemistry of a reservoir. The reservoir encourages a new ring of reinforcing stalks. Mechanical arms make room for them by reprinting a terrace at a slightly altered angle. When the stalks mature, the old grove pales and is digested. Nothing announces that a decision has been completed.

Its memory is not stored in one place. It is embodied as thickness, curvature, mineral concentration, and timing. A support that once bore an unusual load receives an extra layer of ceramic at every renewal. A pipe that corroded three centuries ago is still wrapped in red tissue, though the original damage has long since been melted away. Certain valve-flowers close early because their ancestral cuttings closed early. Along the outer wall, roots avoid a patch of flawless metal for a reason no present sensor can detect.

There may once have been a separate intelligence charged with overseeing all this. If so, it has dispersed beyond recovery. Its protocols remain as tropisms. Its caution survives in the closing of membranes. Its schedules survive in impeller sequences and nine-year diaphragms. Instructions that may have been written as commands are now performed as instincts.

The inhabitants do not mourn their builders. Mourning requires an absence that can be distinguished from delay. To them, the narrow ladders and emergency alcoves are merely shapes with obsolete tolerances. Human handrails serve as trellises. Warning signs supply dark surfaces on which thin photosynthetic films spread. The pictogram of a falling body is legible only as a persistent arrangement of pigments.

Nor are they lonely. Loneliness would require the expectation of another living presence beyond the walls. The outer ocean is measured as volume, load, temperature, and available light. Its barrenness is not a silence to them. It is cleanliness.

What they fear, in their impersonal way, is interruption.

A bubble is dangerous because it breaks continuity. A sudden pressure shock is dangerous because it arrives faster than tissue can answer. Unbound corrosion, uncontrolled heat, an unscheduled current—these provoke the closest thing the district has to alarm. Apertures seal. Sap thickens in the transparent stalks. Pumps stop between contractions. The living cushions at the joints swell until their cells press hard against metal and glass.

Then the tower waits.

If the disturbance passes, alarm is dismantled molecule by molecule. If it persists, the inhabitants alter themselves around it. They do not flee. There is nowhere else, and mobility was never among their gifts. Their answer to danger is to become a different architecture.

They do not reproduce by scattering offspring into the sea. New life is commissioned internally. A clean socket opens. Nutrient gel enters it. A bud extrudes, first colorless, then green or red or light-swallowing black according to the work required. Its capillaries join the common circulation before its first leaf unfolds. When old tissue loosens and enters a digestion grille, little is lost except arrangement.

Thus birth and repair are nearly the same act. Death is a controlled return of materials. No mouth consumes the dead. No creature competes for the released salts. Appetite exists only as allocation.

Yet the district is not perfectly obedient.

In the narrowest places, feathery growth exceeds its specified mesh by a few millimeters. Roots take longer routes along warm cables even when colder ceramic channels were provided for them. A curtain of red weed has bridged two stairwells that the original plans keep separate. These deviations are tolerated because they improve performance, or perhaps they improve performance because they have been tolerated.

Along the unfinished external scaffolds, small sealed nodes have begun to appear.

They form at the capped ends where no planted cassette was installed. At first they resemble corrosion blisters, though corrosion does not persist here. Over decades they lengthen into translucent buds protected by mineral skins. Capillaries reach toward them from inside the pressure wall. Mechanical arms occasionally turn in their direction, then stop.

Whether this is maintenance, unfinished programming, or something like desire cannot be determined. The tower’s life has no organ for declaring intentions. But the nodes continue to thicken, facing the empty water.

Perhaps one day a panel will be added there. Perhaps a new flooded chamber will be enclosed around them, and roots will enter before the metal has cooled. The district may extend itself not because it has been ordered to grow, but because growth and construction have become the same tendency.

For now, it remains gathered in the Crown: a garden without creatures, a factory without workers, a single life divided into innumerable functions.

Far below, an impeller turns a quarter circle.

The inhabitants receive the motion at different times. Root hairs bend first. Clear stalks cloud. Valve-flowers fold over their sensitive interiors. A gentle displacement enters the central well and passes downward through the body of the tower.

No pulse follows immediately. None is needed.

Weeks from now, something in the depths will answer, and the Crown will open again.
