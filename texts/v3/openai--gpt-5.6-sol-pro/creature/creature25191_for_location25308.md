From a high tramway, the inhabitants of the Underleaf are easy to mistake for another migratory species.

They cross the open floors in narrow, orderly streams, keeping to the worn paths between columns. Their coats make them look broad-backed and dark. Lamps glint from tool clasps, breathing cages, polished faceplates. At intersections the streams divide without shouted warning. A hand lifts; a shoulder-frill changes color; guide-lights answer in the paving. Then the movement closes again.

Only nearby does their variety become apparent.

They are not tall. The immense chambers have produced no corresponding grandeur in their bodies. Most adults stand somewhat shorter than the doorways of the oldest districts, though this may be because those doorways have been closing around them for generations. They are compact, low-centered, and difficult to knock from their feet. Their legs bend at two visible joints, with broad, splayed soles that feel pressure changes through floors and rails. In a quiet building, a person knows who is approaching by the cadence underfoot long before seeing them.

Their skin has the subdued finish of unglazed ceramic. It ranges from ivory and smoke-grey to resin brown, blue-black, and the muted green of cut stems. It is not moist. Fine overlapping scales keep volatile fluids from escaping, while softer folds at the throat, inner arms, and abdomen are protected beneath clothing. Age shows first as a network of pale seams around the joints. Very old people appear delicately crazed, as though fired and cooled too many times.

They have faces, but the face is not the whole instrument of expression. The eyes are large and deeply hooded against drifting ethers, with reflective inner membranes that flash silver under ceiling fronds. Their mouths are narrow, made for slicing soft sheets and drawing oils from food bladders. Speech is possible, but expensive: it requires forcing precious vapor across dry vocal reeds. In public, most people speak softly and sparingly.

On either side of the neck lie folded sensory vanes. At rest they resemble layered gills or decorative collars. When opened, they detect vibration, pressure, airborne chemistry, and the minute electrical changes preceding a growth concussion. Embarrassment draws them tight. Alarm spreads them flat. Attention tilts them toward the floor.

Children have little control over these vanes and are therefore considered incapable of secrecy.

Much conversation takes place through gesture, light, and scent. A worker’s cuff may emit a trace of bitter resin to request clearance; a clerk answers with two green pulses from a finger lamp. Close companions develop private pressure-rhythms tapped through tables, walls, or joined wrists. Names are spoken when necessary, but each person also has a registered scent-mark: a precise compound worn on a porous bead beneath the throat. Legal documents are signed by chemistry.

To forge a scent-mark is possible, but difficult to conceal. A forgery may fool a machine while offending everyone in the room.

Their internal fluids are waxes, ionic oils, and suspended mineral gels. Warmth makes them quick-minded and dangerously wasteful; cold makes them slow but enduring. They can remain motionless for hours in an unheated chamber, metabolism drawn inward, watching a pressure gauge or waiting for a wall to move. After strenuous labor they do not sweat. Heat lifts from the plates along their backs in a faint aromatic haze. Factory crews wear condensers beneath their coats to capture and return what would otherwise be lost.

They drink no water. Most have never seen it outside a sealed teaching ampoule.

They feed on pressed starch, cultured pith, fungal proteins, mineral salts, and clarified oils. Meals are small, dense, and ceremonial chiefly because eating exposes soft mouth tissue to the air. Families gather around warming stones and unwrap their food from barkskin membranes: translucent slices, black seed paste, curls of smoked fiber, bulbs filled with sharp green fat. Flavor is perceived as much by the neck-vanes as by the tongue. A well-prepared dish is said to have a shape in the air.

Water has no place at the table, even in metaphor. They do not say that grief flows, that time is a river, or that memory runs deep. Grief closes. Time grows around things. Memory is a scar retained by living tissue.

The people of the Underleaf are born in broodhouses, though not grown by the city organism itself. Their young develop in warm resin cradles whose nutrient compounds are replenished by parents, relatives, or civic attendants. Newborns are soft, almost translucent, with limbs tucked tightly against the body and sensory vanes sealed. For their first year they are carried in insulated wraps close to an adult’s heat plates.

Parenthood is less a matter of pairing than of maintaining a cradle. A household is defined by those who have contributed substance, labor, warmth, or registered attention to a child’s development. Some households contain two adults; some contain twenty. Refinery districts favor large communal broods because shift work makes private care impractical. Migrant frontier towns keep cradles in shock-mounted wagons and teach children to sleep through the groaning of unfinished columns.

Their first public rite comes when a child is old enough to feel the city’s pulse through bare feet. An elder leads them onto an unpadded section of floor during the dark interval. They wait without lamps. Eventually the distant metabolism travels up through the soles: one immense, nearly imperceptible contraction. The child is asked where it came from.

There is no correct answer. The point is to learn that every direction can contain a consequence.

Underleaf clothing is layered, asymmetrical, and made to be repaired. Bast-fiber coats close tightly over the fluid-soft parts of the body. Stiff shoulder pieces protect the sensory vanes in crowds. Workers add ceramic knee guards, copper grounding chains, filter veils, and belts crowded with wedges, sealants, scent tags, inspection mirrors, and lengths of pale cord. Bright colors are rare in ordinary cloth because color itself carries public meaning. Blue warns of forbidden reagents. Amber announces solvent exposure. White belongs to medics, germination keepers, and those entering a building that has begun to change unexpectedly.

Status is shown less by ornament than by silence. The wealthy can afford quiet machinery, thick pith floors, and rooms far from rail vibration. Their homes contain pauses: blank walls, unlit alcoves, unused chambers preserved against closure. Poor districts are not necessarily noisy, but they tremble. Pumps, freight lines, and induction collars send constant information through the floors. Residents learn to sleep in a thicket of other people’s labor.

Professions grow directly from the city’s uncertainty.

Vein-readers are engineers, diagnosticians, and something like diviners, though they object to the last word. They map visible conduits, sample electrical pulses, compare rates of healing, and study the minute orientation of fibers around proposed cuts. The best can place a palm against a wall and tell whether it is load-bearing, feeding a distant organ, preparing to bud, or merely remembering an old wound. Their calculations are rigorous. Their conclusions are often not.

Wound architects cultivate habitable scars. They work slowly, sometimes on projects that will outlive them. A room is not considered complete when opened but when its rate of closure has become predictable. Architects leave growth margins around doors and windows, and their plans include the shapes a building will assume after ten, fifty, and two hundred years. A celebrated structure may be admired not for resisting the living column but for becoming unusable beautifully.

Closer-gangs maintain old neighborhoods. They shave calluses from windows, brace narrowing halls, harvest stair-buds, and mark rooms that must be surrendered. Their work is intimate and mournful. A family may wake to find a thin white membrane across a childhood doorway: the building has begun to reclaim it. Nothing is demolished casually. Furniture is removed, wall veins are sealed, and the room is allowed to fill.

Frontier detachments live nearest the active margins. They tend to be leaner, heavily insulated, and impatient with metropolitan caution. Their houses have root-hooks and smooth undersides. Every possession has a transit latch. Children learn evacuation routes before scent-script. When the floor rises or a valley begins to seal, an entire town may loosen itself overnight and slide along greased tracks to the next hardened chamber.

Refinery people carry a permanent bitter trace from filtering sap vapor. Tram workers develop unusually sensitive feet. Garden persuaders dust their hands with hormonal compounds and often wear finger caps to prevent accidental expression in domestic walls. Burrower tenders keep glass-backed animals in warm pockets and release them into sick buildings. Hushcat wardens patrol upper ribs with mirrored poles, reading the minute breaks in texture where a predator has flattened itself against the wood.

The non-sapient animals are not separate from civic life.

The pale browsers are herded through districts where new films must be trimmed. They respond poorly to sound but readily to scent lines painted on the floor. Their mineral teeth grow continuously and are collected after shedding for use in cutters and surgical rasps. A browser that repeatedly chooses the wrong path may indicate a chemical leak before any instrument detects it.

Courier moths belong partly to the fronds and partly to the postal service. Clerks raise them in dark cabinets lined with destination scents. They carry pollen, machine codes, legal seals, and intimate messages too delicate for the electrical network. During mating season they become unreliable, favoring luminous ceilings over municipal duty, and whole bureaucracies slow.

Glass-backed burrowers are household nuisances until ventilation fails. Then their soft chewing inside a wall becomes the most comforting sound in the building. Children mark their moving shadows with chalk and give them names. It is considered unlucky to kill one while it is traveling upward.

Hushcats are feared, protected, and occasionally worshipped. They cannot be domesticated, but old transit stations sometimes acquire one that learns freight schedules and ignores people. Workers leave rendered fat on upper ledges in exchange for fewer attacks on draft animals. A hushcat shedding is valuable: its deadwood-colored skin can be laminated into camouflage cloth. Possessing a fresh skin without a warden’s seal is a serious crime.

There are smaller lives as well. Static mites cluster in warm coat seams and make the wearer’s gestures sparkle. Resin worms clean pressure vessels after shutdown. Paper-thin scavengers fold themselves into cracks when lamps brighten. In abandoned rooms, colonies of needle creatures arrange their bodies around leaking current and resemble frost.

The inhabitants distinguish none of these absolutely from machinery. A pump may contain cultured valves. A tram brake may be an animal muscle fed from a wax cartridge. A ventilation colony may be licensed as civic equipment. The question is not whether a thing is alive, but whether it can suffer, whether it can consent, and who is responsible when it grows beyond its assigned shape.

Their politics turns repeatedly on this last question.

Some believe the Underleaf is a gift: a new organ offering chambers, energy, and endless habitable space. They call themselves the Accommodants and argue that the city should listen more closely, cut less, and allow streets to follow the organism’s intentions.

Others believe the hollowing is disease. To them, the open chambers are lesions and the city is opportunistic life inside a wound. They monitor distant pulses for signs of fever, collapse, or immune response. Their public buildings are detachable.

A third position holds that health and illness are concepts too small for something several kilometers thick and perhaps continental in extent. The organism may be transforming rather than sick, and the city may be neither parasite nor partner, only one temporary tissue among others.

This belief has produced the Underleaf’s most characteristic religion. Its temples are empty chambers left unscored and unfurnished. Worshippers enter barefoot, extinguish their lights, and wait to feel what the structure is doing. There are no sermons. Sometimes the wall buds during a service, which can be interpreted as blessing, warning, coincidence, or an instruction to evacuate.

Death returns a person to the civic metabolism. Bodies are not buried; there is nowhere to bury them, and exposed water-bearing decay would be catastrophic. Instead they are cooled, desiccated, separated into stable oils and mineral ash, and given back according to the deceased’s contracts. One person may become lamp fuel for a public archive. Another enters a broodhouse reserve. Some request that their salts be pressed into black germination pegs and set along a future street.

The dead are therefore said to continue as infrastructure.

This is not regarded as grim. To remain useful without remaining awake is considered a gentle end.

What the people fear more is unplanned continuation: spores in the joints, uncontrolled budding, a personality altered by solvent loss, a body surviving after its scent-memory has vanished. Their medicine is precise and cold. Physicians seal wounds with compatible wax, replace damaged ionic fluid, and graft thin cultured plates over fractures. Hospitals smell of citrus metal and extinguished lamps. No water is used, even for cleaning; instruments pass through hot oil, ether vapor, and abrasive salt.

In private, the inhabitants are less restrained than their public quiet suggests. They dance by stamping complex patterns into sprung floors, whole halls answering underfoot. They compose music for light shutters, fragrance organs, and tuned structural beams whose notes are felt through the skeleton rather than heard. Comedies rely on involuntary neck-vanes, badly forged scent signatures, and buildings closing at socially inconvenient moments.

Their loudest festivals occur without sound.

Once each metabolic year, when the civic squares bloom silver, people gather in reflective masks. They walk through the cool petals until every motion scatters light onto the columns. Courier moths are released without tags. Children paint harmless blue droplets on one another and shriek in whispered scandal. At the final dimming, everyone stands still while sweepers harvest the square around their feet.

Then the lamps warm. Freight resumes. The petals become insulation. Nothing is wasted, not even celebration.

They do not think of themselves as underground. Underground would imply another, more rightful surface above them. The mineral crust is merely the outer rind, sterile and distant. Their world is here: in the interval between ribs, under breathing fronds, among cultivated wounds and unfinished horizons.

Nor do they consider the city enclosed. New chambers are always opening. Old ones are always closing. Distance is measured not only in kilometers but in growth states: hardened country, trembling country, country not yet hollow.

A citizen of the Underleaf lives with the knowledge that every room is temporary, though some will last longer than dynasties; that every road presses a memory into living tissue; that the floor beneath a sleeping child may be the surface of an organ still deciding what to become.

They are a careful people, but not a timid one.

They wear their warmth close. They speak in light and pressure. They build by wounding and maintain by apology. They herd quiet animals beneath white leaves, distill fuel from the bloodless sap of their world, and send their dead forward as lamps along roads that have not yet grown.
