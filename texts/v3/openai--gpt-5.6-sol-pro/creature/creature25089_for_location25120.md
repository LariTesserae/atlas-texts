At first it appears that nothing lives in the court except what has been planted there.

The ferns tremble because everything trembles. Algae stream because water passes over them. Moss swells, blackens, and releases clear beads in response to heat and condensation. Even when a dark shape shifts beneath the eastern growth, the movement can be attributed to the machinery. The whole court is full of motions that do not signify intention.

Only after watching for a long time does one movement fail to repeat.

A frond bends against the current of vibration. Something behind it unfolds.

The inhabitant is approximately human in height, though it seldom stands fully upright. Its habitual posture is a low, careful stoop, one hand near the paving and the narrow head advanced between the shoulders. This makes it seem smaller than it is. When it rises to reach a grille or inspect a pipe seam, its limbs reveal themselves as disconcertingly long. It can touch the overhead service rail without climbing the steps. It can cross from one dry joint in the paving to another without putting a foot into the basin.

Its proportions are not an imitation of a person. They are an accommodation to a place constructed for people: ladders, handrails, valve housings, inspection gaps. Whatever first shaped it had learned those measurements thoroughly.

Its surface resembles several materials at once. Across the back and upper arms, the skin is plated with overlapping gray scales, dull as unglazed ceramic. Along the flanks it is softer and nearly translucent, with dark vascular threads visible beneath. The lower legs are green-black from immersion, and small liverwort leaves grow in the creases behind the knees. Mineral water has deposited pale rings around its wrists and throat. These accumulate slowly, then split away in thin curved pieces that are easily mistaken for flakes of old pipe lining.

The head has no visible ears. There are shallow sealed depressions where ears might have been, but sound in the court is too abundant to be useful in the ordinary way. Instead, the inhabitant receives vibration through its feet, palms, jaw, and ribs. When it pauses, it often presses the flat of one hand against the wall and opens its mouth slightly. The pose resembles listening, though it is feeling the machinery through bone.

The roar is not noise to it. It is structure.

It distinguishes the neighboring pumps as separate steady masses. It knows the fine pulse of each impeller and the long electrical undertone of the turbine hall. The regular fall over the spillway passes through its breast at a frequency to which its breathing has adapted. A human voice has almost no place in this sensory field. Shouting at it produces little response. A boot striking a handrail, however, may cause its head to turn from the far end of the court.

Its eyes are small, clear, and colorless. In direct noon light they contract until they resemble droplets trapped beneath glass. They seem poorly suited to distance, but they are sensitive to changes in reflection. The creature watches the basin rather than the objects around it. A leaf landing on the water, a new disturbance around the sump, or the thin rainbow cast by leaked oil will draw its attention immediately.

Its hands are broad, with five long fingers joined by shallow webs. The nails are not claws. They are hard, copper-colored edges used for scraping mineral crust, parting root mats, and lifting algae from the red grilles. The feet are wider still, their undersides soft and finely ridged. They spread its weight so completely that it can move through the ankle-deep margins without raising more than a faint crescent of water.

It never steps into the black center.

Most of its activity is maintenance, although no single act appears important enough to explain its existence. It combs dead tissue from the filter beds. It pinches fern roots before they reach the sump channel. It gathers fallen reeds and lays them along the slow intake seams, where their decay will be drawn into the sediment traps. It clears each red grille with the same repeated motion, left to right and then downward, until every opening is visible.

It does not clean the court. It preserves its rates of accumulation.

A certain thickness of algae is allowed on the spillway. Moss may occupy old lettering but not active fasteners. Sediment may collect in the western shallows until it reaches the upper edge of the second expansion joint; then the inhabitant presses it into dense black cakes and carries them to the filtration beds. The cakes disappear beneath the ferns over several weeks. New fronds grow brighter above them.

It grazes as it works. Its mouth is narrow and almost lipless, fitted with rows of small, flat mineral teeth. It eats algae, softened reed fiber, fungal films, and the dark organic skin that forms on standing water. It does not eat the drainage moths. They sometimes rest on its shoulders. The gray snails cross its legs without being removed, leaving bright wandering trails over the dull plates.

Nothing flees from it because it does not pursue.

During the palest morning light it remains close to the eastern slag mass, where the return flow warms the porous glass. At noon it comes into the open. It stands beside the blue ceramic waterline mark and places one finger against it, not touching the water but touching the wall exactly where the water ends. It holds this position for several minutes.

In amber evening it tends the plants. After the overhead light fades, it withdraws beneath the three service steps.

There is no nest there. The cavity under the steps is crowded with pipes, root fiber, wet felt, and a pale folded tissue that might initially be taken for fungus. The inhabitant presses itself into this material until its limbs can no longer be distinguished from it. By night only the curve of the back remains outside, plated and motionless among the mossy fittings.

This suggests that the creature sleeps there.

Closer examination suggests something else.

The pale tissue continues behind the steps, beneath the filter beds, and through cracks in the ceramic facing. Fine strands of it wrap the oldest pipes without constricting them. They pass through steel mesh with the fern roots and spread into the crushed brick below. Nodules occur wherever mineral runoff is richest. Some pulse at intervals unrelated to the creature’s visible breathing.

The body that walks around the basin is not the whole inhabitant.

It is a gathered portion: a mobile knot raised by a much larger colonial organism distributed through the perimeter of the court. The beds that appear to have merged are joined not only by moss and wandering roots but by this hidden tissue. It binds their chemistry together. Water entering at one wall is sampled before the walking body tastes it elsewhere. A trace of copper at the northern seam produces discoloration beneath the inhabitant’s tongue. Excess iron causes orange lines to brighten along its fingers. If the water grows too alkaline, the plates on its back become chalky within a day.

Old enamel labels identify the ferns, reeds, liverworts, and algae. Behind one bed, nearly covered by growth, another sign remains:

**EQUALIZING CULTURE — DO NOT SEVER**

The rest is illegible.

The walking form may once have been one of several. There are shallow recesses beneath the western wall large enough to hold other folded bodies, and mineral outlines cling to the tile inside them: shoulders, hands, the arc of a bent spine. Whether these were earlier inhabitants, discarded forms, or merely structures grown and reabsorbed as needed cannot now be determined.

The present one bears no wounds and no obvious signs of age. Parts of it are continually replaced. A finger damaged on broken ceramic softens, darkens, and is drawn back into the hand; several weeks later a new finger separates from the webbing. The plates of the back contain decades of mineral layers, but the flesh beneath them is new. It is ancient in the same way the court is ancient: not by preserving its original substance, but by replacing loss without altering arrangement.

Its most important function is so slight that the machinery almost makes it absurd.

The organism is a final buffer.

Its buried tissue holds water in thousands of capillary chambers. When inflow briefly exceeds discharge, the beds swell by an amount too small to see. The walking body grows heavier and smoother, its translucent sides rounding as water is drawn into it. When the level falls, moisture returns through its feet and through narrow pores in the submerged tissue. It cannot compensate for a flood, a broken main, or a stopped turbine. It corrects tremors: the passing discrepancies that lie beneath the sensitivity of the station’s oldest regulators.

Thus the waterline touches the blue mark without crossing it.

The inhabitant does not understand the court as a garden, a machine room, or a prison. It knows gradients, permitted blockages, useful decay, stable vibration. It knows the red grilles must remain open. It knows the sump as a downward pull extending beyond the range of its body. It does not touch the chain. It circles the valve wheel but never braces its hands against it.

Objects dropped into the basin disturb it. A bolt, tool, coin, or piece of glass will bring the walking form to the nearest edge. It watches until the object settles. Sometimes it extends an arm over the shallows, but it does not retrieve anything from water deeper than its wrist. The debris remains where it fell, slowly varnished by iron, while the organism adjusts the currents around it.

It obeys the rules because the rules are also its anatomy.

What it would do in silence is unknown.

No complete halt has occurred since the surviving records began. There have been brief fluctuations: a failing bearing, a distant impact, the momentary loss of one phase in an electrical storm. During these events the inhabitant is said to rise at once. The low stoop vanishes. Its spine straightens, its throat opens, and the hidden tissues throughout the planting beds tighten until every fern leans toward the basin.

It then becomes very still.

Not calm. Alert in a way the court has almost forgotten.

The steady roar returns, and the body slowly folds again. Its hand goes to the wall. Water continues over the polished lip. The beds release what they have held.

By noon it is once more only a dark figure among manufactured greenery, touching one finger to the blue mark while beneath it, through brick dust, steel mesh, slag glass, and sediment, the rest of the inhabitant keeps the court level.
