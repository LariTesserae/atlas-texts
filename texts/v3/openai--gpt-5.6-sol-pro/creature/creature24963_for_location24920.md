The city belongs, in daylight, to creatures that can endure exposure.

The gulls are the most visible, though not necessarily the most numerous. Their whiteness is deceptive. Up close they are smoke-stained and yellowed, their breast feathers clotted by grease, their feet roughened by salt and hot roofing. They nest behind parapets in beds of wire, plastic cord, fish skin, insulation, and scraps of woven sack. Chicks hatch among machine housings and learn the vibration of compressors before they learn the wind. The adults recognize shift whistles, truck models, the opening of loading doors. They know which siren means a spill and which means nothing edible. A new worker may see chaos when a flock rises; the birds are making distinctions.

Different roofs belong to different colonies. Trespass is answered with lowered heads and choking cries, but these boundaries dissolve when food appears. Then the city’s white surfaces detach themselves. Thousands of birds converge on one yard, turning in the steam above a truck or settling so thickly around a sluice that the concrete disappears beneath their bodies.

The terns live higher and more narrowly. They choose crane arms, aerials, cable stays, anything projecting into moving air. They are cleaner than the gulls and seem almost unreal against the industrial haze, but they too have learned the machinery. They hover beside outfall channels where stunned fish emerge in warm water. Cormorants occupy the canals below them, black and heavy, diving beneath oil sheen and resurfacing between floating scraps. They stand afterward on pipes with their wings spread toward exhaust vents, each bird holding the posture of something burned onto the air.

Pigeons remain close to the grain works. Their flocks feed inside loading sheds and under rail wagons, walking among kernels pressed into black mud. They breed in hollow girders where their droppings set like pale mortar. Falcons watch them from transmission towers. These predators are lean, soot-darkened birds whose nesting ledges contain rings of wire, vertebrae, pigeon feet, and the iridescent drift of plucked feathers. They hunt along the straight corridors between silos, using the walls to force prey into predictable turns.

Near the ground, the city is divided among mammals.

The dogs own the levees, the margins of housing compounds, and the long roads after dark. They are not one population but many small nations. Some descend from guard dogs abandoned when a factory closed; some are the offspring of household animals; some have lived outside human keeping for generations. Their bodies show the city’s selective pressures. They are long-legged enough to cross flooded ground, short-coated, narrow through the ribs, with scarred muzzles and feet spread broad from walking mud and hot concrete.

They seldom bark without purpose. A pack communicates by glances, tail height, shoulder contact, and changes of pace. During the day they sleep beneath tanker trailers or in the shadow cast by floodwalls. At dusk they rise, stretch, and begin their circuits. They know where kitchen refuse is thrown, where pigs have piglets, where rats cross open ground, where guards tolerate them, and where men with hoses drive them off. They drink rainwater before it has stood long enough to sour. When storms approach they move uphill early, gathering on old embankments to wait with their noses turned from the rain.

Some attach themselves loosely to people. A gatehouse dog may accept food from three shifts of workers while belonging to none of them. It knows uniforms rather than names. It sleeps outside the barrier, accompanies the first bus for part of its route, and still joins the levee pack when they pass at night.

The pigs are less visible until they move. In the mud beneath wharves they resemble heaps of soaked refuse: gray-backed, black-bristled, sometimes pink where grease or contaminated water has thinned the hair. Old sows dig hollows under loading platforms and lead their young through gaps in fencing that seem too narrow for them. Their snouts are callused from rooting in clinker and shell. They eat fish heads, grain paste, crabs, rats, leather, paper softened by rain, and the thick organic sediment caught behind drain grilles.

They have learned to wait. A sow may lie all afternoon within hearing of a rendering line, apparently asleep, then rise minutes before a waste gate opens. At such moments the pigs become violent and fast. Their squeals echo inside the concrete yards while gulls strike at their backs and dogs circle outside the crush. When the food is gone, the sound stops almost immediately. The pigs return to the shade with blood and mud drying together on their faces.

Rats fill the architecture more completely than either dogs or pigs. They inhabit the city in layers. Wharf rats live among tidal cavities and can swim through culverts against the current. Silo rats are pale with grain dust and rarely see open weather. The rats in cold stores have dense coats and nest against warm motor housings. Others occupy apartment drains, transformer chambers, abandoned offices, ferry hulls, roof insulation, and the dry interiors of old floodgates.

Their paths are worn into grease along pipes and wall bases. Generations use the same openings until the edges become polished. They understand doors as recurring events and machinery as climate. When a compressor starts, warmth will appear elsewhere; when a conveyor stops, kernels can be gathered safely; when pumps engage during rain, lower tunnels must be abandoned. Before a flood, rats rise through the city in a quiet migration, emerging from gratings and cable ducts to crowd stairwells, viaducts, and warehouse rafters.

They are hunted there by snakes. The snakes live above the flood line in cable galleries, wall cavities, and the suspended trays that carry electrical lines between buildings. They are often discovered only as shed skins: long translucent sleeves caught on bolts or hanging from conduits. Heat from the cables incubates their eggs. Some become large on rats and pigeons, though they move so little that workers may pass beneath the same animal for weeks without noticing it.

Monitor lizards govern the drains. They lie on sloping canal walls with their limbs spread and their throats pulsing, colored like wet concrete and old rope. When alarmed they enter the water with barely a splash. They take crabs, eggs, carrion, young rats, and anything trapped against a grille after heavy rain. The oldest carry missing toes, broken tails, and scars from propellers or dogs. They shelter in fractured seawalls, using chambers where corroded reinforcement has opened cavities behind the concrete.

The crabs are not so much residents as a tide made articulate. At low water they emerge from mud, culverts, collapsed basements, and the submerged courses of former streets. Small ones sift sediment beside drain mouths. Larger ones enter the city, climbing curbs and collecting beneath parked equipment. Their shells bear the local palette—rust, silt, soot, weak violet, the green-black of oxidized metal without any resemblance to leaves. They pick grease from puddles and flesh from fish frames. Rats seize them from behind; gulls drop them onto roofs to crack them; pigs consume them whole.

Their clicking travels ahead of them in darkness. A person crossing a yard may first mistake it for rain beginning on sheet metal. Then the ground shifts.

Insects occupy every interval too small for the larger animals. Flies rise from waste channels in clouds and settle again as a living nap on walls. Their larvae work inside heaps that steam in the rain. Cockroaches inhabit the warm inner city: boiler rooms, service tunnels, wall voids, kitchens, motor cabinets. Some are dark and glossy; others are almost colorless from generations underground. When a light is switched on, the floor appears to contract as they vanish into seams.

Mosquitoes breed wherever neglect holds water for more than a few days. Their populations bloom after storms in flooded lift shafts, tires, broken cisterns, roof channels, and the hollow legs of machinery. Workers burn coils at gatehouses and sleep under patched mesh. In the housing blocks, dark fans turn all night over beds, not cooling the rooms so much as making it difficult for mosquitoes to land.

Moths gather where light and residue coincide. They cover lamp housings, pale concrete, and the warm screens over ventilation intakes. By morning their wings have absorbed soot and moisture. Geckos and small lizards wait at the edges of these congregations, nearly transparent against stained walls, taking one moth after another until their bellies hang round.

The bats take what rises above the lamps. They spend the day packed into expansion joints, bridge boxes, disused tanks, roofless mills, and upper floors where rain cannot reach the deepest corners. Their colonies darken ceilings and generate their own heat. Beneath them lie banks of guano alive with beetles and mites. At dusk, the first bats circle inside their chambers as if testing the hour. Then the colony pours out through cracks, thousands passing the same narrow opening without collision.

They do not scatter at random. Some follow canals, taking mosquitoes above the water. Others hunt moths around lamps. The largest pass over fish yards, where insects rise from the drains. Their wings skim close to concrete, vehicles, and human faces, guided through a city whose hard surfaces return every sound.

Out on the mud, life becomes less enclosed but no less ordered. Sandpipers, stilts, herons, egrets, and other waders advance and retreat with the water. Each works a chosen depth. Some probe rapidly in softened silt; some stand still beside drainage trickles; some stir the mud with their feet. Their pale legs become dark to the joint. Behind them fish move through the shallow channels—mullet, eels, hardy scavenging species able to tolerate warm water and sudden changes in salinity. At the discharge mouths they gather in unnatural abundance, feeding on particles carried from the city until low oxygen or contamination kills them. Their deaths produce another feast.

Less visible still are the inhabitants that make the entire system possible. Bacteria live in the warm films coating drains and settling ponds, in the black sediment under fuel sheen, in grease traps, wall seepage, animal intestines, rotting fish, and porous concrete. Fungi spread through soaked cardboard and old plaster, producing no green, only gray bloom, black spotting, or soft colorless threads. Worms and small crustaceans churn the mud where oxygen reaches it. Maggots reduce flesh in hot bins. Mites consume what remains in nests and guano. The apparent barrenness of the city is largely an illusion created by scale. Its bare surfaces are inhabited down to their pores.

The human residents live differently from all these creatures: not by enduring the open city, but by crossing it in sealed compartments.

Before dawn, buses collect them from the housing blocks. Their windows are fogged from breath and air-conditioning. Workers descend at gates wearing rubber boots, reflective cloth, masks, gloves, and hearing protection. They pass indoors and become parts of systems larger than themselves: cutters, sorters, mechanics, pump attendants, refrigeration engineers, crane operators, cleaners, inspectors, guards. Many know the outdoor animals individually without admitting that they do. A crane operator recognizes the same tern returning to a railing. A watchman leaves fish skin for a one-eared dog. Drain crews know which tunnels contain snakes and which flood too quickly to enter. Warehouse hands can judge from the gulls’ behavior whether a spill has occurred beyond the wall.

Their children inhabit the city’s few sheltered margins. They play beneath housing blocks, in covered passages, on concrete landings above the flood stain. They collect crab claws, pigeon feathers, machine labels, colored bottle caps, and pieces of glass rounded in drainage channels. They are warned away from canals, substations, pigs, dogs with litters, and puddles whose depth cannot be seen. Rain does not send them home immediately. Often it brings temporary relief from the heat, and they stand under overhangs watching the courtyards become shallow brown lakes.

The adults speak of animals as nuisance, theft, contamination, weather signs, or unofficial infrastructure. Gulls reveal waste leaks. Rats reveal broken seals. Mosquitoes reveal standing water. Crabs clear the lowest drains. Dogs keep other dogs away. Pigs dispose of refuse until they breach a fence or attack someone. Bats are tolerated because they eat insects, except when their colonies occupy machinery. Every creature is useful at one distance and intolerable at another.

There are also people who live almost as secretively as the animals: night mechanics in pumping stations, families occupying unauthorized rooms in abandoned blocks, scavengers sorting metal after storms, fishers setting lines in outfall canals despite warning signs. They know the dry routes through flood time and the shaded routes through heat. They move along old infrastructure ignored by official maps. Their shelters occupy the same elevated niches favored by snakes and rats—bridge approaches, upper warehouse rooms, the decks of grounded vessels—though they defend them with lamps, wire mesh, smoke, and dogs.

No species rules the whole city. Dominion changes by height, hour, tide, and industrial schedule.

At noon, heat grants the roofs to birds and drives mammals below concrete. In the service tunnels, cockroaches and rats move through artificial night. At low tide, crabs take the streets nearest the sea. At dusk, bats inherit the air. During a spill, gulls and dogs seize the yard. During rain, gutters become rivers and fish enter places where trucks stood an hour earlier. During flood, everything living climbs: rats, snakes, insects, pigs, people, each seeking the same diminishing band of dry structure.

Afterward they descend in an order determined by hunger.

The gulls return first, circling through steam. Crabs spread from the channels. Dogs test the water with their feet. Rats emerge behind walls. Workers in rubber boots open grilles and restart stalled machinery. Flies settle on whatever the rain has uncovered. From the mud to the highest crane, the city repopulates itself—not as a wilderness reclaiming industry, but as a life made from industry’s heat, cavities, waste, and rhythm.

Nothing here waits for the city to disappear. Everything lives from what the city is now.
