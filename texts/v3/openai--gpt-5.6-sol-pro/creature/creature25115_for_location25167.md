The first inhabitants one notices are the quick ones.

They are not necessarily small, but they live quickly: hatching into conditions that may last only hours, feeding before their mouths have hardened, mating while still shedding the membranes of birth. They seem less like established species than answers the plateau gives to temporary questions.

A warm pool asks for swimmers. By evening it is white with spindle-bodied larvae, each equipped with a ring of paddles and a transparent gut full of mineral light. A rainstorm asks for wings. Black husks split along wet slopes, releasing thin creatures that fly upward through the falling water and vanish when the cloud closes. A new fissure asks for heat-eaters. Soft, eyeless animals gather at its lip and unfold reflective organs into the orange glow.

When the pool drains, the storm rises, or the fissure seals, these creatures do not always die. Some contract into seeds. Some merge with their siblings into ropes of dormant tissue. Some surrender their shapes altogether and persist as a tendency in the rock: the next time water gathers nearby, it will gather with eggs already in it.

Nothing dead remains singular for long.

A carcass is quickly divided among competing futures. Its bones sprout red filaments. Its hide becomes a nursery for condensation cups. The final electrical patterns of its nerves seep into piezoelectric seams, where they may later emerge as blue flickers during an earthquake. Scavengers do not merely eat the body; they redistribute its habits. A grazer that feeds on the remains of a burrowing animal may begin digging. A vine absorbing a dead flyer may produce leaves that flex uselessly like wings.

The six-limbed grazers are among the plateau’s most numerous animals. Individually they are delicate things, no higher than a dark reed’s lower joint. Their heads are narrow and almost featureless except for a moist seam that opens to rasp spores from stone. Each of their six feet chooses its placement independently, feeling several possible surfaces before committing to one. This makes a moving herd appear hesitant at its edges and fluid at its center.

The herd is the durable organism. Individuals are born, reproduce, and disappear within it like cells in circulating blood. When cliffs tilt, thousands link limbs and become living nets. When sideways gravity sweeps through a forest, they catch against trunks in braided masses. During droughts they arrange themselves in shallow bowls, dark backs outward, collecting dew in the warm hollow between them. Their young swim there until the herd moves on.

They have no leaders. Direction enters them as a change of tension. One animal pauses, another shifts its weight, and soon a dark current turns through the undergrowth. Sometimes the cause lies hundreds of kilometers away: a valley beginning to form, a rain that has not yet fallen, a concentration of edible spores along a route the herd will not reach for several generations.

Above them feed the sail-backed browsers.

These are immense, slow animals, each carrying a forest of ribbed membranes along its spine. Their legs are columnar but many-jointed, allowing them to lower their bodies almost flat when the ground convulses. A browser standing at full height may comb spores from several atmospheric layers at once. Its mouth is not in its head. It consists of hundreds of narrow openings along the sails, each lined with cilia and mineral teeth.

The small structure at the front of the body serves chiefly to test distance. Two branching organs extend from it, touching not objects but their nearness. In folded country the organs may probe a tree visible a day’s journey away and discover it is close enough to bite.

Old browsers become landscapes in their own right. Mosses thicken over their lower flanks. Rain cups colonize the hollows between armor plates. Grazers shelter in the warm shade beneath them, and winged animals sleep hanging from their ribs. Some carry enough soil for groves to grow along their backs. Because the plateau permits nothing to remain in one place too long, these moving gardens are among its nearest approximations to stability.

The browsers are quiet even when wounded. They communicate by altering the pressure of fluids in their sails. Nearby leaves incline toward them. Pools tremble. Buried animals turn in their tunnels. A herd may conduct a warning across a basin without making a sound.

The winged creatures are less easily divided into kinds.

Some have bodies from which wings extend. Others are wings that temporarily grow bodies. On the cliffs they roost folded into black tessellations, each animal fitted so precisely against its neighbors that the colony appears to be a single hide laid over the rock. At dusk—or at whatever local condition passes for dusk—the hide loosens. Seams open. Thousands peel away.

They fly poorly through ordinary space. Their membranes catch gradients of gravity, heat, sequence, and repetition. A flock may gain altitude by circling the same-shaped mountain three times. It may cross a canyon by matching the rhythm of its wingbeats to cracks on the opposite wall. In regions where distance has tightened, the flock compresses into a dense, many-eyed knot. Sometimes it fails to unfold afterward.

Such fused flocks become the great aerial animals.

These beings may span provinces. Their bodies are loose federations of former individuals: wings functioning as lungs, tails as sensory streamers, clustered heads as heat-exchanging organs. They drift above the plateau on currents too high and thin for weather. Beneath their translucent tissues, old flight formations remain visible like bones.

One passing overhead can darken a thousand kilometers. It may take days for the leading edge of its shadow to be followed by the trailing edge. Forests close beneath it. Sail-browsers lower themselves. Cliff colonies become perfectly still.

The aerial giants do not seem to hunt. They filter something from the upper dimness—ash, spores, electrical charge, perhaps unrealized forms. Occasionally one releases a long curtain of translucent eggs. These descend for months, passing through clouds and becoming stained by weather. Most never land. Those that do may hatch as flying animals, ponds, parasitic storms, or patches of new sky.

The predators below are quieter and more exact.

They wait where events become likely.

A common kind resembles a low arrangement of black blades laid radially around a warm central body. It has neither eyes nor nostrils. Beneath each blade is a chamber lined with branching crystals, sensitive to minute concentrations of possibility. The animal settles beside blank stone and samples futures until one tastes of blood.

Then it waits.

It may wait through storms, uplift, the growth of a grove, and the grove’s destruction. Its tissues slow until fungi mistake it for terrain. At the appointed moment, empty ground buckles and prey arrives—perhaps fleeing something that has not yet appeared. The predator closes before the prey understands where it is.

These ambushers are not always correct. One sometimes waits beside a fissure that never opens, growing thin over decades. Yet even a failed prediction can become a trap. Grazers shelter beneath its blades. Vines bind it to the ground. A pool forms against its flank. Life accumulates until prey comes for reasons the predator never foresaw.

Other hunters cultivate their own inevitability. They circle an area until repeated tracks attract one another and the paths collapse into a single route. Anything crossing the region is then drawn toward that route, where the hunter lies with its mouth open.

In the sound basins live the listeners.

They are pale, almost boneless animals with long glassy antennae and bodies folded around resonating cavities. Most remain buried in the murmuring floor, exposing only their sensory crowns. They feed on displaced noise: thunder without storms, footsteps that have crossed the plateau underground, the wingbeats of flocks not yet born.

A listener lowering its antennae into a rich deposit begins to tremble. Colored pulses travel through its transparent flesh. Old sounds nourish it best, especially sounds whose sources no longer exist.

As they mature, listeners incorporate what they consume. One fed for years on volcanic cracking develops a hot, segmented shell. One drinking the calls of extinct flying animals may grow useless membranes and drag them through the dust. Rare individuals consume a coherent sequence—a whole storm, avalanche, migration, or death—and become able to release it.

They do so when threatened.

A predator approaching a listener colony may suddenly be struck by the stored collapse of a mountain. No rock falls, but its bones break as if buried beneath one. A grove may flatten under the released passage of an ancient hurricane while the air remains still.

Deeper beneath the crust move the pale burrowers. Their flesh is cooler than the rock surrounding them, so stone softens in their presence and closes behind them without leaving tunnels. They follow mineral fluids and carry underground ecologies in grooves along their bodies. Their mouths separate ores. Their waste hardens into veins of metal.

They are born near the surface as thumb-sized, soft animals. The older they become, the deeper they descend and the larger the pressures they can endure. The oldest may be hundreds of kilometers long. Their movements are mistaken for tectonics.

Perhaps they are tectonics.

This uncertainty grows with every attempt to distinguish the inhabitants from the place they inhabit.

The black filaments extruded by hills become plants, but they flex before rooting as if searching. Crystal fields respond to strain, but their flashes travel in patterns too coordinated to be merely mechanical. Faults avoid certain groves and pursue others. Rivers return, with variations, to valleys they appear to remember. Mountain ranges walking eastward pause when they encounter one another. Sometimes they exchange forests.

Even the armor-plated herds, which seem plainly animal, function as mobile organs of geochemistry. Their shells host catalytic colonies that refine whatever province they cross. Iron-rich bacteria occupy the shoulders; sulfur colonies bloom near the warm joints; delicate silver precipitates along the spine. The animals migrate between volcanic regions in routes that complete enormous chemical cycles. No individual knows the route. No herd completes it in a lifetime. Still, the circulation continues.

From high enough above, their migrations resemble blood moving through a body.

The fissures resemble capillaries.

The basalt shafts breathe.

The forests increase the exchange surface of the skin.

The piezoelectric crystal fields carry impulses in branching waves.

And the coordinated upheavals—those hours when vents pulse together and mountain chains reorganize—resemble neither eruption nor weather. They resemble attention.

What lives there is therefore not merely an ecology spread across a plateau. The plateau itself is alive, though “itself” may be too simple a word for it.

It has no central brain. Thought travels through it as phase changes: a pressure entering the mantle, a crystal field igniting, a forest retracting its roots, a million animals turning east. An idea may take a century to cross its body or may appear everywhere at once. Memory is stored in repeated landforms, in trapped sounds, in the inherited routes of herds, in landscapes sealed inside mountains. What it forgets becomes ordinary stone.

It does not command its inhabitants. They are its senses, symptoms, digestion, dreams, and offspring. Yet they are also separate enough to fear it, feed upon it, evade its changes, and be surprised.

The plateau is young because it is still growing into the concept of having a shape. Its peaks remain sharp because they have not been held in existence long enough to weather. Its distances fluctuate because size is not settled. Its laws are ecological because life came before consistency and has forced the local world to accommodate it.

Perhaps the continentally vast winged things above are adults of its kind, having eventually shed geology for air. Perhaps the walking mountain ranges are limbs not yet joined to the body. Perhaps every sealed inner wetland is an egg, and every migrating grove carries a fragment from which another upland might begin.

No creature on the plateau could see enough to know.

They know only the signs of its passing thoughts.

Condensation begins tapping faster beneath the leaves. Burrowers turn upward. Probability hunters abandon futures they had waited years to catch. The great browsers collapse their legs and press close to the ground. Across a hundred basins, listeners withdraw their antennae from the stored murmur.

Then a pressure moves beneath everything.

The crystal fields brighten one after another, outlining for an instant a branching anatomy larger than nations. Rivers lift from their beds. Forests loosen their roots. On the horizon, the black ridges rise and flex—not as land displaced by force, but as tissue answering an impulse.

For a moment, every living thing is still.

Then the plateau changes its mind about where it ends.
