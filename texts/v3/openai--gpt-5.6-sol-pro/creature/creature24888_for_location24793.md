What lives here has learned not to stand up.

The upright are taken first. A blade lifted into the current is twisted until its holdfast fails. A shell perched proudly on a stone becomes a lever for ice. Branching things collect sand, and the sand gives them weight, and the next tidal roll tears them away. Even softness is dangerous if too much of it is exposed. The plain permits life chiefly in films, crusts, seams, and burrows—in forms that can be mistaken for stains or overlooked as grit.

On the higher stones, where a little summer light survives, algae inhabit the rock as color. They are not meadows. They have no visible leaves. Olive cells lie beneath translucent skins; rust-colored patches occupy old scratches; black-green threads fill pits too small for gravel to enter. Their growth is measured against the slow bleaching and darkening of stone. A patch may spread no farther than the width of a hand in many years, yet within it generations succeed one another rapidly, dividing whenever light, temperature, and the brief drift of nutrients allow.

The algae make the stones slightly slick. That is enough to support grazers.

Tiny limpets fasten in shallow hollows, their shells so closely fitted that the current cannot easily find an edge beneath them. They move mostly during the slack water, crossing a few finger-widths of rock to rasp at the living film, then returning to depressions worn to the shapes of their bodies. Some of those depressions outlast the animals that made them. Empty, they gather dark sediment and the eggs of smaller creatures.

There are snails no larger than coarse sand. There are mites and minute crustaceans translucent enough to borrow the color of whatever lies beneath them. When the water is quiet, they venture across the algal stains in numbers. At the first trembling of gravel, they vanish into cracks. One instant a slab appears dusted with moving specks; the next it is only a slab.

Beneath nearly every movable stone is a temporary household.

Amphipods crouch there with their bodies bent like pale commas. Their many limbs hold against the underside while the current scours the exposed face above. They eat what is brought to them: torn algae, rotting tissue, the softening skin of driftwood, fragments of animals killed by cold or pressure or ice. They do not require abundance. A shred of kelp lodged among cobbles can feed them for days. A dead fish, when one reaches the bottom, becomes a crowded country.

They arrive first from down-current, following dissolved traces too faint for any larger animal to notice. They enter through the mouth and gills, through wounds, beneath loosened scales. For a time the carcass seems to breathe as hundreds feed within it. Worms gather. Small crabs come sideways over the gravel, testing the water with raised claws. A starfish may settle over what remains, folding its pale underside against bone and skin. Then a tide shifts the body, or anchor ice lifts it, or sand buries it. The congregation disperses as suddenly as it formed.

Most of the plain’s permanent inhabitants live lower than its surface.

In the packed sand are worms built almost entirely for waiting. Some line their burrows with mucus and shell dust. Some extend a crown of delicate tentacles only when the flow weakens, gathering particles from the water, then withdraw before the gravel begins to move. Others swallow the sediment itself. They take in glacial flour, decayed algae, bacterial cells, powdered shell, and all the fine residue of distant lives. What cannot nourish them passes through and is laid behind them in faint coils that the next current smooths away.

Small bivalves lie buried on their sides, showing nothing but paired siphons at the mud. Their shells are thin because there is little mineral wealth to spare, but they are protected by depth. When colder brine spills into a hollow, the siphons close. The animal inside slows until its needs are almost absent. It may remain sealed through a long winter, living on what it has stored, while silt covers the place where it breathed.

There are also lives beneath the depth of burrowing animals: bacteria in the dark pores between grains, feeding on what filters down. Near the surface they use what little oxygen the water delivers. Deeper, other kinds take over, working with compounds drawn from mud and stone. Their boundaries shift whenever the sediment is disturbed. An iceberg furrow may expose a buried layer to oxygen for the first time in centuries, awakening one community and poisoning another. The pale raw trench appears empty, but within hours invisible occupations have begun.

The larger residents are few, broad-bellied, and close to the ground.

A sculpin waits between stones with fins spread like hands. Its skin carries the basin’s colors: gray, brown, dull red, the pallor of shell grit. Much of its life consists of not being moved. It faces the current, presses its body into the gravel, and lets water bring the world to it. Its eyes stand high enough to watch while the rest of it remains hidden. When an amphipod breaks shelter, the sculpin’s stillness ends in a single opening of the mouth. Water and prey vanish together. Then the fish settles back, and the gravel seems to have imagined the motion.

Its eggs, when the season comes, are placed deep in a crevice or under the lip of a slab. They cling in a compact mass, guarded not from warmth or cold so much as from being filled with silt. The parent fans them when the current cannot. Many are lost. A stone shifts half a hand’s breadth and crushes an entire brood; ice scrapes the nursery open; a starfish finds the cluster. Enough remain.

Flatfish occupy the sandier hollows. They lie with one side buried and the other stippled like the bottom. Fine sediment settles over their backs until only the eyes remain. They tolerate water that would drive more active fish away, but not the deepest brine. When such a sheet enters, the flatfish rise all at once from the mud. For a moment the hollow fills with pale shapes and their abandoned impressions. They move upslope and settle again, each making a low shiver that draws sand over its body.

Cod are less inhabitants than weather. They come with water of a certain temperature, with capelin or small crustaceans, with the shifting edge between one current and another. A solitary fish may hang above a ridge for an hour, balancing itself with motions too small to see. Others pass in loose groups, appearing one after another out of the suspended flour. They feed, turn, and are gone. Their absence can last a season.

Starfish are more faithful. They survive by fastening themselves to the same world that tries to drag everything loose. Their tube feet explore the undersides of stones, the seams between shell fragments, the buried rims of clams. A large one crossing open bedrock seems exposed and helpless, yet the current presses its flattened body down. If overturned, it bends patiently until an arm finds purchase. If an arm is crushed, the wound closes. Its manner is not resistance in the heroic sense. It yields locally and continues elsewhere.

Crabs move in the hours when the tide’s force changes direction. They shelter during the strongest flow with legs wedged between cobbles, then emerge into the confused interval before the water gathers itself again. Their tracks persist in soft mud for minutes or hours. A crab knows the plain at the scale of odors and pressure: food upstream, danger overhead, fresh water descending, brine entering from below. It does not need a landmark. Its world is a succession of gradients.

The kelps, where they occur, are severe little things. Their holdfasts enter old fractures in bedrock and grip around embedded stones. Their blades are narrow, leathery, and scarred along the edges. Most point continually in one direction, then reverse with the tide. Amphipods inhabit the folds near their bases. Snails graze them. Fish shelter briefly in their lee. Each plant creates a small region where the current is fractionally weaker and falling particles may remain.

For that reason a single kelp can become the center of more life than many yards of open plain. Its loss is correspondingly large. When ice takes it, the holdfast may come away with a piece of rock and all the animals hidden there. For a while the uprooted kelp travels along the bottom, catching and releasing stones. It may strand in a hollow and decay, feeding scavengers in darkness. Thus even destruction carries a harvest somewhere else.

Seals enter from above as creatures of another element. To the bottom-dwellers they are sudden eclipses accompanied by pressure, bubbles, and the rapid change of water around a large body. A seal may investigate a ridge where cod have gathered, touch the bottom with its whiskers forward, and turn in a space that seems too small for it. It does not rest here. Its warmth is extravagant. Its heartbeat, speed, and need for air belong to a quicker world.

Sometimes blood follows it through the water. Sometimes a fish falls injured into the stones. Sometimes nothing remains but a loosening trail of bubbles rising toward a surface the buried animals will never see.

There may be whales in the strait, but the plain knows them mainly as sound: low pulses entering the rock, distances without direction. A call can pass through the basin and set delicate appendages trembling in their burrows. Later comes the remote concussion of ice. To a worm, the two may be equally without source.

No inhabitant possesses the whole place. The cod knows moving corridors of water. The crab knows the shelter of one ridge. The limpet knows a circuit on a single stone. The worm knows the collapse and reopening of its burrow. The bacterial colony may occupy a boundary thinner than a shell. Even the seal, which can cross the basin swiftly, sees only through a brief cone of dimness before returning upward.

Their lives are therefore not arranged around the landscape as people arrange themselves around a valley, river, or road. They are arranged around intervals.

The slack before the tide turns.  
The summer pallor.  
The fall of edible dust.  
The years between ice scourings.  
The few minutes before a brine pool fills.  
The century during which one stone remains stable.

To live here is to make use of an interval without mistaking it for safety.

After a berg has passed, the fresh trench appears lifeless. Its walls are pale; crushed shells shine in the raw sediment; torn worms contract in the open. Fish arrive first to take what has been exposed. Then scavenging amphipods gather. Bacteria bloom over damaged tissue. Fine silt begins to settle. Burrowing animals enter from the softer edges. Algae darken any stone that receives light. A starfish crosses the ridge pushed up beside the furrow.

For a while the scar becomes richer than the undisturbed bottom because it has opened all that was hidden.

Then the trench softens. Gravel rattles into it. Sand covers the dead. The brief abundance is consumed or buried, and the inhabitants thin out again until life returns to its usual form: not emptiness, but concealment.

What lives on the drowned plain is seldom magnificent when seen alone. It is small, slow, flattened, cold, and often hungry. Much of it is color on stone or movement beneath stone. Its most successful gestures are closure, adhesion, withdrawal, and waiting.

Yet taken together, these lives occupy almost every refuge the sea has failed to erase. They enter pores in rock, films on gravel, water inside shells, mud beneath ice scars. They eat the faint rain descending from above and one another’s remains. They are crushed, frozen, uncovered, transported, and buried. They return without proclamation.

The plain is becoming flat, but it is not becoming dead. Life persists there in the same manner as the seabed itself: grain by grain, close to the ground, surrendering every shape it cannot keep.
