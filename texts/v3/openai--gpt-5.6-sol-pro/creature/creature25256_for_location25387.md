From across the basin, the inhabitants look human.

They move upright between towers. They gather at crossings when signal bands darken. They lean over railings to watch rain strike the lower roofs. Freight crews walk in files beside the slow conveyors, and technicians climb the rim with cases fastened to their backs. There are elders, apprentices, laborers, officials. There are children who run until the thin air catches them and then stand doubled over, laughing soundlessly into their masks.

Only at close range does the resemblance become uncertain.

The summit people are generally narrow-bodied, with deep chests and little wasted mass on their limbs. Their movements have the economy of those for whom every exertion incurs a debt of breath and water. They do not turn suddenly if a glance will do. They do not call to one another across streets. Even in private, their gestures tend to finish gently, hands coming to rest against a wall or folded into sleeves, as though the prohibition against shock has entered their manners.

Their outer clothing is built in overlapping layers of ash cloth, metallic mesh, and thin fired scales. The windward side is pale and reflective. The inward side is dark, made to receive heat from floors and pipes. Coats hang unevenly because one half may be meant for the cold of an exposed bridge and the other for the radiant wall beside it. There is no single way to be dressed for the city. Every body is a border between climates.

The masks are what strangers remember.

They cover the mouth and nose and often extend along the jaw in narrow ceramic chambers. Exhaled moisture condenses inside them and returns by capillary threads to a soft reservoir beneath the tongue. Fine metal combs remove ash and reactive vapor. On a cold morning the masks click as their valves contract, so that a crowd waiting for a lift produces a faint, insectile ticking.

Some masks are plain civic issue. Others are old, repaired with wire and mineral cement, their surfaces carrying decades of hairline glaze. Family masks may contain pieces from several generations: a valve plate cut from a grandparent’s respirator, a condensation coil made when a district stood eleven meters lower, a child’s first mouthpiece set into the adult housing as a blue decorative shard. To expose the whole face in public is neither forbidden nor intimate in the ordinary sense. It is simply extravagant. Breath becomes visible. Water is lost.

Beneath the coverings, summit faces are dry and finely creased. The nostrils are small; the inner eyelids, where visible, are dark and translucent. Hair is usually clipped short or bound beneath conductive cloth to prevent static around vapor lines. Skin tones vary, but mineral staining confuses them: iron reds at the fingertips, pale salts around the eyes, black dust embedded permanently in old scars. Those who work in receiving halls may acquire a glassy gray sheen across their knuckles that cannot be scrubbed away.

Their blood is very dark. It carries oxygen efficiently and seals quickly when exposed to the air, forming a lacquerlike crust. Physicians say this is adaptation. Foundry workers say it is proof that the mountain has already begun replacing them.

No census fully agrees on what constitutes one inhabitant. A person living entirely in an oxygenated interior may be counted by room. A rim technician is counted by suit and emergency allotment. Certain pressure-engine custodians are recorded at several addresses because their life-support apparatus passes through several districts. Infants are not entered into the public registers until they have breathed summit air outside a nursery. The dead may remain on maintenance rolls for years if their recorded expertise is still being consulted by successors.

Childhood takes place in the most humid rooms available.

Nurseries occupy the centers of condenser towers, where warm pipes cross and the air is almost heavy. Their walls are tiled rather than painted, and every surface curves toward a drain. Children are allowed bowls of open water there—not much, but enough to touch, pour, spill, and learn the scandalous behavior of liquid before it disappears into the floor.

They make miniature cities from ceramic scraps. The towers are always hinged. The bridges slide. A child who fixes a toy building rigidly to its base is corrected.

They are taught early to read strain. Before letters come the public marks: widening, compression, safe flexure, hot void, expected shear. They learn which seams in a wall are structural and which are merely old. They are told never to put a finger into a crack, no matter how narrow it appears, and never to step over an alignment cord after its mirrors have broken rank.

Their games are quiet. One consists of balancing metal slivers on a warm plate and guessing which will move first as the plate expands. Another is played along sacrificial alleys, where children chalk imaginary doors and windows onto walls that will someday split apart. After rain they launch folded pieces of slag-foil into the gutters and run beside them, watching the little vessels vanish one by one through cistern grilles.

They are not taught that the mountain is alive.

They are taught that it moves, remembers stress, takes pressure, releases heat, and answers interference. The distinction is maintained carefully in schools and ignored almost everywhere else.

Adults address old fractures before cutting into them. Engineers rest a hand against load-bearing pillars when arriving for work. Engine crews leave a pause between the final inspection and the first opening of a valve, a pause with no technical function. When a new seam appears in a dwelling, the household sets out a shallow ceramic dish beneath it for one night. Usually nothing enters the dish. Occasionally there will be dust, a bead of dark water, or a thread of metallic deposit. Whatever appears is sealed into the wall beside the date.

No one calls this worship. The city has several precise words for respect toward a force that cannot notice you.

The inhabitants do not organize their lives around fixed ground. Their dwellings are claims upon relationships: this room remains above a certain heat main; this workshop retains access to a condensation chain; this family keeps the third chamber outward from a particular structural spine. When walls migrate, the dwelling migrates with them. A household may wake for years to a widening gap down the middle of its kitchen and eventually install a flexible table across it.

Furniture has many feet. Cabinets are hung on adjustable frames. Beds are shallow suspended trays, partly for comfort and partly so that a change in floor angle will not roll a sleeper against hot masonry. Cups are weighted. Bowls have lids. Nothing valuable is set near an expansion joint.

Possessions tend to be small, but inheritances can be immense. A family may own half of a bridge foot, seven minutes of condenser flow during rain, or responsibility for a kiln scheduled to open in the lifetime of descendants not yet born. What passes from parent to child is often not an object but a pending obligation.

Some projects outlive every person associated with their beginning. To manage this, the summit people form continuities: groups that inherit a task along with its name. A continuity may design one pressure shell for four hundred years. Its members are born, recruited, disagree, die, and are replaced, while externally the continuity is treated as a single legal worker. It signs documents. It receives blame. It can be fined portions of future water. It may apologize for an error committed six generations earlier.

Individuals sometimes take the names of their continuities as a second name, but the relationship is not ownership. A person belongs to several durations at once: the brief duration of the body, the longer duration of a household, the engineered duration of a bridge or furnace, and the uncertain duration of the mountain beneath all of them.

Their speech reflects this. There are ordinary past and future tenses, but much of technical conversation uses states of completion rather than dates. A building is not “unfinished.” It may be awaiting lift, approaching fracture, under inheritance, or complete until moved. A person may say that an ancestor “is still installing” a pipe if the pipe’s planned deformation has not yet ended.

Conversation is low and close. Public debate occurs through illuminated text, hand signs, and boards that accumulate arguments in branching diagrams. In assembly halls, speakers stand beside pressure-sensitive panels and lay out proposals by touch. The audience answers with changes of color at their seats. A heated dispute can unfold in near silence, the chamber flushing iron-red, white, violet, then red again.

Anger is considered most dangerous when rhythmic. Stamping, pounding tables, or striking walls is treated not merely as loss of control but as reckless loading. Children are warned that the mountain does not know the difference between a machine and a tantrum.

Music exists, though much of it cannot be heard in the usual way. It is performed through tensioned floors, warm rails, hanging chains, and columns of contained air. Listeners sit with hands on polished plates or teeth resting lightly against ceramic bars. Compositions may last hours and contain only a few changes: a pressure building in the palms, a pulse passing from bench to bench, droplets shaken loose from a suspended web. Rim music is written for resonant rods and summit wind. No two performances are alike because the mountain retunes the instruments from below.

Their visual arts are more immediate. They polish stone to reveal arrested flows. They fill fracture maps with powdered metals. Portraits often omit the face and instead show the subject’s pattern of habitual movement through the city: lift descents, work stations, oxygen pauses, rain shelters, places where a hand commonly touched a wall. An elder’s portrait may resemble a plumbing diagram until one understands that every line is a journey repeatedly taken.

Food is one of the city’s guarded obscurities.

Some arrives from lower elevations in sealed freight capsules: dense meal bricks, oils, dried tissue, compounds that cannot be made in the basin. Yet the city could not live entirely on imports. In warm interior chambers, behind doors marked as chemical facilities, broad ceramic surfaces are kept damp with measured condensate. Translucent films spread there in branching layers, concentrating metal salts and fixing carbon from industrial gases. They are scraped, washed repeatedly, and rendered into gray pastes with a sour electrical taste.

Official documents call the films production cultures and distinguish them from the responsive deposits found wild in condensers. Workers make no such firm distinction. They say the cultivated sheets sulk when moved, flourish near certain engines, and sometimes reproduce the patterns of electrical faults before those faults are detected. A few old facilities maintain lineages of film said to predate the district around them.

The summit people eat these cultures. They also use them in medicines, seals, sensor plates, and the first soft linings of infant masks.

Thus the unresolved question of whether the deposits are alive is not abstract. It is breakfast, infrastructure, ancestry, and possibly citizenship.

The flange crawlers occupy a similarly unstable category. Maintenance offices classify them as mobile corrosion events. Pipe crews give them private names.

A crawler found on a warm conduit is rarely disturbed unless the conduit is failing. Its presence is sometimes taken as evidence that a seal is good; sometimes as evidence that failure has already begun. Workers leave washers despite regulations, arranging them in little stacks near pipe collars. In some districts the washers disappear. In others they are found days later sorted by alloy.

Children claim crawlers carry messages through utility spaces too hot and narrow for people. Engineers deny this, then quietly inspect any pipe beside which several crawlers have flattened themselves in the same direction.

There are stories of a crawler entering the mask of a sleeping technician and repairing a cracked valve from within. There are also stories of crawlers stripping the contacts from a living person’s respirator. Neither kind of story has produced a verified report. Both are told as warnings about gratitude.

The pale sail-like things above the basin are treated with greater formality. When one appears, work does not stop, but many faces turn upward. Its passage is entered into atmospheric records. Children are brought to windows. Some continuities suspend negotiation until the shadow has crossed.

They are called by a word that can mean visitor, shed surface, or thought passing over.

No one claims they are divine. No one tries to capture one. The one recorded attempt, centuries ago, is remembered chiefly as an engineering embarrassment involving three destroyed wind combs and six months of lost condensation.

Death on the summit is an issue of water, space, and dangerous sentiment.

Bodies are not buried; there is nothing here that accepts burial. Nor are they burned casually, because heat is public wealth and vapor must be accounted for. The dead are taken to reclamation chambers. Water is recovered. Volatile compounds are condensed. Mineral remains are separated, tested, and returned to the household in a small fired block.

These blocks are commonly set into moving parts of buildings: counterweights, sliding doorframes, bridge feet, expansion carriages. The dead do not become monuments. They become allowances for motion.

A person who tended a condenser may be placed in its turning ring. A lift keeper may enter the balance stack. Children who die before registration are sometimes incorporated into nursery walls, where the warm tiles make minute sounds at night.

Names endure only while something still depends on them. When the final structure bearing a person’s responsibility is dismantled, their records are closed. This can happen weeks after death or eight hundred years later.

Yet there are inhabitants for whom death may not mean the same thing.

In the deepest pressure halls, certain custodians have remained at their stations far beyond an ordinary lifespan. Their bodies are enclosed in support frames linked to oxygen, cooling, and mineral-feed lines. Over time the distinctions become difficult to inspect. Ceramic mask housings fuse to facial plates. Sensor threads enter old scars. Limbs are replaced by manipulators too heavy to lift elsewhere. Speech slows to a few transmitted pressures each day.

They are not described as machines. Neither are they visited as relatives. They are listed as occupants.

A deep custodian may spend a century listening to one chamber wall. When the pressure changes, it flexes a finger, dims a band of light, or alters the city’s water routing by a fraction. Some have not issued a signal in generations. Removing them would require stopping the systems that keep them alive, and stopping those systems would endanger districts above. Their survival and their function have become the same fact.

Perhaps this is the form toward which all summit life tends: not mineral replacing flesh, nor machinery imitating life, but obligations accumulating until organism and structure cannot be separated without killing both.

The city itself encourages that confusion. It breathes through vents, drinks through roofs, warms its extremities, seals wounds, and adjusts its weight as the ground rises. Its citizens move through it like blood cells or thoughts. The responsive films follow its electrical gradients. Crawlers clean its joints. Pale sails feed, perhaps, on the columns of heat it releases. Deep custodians sense pressures no unaided body could perceive. Beneath them all, the plug ascends.

Ask a summit inhabitant whether the mountain is alive and they will answer no.

Ask whether the city is alive and they will ask what measurement you intend to use.

Ask what lives there, and after a long enough pause they may place one hand against the nearest wall. Not because the wall is the answer. Not because they are.

Because here, to live is to participate in a motion too slow for any single body to complete.
