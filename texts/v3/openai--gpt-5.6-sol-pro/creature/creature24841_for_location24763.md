Those who live beneath the leaf are difficult to distinguish from the city at rest.

A curled sleeper resembles a knot of wet root fiber. A water-keeper crouched over a vein might be mistaken for a pale gall until the gall raises one narrow hand and lays its fingers against the wall. Even in motion they seldom disclose themselves all at once. First there is the pressure of a foot on a bridge, then the retreat of a sleeve, then two dark eyes briefly interrupting a row of shell markers.

They are small enough to shelter beneath a snail’s lifted lip, though few willingly do. They are slight rather than delicate: narrow through the chest, long in the forearm and foot, with joints made prominent by generations of climbing, bracing and passing sideways through places that close without warning. The youngest move almost on all fours. The oldest often do so again. Their shoulders can draw inward until the arms lie close against the ribs, and their hands are broad-palmed, with blunt nails and sensitive pads at every fingertip.

There is no single color to them. In the bruised green interval they appear gray, brown, cream, or faintly violet, but these are mostly the colors of what clings to them: pith dust, tannin stains, powdered fungus, algae rubbed from a low ceiling. Their own skin is pale where it is usually covered and darkened at the face and hands by sap, cold and constant abrasion. Fine hair lies over much of the body. It does not keep out the cold so much as reveal the movement of air. A sleeping citizen will wake when the hairs along one cheek bend toward an opening passage.

Their eyes are large, but sight is not the sense by which they trust the world. Brightness pains them. Distance deceives them. Many cover their eyes during the green hour with thin masks cut from inner bark. They recognize one another by touch, breath, gait and the tiny private rhythms of the hand. A greeting is commonly made wrist to wrist, where pulse, temperature and tension can all be felt. Close kin may press foreheads and remain that way long enough to learn whether the other is hungry, fevered or afraid.

Their ears are small and often folded beneath furred caps, yet the bones of the jaw and face are acutely responsive to vibration. When uncertain, they set their teeth against a tool handle, bridge cord or exposed root and listen through it. A whole gathering may kneel with mouths closed against the floor while something heavy moves outside the leaf. What they hear in this way is not sound as larger creatures know it. It is direction, mass, hesitation. A beetle is a buried sequence of shoves. A snail is continuous pressure with a faint rasp inside it. A salamander is weather advancing on four points.

Their clothes are layered for water rather than air. The innermost wrappings are soft moss felt, frequently changed. Over these they wear woven root hair, shed larval silk and narrow strips of pith beaten until flexible. The outer layer is usually a coat of overlapping seed scales, oiled with wax and tied loosely enough to be discarded if it freezes to a wall. Hoods carry long tactile fringes that brush the ceiling before the skull does. Belts are hung with wedges, resin pads, cord, shell scrapers and small empty vessels. Nearly everyone carries something with which to close a wound in the plant.

Nothing is allowed to hang freely. A dangling clasp can strike a stalk. A loose tool can betray a stairwell. Ornament is therefore sewn flat: a blue-black sliver of wing case at the throat, rings of white shell around a cuff, the translucent skin of an egg stretched over a tiny frame. Status is not announced by brightness but by materials that remain serviceable after long use. An elder’s coat may be patched with fragments from twenty floods, each repair recognizable to those who know where its cloth was grown.

They do not appear to have come from one people.

Some families are long-footed and nearly hairless, accustomed to the wet lower courts. They can stand motionless in cold films of water while feeling for pressure changes through their soles. Others are compact and heavily furred, with powerful hands suited to splitting pith and hauling bridgework. A few lineages have lived so long near the warm roots that they sicken in the outer districts; their children are born pink-skinned and impatient, always seeking fermentation chambers, kitchens and crowded sleeping rooms.

There are disagreements about whether these differences preceded the settlement. The city is young, but its people arrived with old habits and incompatible stories. Some say they descended from reed cavities high above the marsh. Some name a drowned settlement beneath the black water, where houses were built inside hollow stems and the sun could sometimes be seen as a white disc. Others refuse to speak of an earlier home at all. To ask where they came from is, in certain households, to ask what was lost.

They have no ruler seated above the rest. There is nowhere reliably above, and no seat would stay authoritative for long.

Instead, obligations gather around kinds of attention. Water-keepers hear the veins. Turners study where new tissue will split. Wall-tenders manage fungus and scar growth. Cold-handlers work near the frozen margins, cutting wedges and storing food where decay slows. Quieters inspect workshops and bridges for dangerous noise. Track-readers interpret the passage of animals. Those who know warm roots oversee hatcheries, births and sickness, though the root itself is considered to have the final judgment.

These offices are not quite professions. A water-keeper who fails to notice a rising vein may become a bridge-plaiter, but does not cease to know water. Skills accumulate across a lifetime because every district can vanish. No one is permitted to become useful in only one place.

Children learn the city first as a sequence of textures. Before they are taught district names, they are led blindfolded from home to the nearest high refuge. They crawl with one hand on a guide cord and the other reading the floor: slick vein, rough scar, three shell chips, soft bridge, cold wall, upward root. They repeat the route until they can follow it half asleep and with water around their knees. Later they learn alternative routes, then routes that do not yet exist but may open when the plant contracts.

Their games resemble rehearsals for disaster. They build districts from rind scraps and let snails move through them. They race falling drops along channels, redirecting the water with splinters. They hide warm seeds for one another to find by touch. In one game, a child becomes the salamander and must locate the others by vibrations while they cross a stretched membrane. Adults disapprove of the game’s noise and teach it anyway.

Birth takes place near warmth, but never directly upon a warm root. The people believe that roots remember pressure. A laboring parent is supported in a sling while attendants place their feet and hands against living surfaces, spreading the strain so no single rhythm carries far. Newborns are quiet. Those who cry strongly are wrapped against a warm chest, and the chamber is thickened with moss until the sound is swallowed.

The city’s hatcheries are not only for their own infants. Beetle grubs, moth eggs, snail clutches and certain pale crickets are tended there in separate cells. Some are food, some provide silk, shell, oil or frass, and some are kept because their behavior gives warning. A particular transparent mite leaves warm tissue several hours before it floods. A roof cricket falls silent when a large predator presses against the outer leaf. Tiny snails climb before the lower channels rise. The inhabitants read these creatures as other peoples might read instruments.

Their relations with the city’s animals are never simply those of keeper and kept. A beetle large enough to crush a house may also reopen a sealed gallery. Moth runners eat food stores but carry fungal spores needed to soften old pith. Springtails clean rot from the bridge undersides until, all at once, they strip the bindings too. Snails are tolerated, harvested, redirected, marked and occasionally venerated. The slow spiral of a shell is one of the few forms that seems stable here.

Predators are spoken of with precision and without hatred. The needle-faced hunters are called by the channels where they wait. Spiders are named for the geometry of their lines. The salamander has no personal name. Naming it as an individual would imply that it can be bargained with. It is called the Near Moon, the Pink Pressure, the Mouth at the Roof, or simply the Arrival.

When it comes, the people do not run unless a path has already been chosen. They flatten themselves into wall recesses, draw their limbs beneath their coats and cool their breath against wet moss. Some carry sealed bladders of stale air for this purpose. Track-readers lie with palms on the floor, passing warnings through nearly imperceptible taps. Two taps close together mean weight. A long pause and one tap mean tongue. A pattern that begins but does not finish means the sender has been taken or has judged further tapping too dangerous.

Loss leaves little behind. Bodies cannot be buried in a ground that drinks and rearranges itself. Nor can they be burned. The dead are washed in cold vein-water and wrapped in material taken from their own houses. Tools are removed, repaired and redistributed. Then the body is placed in an old pith chamber with fungus cultures, scavenging mites and a layer of torn leaf.

This is not described as returning to the plant. The inhabitants are too observant to claim a gentle unity where there is digestion. They say instead that the dead are “made available.” What the city takes may rise as warmth, thicken a road, feed a pale vine or vanish into a beetle. Grief resides in the inability to know which.

Names are temporary in a different way. A child receives a household touch-name, a short pattern traced on the palm. Later names attach to acts, places or accidents: She-Who-Heard-the-Closed-Vein; Moss-on-the-Third-Notch; Bent Awl; Two-Bridges; Found-under-Ivory. Because streets alter, a place-name may outlive the place and become incomprehensible. Because work changes, an act-name may become ironic. A person may bear several names at once, using each with a different district.

Spoken language exists, but much of it is breathed rather than voiced. Consonants are softened. Words are clipped before they can resonate. Important discussions take place under hanging mats or inside chambers padded with fungus felt. For distance, they use root-tapping codes, scent marks made from crushed plants, and cords tied with arrangements of knots and hard beads. A message carried physically is considered more trustworthy than one transmitted through the city, since plant tissue can distort a tap and predators can imitate simple rhythms.

Their laws are similarly material.

Do not take water without closing the wound.

Do not cut across a vein you have not listened to.

Do not leave salt on living tissue.

Do not block a path merely because it leads nowhere today.

Do not claim a wall that can grow.

Do not make another household bear your noise.

Nearly every serious offense is understood as forcing one’s danger outward. Theft is condemned less because an object has an owner than because its absence may leave someone unprepared. Violence is terrible because blood provokes contraction and draws hunters. Reckless construction is a crime against districts not yet formed. Hoarding high ground during a rise is among the worst acts imaginable.

Disputes are brought to open scars, places where old stalks have detached and the plant’s surface is comparatively insensitive. There the involved parties lay out tools, cords, broken seals or samples of damaged tissue. Witnesses touch these objects in silence before anyone speaks. Judgment is usually an obligation to repair, carry, watch or yield space. Confinement is rare; every able body may be needed when the water rises.

They do not count wealth chiefly in dwellings. A house can close around its owner. Wealth lies in dry cord, viable fungus, uncracked shell, remembered routes, trustworthy hands and favors that can be called upon quietly. The most prosperous households maintain sleeping places in several districts and insist that none is their true home.

Meals are small, frequent and communal. They eat softened starch scraped from old plant stores, fungal curds, algae paste, mite eggs, slivers of snail, fermented pith and the sweet contents of certain capillaries when these can be taken without harming the crown. Food is warmed in sealed vessels nested against metabolizing roots. Nothing is roasted. Texture matters more than flavor in the cold: crisp shell edge, elastic fungus, fibrous root, smooth fat. Children are given warm sugar sap during a flood rise, both for strength and to keep them still.

They cultivate bitterness. Bitter compounds slow spoilage and discourage small grazers. Most adult food would seem medicinal to an outsider. Sweetness is associated with danger as much as pleasure; it signals rising sap, new growth and the animals that follow both.

Rest is taken in groups. Solitary sleep is considered impractical, almost extravagant. Bodies are arranged in layered hollows around a warm vessel or root, the outer sleepers rotating inward over the course of the rest period. One remains partly awake to watch the behavior of mites on the wall and feel for changes under the floor. Dreams are recounted not for prophecy but for environmental clues. A sleeper may have felt a slow constriction and turned it into a dream of narrow tunnels. Several similar dreams can prompt an inspection.

They have music, though they rarely permit it to enter the walls.

It is made against the body: fingertips drummed on the hollow of another’s palm, teeth clicked with the mouth covered, strings stretched between the player’s own wrists, breath passed across tiny shell chambers held inside thick wrappings. A gathering may share a song that no one beyond the circle can hear. The melody is partly pressure, partly pulse. To an observer it would look like a group holding hands in silence.

Their oldest stories are told this way. They concern enormous exposures: the ripping open of a roof, the appearance of unfiltered light, rain as broad falling bodies rather than drops, wind strong enough to move the world without touching the ground. Whether these are inherited memories or inventions is unclear. Many listeners find them horrifying. A world without a ceiling seems less like freedom than the absence of shelter.

They do not worship the mother plant as benevolent. It crushes houses, starves channels, seals the lost into walls and makes no distinction between careful citizens and careless ones. Yet they grant it a kind of slow personhood. Not a mind like theirs, but an accumulation of responses too coherent to call accidental.

When a street thickens beneath repeated feet, they say the plant has learned weight.

When a scar curls safely around a dwelling, they say it has accepted company.

When a district constricts after bloodshed, they say it has refused the manner of living there.

The oldest negotiators spend hours with their skin against new tissue before recommending a cut. They test it with warmth, pressure, water and diluted sap. They may postpone a needed stair because the root has “not answered.” Younger builders sometimes mock this caution, right up to the moment a careless wall begins to heal over their tools.

At the Still Court, during an ordinary low phase, the people are most visible. Water-keepers stand on their thin platforms with toes gripping the fiber. Scavengers sort what the channels have delivered. Children search for useful shell. Messengers arrive along the white-root road and touch wrists with those waiting there. The black film reflects none of them clearly.

When the water reaches the first notch, conversation shortens.

At the second, households divide according to duty. The quick and unburdened carry tools, food vessels and hatchery trays upward. Wall-tenders seal lower doors. Quieters walk the bridges once, cutting any line likely to snap under water. Those unable to climb are bound into lifting slings. No one is abandoned merely for moving slowly; slowness is too common a condition to be treated as failure.

At the third notch, the people ascend among everything else that lives there.

Their order mingles with the city’s larger migrations. Snails pass beside them. Worms emerge from pith under their hands. Springtails flow over their boots. Mites stipple their coats. Pale crickets fold themselves into the same crevices. The distinctions between citizen, livestock, pest and wild thing become temporarily useless. All are bodies seeking the remaining air.

In the upper chambers the inhabitants pack close enough to feel one another’s hearts. Tools are wrapped. Infants are passed inward. Every surface sweats. The plant swells, narrowing the refuge until backs press the roof and knees press strangers. It grows warmer, but no one mistakes the warmth for safety.

They wait without speech while the city becomes an organ around them.

Some keep count through touches. Some recite routes that may no longer exist. Some hold fragments of old metal, not because metal can help them then, but because it has survived other places and other pressures. A dull bright needle passes from hand to hand. A green hook is pressed into a child’s palm. Bent Awl finds Two-Bridges in the dark by the patch pattern on a sleeve.

Then the water withdraws.

They do not descend immediately. First the track-readers listen. Then a water-keeper tests the wall. A small animal is allowed ahead, often a snail marked with a thread of white fiber. Only when its movement remains ordinary do the first scouts go down.

They find doors displaced, bridges softened, fungus bloomed across familiar markers. A new ridge may have split a plaza. A house may have become a sealed lump in the rind. Occasionally an entire avenue is gone, closed so perfectly that newcomers doubt it was ever there.

The inhabitants grieve, measure and begin again.

They scrape back the fungus but preserve a little for the doorway lamps. They cut trapped cord free. They collect drowned mites for the hatcheries and salvage shell from the lower streets. They listen to new veins. They test the ivory shoots. They retie survey lines in places the plant has not yet refused.

Soon the tapping resumes: dispersed, careful, almost indistinguishable from water.

That is how one knows the city is inhabited—not by seeing its people, but by hearing intention return to the dark.
