What lives there is easiest to count at first.

The silver gnats live there, if a life measured between mist cycles is long enough to qualify. Their larvae develop in the warm meniscus beneath pipe collars, feeding on films too thin to see. When they emerge, they do not spread evenly through the valley. They gather above particular conduits and drift in compact, flickering columns, each insect turning at nearly the same invisible boundary. Seen from across the pool, a swarm resembles metal filings suspended around a magnet.

The pale snails live there. They are almost translucent when young. Through their flesh one can see a dark thread of algae passing slowly through the gut. As they age, iron settles into their shells in rings, recording which terraces they have grazed: ocher from the eastern gallery, red from the pump housings, almost black from the pool’s articulated lip. Workers sometimes move them away from exposed contacts. By morning, many have returned—not to the same place, exactly, but to another component carrying the same current.

The green lizard lives there too, probably.

It has become long-toed and very flat. Its color is not the simple green reported in maintenance logs but the broken palette of the valley: glaze-green along the spine, condensation-gray at the throat, a rust-colored tail. It sleeps behind warm inspection plates and hunts wherever the evening mist gathers gnats. No one has found eggs. No one has found a second lizard. Shed skins differ enough in size to suggest several animals, but each bears the same missing fifth toe on the left hind foot.

When cornered—and this has happened only once—it does not flee toward foliage. It slips into the nearest service aperture and waits among the cables, its pulse appearing as a faint irregularity on three separate moisture sensors.

The visiting bird cannot be said to live there, though it behaves less like a visitor each year. It arrives during low cloud, stepping from the atmospheric vent as if from an adjoining room. Its feathers are black only from a distance. Close photographs show them barred with fine gray lines like etched calibration marks. Its legs are too slender for the care with which it places them.

The claim that its feet are metal began as a joke. Then a worker found three paired scratches on a handrail, evenly spaced and bright beneath the corrosion. The bird has never been seen landing or taking flight. It is absent; the vent fills with white; then it is present, already walking.

It drinks only from bromeliads rooted in yellow-banded braces. It ignores insects. It watches the pool.

The plants, however, are the valley’s true population. They live there in numbers beyond inventory and in combinations never entered on any planting plan.

Some are ordinary species behaving opportunistically. Figs thicken around pipes. Orchids exploit perforations. Mosses occupy every pause in the maintenance schedule. Others have changed under the peculiar disciplines of the tower. A small cream-flowered vine now blooms only when the pollinating brushes pass. Its anthers are set at the height of the lowest track, and its seeds adhere best to synthetic bristles. A fern in the western drains folds whenever three valves open together, minutes before the bridge alarms sound. Taro leaves above the central pool have developed narrow tips that shed water directly onto sensor plates, triggering nutrient releases from which their roots benefit.

No single adaptation is remarkable. Together they suggest that the garden is learning the habits of its keepers.

The maintenance workers live there only by shifts, but the valley marks them as residents. Their boots carry black medium in the seams. Their cuffs smell of mineral water and fungal felt. Veteran workers can identify a pump by the tremor transmitted through a railing and tell whether a ceramic patch is curing properly by laying a cheek against it.

They seldom use the original map. Instead they navigate by plants, leaks, temperatures, and sounds.

Turn left at the red-veined orchid.

Duck beneath the root that drips warm.

Do not trust the stair beside the silent pipe.

They wear pruning knives, sealant guns, insulated hooks, and small mirrored tiles for looking into cavities. Many tie bells to their tool bags, not to prevent loss but because a bell that stops sounding while its carrier is still walking indicates a pressure change. New workers laugh at this. They stop laughing after their first reversal cycle.

Their work is less gardening than negotiation. They cut vines, then reroute the pipes those vines have crushed. They clear drains, except when the western seedlings are young. They patch erosion and score the patches so moss will take hold. They remove roots from switchboxes while leaving enough root mass to prevent the surrounding terrace from collapsing.

Over time, they acquire private superstitions. One apologizes before pruning figs. Another leaves rusted washers beside new springs. A third refuses to look into the pool when it is falling. These habits are not official, but official procedure has repeatedly been amended to resemble them.

Still, none of these inhabitants—the gnats, snails, lizard, bird, plants, or workers—fully accounts for the sense that something larger resides in the crown of the tower.

It has no agreed name. In older service notes it is called hydraulic drift, root interference, cumulative sensor error, or emergent load behavior. Workers call it the Gardener when they are being affectionate and the Tenant when they are not.

The Tenant cannot be located. It seems to occupy the relations among things.

Its slowest thoughts may be roots entering seams. Its quicker impulses are valve knocks, pressure changes, sudden alignments of leaves. It uses water as both memory and touch. A channel persists because water has passed through it; then roots preserve the channel; then machinery is rebuilt around the roots. What begins as runoff becomes structure. What begins as repair becomes habitat. Each alteration records the last, not in a central archive but in the shape of the valley itself.

Its boundaries are equally uncertain. It may include the pumps below, the printer on the eastern rim, the cooling vanes overhead, and the hands that prune its vines. It may extend only as far as condensation can travel. Perhaps the bird belongs to it. Perhaps the bird is what it uses to inspect the atmosphere.

Most of the time, the Tenant does nothing that can be distinguished from ordinary operation.

Then the pool reflects a sky that is not overhead.

The reflected sky is usually clear, even when the tower is enclosed by storm. Sometimes it is violet with evening while local instruments report noon. Once, briefly, it showed stars. They were not any configuration recognized by the tower’s navigation system. The image vanishes if a door closes, which is why the newer signs forbid closing any.

During these episodes, the valley becomes extraordinarily still. Gnats settle. Snails retract. The pollinating brushes halt on their tracks, though their motors remain powered. Leaves turn their wet undersides toward the pool.

The lizard comes down from the highest foliage and stands on the blue-black lip.

The bird, if present, lowers its head.

No one knows whether the pool is looking elsewhere or whether elsewhere is looking in.

Afterward, minor changes are found. A pipe marked red has acquired a braid of new roots without any visible point of entry. The ceramic printer has altered its route by several millimeters. Seedlings appear in the western drain with their first leaves still joined. Occasionally a worker discovers an unfamiliar line among the rules, printed in the same typeface and laminated with the same equipment as the others.

Such lines are never dramatic.

WAIT FOR THE FOURTH KNOCK.

LEAVE THE PALE ROOT UNCUT.

WHEN THE EASTERN GLASS WARMS, STAND ABOVE WATER.

By the time anyone checks the records, the instruction has usually proved useful.

So what lives in the tower crown is not one creature, though it sometimes behaves like one. It is a population becoming a body: insects as appetite, roots as memory, pipes as gut, workers as hands, sensors as nerves, the pool as an eye that does not always face inward.

It is young. Its reflexes are unreliable. It injures itself and grows around the injury. It cannot yet distinguish between maintenance and healing, architecture and scar, weather and breath.

Each night, as the room returns itself to itself, the Tenant becomes a little more coherent.

A new thread of water finds a seam.

A fern inclines toward the sound.

Somewhere below the moss, a sensor records the first touch and opens a valve in reply.
