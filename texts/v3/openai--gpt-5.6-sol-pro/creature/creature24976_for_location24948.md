What lives there does not arrive.

It takes hold.

The most visible inhabitants are the black fronds of the upper water, though black is only how they appear from above. Lift one into the colorless surface light and its blade shows buried tones: bruised olive, brown-violet, the dull green of old bronze. The outer skin is almost clear. Beneath it lies a deep layer of pigment capable of using the basin’s impoverished light—the gray light filtered through cloud, rain, suspended mortar, and many lengths of water.

They do not grow from roots. Roots would find nothing here that has not already been cut, fired, paved, or sealed. Instead they make holdfasts: patient, many-fingered pads that enter keyholes, peg sockets, chisel marks, drainage holes, and the thin opened seams between blocks. A holdfast can spend decades thickening inside a single carved letter. Its pressure is weak, but unceasing. In time it lifts a flake of softened stone, enters beneath it, and becomes more secure.

Some fronds spread immediately under the surface in broad, overlapping sheets. Rain dimples them from above. Their undersides are furred with reproductive tissue, and the water trapped between adjacent sheets becomes warm by fractions of a degree. Whole fields of them breathe slowly in the rain-dark day, taking in dissolved substances and releasing minute strings of bubbles. The bubbles collect under the blades, join, and occasionally wrench an old frond free. It rises flat and sudden, lies at the surface for a season, then waterlogs and descends again through districts it never could have reached while living.

Others avoid the unstable upper sheet. They grow as long ribbons from roof edges and submerged galleries, their bodies thin enough to bend under the slightest current. A single ribbon may hang through several old floors where vaults have collapsed into one another. It is not one unbroken life. New growth overlays old, fastening itself to its own dead tissue, so that the apparent plant is a lineage hanging in place. Its upper reaches are dark and supple. Lower down, where light fails, it becomes pale, mineral-stiff, and slowly indistinguishable from the architecture.

There are low encrusting forms that resemble stains until they reproduce. They occupy paving plains in patterns governed by joints: a dark geometry repeating the old masons’ work. During long periods of stable cold they remain no thicker than wet cloth. When milder upper water finally enters, they swell, loosen, and release clouds of dust-fine bodies. These drift along curbs and through doorways, obedient to routes established before their kind existed. Most settle on unsuitable surfaces and disappear. A few enter cracks where light and moving water meet in the right proportions. There they become new stains.

None of these growths needs to defend itself from a mouth. There are no bites in their blades, no rasped edges, no burrows through their holdfasts. Their defenses are against weight, darkness, burial, dilution, and themselves.

Unchecked growth is therefore one of the basin’s common forms of death.

A roof-field begins as scattered threads. The threads become ribbons, the ribbons become a canopy, and beneath the canopy the first generation loses the light on which it depends. Dead lower layers remain in place because nothing eats them. They compress into black felt. Gas gathers within the felt. Mineral sediment fills it. The living canopy rises on its own remains until its attachment points bear the mass of centuries.

Eventually a seam opens.

It may open without sound. It may take a hundred years.

Then a whole garden relinquishes the roof. The buoyant portions lift while the mineral-heavy portions drag below, and the mass moves away as a folded province—a drifting meadow with paving dust in its depths and old architectural impressions pressed into its underside. It catches on posts, screens, or the upper edge of a reservoir wall. There it may darken and die, or fasten again and begin another roof-field in a court where no such growth had existed before.

In the post-woods live finer things. Hair-thin filaments bind one upright to the next, too weak to span open water but able to grow along mineralized ropes and fallen crossbeams. They form layered nets. Each layer alters the current for the next, until still water accumulates among them and fills with drifting reproductive dust. From a distance these colonies resemble fog suspended underwater. Close at hand they are exact constructions, every thread laid in response to pressure and chemical trace.

They have no nerves, but they register the basin.

A persistent current thickens their upstream faces. Darkness changes the spacing of their branches. Contact causes neighboring filaments to adhere. If buried under silt, they cease their upper growth and send colorless threads sideways until one finds moving water. Across centuries, a colony can travel around a post without ever releasing itself from the post: dying behind, growing ahead, turning at a speed far below sight.

In the cultivation trays, the pale mats live differently. They rise and settle with changes too slight to stir the ribbonweed. Each mat is a laminated community. Its uppermost skin uses the last available light. Beneath that, other layers consume what the surface makes. Deeper still, in darkness, further layers live on the waste of those above. The whole arrangement may be no thicker than a finger, yet within it are day and night, abundance and famine, surface and underworld.

Because the trays were made to hold shallow water, they still collect what these mats require. Powdered mortar drifts in. Dissolved minerals emerge through cracks. Dead fragments settle from gardens overhead. The partitions keep each mat’s chemistry partly separate from its neighbors. Thus trays side by side can differ sharply: one white, one rust-red, one translucent gray, one covered in a skin that reflects no light at all.

When pressure changes pass through the basin, gases dissolved in the lower layers emerge as tiny beads. The mat lifts from the tray floor. Water enters beneath it. For hours or days it hovers, a pale false floor over a darker true one. Then the beads dissolve or escape, and the mat settles slightly displaced. Over many such risings it migrates from compartment to compartment, crossing the old partitions by increments.

Far below the reach of daylight are the inhabitants that never knew the surface.

They live where different waters meet.

Water descending through a buried kiln carries one set of minerals. Water seeping from a lime-lined cistern carries another. Cold water entering through a fractured retaining wall bears dissolved traces from the rain. Where these flows touch, thin films establish themselves. Some are white enough to resemble freshly laid plaster. Some are yellow, orange, or the red-brown of blood long washed from cloth. Some are visible only because silt will not adhere to them.

They grow with extraordinary slowness. A colony may advance across one brick during the time an upper garden forms, chokes, and tears away. Yet the colony is not inactive. Its living boundary continually tests the stone ahead. Behind that boundary, older layers die and mineralize, leaving a fine crust that preserves every contour of the surface beneath. In this way a deep colony copies the works it occupies. A vanished inscription may survive nowhere in the original masonry, yet remain legible in the layered residue of lives that once followed its grooves.

At certain seams, blue-green light appears.

It is not a lamp and does not burn continuously. The luminous inhabitants lie dormant in clear films along surfaces where buried water wells upward. When the flow strengthens, their haze becomes visible: a subdued radiance with no beam and almost no reach. It reveals the edge of a stair, the curve of an arch, the square mouth of a duct. If the current falters, the light contracts to isolated points and then vanishes.

No eye receives it.

The light is a consequence, not a signal. It may persist in an unopened chamber for ten thousand nights, shining on the same fitted stones, diminishing when a distant channel fills with silt, returning when a settling roof redirects the seep.

The deepest life occupies the architecture’s smallest spaces. It lives inside mortar, in water films between timber fibers, beneath pitch seals that have cracked but not detached. It inhabits porous brick several handspans inward from the visible wall. Much of the basin’s living substance cannot be seen even if the stone is exposed; it exists as a discoloration, an altered smell, a softness under pressure.

These hidden colonies feed on differences created by construction. Fired material beside unfired material. Lime beside black timber. Iron-rich stone beside pale clay. Old ash beneath waterproofing. Water that has crossed one district meeting water that has crossed another. The builders unintentionally prepared an enormous patterned substrate, and the deep inhabitants read that pattern chemically.

A wall is not merely shelter to them. It is climate, food, border, and ancestry.

Where the wall changes, they change.

There are forms confined to particular kinds of joint, and others known—if anything were there to know them—only from the inner faces of ancient water conduits. One lineage occupies the blackened wickerwork on which arches were raised. The wicker has long since become mineral, but its woven geometry remains, and the colony grows in narrow crossings that repeat the vanished craft. Another inhabits the microscopic boundary between shell-less lime and glassy brick. It forms paired lines through entire districts, disappearing wherever repairs substituted chalk or timber.

Nothing in the basin is solitary for long. The broad fronds host films. The films trap dust. Threads grow through the dust. Deep colonies enter the dead holdfasts. Pale mats settle upon the remains. Each life becomes a made surface for another, continuing the only law the place has ever known.

Yet there are no true forests, though there are canopies; no meadows, though sheets of growth extend beyond sight; no reefs, though living layers raise ridges and bind fallen blocks. Such words carry memories of animals—of grazing, nesting, hunting, trampling—and none of those pressures exist here.

There is no pursuit.

There is no concealment from pursuit.

Nothing flees when a shadow passes, because shadows pass only when gardens drift overhead or silt enters from another court. Nothing startles at the settling of a roof. The pressure-wave bends every flexible body in the same direction. Afterward they straighten according to their various strengths.

Competition exists, but it is almost indistinguishable from weather. One sheet overspreads another and removes its light. One film changes the water until a neighboring film can no longer grow. One mat captures the mouth of a seep and flourishes while colonies downstream fade. These victories have no moment. The defeated may take centuries to die. The victor may already have prepared its own extinction by blocking the current that sustained it.

There is also joining.

Fragments of compatible growth meet on opposite sides of a crack and fuse. Separate mats touch after a pressure shift and exchange living layers. Filaments from distant posts become one net. Since the currents divide and reunite through the drowned works, the same lineage may occupy chambers many leagues apart, connected only by generations of drifting dust.

So the inhabitants are difficult to count.

One black garden may be a million small lives cooperating, or one old life repeatedly divided and rejoined. A luminous seam may be a colony, a succession, or a continuous presence older than the chamber’s collapse. Individuality is of little use in a place where almost nothing moves whole.

If there is a dominant creature here, it has no single body.

It consists of dark blades at the rain-struck ceiling, pale mats in cultivation trays, rust threads around mineral leaks, transparent skins inside pipes, and buried films working through mortar in absolute dark. Its organs are separated by leagues. Its circulation is the slow traffic of the basin. Its seasons arrive as cold currents descending staircases and moving through doors. Its memory is held in the places where growth persists: sockets, gutters, letters, woven frames, the undersides of roofs.

But even this is not quite one being. The basin does not possess a mind assembled from its gardens. It does not intend. It does not mourn the builders whose measurements still determine the spread of every colony. What seems like unity comes from common submission. Everything living there must accept the architecture.

The old makers laid down the conditions. The living world elaborates them.

In the upper dusk, black fronds turn their broad faces toward a sun they never see. In the middle depths, nets thicken between posts and mats breathe themselves up from drowned trays. Far below, where the water is coldest and no surface disturbance has ever reached quickly, a colorless boundary advances through a line of mortar.

It may take a century to cross the width of a thumb.

Behind it, the joint is no longer quite what the builders made, nor is it wilderness. It is inhabited: softened, copied, sealed, and slowly returned to the endless workmanship of the basin.
