At first the inhabitants seem made of the same limited substances as the plain.

They are clay-colored from dust, ash-dark around the eyes, and glossy at the shoulders where condensation settles on their clothes. Their garments are wrapped rather than tailored: aprons of oiled hide, stiff kiln-cloth, braided cord, overlapping ceramic scales tied loosely enough to kneel in. Nothing hangs freely if it might catch on a projection. Hair is bound flat beneath caps or plastered against the skull with the translucent cistern film. Ornaments are worn inside collars and sleeves, where they cannot fall into machinery.

They are not a small people, but low ceilings have entered their posture. Even in open courts they stoop slightly, as if passing beneath an unseen lintel. Their feet spread wide from a lifetime on wet tile. Their toes are strong. Many can pick up a wedge, steady a rolling jar, or test the vibration of a floor without bending.

Their hands distinguish them most clearly. Fingers are broadened by work, nails worn short, fingerprints filled with pale mineral paste. Burns make smooth islands across palms and forearms. A person’s craft is readable in the distribution of scars: rope workers have dark grooves at the bases of their fingers; sounders carry bruises along one ear and one knee; kiln tenders have lashes singed blunt; cistern divers bear white seams where salt has opened old cuts again and again.

Children learn surfaces before words. They are encouraged to slap walls, scratch mortar, breathe against glaze, and press an ear to the pavement. Their first toys are nested cups, knotted strings, clay rattles with different wall thicknesses. Adults hide these rattles in sleeping chambers and ask children to find them by tone. A child who can hear hollowness early is praised. A child who hears too much is watched carefully.

Almost everyone develops a fine sensitivity to tremor. Conversations pause when jars quiver. Sleepers wake when a familiar drip fails to strike its usual basin. Some claim to recognize relatives by the rhythm of footsteps through several walls. Deafness is therefore feared, but not treated as uselessness. The deaf are often entrusted with watching pressure columns during communal hammering, since they are less easily confused by echoes. No capacity is discarded if it can be turned at another angle.

They organize themselves less by blood than by obligations to particular structures.

A household may consist of the descendants of those who maintain one kiln throat, the adoptees of a drain crew, an elderly map-firer, three orphaned children, and a stranger who once noticed a ceiling beginning to shear. They call themselves by the thing they keep: the People of the Ninth Sluice, the West Lip, the Warm Arch, the Three Cracked Jars. If the structure is destroyed, the household does not necessarily dissolve. It carries a fragment away and installs it elsewhere. Thus a family named for a door may live for generations in a district where no door exists.

Authority belongs temporarily to whoever understands the present danger. During a flood, gutter carriers command. During a ground hum, sounders command. At a kiln collapse, the oldest potter may be pushed aside by a child small enough to crawl beneath the sagging shelves. Leadership that persists beyond its usefulness is regarded as another kind of blockage.

Still, certain people accumulate influence. These are the Rememberers, who know what lies beneath a district—not from a complete map, for no complete map exists, but from inherited sequences of failure. They can recite that beneath the blue-edged court is a plugged reservoir, beneath that a room of leaning jars, beneath that a pavement which rang clear in the time of someone’s grandmother. Their knowledge is sung because prose is too easily altered. The melodies are awkward, full of pauses indicating unknown chambers and notes held long to represent load-bearing walls.

They distrust smooth accounts. A map without corrections is presumed false. A person who answers too quickly is thought dangerous.

Birth takes place near warmth, usually at the back of a cooling kiln. A blue stone, if the household possesses one, is placed beneath a bowl of water so its color wavers on the ceiling. The newborn’s hand is pressed into a tile before washing. The tile is fired but not displayed. It is set into some necessary surface—a drain wall, a stair, the lining of a cistern—so that each life begins by becoming load-bearing.

Names are provisional in childhood. A child may be called Drip, Little Wedge, Ash-Ear, or Seventh Step until some act reveals a better name. Adult names commemorate observations more often than bravery: Heard-the-Change, Found-Warm-Water, Turned-the-Gutter, Did-Not-Cross. The highest praise is not that someone built well, but that they noticed in time.

The dead are rarely buried. There is nowhere that is not already occupied, and digging downward is perilous. Bodies are dried near kilns, wrapped in clay, and incorporated into memorial columns or sealed benches. The shape is not preserved. To resemble a person would make the object merely commemorative; to bear weight allows the dead to continue their obligations.

This practice produces unease around old supports. Some contain ancestors. Some contain rubble. Some contain nothing but a hollow around which later generations built. Knocking upon a memorial is disrespectful, but failing to test a structural column is negligence. Before tapping an uncertain support, sounders apologize.

Food arrives by routes few individuals travel from end to end. Dense cakes of salt, fat, fermented cistern film, and pale meal are exchanged in sealed jars. The meal may come from creatures raised in warm chambers, or from a district where something grows without light; answers vary with the merchant. Small drain animals are eaten after floods, though children often hide them until they can escape. The black cistern swimmers are not eaten. Their bodies resist knives and leave tools smelling of storms.

Nothing edible is wasted. Grease lights lamps before entering stewpots. Bone becomes pegs, pegs become grog, and grog strengthens clay. Even breath is sometimes captured beneath inverted cups to test whether a sealed room contains air that can sustain life.

The most honored workers are not necessarily potters, despite the abundance of clay. They are listeners.

They travel in groups of three or five, carrying sounding rods, cups of colored water, chalky slips, and fired tablets covered in earlier readings. One strikes. One listens close. One listens at a distance. The others watch walls, water, flame, and one another’s faces. They distrust any conclusion reached by a lone ear.

When a note changes during the striking, discipline gives way to speed. Nearby lamps are pinched out to preserve air. Children are bundled into carrying nets. Doors are wedged open so pressure cannot seal them. No one runs until the sounder runs; then everyone does. Afterward, survivors may argue for years over whether the pitch truly changed or whether fear changed the listener.

Their religion, if it is religion, has no creator. Creation is an alarming idea to them: too final, too deliberate, too much like the steady rhythm.

They offer no thanks for the world’s existence. They acknowledge continuance. Small cups of water are left beside repaired cracks, not as gifts to spirits but as invitations to reveal leakage. Before raising a wall, builders place one intentionally flawed brick where it can be reached. Perfection is considered brittle. A thing without allowance for correction is already a trap.

Their ceremonies are practical actions repeated until practicality and reverence cannot be separated. Routes are chosen by setting blue stones on fired maps and watching where condensation gathers. Marriages are marked by splicing two load ropes, which the pair must later use in actual work. Quarrels may be settled by requiring both parties to carry opposite ends of the same gutter through a crowded district. Those who cannot match pace cannot maintain hatred with dignity.

They fear regularity more than chaos. A random knock may be settlement, water, labor, or some blind creature below. A measured knock implies intention. Children are stopped from drumming evenly on bowls. Work songs drop beats and change tempo. Public hammering is coordinated through patterns designed never to repeat exactly. Master rhythm-keepers introduce mistakes with great precision.

During the nights of answering taps, everyone participates. The old strike floors with canes. Infants are given rattles. Potters beat kiln sides with padded mallets. Even the dying are brought a shard and something hard enough to sound it.

They do not believe their noise silences what is below. They believe it answers a question with ambiguity.

Other lives occupy the plain, though the people disagree about where life begins.

The translucent cistern skins recoil, divide, and creep toward hairline cracks. Kiln workers insist they are only mineral appetite. Sealers speak to them softly and feed them smoky water. In some reservoirs the films arrange themselves overnight into angular patterns resembling district maps, though never maps of the district in which they are found.

The white drain creatures are tolerated as weather signs. If they emerge carrying droplets in single rows, ordinary seepage is expected. If the droplets cluster, pressure is rising. If they emerge dry, whole lanes are evacuated. No one knows why.

The black swimmers respond to submerged bells but not always by approaching them. Sometimes they form circles beneath the sound. Sometimes they vanish for a season. Once, according to a fired record kept in the Basin district, every swimmer in seven cisterns rose at once and held itself vertically in the water, all facing the same direction. Excavation in that direction uncovered a warm door. The door has never been opened.

As for the vast thing in the utility passages, the inhabitants refuse to give it a single body. They say “the drag,” “the polishing,” or “the pressure that bends grates.” A limb seen in one century may not belong to the same creature as a feeler seen in another. It may be an animal, a colony, a mechanism made of flesh, or the exploratory part of something living beneath many levels at once.

The brick rings erected around its appearances are both warning and accommodation. Offerings are not left there, but routes are kept clear. Some believe the thing is trapped below. Others believe the people are living on the inside of its shell.

And in the empty districts, under the ordered tiles, are the possible inhabitants no one sees.

The tapping there is too patient for collapse and too widespread for hands, unless there are very many hands. Some Rememberers say the lower builders are ancestors sealed beneath successive pavements, still maintaining their own inverted districts. Some say they are future inhabitants, constructing upward toward a world that is not ready for them. A few suggest there are no beings below at all—that the layers themselves have learned the habits of labor from the blows falling through them for ages.

This last idea is considered impious in some quarters and obvious in others.

For the people of the plain, a living thing is whatever notices pressure and alters its work.

By that measure the crustaceans live, the films may live, the buried creature certainly lives, and the plain itself has not yet decided.
