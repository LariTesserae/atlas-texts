What lives in the Lower Nursery does not live there in the manner of an animal.

There is no furtive population behind the leaves. No pale custodians sleep in the boiler rooms; no children have learned to balance on the root-hung walks. The residences are empty. Beds, where they remain, are shelving for seed trays. Cupboards have swollen shut around coils of irrigation cord. In tiled washrooms, ferns rise from the basins and send their roots through the traps. Whatever human routine once passed between lamp, bench, locker, and canteen has been reduced to fixtures in a larger routine that no longer requires witnesses.

The most obvious inhabitants are the plants, but even they are difficult to separate into individuals.

A vine may begin in a freight bin, cross three roofs, descend a ventilation stack, root again in a gutter, and emerge fifty yards away as what appears to be another plant. Curtains of climbing fig are communal bodies whose oldest stems have fused into woody mesh. Reeds in distant settling tanks share cuttings from the same original stock. Moss is transferred inadvertently by maintenance brushes, pressed in green signatures along every route the machines travel. The trees in the loading hall exchange water through grafted roots and branches rubbed raw against one another until their tissues joined.

Thus the Nursery contains fewer distinct lives than its abundance suggests, and larger ones.

One of these lives occupies most of the western courtyards. It has no local name now, though old aluminum labels buried in its roots carry several catalog numbers. It began as a shade vine trained against a residential wall. Its leaves are hand-sized in the brighter galleries and broad as trays in darkness. Over generations of cutting and rerooting, it has spread through cracked planters, pipe insulation, roof beds, and suspended troughs. A single old stem passes through the wall of the pump house and has thickened until the bricks fit around it like teeth. Newer runners extend from this central mass in every direction.

The vine responds to the district slowly but comprehensively. When a lamp fails, its leaves turn over the course of weeks toward neighboring light. When a steam line is shut down, roots abandon the cooling masonry and thicken around the next warm conduit. Its extremities are always exploring: pale tips entering keyholes, drain mouths, cable sleeves, and the narrow seams around inspection plates. Maintenance blades cut them back. They return—not stubbornly, because stubbornness would imply opposition, but because an open seam is as legible to the vine as a street.

Elsewhere, tougher colonies keep to moving water. Liverworts lie flat against the channels, black-green and translucent, enduring periodic scouring when the cisterns overflow. Sedges inhabit the marsh court in dense rectangular nations corresponding exactly to buried foundations. Their roots have filled the drowned rooms below with fibrous masses. Each time the fans change speed, their blades incline together. They do not seem to bend so much as acknowledge a pressure passing through them.

The hall trees are the nearest things the district has to elders.

They were never meant to become large. Their freight-bin beds are too shallow, their spacing too close, their roof too low. In response, they have become architectural rather than graceful. Their trunks widen sideways against bin walls. Their roots travel along the rails and enter neighboring beds. Their crowns flatten under the trusses, producing long horizontal limbs that rest on steel, pass through window frames, and return from the roof cavities clothed in condensation.

Their bark contains the history of the hall. Wire tags have vanished into it. A ladder is held upright inside one trunk’s growth. Insulators, bolts, broken lamp cages, and the porcelain handle of a crane control have been enclosed halfway, as if the tree were slowly remembering them. Where branches touch active machinery, scheduled cutting keeps a narrow clearance. Those wounds form ridged lips of callus. The trees carry dozens of such boundaries: records of every place where growth met a rule and was redirected rather than stopped.

Beneath them live the less visible inhabitants.

Films of bacteria line the ceramic pipes, arranged in bands according to temperature and mineral content. Pale fungi penetrate the leaf beds and dead roots. They fruit rarely, and only inside enclosed digesters or beneath loose tiles where the managed air cannot carry their spores far. Their threads lace the accumulated litter into wet felt. They are the Nursery’s quietest workers, breaking down what has fallen while sealed vats perform the same labor more rapidly nearby.

There are no worms to open the beds, so fungal cords occupy the channels that worms might have made. There are no insects to carry pollen, so flowers address the machinery.

They open according to the lamp cycle. Some produce excessive pollen, dusting nearby leaves yellow before the collection fans start. Others hold their blossoms at a uniform height beside the pollination rails. The brushes arrive with the deliberation of weather: soft cylinders passing from flower to flower, vibrating stems, withdrawing powdered with one row and delivering it to the next. Certain plants have, over many propagated generations, come to fit this visitation precisely. Their flowers face the aisle. Their stamens stand where the bristles reach. Blossoms turned toward walls contribute nothing to the next stock and gradually vanish from the lineage.

The flowers are not beautiful in any generous sense. They are functional, repetitive, sometimes almost grotesquely accessible—wide throats, exposed structures, pale markings visible beneath electric light. With no animals to entice, their color has drifted toward what the cultivation system recognizes and preserves. Some are nearly green. Some reflect sharply under the propagation lamps while appearing gray elsewhere. Their scent, where it persists, accumulates uselessly in the still air: pepper, hot wax, bruised fruit, damp cloth.

Among all this growth move the ward’s mechanical inhabitants.

They are not clever. Nothing in the Nursery thinks its way through a problem. The crane crosses the loading hall because a controller closes contacts at appointed hours. Brushes pollinate because cams and rails carry them. Rakes draw leaf matter from gutters. Float valves call pumps into motion; pressure switches release them. Inspection trolleys creep through pipe galleries trailing sensor wires and cutting back anything that protrudes into their measured clearance.

Yet age and repetition have given these machines something like temperament.

One pump strikes harder on cold starts and settles after nine beats. A ventilation damper resists opening until pressure bows it, then gives with a report felt across two courtyards. The hall crane pauses near its eastern rail because the track there has subsided beneath a root. Its motor hums, the suspended hoses sway, and then the wheels climb the imperceptible rise. This hesitation happens on every crossing. Leaves have grown according to it. They close around the motionless load during the pause and are dragged gently aside when it resumes.

The maintenance rakes are low, broad devices with slatted fronts. Their original paint survives only beneath their housings. They travel grooves cut along the gutters, gathering soaked leaves until drag exceeds a set limit, then reverse toward the digesters. Roots sometimes seize their rails. When that happens, the rake repeats the obstructed section for days: advancing, loading itself against the root, withdrawing, and advancing again. The repeated abrasion eventually kills the soft tip or polishes a channel through it. Thus the machine and plant negotiate without either recognizing the other.

Anything capable of moving has acquired such a zone around itself. The district can be read by these clearances. Narrow tunnels through foliage indicate valve arms. Ovals of bare brick mark fan wash. Semicircles cut into moss show the sweep of access doors that still open automatically, though no one enters. In this way absent human bodies remain implied throughout the Nursery. Handrails are kept clear for hands that do not come. Walkways are brushed to the width of shoulders. Work lamps brighten before scheduled maintenance shifts and dim afterward.

The systems continue to prepare the district for its keepers.

At intervals, an enamel cabinet unlocks with a heavy click. Inside, indicator lamps report reservoir depth, steam pressure, nutrient concentration, rail position, and air cleanliness. A printer advances a strip of paper and records figures in fading purple. The reports gather in the tray until humidity binds them into a single swollen block. When the tray fills, later pages concertina onto the floor. Climbing fig has reached them and rooted through the record, so that years of measurements now hang beneath the cabinet as a damp, veined beard.

There may once have been a final worker.

Evidence of habitation persists in one room above the marsh court: a kettle scaled white inside, rubber boots hardened beneath a bench, a row of hooks labeled with names. On the wall, someone kept a calendar long after the printed dates ceased to matter, marking pump inspections, cuttings, filter changes, and fan reversals in increasingly compressed handwriting. The marks stop not at a warning or farewell but halfway through an ordinary maintenance cycle.

Whatever happened afterward, the systems did not interpret it as an ending.

Water continued to be lifted. Lamps came on. Brushes crossed the flowers. The crane carried cuttings from racks that were replenished for a while by automatic propagators, then carried empty racks, and later carried vines that had rooted into the racks themselves. Steam kept arriving from the city. The ward breathed on schedule.

Now its true inhabitants are not merely the plants or machines but the arrangements between them.

Warmth inhabits the iron and calls roots toward it. Vibration inhabits the active pipes and keeps their surfaces bare. Water inhabits every downward path until a pump returns it upward. Foliage inhabits whatever remains still. Fungi inhabit whatever foliage falls. Machines inhabit the corridors their motion continually creates. Each presence makes the boundary required by another.

No creature oversees the whole. The district’s coherence exists among countless small responses: a float rising, a valve opening, a stem turning, a condensate drop falling, a root encountering heat, a rake encountering the root. Taken separately, none maintains the Nursery. Together, they have become maintenance itself.

If something can be said to live throughout the Lower Nursery, it is this compromise.

It has no face and no hidden central body. Its pulse is staggered across pump rooms. Its breath comes through fans. Its memory is held in tree scars, mineral shelves, redundant pipes, and paths repeatedly cleared through green growth. Its skin is not the brick boundary seen from outside but the shifting edge between what may close and what must remain open.

It grows slowly into every mistake. It wears out in several places at once. When a lamp dies, a little region of it turns toward darkness; when a drain blocks, it develops a marsh; when a machine stops, leaves begin at once—though almost imperceptibly—to convert the silence into structure.

The Lower Nursery is inhabited by a life too distributed to flee and too entangled to die all at once.
