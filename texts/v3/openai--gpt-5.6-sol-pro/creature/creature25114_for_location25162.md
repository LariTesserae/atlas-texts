The people who live there are not, at first, the most visible inhabitants.

From the southern stair, a newcomer notices birds before people: bronze-backed birds hanging motionless over the northern rim, black pigeons erupting from shrine ledges, nectar birds flickering among folded leaves. Then come the monkeys, watching from roof rails with solemn, thieving hands. Only after these do human faces resolve from the same colors as the town—russet cloth, mineral-stained skin, pale bark-fiber hats, eyes darkened against dust.

They tend to be compactly built, though not uniformly small. Broad feet and strong toes are common, as are thickened palms and a slight outward turn of the knees acquired from a lifetime on stairs. Even the elderly place a foot with remarkable precision. People seldom walk abreast. They move in loose single files, touching walls, rails, trunks, and one another as they pass. A hand at the back or elbow is not an intrusion but ordinary courtesy: a quiet confirmation that another body is secure.

Their balance is admired, but caution is admired more. Recklessness has no heroic place among them. A child who performs near the edge is not praised for courage; the adults become cold and silent, which is worse than shouting. The highest compliment paid to a porter, mason, midwife, or stair keeper means, approximately, *nothing entrusted to them is lost*.

Infants spend their first years in close slings, carried against chest or back. Their cradles are shallow baskets tied to two points. Once they begin to walk, they wear little harnesses in the outer quarters, though these are not spoken of as restraints. They are called second hands. Children first learn the town as a sequence of grips: the root beside the dye house, the three wall hollows below the moth garden, the smooth rail at the shrine turn. Adults give directions in the same way. A route is not “left, then north,” but “under the bitter vine, hand to the cool wall, past the place where the wind stops.”

Many inhabitants never descend the mountain. Some never see the southern stair below its upper gate. They know the low country through goods that arrive powdered with alien dust, through traders’ stories, and through the behavior of haze. Their languages contain precise distinctions for kinds of height, exposure, footing, shade, and moving air, but descriptions of broad rivers or uninterrupted plains remain metaphorical. A lake may be called an uncovered cistern. A grassland is a roof garden without walls. The sea, when spoken of, is generally treated as a traveler’s exaggeration.

Their skin is rubbed with oils, ash, and aromatic resins against wind and dryness. Hair is usually braided close or tied beneath cloth, not only for neatness but because loose hair catches seeds, dust, sparks, insects, and low branches. Water is not used for casual washing. People clean themselves with fine clay, smoke, scraped fibers, warm stones, and oils pressed from bitter kernels. The resulting human smell is not unpleasant but dense: resinous, mineral, smoky, and faintly animal.

Clothing is wrapped and repaired rather than cut into elaborate shapes. Long scarves protect the mouth during dusty weeks and become carrying slings, shade cloths, bandages, filters, or rope padding as needed. Garments acquire visible histories. A grandparent’s robe may survive as a child’s jacket, then as seed bags, cistern seals, wick fiber, and finally plaster reinforcement. Bright color is expensive, but small areas of it are loved intensely. A blue thread, a copper bead, or a feather of clean yellow may be moved from garment to garment for generations.

They do not regard patched things as poor things. An object without repairs may be admired for novelty, but it has not yet demonstrated character. The most valued bowls show dark lines where cracks have been bound and sealed. Door lintels bear the impressions of several architectural periods. Even ceremonial blades are sharpened until their original forms become narrow and strange.

No single occupation is sufficient on the crown. A potter may also keep moth flowers and maintain three lengths of public rail. A singer may know how to seal cistern lids. Children are taught to mend baskets, identify dangerous stone sounds, and recognize the taste of spoiled condensation before being permitted to specialize. Expertise exists, sometimes at astonishing depth, but everyone is expected to understand the first principles of survival.

Water keepers hold particular authority. They are not rulers, exactly, and they do not own the cisterns. They remember capacity, seepage, household claims, contamination, and the sound of jars by weight. Their hearing is trained until they can distinguish a vessel half full of clean water from one holding slightly more water tainted by mold. They inspect night cloths before dawn, supervise seals, and decide when a cistern chamber must be opened.

They are watched as closely as they are respected. No person is permitted sole custody of water records. Measures are witnessed. Cistern marks are cut publicly into clay tallies and repeated aloud by children selected from unrelated households. Suspicion around water is considered healthy; secrecy is not. The greatest crimes are deliberate fouling, concealed leakage, and false measures. Theft is serious, but waste inspires a deeper horror because it benefits no one.

Their hospitality therefore differs from that of wetter countries. A guest is not immediately offered a brimming cup. To do so would be theatrical and perhaps insulting. Instead the host touches the sealed drinking jar, names the household’s water, and offers a measured mouthful in a small dark bowl. The guest drinks all of it. No residue is left to evaporate. Among close friends, the bowl is passed without wiping.

Food is dry, oily, sour, and concentrated: dwarf-grain cakes, mashed beans, roasted beetles, antelope curd, smoked lizard, bitter leaves, pollen paste, wax-sealed fruits, seeds ground with resinous spices. Fresh fruit is eaten before sweetness draws too much moisture from the plant. Soups are rare and ceremonial. Steam from cooking is trapped beneath fitted lids and condensed on sloping tiles. A cook who allows vapor to escape is considered careless.

There are many tiny meals and few large ones. People chew slowly. Roof gardens are harvested plant by plant rather than by plot, and some families know individual bean vines by name. The distinction between cultivated and wild food is uncertain. A shrub growing from a monkey-dropped seed may be claimed by the household that shades it, tended by the family above, pollinated by moths from a shrine garden, and harvested by children from the lane below. Ownership is therefore layered. Arguments concern duties as much as rights.

Houses are similar. A room may belong to one lineage, its roof to another, the tree piercing both to a shrine association, and the stair along its wall to the public. The town’s law is largely a law of attachment: what rests on what, who must support it, who receives its shade, who clears its fallen leaves, who repairs damage when roots shift the stone.

No one possesses an unrestricted right to alter anything structural. Before a wall is raised, neighbors gather to study its shadow and wind. Before a branch is trained, those living above and below must agree where it may thicken. Disputes can take years, but impatience is viewed as evidence that a proposal has not yet matured. The town has survived partly because most decisions are allowed to become slow enough for hidden consequences to appear.

Their governing assemblies meet in several cramped courtyards rather than one grand hall. There is nowhere large enough for the whole population. Messages pass between gatherings through runners who move by interior routes and roof bridges. Each runner repeats a statement exactly and carries a knotted cord confirming its sequence. Thus public debate travels through the town in waves. By the time an argument reaches the western quarter, the eastern quarter may already be answering it.

Voices matter. The crown’s people cultivate distinct speaking registers for narrow spaces, open roofs, windy rims, and cistern chambers. Private speech is low and close. Market speech is pitched to pass beneath awnings without startling animals. Rim calls use long vowels that remain intelligible in rising air. Certain families specialize in carrying announcements across the town, not by shouting louder but by choosing tones the stone returns.

Music is similarly architectural. Flutes are played into wall hollows. Drums are set on particular pavements so that their vibrations travel through adjoining houses. Clay bells identify wind direction, time of night, and occasionally danger. Some passages produce fixed notes, and singers gather at their mouths to braid harmonies with the mountain’s moan. A song may require a doorway, a season, and a specific direction of wind; performed elsewhere, it is considered incomplete.

Silence also has forms. At the daily opening of the condensation cloths, households avoid loud work. Not because sound harms the water, but because the interval is used to listen for drips, torn fabric, jar fractures, and changes in the wind. At funerals, conversation ceases along the route to the western chambers. Birdsong and monkey calls continue, so the silence is never absolute. The dead leave amid the ordinary noise of other lives.

The people do not imagine death as a fall. Falling belongs to accident, severance, and terror. The dead are placed horizontally within the mountain, knees slightly bent, hands wrapped around a seed packet or a piece of repaired household ware. Their names are carved near the chamber mouths, but the mineral skin slowly softens the marks. This erasure is expected. Descendants recut a name for as long as it remains actively remembered; when no one can speak of the person except as an ancestor, the carving is allowed to close.

Some chambers have been sealed for so long that roots have entered them. Such roots are never cut. Plants emerging nearby are called return growth, though no one insists that the dead literally become trees. Belief on the crown tends to resist hard boundaries. A thing may be ancestor, mineral, memory, and useful shade at once.

Their sacred beings are less like distant gods than enduring relations: the mountain that bears without promising kindness; the wind that enters every house but stays in none; the night haze, which gives almost nothing and yet gives what water there is; the roots that find paths no mason can see. Shrines are small because sacredness does not require empty space. Offerings consist of resin smoke, first seeds, polished stones, insect wings, knotted fibers, and spoken accounts of completed repairs.

Water is almost never offered. A few drops may be used in the rarest rites—to receive a newborn with no living siblings, to reopen a long-sealed cistern, or to settle a feud involving death. The drops are touched to the participants’ tongues, not poured onto stone.

Their priests are often gardeners, undertakers, weather readers, or keepers of old structural knowledge. One religious office belongs to those who crawl into spaces beneath houses and listen. They interpret cracks, insect activity, root pressure, air movement, and the hidden resonance of bedrock. Their pronouncements may sound prophetic, but much of their power comes from observation accumulated over generations.

A separate fellowship cares for the southern stair. Its members are lean, scarred, and unusually familiar with distance. They descend in teams, carrying replacement rungs, pegs, rope, food, and sealed water. Unlike most inhabitants, they know the mountain’s sides: the places where sun strikes bare stone, the ledges where travelers freeze against the cliff, the caves occupied by hawks, the handholds that become treacherous under dust.

Stair keepers are admired and considered slightly foreign even when their families have lived on the crown for centuries. They return speaking loudly, craving enclosed sleep, and dreaming of unsupported space. They bring news, but news is treated as one treats imported fruit: desirable, perishable, and likely to contain something troublesome.

Traders who reach the top are lodged near the southern gate until they learn the basic rules. Their water is counted. Their loads are searched for burrowing insects, mold, and seeds capable of overwhelming shallow gardens. Metal is weighed against communal need before private buyers bid for it. Outsiders often mistake this caution for hostility. In fact, crown households are intensely curious. During a caravan stay, visitors may find themselves questioned for hours about rain, rivers, wheeled carts, deep soil, large predators, and the color of an unobstructed noon.

Children are particularly fascinated by mud. They have clay, plaster, and wet plant pulp, but few have seen ground become soft under rain. Tales of sinking ankle-deep into a road produce equal parts delight and disgust.

The animals living among them are not domesticated in any simple sense. Antelope are marked by neighborhood and discouraged from certain gardens, but no one fully owns them. Monkeys are cursed, bribed, trapped, released, and occasionally adopted when orphaned. Pigeon-like birds provide eggs, feathers, and droppings, yet nest where they please. Families maintain favorite gecko lines within their cistern chambers and may exchange the animals as carefully as seed stock.

The heavy owls are regarded with formal respect. A roof chosen by one is considered fortunate, despite the pellets and bones beneath its perch. Foxes are tolerated until they begin taking sleeping birds. Snakes are identified individually when possible: some are removed to the cliff gardens, some left beneath granaries to hunt mice, and a few killed immediately. The town’s children know these distinctions early. “Animal” is too broad a category to determine conduct.

Nor are people understood as separate from the crown’s ecology. A household may describe itself by the species it tends: the night-flower people, the red hive people, the keepers of the western thorn. Marriages transfer not only kinship obligations but pollination routes, seed knowledge, roost access, and rights to gather dung. A poorly chosen feud could interrupt a chain of care on which several gardens depend. Social peace is therefore practical before it is virtuous.

Marriage often begins with work. Two people may agree to restore a roof tray, train a vine, or maintain a section of condensation cloth together for a year. If their partnership survives heat, scarcity, relatives, monkeys, and repeated repairs, a household is built around it. Households need not consist of a single couple. Widowed siblings, unrelated craftspeople, elderly teachers, foster children, and itinerant stair keepers attach themselves in durable combinations.

Because space cannot be created easily, joining a household is partly an architectural negotiation. Where will the new sleeper lie? Whose smoke hole must be moved? Can a niche be deepened without weakening the wall? A marriage may wait three years for a room the width of two sleeping mats.

Beauty is associated with steadiness, attentiveness, and skillful hands. Songs praise well-placed feet, a voice that does not waste breath, shoulders shaped by carrying, and fingers able to free a moth from sap without damaging its scales. Decorative scars from accidents are not hidden, but deliberate scarification is uncommon; the body receives enough marks from work.

The elderly are rarely removed from daily life because no part of town is exclusively residential or productive. An old person seated in a doorway may sort seed, watch toddlers, note which antelope entered a garden, hear a change in a cistern seal, and settle three questions of kinship before noon. Memory is infrastructure. Elders recall what lies inside walls: a disused flue, an old crack, a beam no longer visible, a burial niche behind later masonry. When memory fails, parts of the town become dangerous or inaccessible.

For that reason, knowledge is repeated obsessively. Work songs encode mixtures for plaster. Children’s games rehearse safe routes during smoke or tremor. Family stories explain why a certain wall must never bear another room. Genealogies include trees, cisterns, and repairs alongside births. A person may say, “My mother’s mother opened the east chamber, and in her time the white-root lifted the stair.”

They do not count age only in years. Someone may be “born before the splitting of the third hive,” “five flowerings older than the north bridge,” or “of the generation that heard rain.” Actual rain reorganizes memory. Those who have witnessed it describe the whole town becoming briefly unrecognizable: roofs drumming, gutters shouting, leaves unfolding in daylight, people laughing and panicking as water appears everywhere at once.

During such rain, discipline does not disappear. Jars are set out, channels cleared, cistern mouths opened in sequence. Yet afterward there are stories of adults standing bareheaded in the lanes, mouths open, wasting drops on their faces. This is forgiven. Some abundance is too strange to meet with dignity.

What most distinguishes the crown’s people is not austerity, though outsiders often see only that. They are not perpetually solemn. Crowding has made them comic, nosy, theatrical, and skilled at quarrels conducted inches apart. They imitate monkeys. They compose insulting songs about bad masonry. Neighbors conduct romances across shared air shafts and maintain grudges over shade boundaries for decades. Festivals fill the roofscape with masks of bark and feathers. Dancers perform in place, using shoulders, hands, eyes, and minute shifts of balance because there is no room for sweeping steps.

Their luxuries are shade, fragrance, music, color, and time given freely. A person with little water may still own twelve flowering plants. A family may spend years breeding a moth whose wings show a violet edge at dusk. Resins are blended into perfumes that evoke different quarters of the town. One smells of kiln smoke and bitter blossom; another of warm feathers and the northern wind.

At evening, when the leaves unfold, people emerge onto roofs. Work continues, but conversation loosens. Bats pass close overhead. Birds settle by the hundreds. Condensation cloths are stretched and checked. Children are called back from the outer lanes. The day’s heat rises from the black pavements into bare feet and sleeping mats.

From a distance, the inhabitants might seem trapped on their pillar. They do not usually describe themselves that way. Constraint, to them, is not the opposite of freedom. It is the shape within which skill becomes possible: the ledge that teaches the foot, scarcity that teaches the measure, crowdedness that teaches attachment.

Their common word for the whole inhabited crown—the houses, roots, animals, graves, gardens, cisterns, and summit stone together—also means something like *that which holds because everything is holding everything else*.

They apply the word to the mountain.

They also apply it to themselves.
