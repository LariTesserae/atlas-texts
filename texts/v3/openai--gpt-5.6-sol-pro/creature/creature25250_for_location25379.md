At first the inhabitants appear fewer than they are.

This is partly because they avoid the center of streets, where falling water must be given precedence, and partly because their clothing takes on the colors of whatever stands behind it. Waxed coats are rubbed with lime, soot, green copper, or brick dust according to district. A person waiting beside a wall may look like an irregularity in the masonry until the wall lifts one hand.

They are human, or near enough that the distinction has little daily importance. They are generally slight, with strong wrists and shoulders developed by ladders, valves, and hand pumps. Their skin has the pallor of things seen through water, though not all were born below. Some have the brown or ruddy complexions of the upper districts, preserved for years in the chamber’s amber light. Age arrives strangely on them. Children look solemn because their hair is always plastered flat; the elderly look youthful at a distance because damp softens their faces. Up close, one sees mineral stains in the creases of their hands and pale crescents where moisture has thickened the nails.

Their eyes are not unusually large. That is an upper-world invention. They simply look longer.

A citizen may stand before a gauge for ten minutes without touching it, watching the needle tremble through changes too small for a visitor to notice. Conversation pauses for drips. Heads turn when a frog shifts inside a pipe. Even infants learn the difference between ordinary silence and the silence preceding a valve’s release.

No one walks quickly unless carrying fire.

Their soft-soled gait gives the settlement its initial impression of emptiness. The soles are layered from felt, eel leather, and a white fungal rubber grown in warm culverts. Children are taught to place the whole foot down at once. Heel-striking is discouraged, both for noise and because old flagstones sometimes contain hairline reservoirs that burst upward under a sharp tread.

The city’s oldest families call themselves bank people, though no open river has occupied the chamber within memory. They live in the brick houses nearest the channel and inherit responsibility for stretches of retaining wall. A family does not own the wall. It belongs to the wall in the sense that its marriages, apprenticeships, and sleeping arrangements are reorganized around whatever the masonry requires.

Names pass through these obligations. Someone may be called Mara Third East Joint, Ilven Under-Spout, or Old Sen of the Bowed Course. Such names are not titles of rank. They indicate where a person can most reliably be found.

There are other lineages: the column keepers of the Condensary, the root wardens, the gauge readers, the wet-house attendants, the sump technicians, the cooks, the animal counters, and the quiet masons who turn worn stairs in the night. Trades overlap. Nearly everyone knows how to wrap a leaking pipe, identify six kinds of mold by touch, and lift a sleeping fox without being bitten. Specialization begins only where a mistake might take longer than one lifetime to become visible.

The youngest apprentices are assigned to droplets.

Each receives a numbered drip and keeps its record for a year: interval, weight, taste, temperature, cloudiness, the shape of its splash, and any creature that visits the resulting cup. The work is considered foundational. It teaches that repetition is never exact and that small differences, given patience, become architecture.

Apprentices often become attached to their drips. They give them secret names and defend them from drafts. At the end of the year, some cry when the record passes to another child.

Adults do not find this foolish.

Households are larger than families and less permanent. A dry room may contain three generations, two unrelated valve workers, an invalid from the western wall, and whoever has been displaced by a newly active seep. Beds hang from ceiling chains so damp masonry cannot reach them. People sleep in narrow cloth cocoons filled with shredded reed and warmed stones. Before entering, each sleeper washes their feet but wets the threshold again afterward.

Privacy exists, though it is understood acoustically rather than spatially. A felt curtain means do not look. A copper bowl placed upside down means do not listen. Lovers meet in abandoned pressure cabinets, on high galleries, or behind the Condensary drums, where the machinery’s slow breath swallows small sounds. Courtship often begins with the lending of a dry object: a book, a box of matches, a paper flower. To entrust someone with dryness is intimate because dryness must be guarded.

Children are plentiful enough to surprise visitors. They occupy overhead places: pipe racks, cross-braces, roof ledges, the warm tops of transformer housings when the foxes permit it. They build tiny channels from wax and ground ceramic, then release spoonfuls of water through miniature districts. Their games are elaborate disputes between flood, thirst, blockage, and lawful dripping. The child playing the city usually wins, but only after surrendering several buildings.

They are warned never to follow the silver gnats and never to count the bats aloud. They are also warned about the sump, though less effectively.

The settlement has no single ruler. It is governed by custodianship, which means authority gathers temporarily around the person responsible for whatever is happening. During a pressure loss, the pipe keeper commands. During a Long Wetting, the western surveyor does. If a bell carrier blocks a public drain, the youngest certified animal handler may order senior machinists out of the street.

Civic meetings take place on the central bridge. Speakers do not address the crowd continuously. They state a proposal, then wait through one complete cycle of the brass drip. The pause can last several minutes. Impatient people fare poorly in public life.

The inhabitants do not worship water. They consider worship too simple a relation.

They bargain with it, categorize it, apologize to it when diverting an established course, and occasionally curse it with great technical precision. Funeral water is kept separate from drinking water for one hundred turnings of Wheel Seven. Birth water is added to the household threshold dish. When someone leaves permanently for the upper world, a cup from their home is poured into the channel so that some portion of their accustomed moisture remains.

The dead are not buried in the chamber. Burial would obstruct seepage, and burning would damage gradients. Bodies are carried up a narrow shaft beyond the root garden. What returns is a small fired tile impressed with the person’s thumb and a groove representing the course of water beneath their home. These tiles are installed wherever repairs need a shim. Thus the dead continue as tiny corrections: under a stair, behind a pipe bracket, between a warped doorframe and the wall.

No one knows how many of them support the Condensary.

Their cuisine is pale, soft, and strongly scented. Fungal loaves steam in communal kitchens. Eels are baked in clay sleeves with bitter root tips and translucent cave onions. Fox grease is never eaten, despite recurrent dares among children. Mineral salts are valued with the seriousness other places give spices. A pinch from a root well tastes green; residue from the northern vanes gives food a faint metallic chill. Meals are taken after work shifts, but shifts themselves are set by machinery rather than day. Consequently breakfast may occur beneath the gray shafts of noon, while supper is eaten when rain has only just begun above.

The cooks are among the loudest citizens. Their rooms tolerate clatter, steam, argument, and occasional singing. Songs below are not built around melody so much as return. A phrase is repeated at widening intervals until listeners begin to anticipate it incorrectly. The finest singers can hold a room silent for half an hour before completing a line.

Not everyone is content.

Some younger residents resent the prohibition against moving drips. They point out that reverence and neglect can look alike. They want brighter lamps, faster wheels, dry archives, and pumps powerful enough to test the sump rather than wait upon it. They meet in the upper warehouses and call themselves, half mockingly, the Hasty.

The oldest citizens do not forbid them. They assign them maintenance of the fastest machinery available: small emergency turbines that can tear themselves apart in seconds. This either cures their impatience or gives it discipline.

A few people go above. Most return within a year. They complain that the sky has no ceiling by which to judge distance, that wind confuses smells, and that sunlight reveals too much at once. Those who remain above become intermediaries with cistern keepers, quarry offices, monasteries, drainage boards, and municipal departments that have forgotten the city exists but continue unknowingly to pay some of its invoices.

Conversely, strangers sometimes descend: engineers, fugitives, surveyors, lost sewer workers, children retrieving dropped coins from wells. The city receives them cautiously. Every newcomer is asked the same three questions:

What have you brought that burns?  
What have you brought that dries?  
What followed you through the water?

The last question is not ceremonial.

For the human inhabitants are not the only citizens, and may not be the oldest.

The frogs in the gauges are treated as unofficial inspectors. A gauge abandoned by its frog is taken apart, even if it reads correctly. Eels are marked by household and exchanged during breeding seasons. Bats have right of occupation over several upper rooms; rent, in the form of guano, is collected once a month. Tunnel foxes belong to no one, but their sleeping places are entered on utility maps because a transformer warm enough to attract three foxes may be approaching failure.

Bell carriers receive the greatest courtesy. Small ramps are built for them beside stairs. Grates are cleared before their customary arrival. If one dies, its mineral cup is removed and placed in the archive, where the layers can be read like a local history of water. The body is returned to the channel.

Some cups contain mineral bands older than the city’s foundation records.

There are quieter forms of life as well. Fungal membranes inhabit the walls behind kitchens and are fed steam through narrow ceramic mouths. Nearly invisible worms live in wetted thresholds, curling into letters no alphabet claims. The white reeds in the garden are each regarded as a single continuous organism, though their roots do not touch. During the rare years when one flowers, sleeping people across the city dream of open rain.

Then there is the Condensary itself.

No resident will plainly say it is alive. They say instead that Column Four dislikes limestone water, that the third drum becomes restless before storms, or that the ceramic vanes remember hands. Repairs made against the machinery’s tendencies fail. Repairs made with them last. In hidden places, wooden gear teeth develop polished hollows resembling joints; fungal paper thickens where strain will occur months later. The system responds not quickly enough to seem intelligent, but too appropriately to seem inert.

The same may be true of the city as a whole.

Its pipes extend beyond maps. Its animals perform maintenance no breeder remembers teaching. Its rules resemble customs until one is broken, whereupon consequences travel through brick, water, machinery, and flesh as though these were organs of one body. A neglected house dries; its frogs depart; dust enters a switchboard; a distant valve closes; pressure changes beneath the garden; one white reed bends. No individual oversees this sequence. Yet the settlement corrects around the injury.

Perhaps the inhabitants are not merely the people under the old ground. Perhaps they are the whole damp arrangement: citizens, foxes, salamanders, molds, engines, walls, root tips, and measured drops—each living at a different speed, each carrying some small function of memory.

The people seem to suspect this. It is why they repair without restoring, why they hesitate before moving anything that has found a rhythm, and why they do not speak of conquering the sump.

They live there, certainly.

But they also live within something.
