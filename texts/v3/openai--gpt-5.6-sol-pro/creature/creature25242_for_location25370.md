The people of the cleft are small only by the judgment of open country.

Seen on the plain, where distance makes a person into a moving stroke, they would appear slight: narrow-bodied, spare of flesh, wrapped against heat in faded layers the colors of salt, soot, old blood, and stone. Inside the town they seem otherwise. There, every limb is lengthened by ladders, ropes, ledges, baskets, and hooked poles. A child hanging one-handed from a balcony may reach farther than an adult standing below. An old woman seated in a doorway may command six roofs with her voice. A man no taller than his neighbors may be enormous because he knows every route through the walls.

They are born into height without ever seeing a mountain view.

Infants are carried close in slings, their heads covered against falling grit and bird droppings. Before they can walk, they learn not to startle when swifts burst past. Before they speak clearly, they learn the difference between the creak of a loaded bridge and the creak of an empty one. Toddlers are tethered on the upper levels, not as punishment but as naturally as a jar is stoppered. Their first independent journeys are vertical: sleeping shelf to floor, floor to threshold, threshold to the next household’s roof. A low fall is laughed at. A high one is not spoken of.

Their feet are broad at the toes and toughened early. Their hands have strong thumbs, ridged palms, and fine scars from stone, cordage, thorns, bites, hot jars, and broken shell. Many have bent little fingers from catching themselves on handholds. Their knees and elbows are polished with callus. They do not move through the cleft as strangers do, facing forward and seeking a clear path. They turn sideways, stoop, swing, pass loads overhead, and step backward onto ladders without looking.

The oldest scarcely appear to move at all. They sit where several passages meet and direct traffic with clicks of the tongue, finger signs, and short calls that carry cleanly through the noise. They know the town through vibration. They can tell whether a grinding stone is running empty, whether a goat is caught by its horns, whether wings overhead are pigeons scattering from a hawk or swifts turning together for no reason visible below. Some elders, nearly blind, can identify approaching relatives by the rhythm with which a ladder takes their weight.

Water has shaped their manners more deeply than hunger.

No one asks another whether they are thirsty. The question would be an obscenity or a joke. Moisture is offered according to need, rank, illness, pregnancy, labor, and debt, but it is never displayed. A stoppered cup passes beneath cloth. People drink with the vessel hidden against the mouth so that no one must watch the swallow. Lips are dampened before speech at weddings, judgments, and deathbeds; this is considered enough to make words binding.

Tears are treated uneasily. Children are taught not that crying is weak, but that tears belong inside the body. Mourners press ash beneath their eyes to catch them. Lovers may touch wet eyelids with a fingertip and place that finger on the tongue. To waste grief on the ground would be intolerable.

Their cooking is moist without appearing so. Leaves are bruised inside sealed pots. Roots are softened in warm ash, then pounded with fat. Meat is hung in shaded drafts until dense, smoked, and shaved into stews thick enough not to spill. Gourds are cut only when their inner flesh has reached the proper coolness. Blood is never discarded. Milk is cultured immediately or cooked into grain. Even the steam from an oven is led through bundles of dry stalks, both to soften them and to catch whatever the vapor will surrender.

They smell strongly to outsiders, though no single odor is dominant. Smoke lies in their hair. Bitter plant oils seal their skin. Clothes carry goat, resin, old salt, and the sweetness of dried fruit. Each household has a smoke mixture by which relatives can be recognized in the dark: thornwood and fig leaf, dung cake and aloe rind, pigeon feather and aromatic root. A lost child follows the smell of home.

Their faces are seldom bare. In the upper town they veil their mouths against feathers and dust. Near ovens they wrap their brows. Among the gardens they wear thorn guards plaited from fiber. But they do not hide their eyes, which are watchful and quick, accustomed to dimness and to movement at the edge of sight. They glance upward constantly. Everyone does. The sky is not above them as it is elsewhere; it is a narrow, inhabited river from which fog, birds, falling objects, danger, and light descend.

They have many professions, though no person practices only one.

A net keeper is also a weather reader, cord mender, bird rescuer, and judge of dawn. A potter must know roots, because jars are made to fit particular plants and particular wall niches. A mason is an undertaker. A pigeon keeper is a gardener. A child who tends beetle traps may also carry messages, turn drying fruit, and listen for mice in sealed stores. No craft can survive in isolation, and no one is admired merely for making a beautiful thing. Beauty lies in the number of purposes a thing can bear without failing.

Their finest objects can be mistaken for rubbish: a horn spoon repaired with copper wire; a cloak quilted from feed sacks and lined with hawk feathers; a ladder whose uprights were once roof beams and whose rungs are each a different wood. Newness carries little prestige. An object becomes honorable as it accumulates repairs. Tools have names only after their first breaking.

Possession is equally layered. A wall may belong to one household at hand height, another at roof height, the pigeons above, and the dead within. A fig tree belongs to the family that waters its roots, the family whose balcony supports its trunk, and the shrine that receives its first fruit. Disputes are rarely about whole things. They concern one ledge at dawn, three arm-lengths of shade in the hot season, the droppings beneath a roost, or the right to gather fallen blossoms before the ants take them.

Their judges are people with long memories and little haste. Hearings may pause for the sun blade to pass, for a goat to be removed from a roof, or for a witness to finish turning grain. A judgment is considered poor if it produces a single winner. The preferred settlement binds the rivals together until neither can profit without maintaining the other’s share.

They count wealth in continuities.

A rich household has many sealed jars, but more importantly, jars whose contents can be replanted. It has daughters and sons fostered on several levels. It has access to a fog line, a pigeon wall, a shaded bed, and a route through neighboring rooms should fire block the main stair. It possesses obligations others are proud to repay. Its dead lie in walls unlikely to be opened by accident.

Their ancestors are not remote. They are structurally present.

Children sleep with a grandmother’s bundle behind the plaster, knowing the outline of her knees by the bulge in the wall. A lintel may contain the hands of a mason who set it, dried and wrapped separately because the rest of him was lost outside. Families speak to walls before altering them. When bones are uncovered, work stops. The bundle is named if it can be named, fed smoke, and consulted through the recollections of whoever is oldest.

Those who cannot be identified are called the Deep Household. They belong to everyone. Their resting places are marked with small ledges where bees often build, and honey taken there is never eaten casually.

Death in the cleft is drying, lightening, and becoming architecture. The dead surrender moisture first, then flesh, then privacy. In time they hold up rooms.

Birth is nearly the reverse. A pregnant person is given the most moisture-rich foods and kept away from the highest nets, not from fear of falling but because unborn children are believed to hear the open sky and become impatient. Labor takes place near the warm downward draft. The newborn is rubbed with aromatic dust rather than washed, shown to the household flame, and touched on the soles with stone from the entrance. Only later is the child carried beneath the roof slit and presented to the birds.

The birds are not gods, exactly. Nothing in the cleft is simple enough to be only a god.

Pigeons are meat, manure, weather signs, nuisances, dowries, racing creatures, and bearers of household luck. Swifts belong to no one and are therefore treated as messengers of the whole town. Hawks are cursed when they kill a favored bird and thanked when they reduce the mice. Bats are protected fiercely, though many people dislike their touch, because the night gardens depend upon what falls from their roosts.

Children collect feathers according to elaborate rules. A feather caught before it touches stone belongs to the catcher. One found in a garden belongs to the plant bed. Hawk feathers must be offered first to the household pigeons, as if asking pardon. Bat bodies are never handled bare-handed, and a dead swift is carried upward as far as possible before being placed in a crack open to the sky.

Goats occupy a different moral category. They are beloved, beaten, bargained with, decorated, milked, restrained, insulted, and blamed for displaying human vices too plainly. Their keepers know each animal by face, voice, and the shape of its droppings. Children are sometimes nursed on goat milk and grow up calling a particular doe elder-sister. Yet everyone has a story of a goat destroying a season’s work in less time than it takes to shout.

Dogs patrol boundaries no map records. They know which ladder belongs to which level, which pigeons may feed on which roof, and which strangers are allowed past the entrance. They sleep across warm thresholds and wake before the air reverses. Their barking is part alarm, part census. By listening to the dogs, the town knows where movement has begun.

The smallest inhabitants are less loved but no less woven into life.

Mice inherit the hidden version of the settlement: the spaces behind jars, between walls, beneath sleeping shelves, and inside abandoned ancestor chambers. They steal seed and distribute it. They gnaw cordage and warn of rot. They are trapped, eaten in lean times, offered to hawks, and represented in children’s tales as shameless householders who claim to have built the town.

Geckos are tolerated everywhere. A gecko above a bed is said to eat bad dreams, though in practice it eats moths. Bees are spoken to politely. Scorpions are killed near infants but carried outside when found near shrines. Ants are watched as prophets of food spoilage and changing weather. If ants abandon a wall, the masons inspect it.

The inhabitants do not imagine themselves ruling these creatures. Rule would require separation, and almost nothing in the cleft is separate.

Instead, they speak of permissions.

The pigeons permit grain to remain if properly guarded. The goats permit gardens where fences are strong. The bees permit honey after flowering. The stone permits a house until it shifts. The fog permits life in quantities that change each year. People, too, are permitted: by their kin, by the walls, by the work they perform, and by the care with which they return what they use.

They distrust open space. Those who descend to the plain move close together and feel exposed beneath the whole sky. Without walls nearby, their hands search unconsciously for holds. They find silence oppressive. At night on the flats, the stars seem not beautiful but dangerously numerous, like holes in a roof no one has maintained.

Some become traders, salt gatherers, clay carriers, hunters, or guides. These are the broad-walkers, admired and pitied. They know distant wells they may not drink from freely, caravan signs, thorn routes, and the moods of strangers. They return with clay, metal scraps, seeds, stories, diseases, spouses, and sometimes water sealed in animal bladders. The water is received without celebration. Celebration would tempt carelessness.

Strangers rarely understand the town. They think it poor because its vessels are repaired, cramped because its houses are small, filthy because no one bathes, and disorderly because birds cross kitchens and roots emerge from tombs. They do not see the distributed precision: who lowers which net, who owns the ladder but not the wall it reaches, which child has memorized the remaining grain, which old man wakes when the draft changes, which widow can graft six kinds of succulent onto one surviving root.

Nor do strangers understand the town’s hospitality. A guest may be given no water at all and yet receive the household’s greatest generosity: a place in the direct sun, the cool flesh of a night-cut leaf, a sleeping shelf away from mice, a cup whose stopper still seals perfectly. To offer plain water to someone newly arrived would imply that they are dying.

The cleft’s people sing, though outsiders sometimes mistake it for calling.

Their songs are built for echo. One voice begins from a low chamber, another answers above, and a third enters from across the gap after the stone has returned the first phrase. The town itself supplies missing notes. Work songs measure grinding, climbing, rope hauling, and the passing of baskets. Courtship songs hide names inside bird calls. Funeral songs are low because the dead are already close enough to hear.

Their language is crowded with words for position: above-but-not-visible, below-within-reach, across-by-rope, behind-a-living-wall, inside-an-old-wall, in-the-path-of-falling-things. There are distinct verbs for carrying upward on the back, lowering by cord, passing from hand to hand, and moving something through another family’s house. Time is often given as light on a particular stone, the opening of a flower, or the direction of the draft.

Secrets are difficult to keep. Walls are thin; echoes are treacherous; children go everywhere. Privacy does not mean being unheard. It means that others choose not to understand what they hear.

Their tempers can be fierce. Crowding produces old injuries. A careless foot can ruin seedlings, a frayed cord can kill, a cooking fire lit at the wrong hour can smoke half the settlement. Feuds persist for generations, but rarely become open war. There is nowhere to exile an enemy without weakening the town, and nowhere to fight without endangering kin, animals, stores, and walls. Anger is therefore cultivated like a thorn hedge: sharpened, bounded, and given uses.

Punishment often takes the form of obligation. A thief may spend a season sealing other people’s jars. Someone who wastes food must tend beetle beds and collect dung. Someone who damages a fog net climbs before dawn to mend it under the supervision of those whose ration was lost. The worst offenders are denied not food, but trust: no one passes them a child, a sealed vessel, an ancestral bundle, or the free end of a rope.

The cleft has no single ruler. Authority collects where necessity collects, then drains away. Net keepers govern before dawn. Oven stewards command during the upward draft. Masons decide when walls are opened. Garden elders allot soil. Bird keepers darken the upper town when hawks are nesting. In crisis, the person who best understands the crisis becomes difficult to disobey.

Above and beneath all of them lives something larger than any council.

It has no name that can be translated cleanly. They sometimes call it the Breathing House, sometimes the Many-Mouthed One, sometimes simply Inside. It is not the stone alone, nor the people, nor the swarms of wings. It is the arrangement among them: fog becoming cord-beads, beads becoming drink, drink becoming blood and leaf; pigeon grain becoming guano, guano becoming fig, fig becoming child, child becoming porter, mason, ancestor, wall.

The town is alive in this sense, but not gently. It sheds households. It consumes labor. It remembers injuries in warped stairs and blocked passages. It grows new organs where a family walls in a ledge, and loses them when bees take a room or roots split a roof. It breathes twice each day. It feeds through a thousand hands and mouths. Its thoughts pass as shouted warnings, inherited rules, dog barks, bird panics, and the trembling of fog lines before dawn.

No inhabitant sees the whole of it.

The net keeper knows the roof slit. The undertaker knows the sealed interiors. The child knows shortcuts too narrow for adults. The mice know the voids. The roots know the fractures. The bats know the air above. Each lives in a different town occupying the same crack.

Together they make the creature that lives there: sleepless, thirsty, quarrelsome, ingenious, feathered in its heights and many-legged in its walls, green wherever dust can be persuaded to hold a root.

From the plain, it is invisible.

Inside, it is never still.
