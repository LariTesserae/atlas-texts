At first, the inhabitants of the Low Works seem too small to count.

They gather where one substance becomes another: at the warm lip of a vent, in the hairline fracture of a cistern, beneath a flange where old refinery salts seep into seawater. Colorless filaments spread there in delicate beards. They have no leaves, no need of light, and almost no shape except the shape imposed by current. When the pumps exhale, they stream outward. When the pumps draw in, they flatten themselves against the iron.

Their colonies are brief in one sense and ancient in another. Individual threads break, whiten, and vanish into the silt, but the seam remains inhabited. Rust opens the metal; the colony feeds upon the opening; its waste hardens over the wound. Thus it both consumes and repairs its dwelling. Some pipes have been kept whole for centuries by organisms that are slowly eating them.

Blind amphipods graze these pale growths. They move with the nervous diligence of livestock in an abandoned field, combing warm joints with their forelimbs and retreating beneath washers whenever pressure changes. Their shells are nearly transparent. Through them can be seen the dark curl of the gut, often full of rust.

The glass worms live deeper in the apparatus.

They choose valves that open infrequently and knot themselves in the recesses behind the seats. At rest, a worm resembles a loop of flawed glass trapped in the mechanism. The first tremor of an approaching cycle passes through it long before the valve moves. It unkinks, withdraws into a drilled passage scarcely wider than its body, and waits while brine shears through the chamber. When the valve closes again, it returns to feed on what the flow has left behind.

A worm may inhabit the same valve for its whole life and never enter open water. To it, the Works is not a drowned town. It is a sequence of pressures: long stillness, warning pulse, violence, warmth.

Higher in the streets, the inspection units travel.

No single unit appears intelligent. Each is a ceramic fist fitted with brushes, probes, gripping hooks, and a lamp like an amber eye. Alone, it is hesitant. It touches the same bolt repeatedly. It enters a doorway, reverses, and enters again. But when several dozen gather, uncertainty disappears. They form bright lines along rails and pipe jackets. They divide around obstructions and reunite on the other side. Some hang motionless in the water while others pass beneath, as if holding an invisible scaffold in place.

They know the Low Works in ways no map could express. Not as buildings or streets, but as tolerances. This weld is thinning. That contact is furred with salt. Here a wall has shifted three finger-widths since the previous circuit. There an electrical path can be restored if six units brace themselves against the current while a seventh scrapes the terminal clean.

They show no alarm when one of their number fails.

The darkened unit is lifted at once. Its companions bear it through windows and over roofs, adjusting their formation to its dead weight. At the central grille they surrender it to hidden mechanisms. Later, some recognizable part may reappear: a brush on one unit, a lens in another, a hook polished by years of previous use. Nothing returns as itself, but almost everything returns.

The courtyard of discarded shells might therefore be called their cemetery, though it is more accurately their sediment. Pale casings lie nested to half the height of the surrounding walls. Amphipods shelter inside them. Bacterial threads bind them together. Mineral films slowly turn the heap into a buttress. Even failure acquires a load-bearing function.

The silver fish that occasionally enters the basin does not belong to this system. It is a visitor, and it behaves like one.

It keeps above the roofs at first, where escape remains visible as a greater darkness overhead. The blue lamps catch along its flanks. Each flash briefly restores a window, a railing, the dial of a clock. Curiosity draws the fish lower, but never far. The amber swarms cause it to turn aside. The slow movement of a bridge sends it darting back toward the rim. It leaves without feeding, carrying perhaps no knowledge of the place except a pattern of cold lights and pressure felt along its lateral line.

Something larger knows more.

It circles outside the basin. Its tracks are broad, deliberate, and spaced as though made by limbs rather than fins. Sometimes there is one circuit of prints; sometimes many, one laid nearly over another. The maker never enters. It may be repelled by the pulses from below, or it may understand them. It may be waiting for what the town releases.

Inside the Low Works, the thirty-seven spheres wait as well.

They seem inert during their years in the courtyards. Salt roughens their darkness. Frostlike colonies spread across them. Fallen residue gathers around their lower curves until each appears rooted in the paving. Inspection units rarely touch them. Pipes bend nearby but do not cross them. Doors that would open against them remain shut for decades, while doors elsewhere continue their cycles.

Yet the Works distinguishes among the spheres. It knows which is newest and which is oldest.

The distinction cannot lie only in their surfaces, for those are buried under accretion. It may pass through the ground as pressure, or through the pipes as a signal too faint to separate from the general pulse. Each sphere may answer in some slow interior fashion. Their apparent stillness may conceal processes for which years are no longer than breaths.

When the Compression Hall enters its ninth cycle, the town gathers itself around the making of another.

Amber units withdraw. Clean chains rise. Metal walkways lean aside. Reservoirs empty in an order that alters the weight of whole districts. The cylinders descend beyond sight and return bearing no visible material. Then the hatch opens, and the new sphere rolls out.

Its pauses at intersections resemble indecision, but the streets respond too precisely for that. A pipe lifts. A wall draws inward. A buried channel clears itself with a discharge of brine. The sphere proceeds as if following instructions written not on any surface but in the arrangement of the Works as a whole.

Only after it has settled does the oldest depart.

For years it has given no sign of buoyancy. Then, without cracking its mineral rind or shaking off its bacterial frost, it rises. Silt pours from beneath it. The courtyard is left with a clean circular hollow, startling amid all the worn surfaces. The sphere passes the catwalks, the capped chimneys, and the rim of the basin. If the circling creature is present, its tracks do not reveal what happens next.

It is tempting to call the spheres eggs. Their number, their long incubation, and their eventual ascent encourage the word. But eggs are made for something that will break them, and none has been seen to open.

They might instead be organs grown outside a body. They might be packets of compressed history, each containing nine cycles of pressure, heat, chemical residue, and mechanical decision. They might be ballast that becomes a messenger only when mature. Or they may be the true inhabitants of the Low Works, with the drowned factories serving merely as the complicated shell of their life.

Even this does not account for the town’s broader awareness.

The machinery does more than continue. It accommodates. It directs maintenance units toward new failures. It allows mineral deposits to strengthen weak railings but scours them from moving contacts. It preserves pockets of warmth for colonies whose crusts preserve the pipes. When ice rain crosses the basin, vulnerable mechanisms close before the sheet reaches them. No central mind announces these decisions. They occur through cams, drums, floats, pressure differentials, and responses passed from building to building.

Any piece of the Works is stupid.

A valve knows only pressure. A piston knows only the limits of its stroke. An inspection hive knows whether its units return. A bacterial mat knows warmth. A sphere, if it knows anything, keeps that knowledge sealed.

Together they produce behavior.

The true resident may therefore be distributed among them: an organism too old and slow to recognize itself, with brick for cartilage and brine for blood. Its nerves are cables whose insulation has hardened to stone. Its reflexes take hours. It heals by rusting, feeds on gradients, replaces its smallest cells, and sheds one dark sphere whenever its internal count reaches thirty-eight.

It does not resist death as surface creatures do. It has incorporated death so completely that decay performs the work of growth. Fallen roofs brace walls. Corroded pipes acquire mineral bones. Lost records become layered tissue in the floors. Broken units enter foundations. The inhabitants feed upon the Works, repair the Works, and are in turn used by it.

There may never have been a moment when the old factory ended and this life began. The distinction has been polished away like every edge in the basin.

What lives in the Low Works is not one creature, though from far enough away it might appear to be one. It is a compact society of pressures and appetites: blind grazers, valve-worms, colorless chemists, amber custodians, patient spheres, and the immense buried habit that coordinates them without command.

They live there at different speeds.

The amphipod lives between pulses.

The inspection swarm lives between failures.

The sphere lives between its making and ascent.

The Works lives between one ninth cycle and the next.

And whatever waits beyond the rim lives by a longer rhythm still, circling the bowl, leaving its tracks, and declining—for now—to enter.
