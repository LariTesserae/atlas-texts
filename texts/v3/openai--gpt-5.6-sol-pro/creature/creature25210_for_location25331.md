At first it seems that nothing lives there. The moving shapes are too infrequent, the films on the submerged rock too clear, the pale bodies too easily returned to glare. Life has not claimed the plateau. It survives in it provisionally, distributed through the water like another trace mineral.

Its smallest inhabitants cover almost everything wet.

They are not visible as growth. There is no slime, no discoloration, no softening of the stone. They form transparent films a few cells deep, laid directly against limestone and quartz. Some draw energy from dissolved iron and sulfur; others live where cold aquifer water meets oxygenated surface seepage. They divide slowly and are scoured away continuously. The water carries the loosened cells downward, through fissures and flooded throats, then sometimes upward again through springs. Thus the base of the plateau’s life is always circulating, never accumulating.

In sheltered pools the films become faintly opalescent. Seen at the correct angle, they resemble oil on glass, though they contain no oil and break into no colors. They tighten when pressure falls. Before a distant constriction shifts or a buried ice chamber cracks, whole submerged surfaces contract by an imperceptible degree. The animals read these contractions.

The commonest of those animals, though still rarely seen, is a small six-legged grazer that lives beneath shelves where water descends in unbroken sheets. Its body is broad and flat, jointed close to the stone, with no part projecting far enough to be caught by the current. The upper shell is the gray-white of old mineral deposits and carries irregular quartzlike ridges. When motionless it resembles a chip left half attached to the wall.

It has no eyes. Along the underside of each leg are rows of soft pressure cups, delicate enough to register water moving through pores in the rock. Its mouth is not a pair of jaws but a pale rasp that passes repeatedly over the transparent films. The marks it leaves are microscopic and are soon recolonized.

It moves with extreme care. Three legs remain fixed while the other three advance; then the pattern reverses. A single crossing of a hand’s breadth may take an hour. If the surrounding roar acquires a new harmonic—sign of a changed jet, a shifting pebble, or a body entering the water—it locks every joint. Mineral salts precipitate readily on its shell during these long stillnesses. Older individuals become crusted into the colors and textures of their chosen ledge. Some can no longer leave it.

They do not seek one another across the plateau. When two happen to meet under the same runnel, each places one foreleg against the other’s shell. They remain connected until the vibration of their bodies synchronizes, then exchange a small packet of living cells through the touching limbs. Either may later deposit eggs in a water-filled pore. The young emerge as nearly transparent specks and are carried deep into the stone before crawling back toward oxygen.

The pool dwellers are softer.

They have narrow, translucent bodies gathered around a dark central thread. Their organs are arranged along this thread in repeated knots, so that the whole animal can bend through sharp angles without injury. In still water they hang vertically, scarcely distinguishable from descending droplets. When disturbed they do not swim across the pool. They fold backward into cracks no wider than a quartz vein, drawing themselves through by waves of muscular pressure.

Around the head—or what serves as the head—is a crown of fine glassy filaments. These strain microbes, mineral-bound cells, and organic fragments from the upwelling aquifer. Each filament is both feeding organ and sense organ. Changes in current pass through the crown as patterns of tension, giving the animal a detailed awareness of chambers it cannot see. It knows the pool less as space than as resistance: the tug of an opening, the pulse of a spring, the dead weight of a sealed passage.

Light is dangerous to it, not because it burns but because it reveals the movement of shadows. It has a single patch of light-sensitive tissue running the length of its body. A passing shape overhead darkens different portions in sequence. Before the shadow is complete, the animal has vanished.

Some pool dwellers never reach the exposed basins. In deep chambers they grow larger and almost formless, their bodies buoyed by water under constant pressure. They trail filament crowns several times their own length and feed upon whatever the aquifer brings. They cannot survive decompression. If forced upward by a blocked channel, they rupture before reaching daylight and become the rare feast on which many smaller animals converge.

The winged inhabitants begin below as well.

Their aquatic young cling in the throats of springs, headfirst against the outflow. They are pale, segmented things with hooked mouths, feeding on the stream of minute particles rising from the caverns. For years they remain in one place while mineral rims build around them. Their bodies lengthen to fit the channel, becoming thin where the rock narrows and broad where it opens. No two adults consequently have quite the same proportions.

The transformation to the winged state occurs only when a precise pressure pattern passes through the water. Across a wide region, perhaps only once in several years, the buried system briefly aligns: deep chambers fill, a set of constrictions clears, and several spring mouths begin to pulse together. The young release their hooks and are expelled.

In the open air their skins split at once. What unfolds is long-winged and almost without weight, clothed in a fine white surface that scatters the plateau’s glare. The wings are not clear membranes but layered fans of hollow fibers. They cannot beat against strong air. Instead they ride the columns of warmer vapor rising from vents and the turbulence generated by waterfalls.

They have eyes, but the eyes detect little except brightness and its polarization on wet stone. This is enough. Reflections that look identical to a human traveler carry distinct patterns to them: open pool, slick wall, deep fissure, white mineral crust. They navigate by the geometry of glare.

Adults do not feed. Their mouths seal during emergence, leaving only a small sensory pit. They cross the plateau in long, wavering flights, solitary until their paths intersect. Courtship has no call and almost no display. Two animals enter the same rising column and circle within it, reading each other through disturbances in vapor. If their wingbeats agree, they join briefly in the air. Soon afterward each seeks a spring whose pressure differs from that of its birthplace and descends into it, folding the wings around the body like a closing fan.

Most are destroyed in the attempt. A spring may crush them against stone or carry them into chambers from which no young can return. The few that choose correctly lay their eggs beneath submerged ledges and die there. Their bodies dissolve or are consumed without leaving a visible trace.

There are also rarer forms whose existence is difficult to separate from the plateau’s processes.

In some flooded passages, white cords extend from wall to wall. They appear to be mineral veins until a pressure wave travels through them and they tighten. Each cord is a colony of many small bodies joined end to end. The outer members dissolve stone chemically; those nearer the center filter the released water; the innermost produce new segments. The colony grows at one end and becomes mineralized at the other, so that it advances through the rock while remaining nearly the same length. Its progress over a lifetime may be less than the width of a finger.

Rounded cavities sometimes contain what looks like a loose curtain of calcite. It is an animal, or an assembly of animals, thin enough for water to pass through. Its surface is covered with minute pits that open and close in response to the booming resonance of the chamber. It feeds most efficiently when the cavity sounds at one particular pitch. If that pitch changes, the curtain contracts against the wall and may wait motionless for decades.

None of these creatures has a territory in the usual sense. A territory would require boundaries, and boundaries here are always crossed by water. They possess instead a pressure signature: a small set of vibrations associated with the chamber, ledge, or fissure where their bodies can function. When that signature alters, they withdraw, harden, drift, or die.

Nor do they form populations large enough to be obvious. Their reproduction is extravagant in duration rather than number. An egg may remain sealed in mineral crust through many years of flow. A larva may pause development until a spring returns to a former temperature. Adults encounter one another rarely, guided not by scent or sound but by coincidences in the stone’s continuous tremor.

Death is similarly unobtrusive. There are no bones scattered among the pillars. Shells dissolve in acidic seepage or become overgrown with new calcite until they are indistinguishable from the ledge. Soft bodies enter the aquifer as particles. Even the larger deep-water forms leave only a temporary cloud in a chamber no light reaches.

The inhabitants therefore seem less like occupants than brief permissions granted by the circulation of water. They live in the narrow differences within apparent constancy: between one mineral concentration and another, between the pressure on either side of a fissure, between direct glare and reflected glare, between two notes buried in the unchanging roar.

A traveler may watch a pool for hours and see nothing. Then a transparent body flexes once beneath the surface. A patch of stone takes six careful steps. High in the brightness, two white wings cross and are gone.

Afterward the plateau appears empty again, though it is not. Beneath every footfall, life is listening through the rock.
