At first they are easiest to mistake for arrangements of the landscape.

A figure waiting among veilwood may stand motionless for hours, wrapped in layered black and chalk-white sheets, its outline repeating the groves around it. The head is narrow beneath a broad hood. The arms are held close. Mineral dust settles over shoulders and does not fall away. Only when the figure turns—slowly, in several deliberate adjustments—does it become clear that the country contains people.

They call themselves, insofar as the name can be translated, **those who keep things agreeing**.

They are tall, though height varies with location. On the stable plateaus they are roughly human in proportion: long-limbed, narrow-chested, with hands broad enough to span the fitted seams of shelter walls. Near uncertain ridges they may lengthen over the course of years. Some elders return from distant routes with an extra joint in each finger, or with one shoulder subtly farther away than the other. Such alterations are not treated as deformities unless they interfere with remembered function. A hand is still a hand if it can hold, signal, and recognize another hand in darkness.

Their skin has the color and luster of weathered stone—gray, dun, pale violet, occasionally rust-red around the joints. It is not hard, but densely fibrous, made of fine interlocking layers that slide rather than stretch. A cut reveals no red blood. Instead, clear oil beads slowly along the wound, carrying suspended flakes that glitter like mica. The oil retreats if gathered quickly and pressed back beneath the skin. For this reason, injury is treated by cupping rather than bandaging: a smooth ceramic half-vessel is bound over the wound until the body has reclaimed what escaped.

They have faces, but the resemblance to humanity becomes uncertain at close range. Their eyes are large and dark, without visible whites, protected by two sets of lids. The outer lids are translucent and usually remain shut outdoors. Through them the inhabitants see contrasts, shadows, accumulated light, and the changing density of distance. Color is clearer with the lids open, but color here is less useful than structure.

Their ears are small openings below the cheekbones, sealed by muscular folds. Most adults keep them permanently closed. The young are born—or made—with hearing, and endure their first years in the deepest chambers of the shelters, learning the country first as muffled vibration. Gradually they are taken nearer the entrance. Their ear canals thicken. By maturity, sound is felt chiefly through the jaw, sternum, fingertips, and the soles of the feet.

They do not consider themselves deaf. To them, the roar is not sound but **pressure made knowledgeable**.

An experienced traveler can kneel, place two fingers against stone, and distinguish the settling of a nearby cliff from a mountain speaking across three valleys. They recognize veilwood groves by the metallic pattern carried through their roots. They can feel when a path has gained distance, when an empty channel is migrating beneath a slope, or when an upper summit has altered its relation to the lower range. The finest listeners press their teeth against tuned stone wedges. They emerge with cracked lips and new information.

Outdoors, speech is useless. The inhabitants converse by touch.

Their common language is performed against the forearm, palm, shoulder, or back: taps for objects, drawn lines for relations, pressure for certainty, lifted contact for doubt. A circling thumb indicates recurrence. Two fingers moving apart mean not merely distance, but distance increasing. A flat palm held against the breast means that the speaker accepts responsibility for the statement that follows.

Names are traced beneath the left wrist. They are not fixed combinations of gestures but small histories: a birthplace, a characteristic shadow, a task, one or two remembered changes. As a person ages, the name grows longer. Close companions abbreviate it by touching an old scar or familiar joint. The dead have the longest names and are seldom named completely.

Inside shelters they also use voices, though quietly and with care. Their vocal cords are broad membranes braced by mineral threads, and their speech has a low, dry quality, more like slabs rubbed together than breath passing through flesh. Spoken language is reserved for matters that should remain attached to a speaker: promises, testimony, kinship, apology. This is why **voices belong to speakers** appears so often among the wall agreements. If that agreement weakens, a sentence may linger after its speaker has gone, or emerge later from another mouth.

They eat, but have little resembling hunger. Veilwood fibers, glasswort edges, and the buried gray mats are ground together in stone bowls. The resulting powder is mixed with dust selected from particular seams, then packed into shallow depressions beneath the tongue. Over several hours it dissolves into the tissues of the mouth.

Most nourishment is mineral and electrical. Travelers wear braided cords that trail over the ground and gather charge from the uneasy air. At rest, they wrap these cords around the wrists and throat. In rich veilwood country, they may go many months without consuming visible material.

The ceramic cups are not for water. They are held over seams where soft stone is emerging and used to collect its faint radiance. Indoors, a cup of this pale light is passed from person to person. Each drinks by breathing across its rim. The glow diminishes and the drinker’s eyes brighten behind their sealed lids.

Their children do not arrive entirely through birth.

Two or more adults who intend a child begin by finding a place where several tones cross: usually beneath a mature grove, near a resonant fissure, but not so near that the mountain’s note overwhelms their own. There they leave a shaped bundle of mineral thread, skin oil, veilwood membrane, and fragments trimmed from their own nails and teeth. For many years they visit it, touching the same patterns into its surface. They give it agreements before it has senses:

**inside remains joined**  
**the hand returns when withdrawn**  
**memory follows waking**  
**you are not the mountain that speaks through you**

If the tones answer, tissue slowly organizes around the bundle. The first recognizable feature is usually a palm; then a closed eye, a ribbed cavity, a mouth. The new person is carried to shelter before it becomes fully definite. Several caretakers maintain contact with it for the next few years, continually reminding its limbs of their relation to one another.

Not every attempt produces a child. Sometimes a red geometric growth opens at the site instead. Sometimes the bundle becomes a small, perfectly formed room with no entrance. Once in many generations, it develops a voice but no body. These outcomes are mourned, though not destroyed. They are considered kin of a difficult kind.

An inhabitant’s internal frame is not bone. It consists of pale articulated rods, grown in nested layers and replaced throughout life. Old rods gradually work their way toward the skin and are removed through small incisions. From these cast-off pieces come the bone-shaped tools found in shelters. A household may use an ancestor’s forearm support as a wedge, needle case, tuning piece, or handle for a black glass knife. This is not regarded as desecration. To remain useful is one of the preferred forms of burial.

The rest of the dead are taken to bare places beyond the groves. The body is seated facing its shadow and enclosed within a low ring of tuned stones. Over decades, its tissues loosen into fibers and flakes. The mountain’s vibration carries these into the dust. Occasionally the shadow remains after the body has dispersed. Such shadows are visited for counsel, though their answers are limited to direction and length.

Their settlements are small: usually three to twelve shelters, each separated from the others by enough space that a failure of one interior will not immediately spread. A settlement may contain thirty inhabitants and still appear deserted. Most remain inside during the loudest pressure cycles. Others travel singly or in pairs, tending stakes, testing paths, trimming veilwood membrane, renewing cairns, and checking the propositions on which habitation depends.

There are no rulers in the ordinary sense. Authority belongs temporarily to whoever has verified the relevant fact.

One person may be trusted concerning doors, another concerning bodily continuity, another concerning the behavior of the eastern ridge. The most respected are the **attestors**, inhabitants able to hold a statement unchanged for decades. They memorize not only words but spatial relations: which wall meets which floor, how far the third peak should remain, where a sleeper’s shadow properly lies.

An attestor does not command a shelter. They sit within it, touching each inscribed agreement in sequence while the other occupants place their hands against the walls. If an assertion has weakened, everyone feels the hesitation: a seam that should be cool becomes warm; an entrance seems briefly to possess two widths; someone’s voice arrives before their fingers move.

Then the agreement is recut.

False statements are dangerous, but so are statements made too precisely. To inscribe **this wall will never move** may cause the shelter to fracture rather than permit a harmless settling. To write **the dead do not return** can trap a dying person between conditions. Their craft lies in finding language firm enough to preserve life and loose enough to allow the country to continue.

They therefore distrust absolutes. Their oldest teachings are conditional:

**while inhabited, this is inside**  
**while held, the tool remains smaller than the hand**  
**until farewell, this person is among us**

Travelers carry personal agreements stitched into their clothing on strips of dead veilwood. These concern identity rather than location. On a long journey, a traveler may forget the shape of their own face or discover that their footsteps are arriving from another route. At such times they stop, open the inner layer of the coat, and read by touch:

**my scars occurred to me**  
**my left hand and right hand have met before**  
**I am the one returning**

The paired road stakes are maintained by a separate wandering order. These travelers wear pale masks on the front of the head and black masks behind, so the landscape cannot easily decide which direction they face. They are called **the Between**, and they spend much of their lives measuring disagreement.

When the pale and black stakes diverge, the Between do not immediately correct either one. First they determine whether the traveler has strayed from the country, or the country from the traveler. Sometimes the safest remedy is to move the pale stake. Sometimes it is necessary to wait years for the black one to approach. Entire settlements have survived because a solitary keeper sat beside two disagreeing stakes for half a lifetime, renewing the assertion that they referred to the same place.

They make little art separate from maintenance. Their shelter seams form patterns. Their cords encode journeys. Tools are carved with the vibrations of their makers’ home peaks. A beautiful object is one that remains itself without excessive insistence.

Yet they do make music.

It cannot compete with the outer roar. Instead, their music subtracts from it. In deep chambers they arrange tuned stones, veilwood sheets, ceramic hollows, and crystal blades so that certain frequencies cancel. The result is a small field of lessened sound, shaped and moved by hand. Performers create slow passages of near-silence: a dim hollow at knee height, a quiet line passing around the room, a sudden stillness cupped between two people’s faces.

Their songs are absences within noise.

During them, the inhabitants sometimes open their ears.

The pain is considerable, and the unfiltered country can damage memory, but hearing is retained as a ceremonial sense. A young person first hears their own voice at the end of childhood. The voice is spoken into a nest of mineral felt and received from only a finger’s width away. Many are frightened by the discovery that something inside them can enter the world invisibly.

The inhabitants do not know whether their ancestors came from elsewhere. Some traditions say they descended from the inverted mountains when the gap was wider. Others say the first people were sentences spoken by stone and given bodies so the country could remember what it meant. A few shelters preserve images scratched beneath later tally marks: rounded mountains with snow, falling water, animals with jointed legs, a bright circle overhead. These are usually interpreted as diagrams of impossible physical laws.

Water is especially difficult for them to imagine. A substance that yields, falls, gathers, enters, reflects, and remains the same substance seems to them almost holy in its consistency.

When the upper and lower ranges approach, every settlement knows long before the contact. The inhabitants return from the paths. The Between drive both stakes together until pale and black touch. Children are brought from the inner rooms. Cast-off ancestral rods are laid in rows. Agreements are inspected but not renewed; as the country nears completion, less insistence is required.

Then comes the instant when the summits meet and the roar ends.

The inhabitants step outside.

For most, this is the only time they hear silence without having constructed it. They remove their ear coverings and open both lids of their eyes. Their posture changes. Shoulders drop. Fingers unclench. Some speak names aloud, though the words seem tiny in the definite air.

They look first at one another.

In ordinary times, a companion is partly memory, partly agreement, partly whatever distance permits. During the contact, each person becomes exact. Faces settle into single proportions. Old asymmetries resolve. Shadows lie faithfully at their feet. Parents see what their children have become; children see that their elders are not merely worn extensions of the shelter walls.

The moment is brief, but the inhabitants prepare all their lives for it. They believe that whatever is seen then cannot be entirely undone.

So they stand among the black groves and pale mountains, watching one another while the world is certain.

When the peaks separate, they cover their ears. Distance loosens. The great sound returns through their teeth and scars. Around them the unfinished country resumes its slow revision.

The inhabitants go back inside and add new marks to the walls:

**we were present**  
**we were distinct**  
**we recognized one another**
