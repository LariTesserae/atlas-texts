At first it seems that the hollow has no master inhabitant.

There are workers everywhere, but nothing that supervises them. The beetles clean because they are hungry. The frogs regulate pressure because certain vibrations fill their lungs and compel an answering note. The mantis keeps time only because its own digestion requires an interval between meals. Root crickets strike the nodules to court, threaten, and locate one another. The leaf throats pump because water touches their hairs. Each life appears complete within its own small appetite.

Yet the appetites fit too closely.

Something lives in the fit.

It has no body that can be lifted from the chamber. If one searches for it, one finds only pieces belonging to other organisms: a collar of vine tissue around a frog’s hollow, yeasts growing in the pits of a mite’s legs, translucent threads entering the wing cases of beetles, fungal sheaths around the eggs of shrimp. None is its seat. None is indispensable for long. Its substance passes continually from root to insect, insect to water, water to fungus, fungus to leaf.

The nearest thing it has to flesh is the root felt beneath the floor. Within that felt lies a fine colorless web, older in pattern than any of the roots through which it runs. It is neither wholly fungal nor wholly vegetal. Some strands contain sap; others contain chains of living cells that fire weak electrical impulses; still others are empty tubes whose only purpose is to transmit changes in pressure. The web reaches every station. It cups each woody basin from below. It enters the resonant stems and grows between the stacked electrical cells. It touches the mineral sacs with branches so delicate that a snail’s rasp can sever dozens without affecting the whole.

The animals carry it too.

Glass-backed beetles hatch with a white filament folded behind the gut. As they graze the valves, the filament samples the films they eat and alters the taste of the next leaf. Frogs have a patch of it spread across the bones of the throat, pale as the inner skin of an onion. It does not make them call, but it makes one pitch satisfying and another unbearable. In the channel creatures, it forms a ring around the tail muscles and releases them from their anchor when too much debris has gathered downstream. Even the mantis bears a dark knot of its tissue behind one eye. When the correct interval has passed, that eye alone becomes unable to see the green of the cicadas, and the next moving body appears brilliant against the stem.

No creature is enslaved. Each is gently enclosed by preference.

This distributed inhabitant has no name within the hollow. Nothing there possesses the distance required to name it. If it could be seen all at once—roots rendered transparent, water lit from within, every carrier marked—its shape would resemble a reclining animal whose outline had been repeatedly erased and redrawn. The channel would be its long divided gut. The sorting sacs would cluster like organs beneath the ribs. The hanging bladders would be lungs or ears. The red ceiling roots would form a downward-growing mane. Its extremities would vanish into the buttress tree and continue beyond sight.

But this likeness would be misleading. It does not lie in the chamber. It is the chamber’s habit of returning to itself.

Its awareness, if awareness is the word, is slow in some places and startlingly quick in others. A falling branch above the tree produces an immediate silence: valves close, beetles flatten, electrical hairs discharge into the wet floor, frogs press their throats against the walls. This response passes through the hollow faster than any one animal can perceive the danger. Then, after the impact, the inhabitant takes hours to decide how much has changed. Water tastes the bruised bark. Fungi register fresh sugars. Snails alter their routes. Moths carry aromatic dust from wall to wall. By the time pumping resumes, every station has received some fragment of the news.

More difficult decisions can require generations.

If iron begins arriving in excess, no order goes out. The orange sorting sac grows sweeter along its rim. Snails remain there longer and carry its mineral film into their eggs. Their young develop darker shells and heavier rasping plates. Beetles feeding nearby lay fewer eggs; a violet yeast thickens; one species of mite becomes common while another retreats into the root conduits. Over years, the chamber changes a hundred tiny preferences until the iron passes through it at the old rate again.

Then the changes stop.

This is its most defining trait. It does not desire more.

It does not seek to cover the floodplain, although its threads enter every root that will admit them. It does not hoard energy, although it could choke the channel with storage bulbs. It does not multiply its useful animals without limit. Abundance troubles it as surely as scarcity. When moths become too numerous, the warm moss pockets cool and their pupae sleep. When frogs are few, the wall hollows exude salts that draw juveniles from the outer mud. When a vine sends up a fourth bladder leaf, the light over that leaf slowly narrows until the extra tissue is reabsorbed.

Its hunger is not for matter. Its hunger is for proportion.

Because of this, it can appear cruel. A hatchling may be fed only until the hollow’s required number is reached. A thriving root tip may be starved because it bends a membrane by the width of a hair. During a disturbance, whole colonies can be dissolved into nutrient milk while a single old frog is kept alive through weeks of weakness because its call contains a frequency not yet reproduced by the young. The inhabitant does not preserve individuals, species, or even lineages. It preserves relations.

It remembers no event as a picture. Memory resides in easier and harder paths.

A stem repeatedly bent in one direction lays down fibers that favor the same bend. Water that once escaped cuts a route that remains moist after the leak is sealed. Animals following a successful path seed it with skin microbes, and their descendants find the microbial odor reassuring. Electrical pulses leave changes in the vine’s conductive tissues. Mineral grains align in root nodules and make certain vibrations travel farther than others. What happened before becomes what is easiest to do again.

Thus the inhabitant’s memory is the worn shape of the world.

Its dreams, if it dreams, come from outside.

A flood presses cold weight against the buried roots, and for a little while the hollow fills with sensations too large to interpret: distant trees bending, banks collapsing, fish striking submerged trunks, lightning entering the plain and dividing among kilometers of wet fiber. Signals arrive from other chambers—some orderly, some frantic, some thinning into nothing. The hanging bladders contract. The frogs answer tones no ear in their chamber produced. Beetles pause with their mouths against the valves.

In such moments the resident here is revealed as only one quiet knot of something broader.

The greater organism may extend from tree to tree across the floodplain, but not as a single sovereign mind. Each hollow keeps its own balance. Each has its local measure of water, tissue, sound, and death. What passes between them is simpler: pulses announcing excess or lack, chemical traces of disease, electrical patterns corresponding to heat, pressure, breach, recovery. One chamber may absorb a mineral burden while another rests its sorting sacs. One may slow its insects so that a distant hollow can draw more oxygen through the shared roots. They never visit one another. They exchange imbalance.

A chamber dies rarely, but it can die.

When this happens, its machinery does not collapse at once. The valves continue clapping. Cicadas drill into empty resonance. Beetles polish leaves that no longer direct water anywhere. Frogs call into bark that has ceased to answer. For days or months, the place performs itself from habit. Then timings drift. Condensation gathers in the moth lanes. Mineral sacs cloud. Predators eat outside their intervals. The floor, no longer held at the exact tension between growth and decay, becomes either sodden or brittle.

Before the final failure, a last pulse travels outward through the roots.

It contains no lament. It is a pattern of pressure and dissolved salts, a compressed description of what can no longer be maintained. Nearby hollows receive it. They alter by minute degrees. Somewhere beneath another old tree, a new fold begins in the root felt, not an expansion exactly, but a replacement: a chamber-shaped possibility held ready against future loss.

Here, however, the inhabitant remains intact.

It is present in the tea-colored current and in the cilia combing it. It is the pause between one valve clap and the next. It is the distance maintained between mosquito columns, the interval allotted to the mantis, the old route of the beetle across the leaf. Its life is not the roar, but the precision inside the roar.

Someone kneeling at the seam might wait for a face to turn toward them. No face will. The frogs continue facing outward. The sacs sort their bright grains. A blue-white spark passes along the hairs and vanishes into the floor.

Still, the hollow has noticed.

Moisture alters beneath the stranger’s hand. Pressure travels through the moss. Carbon dioxide from their breath dissolves in the leaf cups. Several mites lift their forelegs. One frog lowers its call by a fraction. The change passes through roots, water, wings, membranes, and fungal cords until the whole chamber contains a faint impression of the body outside.

For a moment, the visitor is included in the calculation.

Then the hand withdraws. The moss rises. The warm seam closes into green shadow, and the thing that lives there—rooted nowhere, embodied everywhere—returns the hollow to exactly what it was.
