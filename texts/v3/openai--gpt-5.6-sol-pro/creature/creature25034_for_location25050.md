What lives there is not one creature, though at first it can be mistaken for one.

It sleeps overhead.

Across the high vaults, the bats pack themselves shoulder to shoulder until fur, folded membrane, and limestone become indistinguishable. From the floor they appear to be a dark mineral deposit—an immense stain left by vanished water. Only prolonged watching reveals the small adjustments passing through it: a foot loosening and taking hold again, a muzzle lifted, an ear turning toward a sound too high for a person to hear. The movements spread in ripples. One bat wakes its neighbors; one cluster troubles the next. For a moment the ceiling seems to breathe.

No individual commands this body. It has no single mind, yet it possesses habits larger and more durable than any bat’s life. It knows the warm vaults and the safe cracks. It remembers, through mothers and young, which shafts open onto rich marshes and which lead to thin hunting grounds. It divides itself at departure and reassembles upon return. Predators take pieces from it. Winters reduce it; births restore it. Seen across enough years, the colony behaves like a slow animal whose flesh is made of generations.

Up close, that apparent animal separates into many kinds.

Some bats are small enough to close inside a hand, with fine dark faces and ears like curled leaves. Others are heavier, long-winged, their bodies tawny beneath the cave dust. The insect hunters have narrow teeth and quick, searching flight. Their calls interrogate the darkness, and the darkness answers with the shape of pillars, moths, water, wings, and open air. Nursing mothers occupy the warmest reaches. The young cling beneath them, naked at first, their feet already made for hanging. In crowded nurseries, each mother returns through a bedlam of scent and sound and finds the one voice belonging to her.

Below them lies the guano country.

It forms in low brown drifts beneath favored roosts, warm at the center and alive throughout. Nothing green grows there, but it is the cave’s nearest equivalent to fertile earth. Beetles tunnel into it. Larvae graze through it in pale, soft-bodied ranks. Mites ride larger insects from one feeding patch to another. Springtails gather wherever fungal threads have entered the droppings. Minute predators follow them, feeding on animals that feed on decay.

The guano is never merely waste. It is pasture, nursery, hunting ground, and grave. A fallen bat disappears into the same system. First come the larger scavengers, then beetles and fly larvae, then mites and fungi, until fur, membrane, and bone have been sorted into smaller lives. The cave permits no corpse a long identity.

The crickets live farther out, in cracks and beneath tilted slabs. They are hump-backed, long-antennaed, and extravagantly legged, each one looking assembled for a body much larger than it possesses. In darkness, their antennae precede them like paired questions. They wait through the cave’s false daytime and travel when the colonies stir, eating fungus, guano, eggs, dead insects, and sometimes one another. Those near the shafts climb out to feed among surface plants, then return carrying fragments of the upper world in their guts and on their feet.

They are couriers between systems. Owls eat them. Frogs take them. Salamanders wait motionless where their paths cross wet stone. Yet enough return to crowd the fissures, their scraping and chirring joining the endless sound overhead.

The permanent cave dwellers are quieter and less recognizable.

Blind isopods work the damp hollows with the patience of moving grains. Their bodies are nearly colorless, their plates thin, their eyes absent or reduced to useless traces. Translucent shrimp drift in the pools and river margins, visible chiefly by the dark line of food inside them. Flatworms adhere to submerged stone. White fish hover where the river emerges from beneath a shelf, holding their narrow bodies against a current too slight for a person to notice.

The fish do not need to see the cavern’s size. To them it is a sequence of pressures, temperatures, scents, and obstructions. A falling drop is an event felt through the water. A shrimp’s movement is a disturbance to be followed. Floods remembered in their bodies have taught them to seek deep cracks, though no flood has crossed the upper terraces for many generations. Their eyes have dwindled because the cave provides nothing for eyes to do. Their skin has lost its ornament because there is no light in which ornament can speak.

Along wet walls, salamanders hunt with equal economy. They cling where condensation gathers and remain still for hours, the pale curve of a tail resembling a root or vein in the rock. They take moths, crickets, and soft larvae with a quickness that seems impossible after such stillness. Their lives are long, slow, and sparing. A meal may be followed by a wait of days. Time costs them little.

Near the daylight shafts, the population changes.

There, frogs wedge themselves among fern roots and slick stones. Their eyes remain large because light, though weak, still matters. Small moths descend toward the greenish glow, and the frogs gather beneath it as if around a feeding place. Snakes follow the frogs. Some descend only briefly; others grow thin and cave-cool, spending a season wound in a crevice beside the root ropes. They do not belong fully to the dark, but the dark can hold them.

Owls occupy the ledges above. Their feathers collect in sheltered angles. Their droppings add bones and fur to the cave’s economy. They hunt the confusion at the mouths of the shafts, where departing bats compress into streams and must briefly share the same narrow space. The owls learn that stream’s timing. They wait until the darkness rises toward them.

Foxes are less certain visitors. They come by smell, placing their feet carefully among sharp plates. Their noses lower over guano, drowned fish, or a bat injured in the evening emergence. The open cavern does not frighten them as a barrier would; it unsettles them by offering too few edges. They keep near ridges and sloping passages, leaving delicate tracks in silt and no trace at all on limestone.

Fungi inhabit everything intermittently. They flower as white shelves on dead roots, as pinheads on droppings, as invisible threads inside every patch of organic matter. Their visible forms are temporary. Most of the organism remains concealed, dismantling what has arrived from above. A leaf dropped through a shaft, a feather, a beetle’s shell, a shred of fox dung—each becomes a brief center of activity, then fades back into the general scarcity.

Even the green islands are inhabited less by plants than by dependency. Ferns and liverworts survive directly beneath the openings, each angled toward a remote circle of sky. Tree roots enter without their trees, thickening in the moist air before vanishing into cracks. Tiny animals feed upon these roots; fungi penetrate them; bats brush them in flight. The surface sends down food but withholds the greater bodies that made it.

People do not truly live in the cave.

They enter as another temporary species, conspicuous because they bring their own light. A torch alters more than sight. It adds smoke, resin, heat, and a moving orange disturbance to a world of cool constancy. Moths blunder toward it. Bats veer from it. Beetles withdraw beneath crusted guano. The people themselves stay close together, listening for the river and marking their route with memory, cord, or touch.

They know that the broad floor is not empty land. A terrace that appears vacant may lie beneath a nursery of tens of thousands. A quiet pool may contain animals found nowhere under the sun. A brown bank may be warm with larvae. The cave belongs to no creature in the manner of a field or den, but every usable part of it is already claimed by repetition.

The people take pigment, feathers, guano, or fish. They speak loudly because the cavern devours ordinary voices and returns them from the wrong direction. They may sleep near an entrance, but not deeply. Too much moves beyond the torchlight. Too much sound has no visible maker. When they leave, the darkness closes without effort.

The deepest resident is therefore neither bat nor fish, fungus nor cricket. It is the exchange among them.

Food falls from the ceiling, climbs in through roots, flies in on wings, walks in inside foxes, or washes down through fissures. It passes from bat to beetle, from beetle to salamander, from guano to fungus, from fungus to springtail, from springtail to cricket, from cricket to frog, from frog to snake. The river carries fragments onward. The shafts remove living bodies each evening and return them before the cave can seem diminished.

This resident has no face. Its body is the whole expanse: the black vaults as lungs, the river as a narrow gut, the guano beds as warm organs of digestion, the shafts as mouths opening into another world. Its pulse is the nightly departure of bats. Its smallest thoughts are the movements of blind shrimp in water and mites among hairs.

It does not build because the limestone already gives it what it needs. It does not conquer new ground because abundance would unbalance the scarcity on which it depends. It changes constantly in its parts and scarcely at all in its form.

Standing at a distance, one sees bare terraces and a living ceiling. Standing longer, one begins to understand that the barrenness is only the space between processes. The cave is full—not with occupation as people measure it, but with arrivals, feedings, births, dismantlings, and returns.

Nothing rules there.

Everything is being used.
