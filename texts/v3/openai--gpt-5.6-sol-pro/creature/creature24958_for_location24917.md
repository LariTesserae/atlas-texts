At first there seems to be no one.

The black mats are alive, certainly, and the jointed metal-stalks answer the approach of distant land. Pale crawlers leave delicate comb marks beneath stones. Dark gliders occasionally detach from a margin and vanish across the light. But none of these accounts for the objects sometimes found on the plains: three stones nested one inside another, a strip of mineral cloth tied around a tower, a shallow basin kept meticulously free of dust.

The makers are mistaken for terrain.

At rest they resemble low arrangements of weathered slabs. Their outer bodies are composed of overlapping plates, beige or gray according to the country in which they live, with dark flexible material recessed between them. Dust settles on their backs. Mineral mats may grow across them. A motionless individual can remain beside a ridge for years without being distinguished from the ridge itself.

When it rises, no single shape becomes clear. Plates separate. Limbs extend from spaces that had not appeared deep enough to contain them. The body may stand on four supports, then six, then a dozen as local gravity shifts. What looked like the broad back unfolds into thin vanes, jointed spars, sensory threads, and long balancing members that touch the ground only lightly. Their anatomy is not radial or bilateral but provisional. It reorganizes around the current meaning of down.

On a broad, stable fragment they are squat and deliberate, rarely taller than a person’s knee. Near an edge they lengthen. Their front limbs divide into hooked fingers for stone; the rear of the body opens into a dark, pleated sail. In weak gravity they become almost all surface: a loose articulated sheet several meters across, with the heavier plates gathered at its center. Seen crossing a gap, one resembles a piece of old ground that has remembered it can fly.

They have no face that remains in one place.

Sight is distributed among grains of transparent mineral embedded along the plate margins. Each grain admits only a narrow direction of light. Together they give the creature a broken but immense field of vision. It sees horizons well, movement poorly, and stars with extraordinary precision. It can watch several countries at once without turning.

Beneath the plates are pits lined with metallic cilia. These read electrical gradients, tidal strain, radiation, and the minute acceleration produced by distant masses. A living one can feel a continent approaching while it is still only a discoloration among the stars. It can distinguish the pull of an artifact from natural gravity and detect an unstable path hours before loose dust begins to rise.

The inhabitants hear chiefly through contact. When standing on stone, they press a fan of hard filaments into microscopic cracks. Vibrations pass through the skeleton as patterns of pressure and are stored there. A footfall, a shifting seam, the settling of a tower: each enters the body as a tactile event.

Consequently, they do not call across open air. They converse through the ground.

Two meeting individuals arrange themselves several meters apart and place their limbs against the same slab. One taps, scrapes, and exerts long, nearly imperceptible pressures. The other receives not merely rhythm but direction, weight, and duration. A brief exchange may take an afternoon. A serious account may continue for months, interrupted whenever the ground tilts or the participants fold themselves against the cold.

Their messages are spatial. To describe a journey, they reproduce the sequence of its gravitational changes. To describe another living being, they give the pattern of pressures that being leaves in stone. Personal names are not sounds but small tectonic signatures: a lift beneath one limb, a lateral pull, three diminishing pulses, the remembered drag of a particular distant mass.

They can communicate at a distance when the crust permits it. Certain seams carry vibration with little loss, and settlements gather around them. Messages pass along these seams through relays of listeners, though “settlement” is too fixed a term. It may consist of twenty apparent heaps of rock scattered over a hundred kilometers, all dormant until the same tremor wakes them.

Their bodies are composite.

The hard plates are grown slowly from local minerals. Beneath them lives a dense black tissue related, perhaps, to the mats in protected cracks. It does not circulate blood. Charge moves through branching filaments, while packets of volatile compounds are sealed in waxy cells and carried where needed. Water is too rare to remain loose inside them. Most of it is chemically bound and released only during repair or thought-intensive activity.

They feed by lying flat against active seams. Rootlike mouthparts enter the stone and draw on differences in temperature and electrical potential. They also cultivate patches of black mat beneath their plates and carry them from continent to continent. The relationship is so old that it is uncertain whether the inhabitants domesticated the mats or whether the mats assembled the inhabitants as vehicles.

A feeding individual appears dead. Its limbs sink into cracks, its vanes close, and its external temperature approaches that of the ground. It may remain so for decades. During this time dust buries it and nearby plants root around it. Awakening is marked only by a slight lifting of the plates and a darkening along their edges.

They have no childhood in the ordinary sense.

A new inhabitant begins as a bundle of old material. Several adults contribute plates, memory filaments, cultivated tissue, and pieces of mineral gathered from places important to them. These are arranged in a shallow pit during a period when gravity is especially steady. The black tissue joins slowly. Connections form. For years the bundle does not move.

When it finally does, its surfaces are already rounded.

It rises bearing scratches, stains, and impacts inherited from its makers. Some of its memories are inherited as well, though not as a continuous identity. It may know the pressure-signature of a distant ring it has never visited. It may recoil from a cliff that vanished ten thousand years before its assembly. It may possess the ending of a route whose beginning no longer exists.

Thus even the newly made are visibly ancient. The inhabitants do not distinguish sharply between birth, manufacture, and repair. An individual may replace every plate and much of its inner tissue over a long life. Parts removed from the dead are used in the living. Asked how many of them there are, they would have difficulty answering, because continuity belongs as much to arrangements and remembered routes as to bodies.

What persists most clearly is a pattern of travel.

They are not nomads because they seek resources. They move because remaining in one location eventually becomes impossible. Distances lengthen. A home fragment rotates away from useful light. An approaching continent draws the local atmosphere from the ground. A tower begins to disturb the old downward pull. The inhabitants wait, sometimes for centuries, for the proper sequence of conditions; then an entire community unfolds and departs.

Their crossings are careful ceremonies without spectacle. The eldest travelers station themselves near the margin and probe the empty space with conductive fibers. Others lay flat stones in nested arcs, not as decoration but as a calculation. When dust begins to stream outward, they open their sails.

Some jump.

Some walk directly over the edge and continue along a path no eye can see.

Some separate into dozens of articulated pieces connected by fine black cords. These spread through the gravitational current like a net and reassemble on the far side. The smallest ride within the opened bodies of larger travelers. Cultivated mats, dormant crawlers, spores, mineral tools, and fragments of recorded stone go with them.

Failure is common. It is not dramatized. A traveler may miss the destination and enter a long orbit among the fragments. Others can often see it for years, passing periodically as a small dark shape. If rescue never becomes possible, the lost one closes its tissues, reduces thought, and waits. There are individuals in the dispersed world who have been falling alone for geological spans, waking briefly at each near passage to examine the changed arrangement of the sky.

The inhabitants keep maps, but no map is flat.

Their records are objects held under tension: loops of wire grown from metallic stalks, lattices of stone splinters, sealed chambers containing charged dust. A route is encoded as a sequence of deformations. When manipulated correctly, the map pulls at the limbs in the order that the traveler should expect to feel. Old maps are dangerous because the world may have changed while the encoded path still feels convincing.

The most trusted navigators carry no objects. Their bodies have been altered by thousands of crossings. Plates on their undersides bear concentric wear patterns. Their sensory pits are crowded with foreign dust. They can stand beneath several conflicting gravities, loosen every joint, and allow the world to arrange them. From the posture that results, they know what passage is opening.

They build little. Construction here is inseparable from invitation: any erected wall will become old, any straight edge will round, any fixed foundation may awaken on the underside of a continent. Their shelters are therefore folds, hollows, and balanced slabs that can change orientation without collapsing. They prefer to persuade existing stone into usefulness.

A dwelling may be a natural pit modified by a single movable lip. When down changes, the lip rolls around the pit and becomes a floor on the new side. A communal chamber may have no permanent entrance; one waits until the fragment tilts and a gap opens between two resting boulders. Their finest works look accidental until they are used.

They do tend certain places. Black gardens are shielded from excessive radiation with thin mineral screens. Metallic stalks are transplanted along likely migration paths. Warm stones inhabited by crawlers are marked by arrangements of three pale pebbles so they will not be disturbed. On barren fragments, travelers press dormant life into deep cracks, leaving behind tiny reserves that may not wake for ten thousand years.

They do not own territory. They maintain intervals.

A useful gap between two slabs, a repeating conjunction, the brief alignment of three towers—these can matter more than either landmass involved. Communities may identify themselves by an event that occurs only once in several centuries. They disperse across many fragments, then gather when their chosen pieces of country approach one another. For a few days the void between the fragments is crossed by gliding bodies and braided lines of dust. News, tissue, tools, and memories are exchanged. Then the lands separate and the community becomes an astronomical distribution again.

The ancient structures trouble them.

They did not make the seamless towers or the great rings. They understand portions of their behavior but not their purpose. Some towers are approached and touched. Others are observed only through reflections on shielded stone. An inhabitant entering the wrong field may find its plates rearranged, its stored memories reversed, or its body convinced that several incompatible directions are down.

Yet the artifacts are also used. Solitary doorways serve as seasonal passages onto cliff faces. Rings gather dust useful for feeding. Inclined towers can stabilize a departure route if approached at the proper phase. Around certain structures, the inhabitants have placed thousands of small stones, each carrying the pressure-record of someone who entered and did not return.

They have several explanations for the dispersed world, though none is held as final.

One says that the fragments are the plates of an immeasurably large ancestor whose soft parts vanished, and that living beings are its remaining sense organs, moving across the dead body to keep it aware.

Another says the world is not broken but unfinished: each continent is a component being tested in different arrangements, and someday all will be joined in the correct sequence.

A third proposes that the central void is alive and that everything else—stone, tower, animal, traveler—is the slow debris of its feeding.

These are not told as spoken stories. They are performed through posture and weight. During such a telling, listeners may be arranged across an entire plain, each embodying one moving fragment. The narrative proceeds as they pull against the ground and one another. A creation account can last until the actual heavens reproduce the configuration being described.

Their relationship with time is similarly physical. They do not count uniform units. Time is measured by returns: the next lifting of the violet dust, the third approach of the split continent, the interval between two awakenings of a ring. An event that never repeats cannot be placed securely in history. It is simply said to be “outside the sequence.”

Death occurs when the inner black tissue can no longer maintain a common pattern across the plates. The body does not suddenly cease. Different regions fall dormant at different rates. A dying inhabitant may continue to navigate while losing the capacity for names, then retain names after forgetting how to move. Others gather around and receive what can be transferred: routes, warning pressures, cultivated tissue, pieces of shell.

The remaining plates are not buried. Burial has little meaning in a world where depths become surfaces. They are set loose at a margin during weak gravity. Some drift away. Some settle nearby. Some are incorporated into new bodies.

Occasionally a dead plate is found on a country no traveler could have reached. It may have crossed alone for millions of years. The finder places it against the ground and reads whatever vibration remains in its structure: perhaps a partial route, perhaps the memory of pressure under an extinct continent, perhaps only the rhythm of a body waiting in darkness.

During the great storm-states, the inhabitants disappear so completely that the land seems sterile again. They fit themselves into old pits, clamp around buried seams, or divide into flat segments and slide beneath stones. Communities link their bodies through cracks, making flexible networks kilometers wide. The network does not resist the movement of the continent. It feels the movement and yields before it.

Some, however, remain exposed.

These are the storm-readers, old navigators whose plates have become nearly black with foreign mineral dust. They stand on the open plains while mountains turn overhead. Every limb extends. Sensory fibers stream into the charged air. As local gravity fails, they anchor themselves not downward but in several directions at once.

Their bodies become diagrams of the storm.

Afterward they may remain motionless for years, translating what they felt into patterns that others can receive. From these patterns come new routes. Sometimes they also bring back impressions of the central field: enormous slow pressures, repeated at intervals too long for any single body to comprehend.

They are patient, but not serene. The apparent calm of their movements comes from living where haste has little advantage. They know fear as the sudden loss of a predicted sequence. They know affection as the willingness to share mass during a crossing. They know grief as a familiar pressure no longer answering through the ground.

Their closest equivalent to joy occurs when a path believed lost becomes possible again.

Then they gather at the edge of a fragment beneath the ordinary white sun. Dust rises around them in threads. One by one, the heaps of old stone unfold into vanes, limbs, eyes, and sails. Across the void, another country turns its worn face toward them.

For a moment, the distance acquires a shape.

They step into it.
