The people who live here are difficult to distinguish from what they have built.

At a distance they are only movement along the contours: a dark thread crossing scree, a brief gathering on a roof, figures stooped beneath loads so large that the carriers seem to be bundles of wool walking by themselves. Their clothes repeat the colors of the basin—gray felt, brown hide, undyed fleece, faded rust and mineral black. Bright color exists, but in narrow measures: a red cord braided into hair, blue edging on a child’s hood, yellow wool wound around a staff, beads of green stone kept beneath the outer layers and shown only at feasts.

Nearly everyone dresses in overlapping garments. Closest to the body are soft, often-mended shifts of wool or hair fiber. Above these come wrapped coats, sleeveless felt shells, sheepskin mantles, and hooded capes weighted at the hem so the wind cannot easily lift them. Sleeves extend over the hands. Boots are made of hide, grass, felt, and replaceable rawhide soles, broad enough for thick foot wrappings. Snow glare is answered with veils, dark pigment beneath the eyes, or thin pieces of bone pierced by narrow slits.

Their clothing makes them appear thick-bodied, but beneath it many are spare. They have the economical strength of people who climb even when performing ordinary tasks. Children learn to balance on stairs before they are trusted near open channels. Adults walk with a slight forward inclination, adapted equally to steep lanes and loaded carrying frames. Shoulders are marked by straps. Hands are cracked by cold, alkali, wet wool, and stone dust. Cheeks remain wind-reddened long after a person has gone indoors.

Breathing is audible here. New arrivals gasp and sleep badly. Those born in the basin breathe deeply without seeming to notice, pausing on climbs less from exhaustion than from habit, using each halt to inspect a wall, exchange a word, or tighten a load. Speech is rarely thrown across a distance unless the wind favors it. People come close, touch sleeves, and talk with their faces partly hidden in collars.

There is no single people in the basin, though outsiders often imagine one.

The upper wards are inhabited chiefly by old pastoral families who claim kinship with particular slopes, springs, and lines of cairns. River-terrace households speak differently from those near the eastern passes. Seasonal herders arrive with dialects from valleys beyond the mountains. Traders settle, marry, and leave behind loanwords, foreign knots, unfamiliar prayers, and children with relatives several weeks’ journey away. Captives, refugees, debt servants, pilgrims, metalworkers, and dispossessed clans have all been absorbed into the settlement over generations.

As a result, a person may use one language in the household, another in the market, and a third when bargaining for passage through upland pasture. Gesture carries much of what words cannot. Certain signs—an open palm toward a channel, fingers laid on a roofstone, the touching of forehead to another’s shoulder—are understood almost everywhere.

People identify themselves less by the whole basin than by smaller bindings. They belong to a water turn, an oven court, a winter pen, a burial path, a flock mark, a stair whose maintenance is shared by twelve households. A person may say that she is “of the spring beneath the split pinnacle,” or “one of those who rebuild the red wall,” and this can matter more than the district in which she sleeps.

Households are correspondingly broad and irregular. They include grandparents, married siblings, foster children, apprentices, widowed cousins, unrelated herders wintering with the family, and sometimes two lineages joined by ownership of the same animals. Kinship is counted through descent, marriage, milk-sharing, adoption, and sworn labor. Someone who has helped rebuild a collapsed house may acquire obligations resembling those of blood. Someone who has repeatedly stolen water may lose the support of close relatives.

The houses are inhabited vertically.

Animals occupy the lowest chambers, along with fodder, tools, jars, and sledges. Above them sleep people, often several to a platform, wrapped in felt and skins. The warmest inner recess is given not automatically to the household head but to infants, the ill, or whoever must rise before dawn. Roof level belongs to work: drying dung, beating wool, sorting grain, repairing harness, watching children, exchanging news with neighbors on the next terrace.

Privacy is made rather than assumed. A hanging blanket can turn one room into three. A particular hearthstone may mark one couple’s place within a crowded household. Boxes and wall niches hold personal goods, sealed with cords whose knots reveal tampering. People learn to avert their eyes and to hear without appearing to listen. Quarrels are seldom truly private, but dignity depends on allowing them to seem so.

Children are everywhere the ground is least safe.

They run on parapets, crawl through half-built drains, and know which roof edges conceal a drop. Small children are tethered near work areas with soft wool cords, not as punishment but because a moment’s inattention can end at the bottom of a stair or cistern. Older ones carry dung cakes, frighten finches from grain, lead placid cattle through passages, and collect every splinter of dropped fuel. They build miniature retaining walls after rain and release boats into gutters. Their games imitate adult disputes: water allotment, flock counting, wall collapse, inheritance. A child acting as judge sits on a flat stone and solemnly fines the others in pebbles.

They are taught landmarks as soon as they can speak. A sequence of peaks, shadows, springs, and cairns is recited like a family genealogy. Older children accompany herders into the high country and return knowing routes that do not exist as visible paths. Memory is one of the city’s essential technologies.

The elderly carry much of it.

Age does not always bring authority; a person known for unfair measures or reckless fire use may remain distrusted into old age. But those with long memories are called when a channel boundary is disputed, when an abandoned terrace is reopened, or when two groups remember a pasture agreement differently. They recall where water ran before a rockfall, which household first roofed a passage, and what compensation followed the winter when another family’s goats consumed the seed grain.

Some keep records in notched sticks, knotted cords, marked shoulder blades, or rows of stones stored in labeled niches. None of these records speaks entirely for itself. It must be interpreted aloud. Knowledge therefore remains attached to people, and the death of a meticulous elder can unsettle half a district.

Work does not divide cleanly into trades, because nearly everyone must understand animals, weather, walls, and fuel. Yet there are specialists.

Masons test stone by sound and can identify a dangerous wall from the way its face has begun to bulge. Channel keepers walk the watercourses before dawn, clearing ice and removing pebbles placed by households attempting to steal a little extra flow. Feltmakers work in groups, rolling wet wool beneath their feet until whole courtyards tremble with the rhythm. Tanners live downhill where their runoff can be controlled. Bone workers make pins, toggles, scrapers, flute tubes, gaming pieces, and the narrow slats worn against snow glare.

Metalworkers are few and conspicuous. Their fires consume more fuel than neighbors like to spare, and their materials arrive from far away. They are watched with a mixture of respect and suspicion. A copper knife may pass through six generations, growing smaller with each sharpening. Broken objects are never merely discarded; they become rivets, hooks, awls, ornaments, or filings swept carefully from a cloth.

There are healers who know splints, altitude sickness, infected frostbite, difficult births, and the dangerous difference between a sleeping cold person and a dying one. Their medicines are modest: bitter roots, juniper smoke, warmed stones, rendered fat, mold scraped from certain storage jars, willow preparations, pressure, cleanliness, and time. Their most valued skill is often judgment—knowing when to keep someone warm and still, and when to send them rapidly downward toward thicker air.

The settlement’s leaders are not kings in any simple sense. Power gathers around control of necessities, but no one controls all of them.

Pasture chiefs may command many armed herders yet depend on terrace households for grain. Water stewards can fine a wealthy family but cannot enforce the decision without neighboring courts. Temple custodians safeguard meeting places, calendars, heirlooms, and oaths. Merchant houses possess distant connections but need local kin to unload goods and defend stores. Some hill forts contain hereditary lineages who call themselves protectors of the basin; their authority is strongest during raids and weakest when deciding whose gutter has damaged whose roof.

Most disputes begin small. A goat has entered an onion bed. Meltwater has been diverted. A wall has cast a new shadow over a planting terrace. Someone has walked across a roof while its clay was wet. Such matters may expand through kin and obligation until hundreds of people are involved. The preferred settlement is not abstract justice but restored balance: labor, wool, grazing access, fosterage, a marriage contribution, a public apology, or the transfer of a water turn during a crucial week.

Exile is feared more than confinement. There are few places to imprison anyone, and feeding an idle captive through winter is considered senseless. To be denied oven, channel, pasture, and shelter is to be removed from the network that makes life possible. Even those who dislike one another understand that survival depends upon remaining entangled.

Generosity is therefore both virtue and insurance. A household that opens its stores during a bad winter may spend years recovering, yet its name acquires weight. Guests are given hot liquid, a place near the wall away from drafts, and something fatty if it can be spared. The host watches carefully to see whether the guest notices the cost. Gratitude is remembered in detail.

Food is plain, sour, smoky, and intensely valued. Barley appears as porridge, dense cakes, roasted meal, or weak beer drunk before it can freeze. Milk is cultured, dried, churned, or stored in skin bags. Meat is seasonal and distributed according to rules that account for age, status, need, and ritual. Blood is saved. Bones are boiled repeatedly, then cracked, shaped, or burned. Onions, bitter leaves, salt, and dried berries bring sharpness to winter meals. In prosperous houses, guests may be offered imported grain, preserved fruit, or a pinch of spice so aromatic that everyone pauses when the container is opened.

Feasts are less lavish than dense. People crowd into courtyards, roofs, and adjoining rooms. Singers alternate between districts, carrying long narratives in which routes and obligations are embedded. Drums are made of hide stretched over ceramic or basket frames. Flutes, clappers, stamping feet, and voices produce music that can travel farther than ordinary speech. The richest sound is communal singing: many low voices held against the wind.

Their sacred life is not separate from maintenance.

Springs receive wool, milk, polished pebbles, or the first dust swept from a newly ground grain measure. Channels are cleaned ceremonially as well as practically. Oaths are sworn over stones taken from foundations or over bowls filled from several waters. Before a retaining wall is closed, small offerings may be placed inside: a bead, a horn tip, a child’s lost tooth, grain from the previous harvest.

The sky courts of the temples remain open because enclosure would be inappropriate. What is addressed there may be understood as sky, weather, height, the dead, or a power distributed among all of them. Different districts disagree without finding disagreement intolerable. Some say the peaks are ancestors hardened into stone. Others insist that the mountains are younger than humankind and merely dangerous. Herders speak to storms as if they were animals that might be turned. Masons distrust such speech but still touch a wall before removing its first stone.

The dead remain socially present. Their burial places mark routes and claims. Families visit them when pastures open, when marriages are made, and when the first snow reaches the upper terraces. Yet the dead are not always regarded kindly. Some are asked for protection; others are sealed behind extra stone. Forgotten chambers may become shelters, storehouses, shrines, or feared places depending on what happens there afterward.

The basin’s population changes with the year as though the city itself were breathing.

In the warm months, many residents leave for high pasture. Whole lanes become quiet. Shutters are barred, channels narrowed, and dogs left with neighboring households. The people who remain spread onto roofs, rebuild walls, trade, marry, and prepare for the returning cold.

Then the herds descend. Dust rises on every approach. Sheep flood through the streets in gray, brown, and cream-colored currents. Goats climb stairs not intended for them. Courtyards fill with shouting, counting, shearing, bargaining, and accusation. Distant relatives arrive sun-blackened and lean, carrying cheese, hides, medicinal plants, news of blocked passes, births, deaths, and strangers moving beyond the rim.

Winter compresses everything. People, animals, fodder, smoke, tempers, stories, and illness are drawn inside. The lower rooms throb with cattle warmth. Frost forms around roof vents. Work becomes intimate: spinning, mending, carving, teaching, reciting. Old disputes sharpen, but so does mutual dependence. After heavy snow, doors are opened between adjoining houses so that whole neighborhoods can move beneath the surface without entering the wind.

Not all inhabitants are human, and the distinction between livestock, coworker, wealth, and companion is not firm.

Cattle know the routes between pens and water better than some children. Lead goats are trusted to choose safe crossings. Dogs belong partly to households and partly to streets, sleeping wherever their duties carry them. Pigeons are tolerated because they provide droppings, meat, messages, and weather signs, though grain keepers curse them. Ravens are watched for omens and for practical information; their circling may reveal a dead animal, a lost traveler, or predators near a flock.

Mice inhabit granaries despite plaster, traps, cats brought from warmer valleys, and small snakes encouraged to live in wall cavities. Fleas and lice flourish in wool. Moths can ruin a family more quietly than thieves. People store aromatic plants among folded felt and carry bedding into winter sunlight whenever weather permits.

At the settlement’s edge, domestic life frays into the wild. Ibex watch from rocks above grazing goats that resemble them. Foxes learn the slaughter calendar. Wolves test outer pens during storms. A snow leopard may pass over an occupied roof without waking anyone except the dogs, leaving prints at dawn that draw half the ward into silence.

Such animals are not thought of as belonging outside the city. There is no clean outside. The uppermost houses end among boulders; the lowest fields dissolve into braided riverbed. Wild creatures enter, citizens depart, stones fall, channels migrate. The settlement is not a refuge from the mountains so much as one of the forms life has taken within them.

What lives here, finally, is not a single hardy people opposed to an empty land. It is an accumulation of dependencies.

A widow who owns three water turns but cannot repair her roof. A child who knows the hidden path through a burial field. A herder sleeping beside someone else’s flock. A mason listening to frost inside a wall. A copperworker preserving a coal beneath ash. A dog stationed above a grain stair. A juniper rooted where generations have poured wash water. Moss darkening beneath a cistern lip. Pigeons turning together over the river. The unremembered dead holding scree in place beneath the pastures.

Each survives because something else bears part of its weight.

At night the people lie above their animals, below their ancestors, beside stored grain and running walls. They hear wind searching the slate, water tightening into ice, and stone shifting minutely in the cold. They sleep lightly, because nearly everything around them requires tending.

And yet they sleep. The city holds.
