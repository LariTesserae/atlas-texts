At first it seems that nothing lives there except the low plants.

The moss is the easiest to recognize as living. It thickens around flanges, valve housings, cable trenches, and the warm seams of buildings. Its cushions are dense and cold on top, faintly warm beneath. Cut into one and there are no insects, roots, or buried spores—only layer after layer of green filaments pressed against ceramic and metal. The filaments enter scratches too narrow to see. Some continue through the housings they cover and emerge inside, braided around electrical contacts without shorting them.

The liverwort behaves similarly. Its glossy lobes occupy drainage steps in patterns that resemble writing until one follows them far enough and discovers they are also schematics: branching watersheds, rail junctions, pressure networks, the outlines of chambers beneath the rock. These patterns do not grow or spread. They change by degrees of brightness. When a pump begins laboring under the plateau, a corresponding branch of liverwort darkens with moisture. When a tram passes overhead, pale ripples move across beds kilometers away.

The grasses are less legible. They stand in pavement fissures with frost along their edges, each blade rigid and extremely fine. Their roots descend far beyond the depth the plants should require. Beneath stations and plazas, translucent root bundles have been found passing through concrete sleeves as if installed with the utilities. They converge on buried conduits. No one has ever reached the bottom of one.

The reeds in the marshes may be organs rather than plants.

Their hollow stems descend into mud that is scarcely mud at all: a thin skin of mineral sediment over lattices of black pipe. Air passes through them even when the atmosphere is still. A single reed produces little more than a breath, but a marsh contains millions, graduated in height and bore. Together they voice the plateau’s fixed chords.

The sound changes near machinery under strain.

Not abruptly. There is no alarm. One overtone strengthens, another disappears, and the marsh holds the altered harmony until the unseen condition has corrected itself. Then the original chord returns. The reeds are not warning one another. They may be measuring pressure, or setting it. Their pale stems are cool above the water and warm below it. If broken, a stem reveals concentric membranes like the diaphragms of a microphone. The break does not heal, but by the next inspection the reed is whole again—not regrown, simply unbroken.

The great silver lichens on the open plateau are slower and more difficult to understand. From the ground they seem like crusts spread over stone. Seen from a ridge or passing train, their circular forms become precise: radial branches, repeated knots, concentric interruptions. No two colonies are identical, but colonies hundreds of kilometers apart contain matching sectors.

During electrical storms, light travels through them.

It begins at the circumference as a dim blue line and moves inward along the branches. Elsewhere, perhaps days away, another colony answers from its center outward. The exchange may take hours. It may take no time at all, the apparent motion being only the order in which human sight would have perceived a single distributed act.

If the plateau has thought, it is tempting to place that thought in the lichens. They resemble diagrams so strongly that observers imagine memory written into them. Yet nothing demonstrates intention. Their signals may be no more conscious than sap rising in a tree—or the entire circle may be one exposed cross-section of something whose living bulk lies below.

There are also the black fronds.

They cling to warm conduits, turbine walls, and the sheltered backs of substations. Their surfaces are matte in ordinary light, but near infrared they shine like heated metal. Each frond is broad, thin, and divided into squared lobes. Their veins terminate at rivets, inspection covers, and welds. They absorb waste heat when it is plentiful and release heat when the surrounding structure cools. Engineers, if there ever were engineers, may have cultivated them as insulation. Or perhaps insulation is only the role the city has allowed them to occupy.

They flower rarely, if flowering is the word. A black frond folds inward and forms a faceted cup. Within it appears a bead of polished mineral: iron, copper, silica, sometimes alloys not found in the adjacent machinery. The bead remains for an uncertain interval and then is gone. No seedling follows. No creature takes it. There is only a circular impression in the frond, like the place where a component has been removed.

The white flowers around the high lake appear more conventional. They have stems, petals, stamens, and clear drops where nectar should be. But the drops are distilled water, and the pollen is composed of tiny hollow spheres of ceramic. Their roots gather around the descending pipes. When the cooling towers exhale, every flower turns toward the lake rather than toward the wind or sun.

Nothing pollinates them. Nothing needs to.

Their opening is synchronized with distant closures in the city. At the concussion, the flowers contract. A few moments later they open again. The gesture looks like flinching until it has been seen often enough. Then it looks like breathing.

For a long time these are the plateau’s only apparent inhabitants: vegetation without generations, machinery without operators, and water passing among them as blood might pass among organs. But habitation is implied everywhere.

There are heated rooms in the apartment towers. Some contain tables, narrow beds, basins, storage alcoves, and hooks at several heights. Cupboards stand open. Lamps burn over bare surfaces. In certain rooms, condensation gathers on one side of the bed but not the other, as if warmth has recently occupied it. Floor sensors record weight crossing from doorway to window. Elevator systems stop at requested levels. Doors unlock before empty corridors.

No figure appears on the cameras.

Sometimes a tram carriage receives a passenger.

The evidence is slight: its suspension lowers by a few millimeters; the interior temperature dips; mist forms around a vertical absence near the rear doors. A handrail acquires a patch of warmth at a height convenient for no standard human body. At the next platform, the doors open. The suspension rises. Moisture remains on the floor in two long, parallel marks that are not footprints.

These riders have never been seen entering from the platform. They are present only during transit.

Elsewhere the signs differ. A stairwell light illuminates one landing at a time, climbing thousands of steps up a cliff with no visible walker. A safety gate opens, waits, and closes. In the factories, gantries occasionally pause above an empty section of floor while nearby warning beacons turn away from it, as though protecting sensitive vision. The immense presses never descend when such an absence is present.

It is possible that the city contains inhabitants who are transparent, or inhabitants dispersed through mist, current, heat, or vibration. But transparency does not explain why rain falls through the suspected figures without outlining them, why moss is never compressed under their passage, or why their apparent weight varies from that of a child to that of a loaded industrial vehicle.

Another possibility is that these are not bodies at all. They are permissions moving through the system.

A door opens because, at that place and moment, the city recognizes the condition called arrival. A tram sinks because it recognizes the condition called passenger. Lights climb stairs because ascent is occurring, even though nothing ascends. The gestures of habitation persist after the inhabitants have been removed—or before they have been supplied.

Thus the plateau may be occupied by roles awaiting occupants.

The apartments contain the role of sleeper. Platforms contain the role of traveler. Control rooms contain the role of operator. Gardens contain the role of visitor. These roles pass through their circuits endlessly, producing warmth, weight, access, and delay. They are not ghosts of particular people. Nothing about them remembers a face, a voice, or a death. They are more abstract and perhaps more ancient: an urban population rendered as functions, commuting and working without ever becoming flesh.

Yet below the city there are indications of something less abstract.

In the deepest chambers, beyond the bridges that seem to have no destination, the bass vibration becomes structured. It divides into pulses with long intervals between them. One pulse contracts the pipes along the eastern fracture. Another raises the black pools by a fraction of a millimeter. A third travels upward into the tower foundations, crosses the electrical grid, and reaches the reed marshes as sound.

The sequence repeats, but not perfectly. Over centuries, one interval has shortened. Another has acquired a secondary beat.

The buried pipes are not uniform there. They branch, taper, and rejoin. Some possess walls made of steel outside and fibrous mineral within. Others are basalt tubes lined with a wet membrane that withdraws from direct light. Their junctions are wrapped in roots, cable, moss filaments, and narrow silver channels in which a clear conductive liquid circulates.

No boundary separates the constructed from the grown.

A pipe becomes a root without a seam. A foundation pillar opens into vascular chambers. Electrical bundles spread into lichen beneath the rock. Cooling water passes through transparent membranes and returns chemically unchanged but carrying altered rhythms. The city’s oldest furnaces are embedded in masses that resemble organs only from a distance and geological intrusions only from nearby.

Whatever lives beneath the plateau cannot be said to live inside the machinery. The distinction is reversed. The machinery lives inside it—or is the hardened, exposed portion of it, as shell belongs to an animal and wood to a tree.

Its scale prevents it from behaving like a creature of ordinary time. A valve closure may be a muscle twitch. A train crossing the fracture may be a signal moving along a nerve. The stationary storm systems may be exchanges through a respiratory surface. The upward rain in the caverns and downward rain on the ridges form a circulation too broad to perceive as circulation. The mineral slabs entering factories and leaving apparently unchanged may have undergone transformations measurable only in stress, orientation, memory, or age.

The plateau does not erode because erosion would be injury.

The water does not rise or fall because its depth is regulated as carefully as pressure within an eye. Rust stops at its appointed boundary. Cracks remain sharp because they are joints, not wounds. Broken windows persist because their brokenness has become part of the maintained state. Even damage, once admitted, is kept alive.

This suggests that the continent itself is the principal inhabitant.

Not the stone alone, and not the city. It is the conjunction of basalt, wetland, utility network, fixed weather, vegetation, buried cavities, light, timing, and sound. Its body extends from the lowest black pool to the stationary clouds. The cooling towers are neither buildings nor organs, but structures for which those words are two incomplete views. The silver lichens may be sensory tissue. The trams may carry impulses rather than passengers. The empty apartments may be small repeating cavities in which the whole thing rehearses the possibility of a population.

Whether it is conscious is impossible to determine. It does not turn toward observers. It does not answer signals in any language sent to it. It provides heat, transit, light, shelter, and warning with perfect indifference. It may not distinguish a visitor from rainwater entering a drain.

But sometimes, at the high lake, all the distant systems coincide.

The towers flash in sequence. The reeds alter their chord. Every tram on every elevated line stops between stations. The vertical territories of rain cease for one second, leaving drops suspended beneath the clouds. Deep underfoot, the secondary beat passes through the rock.

Then the flowers close.

Across the plateau, doors open onto empty rooms.

For that second, the city seems not abandoned but attentive—as if the vast inhabitant has reached the edge of a thought, or sensed the outline of something small moving on its surface.

Then the rain completes its fall. The trains continue. The flowers open into empty air.

The plateau resumes the endless work of keeping itself alive, and of keeping ready everything that something else might one day need in order to live there.
