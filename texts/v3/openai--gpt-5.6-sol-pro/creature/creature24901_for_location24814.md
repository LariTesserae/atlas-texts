The most obvious inhabitants are the animals, although “animal” is only the first useful mistake.

Seen nearby, they possess all the credentials of separate lives. They have hides, mouths, eyelids, joints. Their bodies show individual damage: a split hoof, a clouded eye, a patch where scales grew back with the wrong pattern. Some carry young. Some carry parasites. A few wear collars of plaited hair or copper wire, suggesting that another intelligence once distinguished them from the multitude.

Yet none is entirely contained by its skin.

The small, many-legged grazers in the valleys share more than resemblance. A stripe disappearing under one creature’s belly may emerge on another animal several paces away. If the ear of one is cut, the exposed inner flesh can sometimes be seen in the ears of a hundred others, though their outer shapes remain uninjured. A beast may have one mouth locally and a thousand stomachs distributed among the herd. What appears to be crowding is often anatomy viewed without the convenience of continuity.

Their senses are distributed in the same fashion. The spoon-eared creatures hear with the whole valley. When a stone is struck near one of them, no nearby head turns, but an ear at the farthest edge may incline by the width of a hair. The sound has not traveled there. It has entered the herd and selected an ear.

The predators are less like hunters than clasps.

They are narrow-bodied, deep-chested things with jaws disproportionately long and delicately hinged. Their teeth do not pierce. They fit into recurring hollows in the hides of their prey, as precisely as keys entering locks. Some predators encircle a neck; others hold a limb, an abdomen, the base of a horn. The contact may pass nourishment, sensation, injury, or merely definition. A grazer without its predator appears blurred at the edges. A predator whose prey is absent is often little more than a row of teeth surrounding a distortion in the air.

There are scavengers, but nothing decays. They occupy the possibility of decay.

These are low, white animals with naked faces and hands in place of forefeet. They gather around bodies that are not dead and insert their fingers beneath scales, into mouths, behind the clear surfaces of eyes. They withdraw nothing. Their bellies are transparent and contain layered images of meat, bone, and emptied skin. Perhaps they store every decomposition the plateau declined to perform.

Above them, the winged inhabitants remain fixed in the air.

Not all are birds. Some have feathers attached directly to hollow rings of bone, with no central body. Some resemble rays stitched from black membrane. Others are crowds of tiny winged organisms arranged into the silhouette of a single larger creature. From below, such a flock has a head, tail, and spread pinions; from the side, it becomes a thin vertical script. Its thousands of component mouths face inward toward a shared, inaccessible center.

They do not fly, but flight continues through them. The muscles beneath their wings are taut. Their bones are light. Their eyes possess the polished severity of creatures watching rapidly changing ground. Each is full of motion that has ceased to require displacement. If touched, a suspended wing may deliver a sensation of great speed: pressure in the ears, streaming cold, the ache of distance passed too quickly. The wing itself does not stir.

The shell colonies belong to a deeper order of inhabitants.

A shell adheres to the skin of a large beast. Within the shell, mites stand around a dark fleck. Within each mite, smaller beings form a ring around some still smaller center. Magnification does not resolve the arrangement. It repeats it. At sufficient depth, the organisms cease to resemble animals and become hooked grains, coiled chambers, toothed apertures and minute cups—yet each shows signs of habitation.

There may be no lowest inhabitant.

The grain of the plateau itself consists partly of curled bodies. Its chalky taste comes from the powdered exoskeletons of creatures that are neither dead nor intact. The rust around fractures is produced by colonies whose iron-rich mouths remain attached to both sides of every break. The green-black seams are long communal organs, buried except where erosion has exposed their outer membranes.

Even the sunlit air is inhabited. Its resinous taste comes from transparent, many-sided beings too thin to cast shadows. They meet one another edge to edge, forming a nearly continuous atmospheric tissue. Lungs do not draw in gas so much as admit these creatures briefly into branching chambers. They enter without resistance, touch the blood or its equivalent, and depart unchanged. This is why breathing feels optional: the exchange has no before and after. One breath remains in the chest beside the next.

The plants are inhabitants only in the way a hand may be considered an inhabitant of the arm.

The black threads in the cracks are extensions of buried structures whose size cannot be estimated. Their ceramic tubes conduct no fluid. They conduct correspondence. Touching one branch may darken an identical branch beyond the horizon. Breakage does not wound the organism; it establishes two matching ends, and the plant may thereafter treat distant places as adjacent.

The copper fans beneath the flocks appear to be organs of attention. Each blade faces not the sun but a particular suspended animal. Their silica edges preserve a narrow reflection of it. In some colonies, every stage of a winged creature’s life appears on separate blades: egg, larva, pupa, adult, corpse. Since none of those stages replaces another, the fan may be the only place where the creature’s life can be viewed in sequence.

The antler shrubs are more ambiguous. They may have been assembled by the makers, grown by insects, or shed by horizon-sized animals whose bodies remain too far away to approach. Their curled capsules occasionally loosen their mouths from a branch. The separation takes years or perhaps occupies the shape of years without duration. When a capsule lets go, the branch remains held in place.

Then there are the inhabitants of the gaps.

They are first noticed by obstruction. A path bends around open ground. Herd animals press against an invisible contour. Winged forms leave an unoccupied human-shaped space among their ranks. Powder holds tracks at the edge of nothing and nowhere else.

These beings have no visible bodies, but visibility is not their missing quality. They are made of exclusions. One is the region into which dust does not settle. Another is the shape no horn crosses. Another can be recognized only because every animal facing it displays the same eye.

The largest of these appears seated between two herds near the central scarps. Its knees rise above the backs of the armored animals. One hand—or a volume interpreted as a hand—rests palm upward. Shelters built along its outline establish the curve of its shoulders and head. There is no material inside the outline, but the absence is crowded with subtle distinctions: hollows where eyes would be, a denser quiet at the mouth, an almost tangible inclination of attention.

It may be one being. It may be the posture adopted by all empty space on the plateau.

The makers resemble it imperfectly.

At a distance they can be mistaken for upright animals or narrow stone posts. Most are small, lightly built, and wrapped in layers of hide shaved until it is nearly translucent. Their heads are long from brow to crown, not grotesquely so, but enough that their shelters accommodate them with widened upper entrances. Their chests are shallow. Their ribs can be counted through the skin. They possess no visible body hair except for a dark fringe along the forearms and the backs of the calves.

Their faces are quiet without being expressionless. The eyes are broad and mineral-bright, generally gray or yellow, with pupils that branch like cracks. The nose is little more than a pair of vertical openings. Their mouths seem made for delicate placement rather than speech: narrow lips, small teeth, a tongue divided at the tip into several fine feelers.

Their hands are less stable.

Seen casually, each has five fingers. Seen while the hand is working, more may become apparent. A maker tying cordage can bring forth a sixth finger to hold tension, then lose it when the knot is complete. One lifting a scale may have seven. One resting may have four, with a smooth palm where the others might emerge. The handprints are not records of deformity or separate species. They preserve the number of fingers required by an act.

Their feet are broad and soft-soled. Powder does not cling to them. A maker’s track is often more distinct than the maker, and the two need not occupy the same location. Some individuals are followed by prints that precede them. Others stand at the end of paths whose earliest impressions begin centuries—or species—away.

They eat, if the word can be retained, by establishing small correspondences.

A maker may place a perforated bowl against the flank of a grazer and hold a sealed cup to their own abdomen. Nothing passes visibly between the vessels. Afterward, a pale mark appears on the animal’s hide and a matching mark beneath the maker’s ribs. They may sit for long intervals with a ceramic tube touching the mineral crust, their split tongues resting against its sealed end. They do not chew. They do not swallow. No refuse accumulates around their shelters.

Their tools are instruments of exact relation.

The hooks bring two surfaces into contact. The needles draw a single hair through skin, stone dust through tendon, shadow through a perforated pot. Stone blades are used to separate things that already appear separate. Hammers tap distinctions into rock. Screens isolate one view from all the others a creature possesses.

Their walls are not fortifications. Their fences do not confine. Their roofs offer no protection from weather. They build in order to say: this animal, from here; this interval, at this scale; this hand, with this number of fingers.

Without such work, identities may spread. A horned beast could become every horned beast. A hollow might open into all hollows. The mark around an eye might repeat until the whole plateau was looking through it. The makers’ small structures hold local differences in place.

They are not rulers of the animals. They are their annotators.

Each maker appears responsible for a limited collection of relationships. One tends the line between an insect and a distant cliff, replacing white chips that have never moved. One sits within a hide frame and watches a particular beast from the angle at which its markings become unique. Another occupies a tendon-roofed shelter around a newborn, touching alternately the mother’s flank and the mineral beneath the young. Through these gestures, distinctions are maintained: parent and offspring, here and there, surface and depth.

They seldom meet, although their shelters can stand close together. Two makers approaching the same place may become views of one another rather than companions. One will see the other from the left, bearing dark scars; the other will see a pale, unscarred figure from the right. If they touch hands, the contact may persist after they part, joining distant palms across the plateau.

Their language is made largely of placement.

A dark stone beside a pale one means a journey can occur between them. Three horn posts mean an animal should be understood as having an interior. A pierced jar laid on its side means an absence has begun to resemble a body. They also make faint clicking sounds with the divided tongue, but these are not strung into speech. A click identifies a particular silence. Repeating it elsewhere carries that silence with it.

Their personal names, if they have them, are arrangements of objects rather than sounds. A maker might be called the bent copper hook beneath the jaw of the third white grazer, together with the shadow of a certain wing. To remove the hook would not erase the name, because the moment before its removal remains available. It would, however, give the maker an additional name.

There are young among them, but childhood is not a stage they leave behind.

A child may lie open-eyed beside an adult while, farther away, the same being works with practiced hands at a stone wall. Another version sits aged and thin beneath a screen. These are not ancestors and descendants. They are the complete posture of a person, distributed across forms that elsewhere would be successive.

This makes counting them impossible. There may be a dispersed nation. There may be one maker repeated at every task. Certain details support the latter possibility: the same notch in many left ears, the same crooked toe, the same branching pupil. But these features change depending on direction, and resemblance on the plateau is often a shared organ rather than shared descent.

The worn chair at the summit belongs to them, though perhaps not as a throne.

One maker is usually seated there.

From below, the figure appears elderly, wrapped in pale skins, its narrow head inclined toward the sun. Closer, age becomes difficult to assign. The face contains fine lines, but some are seams where several faces meet. The hands lie on the knees with five fingers each until someone begins to count. The sitter’s shadow descends the mountain by more routes than the body could cast.

It does not oversee the plateau. Its eyes are closed.

The polish on the seat comes from innumerable occupants, or from one occupant present in every act of sitting. Around the chair’s legs are tiny implements: shell lenses, hair loops, flakes of green ceramic, a copper point hammered thinner than a reflection. The sitter occasionally takes up one of these tools and touches it to the air before its face.

At that contact, something somewhere becomes singular.

One animal ceases, for a moment, to be the herd.

One wing belongs to one body.

One hand retains one number of fingers.

One track follows one foot.

This may be the makers’ central labor. They do not keep the plateau alive; it cannot die in any ordinary sense. They keep its inhabitants from resolving entirely into one another. They preserve the small, fragile fiction that a creature may be itself and not merely a location in the total arrangement.

The horizon-beings require a different account.

Calling them animals may be an error produced by scale. Their apparent ribs could be geological shelves. Their eyes could be dark lakes without liquid. Yet when one of them opens a row of lids, nearby herds reveal moist reflections despite having no moisture in their bodies. Scar patterns across thousands of creatures align briefly into the image of the same enormous face.

The horizon-beings may be what the local fauna becomes when seen whole.

Each immense back could contain all the armored creatures. Each rib could be a migration compressed into an escarpment. The ordinary sun may be fixed at its moderate angle because it is lodged in the eye of something lying beyond approach.

The makers do not build markers toward these beings. They do not frame them. No line of white chips connects them to anything else. This omission feels deliberate.

Perhaps they cannot be clarified.

Perhaps they are already too clear.

Beneath all these inhabitants—or within them—lives the plateau’s least visible population: the moments that did not depart.

The untouched powder remains beside the footprint. The unopened eye remains within the opened one. The unstruck stone accompanies its sound. These prior states are not memories. They retain texture, position, and a limited capacity to be entered. Certain tiny animals nest there. They slip between what occurred and what it displaced, feeding on the difference.

The makers sometimes collect them with sealed cups.

Inside such a cup there is no specimen, only the persistent sense that its lid has not yet been lifted. Held to the ear, it gives off a minute dry scratching. The scratching stops before the cup is opened and continues afterward, but never during.

These may be the only creatures on the plateau that move in anything like the ordinary meaning. They migrate through alternatives. They breed in unrealized gestures. Their burrows are the acts a body almost performed: the step not taken, the bite not completed, the wingbeat held inside the shoulder.

Perhaps they are responsible for the place’s continued life. Not energy, exactly, but distinction flows through them. They keep near and far from becoming wholly synonymous. They carry the faint difference between parent and child, predator and prey, shell and tenant, before and after.

The makers treat them with care. A cup containing one is placed in a shallow pit and surrounded with a ring of stones. A crouching animal is positioned beside it, or has always crouched there. A screen is raised so the cup can be viewed from only one direction.

Nothing more is done.

Nothing more need be done.

For the inhabitants of the plateau do not live by progressing from hunger to satisfaction, birth to maturity, danger to escape. They live by remaining exactly related. Each holds another in place. Herd holds predator; predator holds wound; parasite holds host; maker holds difference; the immense reclining things hold the horizon at its impossible distances.

Even the silent, seated absence among the herds has its function. It gives all that presence a shape to gather around.

No inhabitant is alone, though many are solitary. None is inactive, though almost nothing moves. Their stillness is not sleep, patience, paralysis, or peace. It is the visible form of a labor too complete to require effort.

They attend one another.

And because their attendance never lapses, the plateau does not empty, collapse, conclude, or pass into its next condition. Every mouth remains at its appointed surface. Every eye preserves its particular direction. Every hand continues the gesture that gives it fingers.

They live there as notes live in a chord that is struck once and does not fade—not repeating, not continuing, but remaining fully sounded in the cool and ordinary light.
