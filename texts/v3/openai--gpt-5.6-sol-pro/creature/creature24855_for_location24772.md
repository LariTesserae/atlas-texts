At first there seems to be no large resident of the plain.

There are only indications: a fan of mud displaced from a basin during the night; two parallel grooves crossing a limestone shelf; snail shells gathered in a pocket where no current could have sorted them. In the morning, certain pools are clear while neighboring pools remain clouded, as though something has strained the water. Grass clumps are occasionally pressed outward around a hollow, not broken, but combed into the direction of travel.

The frogs fall silent there before anything can be seen.

What rises is so low that it does not immediately become an animal. It resembles a submerged slab lifting as trapped air gathers beneath it. Water drains from a broad gray-green back. Algae lie in the folds of its hide; rust-colored stains mark its flanks along the habitual waterline. Small snails adhere to it. Once it is fully awake, a pair of dark eyes open near the highest part of the surface, followed by two breathing slits no larger than seeds.

The creature is called, where it is named at all, a **hollowback**.

An old hollowback may be longer than a person is tall, but seldom stands higher than the surrounding sedges. Its body is wide, soft-edged, and flattened by generations of moving through water only inches deep. Four short limbs emerge well apart beneath it, ending in spreading, flexible digits that can grip wet rock without needing claws. Between the limbs runs a pleated fringe of skin. In open water the fringe undulates and carries the animal forward; on exposed shelves it folds close and looks like the eroded rim of a stone.

Its tail is not long. It is a heavy, blunt paddle, useful less for speed than for pushing through mud or anchoring against floodwater. Nothing about the hollowback is made for pursuit. Even when alarmed, it does not dash. It subsides, loosening its body until water covers it, and becomes difficult to distinguish from the basin floor.

The skin performs much of its breathing. It is cool, porous, and coated in clear mucus that prevents it from drying during crossings. On older animals, minerals precipitate in this coating. The back acquires pale scales of carbonate, not true armor but a brittle crust continuously shed and renewed. Lichens sometimes establish on individuals that spend years using the same high shelf. During long rests, dragonflies land on them. Small birds pick insects from the creases around their eyes.

A hollowback feeds by lowering its broad head to the bottom of a pool and opening a mouth hidden almost entirely beneath the body. It draws in water, mud, larvae, algae, tiny crustaceans, fish eggs, and soft aquatic plants. Internal combs retain what can be eaten; water escapes through narrow vents behind the jaw. The result is a faint, continuous stirring, like rain beginning far away.

It will take larger prey when opportunity brings it near. A stranded fish, a cluster of tadpoles, a frog that remains still too long—these disappear with no visible strike. The mouth simply opens beneath them. Yet a hollowback may also spend hours grazing a stain of algae from limestone, advancing less than the width of a hand.

Its feeding creates the signs by which it is found. It clears sediment from solution cups, deepens the channels between pools, and presses soft mud into low rims around its resting places. Over centuries, these habits alter the drainage of the plain. A runnel repeatedly followed by hollowbacks becomes a permanent seam. A sleeping hollow, kept free of vegetation and gently acidified by the animal’s skin, may outlast the animal that made it.

Thus many of the basins are partly theirs.

During dry intervals, a hollowback selects a shaded depression and works downward until its underside touches damp stone. It draws mud over itself, closes its eyes beneath translucent lids, and reduces its breathing almost to nothing. The mucus coating thickens and dries into overlapping gray films. Dust settles. Seeds lodge against its sides. Grass may grow close enough to conceal its outline.

It can remain this way for months.

The first substantial rain does not always wake it. Water must connect several hollows and begin to move. The animal senses the change through pressure and dissolved minerals. Then the crust over its breathing slits softens. One eye opens under the mud. By nightfall, a shape that seemed part of the ground has entered the channels.

This awakening is answered across the plain.

Hollowbacks call through the water rather than into the air. They brace their bodies against stone and contract deep chambers in the chest, producing a vibration below the loudest frog notes. Nearby pools tremble. Pollen streaks fracture into rings. An observer may feel the call through a hand placed on wet limestone before hearing it as a muted pulse.

The calls pass along connected water, weakening at each grass clump and bend but traveling astonishing distances through the shallow sheets. An animal in one basin can detect another beyond the visible haze. Their exchanges are slow. A reply may come several minutes later, from what appears to be an empty part of the plain.

They gather only after the broadest floods, when distinctions between channels, pans, and shelves temporarily disappear. Even then they remain separated, each within the reach of another’s vibration. They circle through the submerged grass, turning gradually, touching snouts or laying their fringed sides together. Mineral crusts scrape with a sound like stones shifted underwater.

Their eggs are placed in the smallest limestone cups, dozens to a hollow, enclosed in clear gel that swells with rain. The adults do not remain to guard them. Instead, they move slowly upstream of the nursery fields and feed there, clearing sediment that might otherwise bury the eggs.

The young are nothing like the adults. They hatch thin and nearly transparent, with external breathing plumes and tails longer than their bodies. In shallow water they resemble detached leaves or strips of colorless algae. Floods distribute them across the plain. Many are eaten by fish, beetle larvae, frogs, snakes, and wading birds. Some are left drying in hoofprints when the water retreats.

The survivors seek cracks that remain damp beneath the surface. For several years they live almost entirely inside the limestone’s flooded seams, feeding on minute cave life and emerging only at night. When they finally become too broad for those passages, they rise into the open pans. Their breathing plumes withdraw, their limbs thicken, and the first pale mineral flecks appear upon their backs.

A hollowback grows slowly and does not stop growing altogether. The largest individuals may be several centuries old. Their movements preserve no memory in the human sense, but they remember gradients: which direction brings deeper water, which hollow becomes foul in late heat, which seam continues to seep after weeks without rain. An old animal carries the plain as a sequence of tastes and pressures. If moved to unfamiliar ground, it will turn repeatedly until rainwater provides enough information to orient it.

They are not plentiful. One may occupy many acres, and years can pass without two adults sharing the same basin. Yet their low calls, old channels, cleared pools, and sleeping depressions bind their territories together. Much of the plain has been touched by them without seeming inhabited.

Their chief enemy is not any predator but interruption: a season when connecting water fails, a basin filling too quickly with mud, salt advancing inland, or several years of rain arriving at the wrong time. Adults endure drought well, but they cannot cross dry country indefinitely. Their world depends on water being shallow, widespread, and slow.

When a hollowback dies, it usually does so in a resting hollow. The soft parts are consumed rapidly by insects, turtles, birds, and smaller scavengers. The mineral coating collapses inward. Beneath it lies a loose frame of porous bone, so pale and saturated with carbonate that it soon resembles the stone below.

Sediment gathers around the remains. Algae darken them. Rain dissolves their edges.

Eventually there is only a slightly deeper basin holding water a little longer than the others. Frogs breed in it. Sedges establish around its rim. Young hollowbacks may stop there during a flood, unaware that the shape sheltering them was made by one of their own.

At dawn, the living adults are hardest to see. They lie beneath the low mist with only their eyes and breathing slits exposed. Dew pearls on their backs as it does on limestone. Birds walk close without alarm. The plain brightens, pool by pool, and the hollowbacks become part of its subdued pattern: neither wholly creature nor ground, but patient bodies inhabiting the last few inches of relief the ancient stone has kept.
