The people of the Low Quarter are difficult to distinguish from people anywhere else, at least until they begin speaking.

They have ordinary faces. They limp, squint, lose buttons, carry bread under one arm. Their gray coats are not ceremonial; gray cloth simply shows the first trace of frost and the darkening touch of condensation. Most wear several thin layers rather than one heavy garment, because a person may pass from a merely cold kitchen into a stairwell several degrees below freezing, then cross a threshold into a room where summer has been retained from an earlier address.

Their gloves have narrow ceramic pads sewn into the fingertips. Their boots are broad and softly soled. Hard heels make statements about the ground.

They tend not to walk in straight lines, even where a street appears straight. They angle toward walls, touch posts, circle patches of paving that give off the wrong echo. Many carry collapsible rulers, chalk reels, pocket levels, and small reels of silver thread. These are not always professional tools. In the Quarter, checking that a bedroom remains attached to its apartment is an ordinary domestic task.

No census has succeeded because residence is not a stable enough condition. A tenant may sleep for twenty years in the same room while the room passes through six buildings. A family may occupy adjoining apartments and be separated by several streets. Some people counted as arrivals insist they were born in houses not yet constructed. Others have lived in the Quarter since infancy but cannot be included because their front doors currently open beyond its uncertain perimeter.

The Foundation Office gives population estimates in the form of weather forecasts.

**Several thousand residents are likely this week. Local increases may occur near the east cisterns.**

This is considered adequate.

### The surveyors

The most visible inhabitants are surveyors, though few use the title. They are the people kneeling at curbs with brass instruments, tapping walls with felted hammers, or holding a strand of weighted thread across an apparently empty doorway. Their work is not to determine where things are. That question has proved too forceful. They establish where things are willing to remain.

They measure in several incompatible systems. Distance may be recorded in meters, footsteps, tram intervals, degrees of reluctance, or the number of turns required before an object can no longer be heard. A room can be four meters wide and two days across. An alley can have negligible length but considerable duration. Both figures go into the municipal ledgers.

Older surveyors have pale scars around their wrists from measuring tapes that returned by shorter routes. They cultivate uncertainty with professional precision.

“A wall seems to be present,” one will say, standing with a palm against it.

“It probably continues upward.”

“The other side may be indoors.”

Their apprentices learn never to answer too quickly. The cleverest apprentices are not those with the best spatial sense but those who can remain undecided without becoming vague. Vagueness is useless. Certainty is dangerous. Between them lies the exact language on which the Quarter is built.

At night, surveyors sometimes leave bowls of flour on floors under investigation. In the morning they study the marks. Footprints are reassuring. Rooms that have moved often leave no tracks, only a faint ridge where the flour has tried to follow.

### The keepers of small continuities

Every block has someone who maintains its minor consistencies.

These keepers wind clocks that do not measure time but keep kitchens occurring in the proper order. They oil hinges so doors remember which side is the opening side. They replace apartment numbers before the numbers become descriptive. They inspect family photographs for newly visible staircases.

Most are elderly, though the occupation does not make them so. Rather, people who have lived long enough in one cluster of rooms acquire a useful weight. Space grows accustomed to them. A keeper sitting beside a stove can stabilize an entire floor.

They are often invited to births, funerals, renovations, and difficult suppers. During an argument, one may quietly set a cup at each place and remark that the table is *probably adequate for everyone*. This can prevent a dining room from dividing according to allegiance.

They maintain cupboards of mundane anchors: teaspoons, worn shoes, grocery receipts, cracked combs, school exercises, jars of screws. Objects repeatedly handled without being interpreted are unusually resistant to spatial revision. A child’s chipped bowl may be more useful than a pressure regulator when a bedroom begins to drift.

The most experienced keepers own keys without teeth. Such keys open nothing by mechanism. They are inserted into uncertain locks to give the door a chance to recognize the intention of being entered.

### The children

Children adapt more readily than adults and are therefore watched carefully.

They learn approximate speech before arithmetic. A Quarter child does not say, “There is nothing under the bed.” They say, “Nothing under the bed appears to concern us.” They are taught never to declare themselves lost. The approved phrase is: “I may be reachable from somewhere else.”

Their games make use of local conditions. They play hide-and-seek across rooms that are adjacent only when no adult is looking. They race echoes through service tunnels. They fold paper houses and leave them near unfinished walls, then return weeks later to see whether the buildings have copied them.

For a time there was a game called End of the Corridor. It was prohibited after three corridors became competitive.

Children collect white moths after the insects die against warm ventilation grilles. The wings are pressed between clear sheets and traded according to the buildings depicted on them. A floor plan with many bathrooms is considered lucky. A plan containing a recognizable home is surrendered to the Foundation Office, particularly if the home has not yet acquired all the rooms shown.

Some children speak of playmates who live “next close,” a phrase adults do not use. These companions are said to inhabit the hand’s-breadth gaps between buildings. They are described inconsistently: thin children, folded people, people seen edge-on, or simply someone breathing where no chest could expand.

Parents leave cups of condensed water by the walls and do not ask whom their children have invited.

### The pressure workers

The pressure workers service the machines that allow incompatible interiors to touch without injury.

They move in pairs. One listens to the room; the other listens to the machine. Their insulated coats are belted with tuning forks, wax pencils, sealing membranes, and soft wedges made of compressed felt. Before opening a regulator, they tie themselves together—not because either is likely to fall, but because one may otherwise become earlier than the other.

They are recognizable by their speech. They conjugate events with great care.

“The seal failed tomorrow morning.”

“The chamber has not yet been repaired at the time of the leak.”

“We were about to have arrived.”

Outside their profession these constructions sound affected. Inside it they can save lives.

Pressure workers sometimes emerge from a service access visibly younger, or with several weeks of beard grown during a half-hour inspection. Such changes are logged as occupational drift. Their union negotiates compensation not only for hours worked but for hours personally undergone, hours remembered, and hours that will continue repeating after the worker has left.

No one is assigned to a loop pylon alone. The event inside may be tiny—a bead falling, a filament cooling—but repetition exerts a peculiar tenderness. Workers who stare too long begin to sympathize with the trapped instant. They report wanting to complete it with their own hands.

The inspection windows are therefore kept small.

### The removers of corners

Beneath the tram station are the depots where surplus corners are stored.

The removers travel with padded carts and instruments like blunt silver scissors. They are called when an apartment has developed a fifth corner, when a corridor turns more times than its shape permits, or when a closet has become acutely triangular and begun cutting objects placed inside.

A removed corner cannot simply be discarded. Corners are relationships, not debris. Even detached, they continue trying to place walls around themselves.

In the depots they are kept in round ceramic vessels under loose canvas. The largest produce a dry, intermittent knocking. Staff do not enter alone and never stand in groups of three.

Corner removers tend to be cheerful people. Constant exposure to severe geometry seems to leave them fond of soft furniture, shapeless soups, and anecdotes without a point.

### Those who work with the forming earth

The geotechnicians of the Quarter behave more like midwives than miners.

They attend outbreaks of new strata in courtyards and cellars. They test softness, temperature, resonance, and intention. They know which black glass can bear weight and which is only rehearsing being obsidian. They wrap immature granite in damp cloth. They feed salt ridges with measured brine. Where the ground has begun forming a fault too early, they speak around it in soothingly conditional terms.

Their hands are always stained: red grit under the nails, chalk in the creases, a sheen from substances that have not settled on a color.

They dislike the phrase *bedrock*. Nothing in the Quarter is old enough to be called a bed, and little of it rests.

When a warm lowest stair ends in clay, a geotechnician will sit beside it with a listening rod. Sometimes the warmth is merely infrastructural. Sometimes it is a buried room attempting to acquire a volcano beneath itself. Sometimes it is the first pulse of a mineral that will not have a name for centuries.

Samples are placed in labeled drawers, but labels remain provisional:

**BASALT-LIKE, WHEN OBSERVED FROM ABOVE**

**POSSIBLE SHALE; AVOID PROMISES**

**LIMESTONE WITH RECOLLECTION OF TEETH**

### The ordinary households

Most residents are none of these things.

They bake dense loaves because yeast rises unpredictably and dense bread is less likely to develop interior distances. They hang washing in enclosed balconies. They argue about money, damp, children, noise, and whose turn it is to empty the collectors. They repair socks beneath municipal-yellow lamps while frost gathers on the inner corners of the ceiling.

They keep furniture low. Tall wardrobes encourage rooms to consider additional floors.

Beds are usually set away from shared walls, especially walls shared with buildings that cannot be entered. Mirrors are covered at night, not from superstition but because reflections may preserve an earlier arrangement of the room and confuse the door in the morning.

People mark their cups. They place shoes side by side. They count family members indirectly—by bowls after supper, by damp coats over the radiator, by the distribution of breathing in the dark. Direct counting has an unfortunate tendency to settle disputed cases.

Courtship is cautious. To tell someone “I will always find you” is reckless and, in some neighborhoods, legally actionable. The customary declaration is gentler:

“I expect to continue looking.”

Marriage contracts specify not permanent cohabitation but a shared right of access under most plausible architectures.

Funerals are held in rooms with at least two exits. The dead are not described as gone. Nor are they said to remain. Their names are loosened from addresses, their keys are blunted, and their everyday objects are distributed among several households so no single place is forced to contain the whole of their absence.

Occasionally footsteps belonging to the deceased continue to emerge from the paving. Families often recognize them. They pause to listen, but rarely follow.

### The tram riders

There are residents who spend more time on the tram than seems prudent.

Some are commuters. Some are researchers. Some have discovered that one particular seat shows the Quarter at an age they prefer. An elderly woman rides twice a week to look through a window where the courtyard of her childhood has not yet acquired its buildings. A young man sits opposite a pane showing deep erosion because, among the isolated stone fins, he can see the future ruin of the apartment in which his missing brother may still live.

The conductor, if there is one, has never been conclusively identified.

Tickets are collected. Doors open. A gloved hand is occasionally seen reflected in the dark glass between cars. Announcements are made in a low voice, but the speaker gives only conditions, never stations:

“Passengers for recently continuous streets may wish to disembark.”

“Those carrying events from later today should retain them until the next stop.”

“Local depth is in effect.”

There are stories of permanent riders who entered when the tram was new and have not yet reached the next platform. From outside, of course, they may have been traveling for centuries. Inside, they are perhaps still brushing rain from their sleeves.

### The moths and the bird

The white moths emerge only in daylight because their wings require ordinary illumination to be legible.

They gather at ventilation grilles in small, trembling committees. Each carries a plan in gray lines: halls, rooms, ducts, stairs, sometimes a whole building compressed across two wings. When a moth opens and closes them, doors in the plan alternately align and separate.

Technicians once attempted to breed the moths in controlled chambers. The resulting generation bore plans of the chambers, including an emergency exit that had not been installed. The project was discontinued after a fire made the exit necessary.

The long-legged insects on the ponds stand on the surface without dimpling it. They move only when no one is judging the depth.

The black bird is regarded with more caution. It may be a succession of identical birds, or one bird making use of the Quarter’s folded distances. Its feathers absorb the municipal light. Its feet grip wire, pipe, railing, and interior molding with equal ease. It always faces north—not geographical north, the surveyors believe, but the direction from which the district is becoming possible.

Attempts to follow it fail. It does not fly away. The observer simply discovers that the wire has become inaccessible.

Children call it the Before-Crow, although it is not certainly a crow.

### The inhabitant between walls

Something drinks from the trays left in narrow alleys.

No one claims to have seen it. This may be politeness.

The maintenance records call it *interstitial consumption*. Children say it is tall but has no width. Pressure workers say the gaps contain a recurring drop in volume consistent with respiration. The old keepers leave water and sometimes a heel of bread softened in condensation. Bread is never taken. Only water.

When the thing passes, adjacent walls draw closer by a few millimeters. Pipes tremble. Silver rails ring once. People in nearby rooms experience a sensation like someone standing immediately behind them, though the wall may be at their back.

There are accounts of residents rescued from narrowing passages by a cool pressure around the ribs, guiding them sideways through a space too thin to cross. There are also accounts of tools, pets, and one municipal inspector disappearing between buildings. Months later, the inspector’s voice was heard inside a wall, calmly revising the wording of his report.

No one calls the inhabitant thin. That would be too definite.

The accepted phrase is *the neighbor with limited frontage*.

### The Foundation technicians

Those who tend the first and last stone spend short shifts in the circular black-tiled room.

They wear hooded coats and face shields not for radiation or debris but to prevent the stone from observing too much of them at once. Its surface is rough, frost-rimmed, and full of small depressions resembling foundations seen from very high above.

The upward-falling frost gathers on their tools. In covered vessels it remains cold without melting. A pinch mixed into mortar can persuade a new wall that it has already stood for years. Too much makes the wall remember demolition.

Technicians who work there for decades begin to acquire contradictory ages. Their hair whitens near the scalp and darkens toward the ends. Scars disappear when they enter the room and return when they leave. Several remember attending the Office’s construction, though the Office predates their birth and may postdate its own collapse.

They do not worship the stone. They check its instruments, collect its frost, and file reports.

Yet before leaving, nearly all place one gloved hand against a black tile.

The gesture may be practical. It may be reverence. The distinction has not been tested.

### The deeper residents

There are indications of life beneath the Quarter, though “beneath” may be premature.

The black reeds chime in patterns too irregular for machinery and too repetitive for chance. Warmth travels from stair ends through clay. In the deepest cisterns, condensation levels fall as though something vast were drinking in minute sips.

Drilling has found cavities with smooth inner surfaces and traces of mineral salts resembling those left by blood, except that the blood would need to circulate through stone not yet formed. Once, a core sample came up wrapped in a strip of insulated cloth of the kind worn by residents. The cloth was fresh. No person was missing.

Some geotechnicians believe the basin’s future inhabitants are already exerting pressure backward through the ground: roots of plants not evolved, foundations of vanished buildings, burrowing organisms from the mature geology to come. Others think the Quarter itself is developing organs and that the tunnels, cisterns, conduits, and warm stair ends are early anatomy.

Officially, there is no evidence that the district is alive.

The notice stating this is replaced often. Its letters keep softening into:

**THE DISTRICT IS NOT YET EVIDENTLY ALIVE.**

And perhaps that is the largest thing that lives in the Low Quarter: not a creature under the streets, nor one hidden between the walls, but the unresolved reciprocity of settlement and settled earth.

The buildings lean downward. The ground rises carefully. Human beings move between them carrying bread, rulers, frost, children, keys. Their habits teach rooms how to remain rooms. Their footsteps impress possible streets into the paving. Their cautious descriptions leave space around the world for it to alter without breaking.

In return, the Quarter shelters them imperfectly but attentively. It grows walls where cold has pooled too long. It misplaces doors before certain visitors arrive. It releases old footsteps when someone is lonely. It brings distant windows within reach, though not always the windows one intended.

No inhabitant belongs there in a simple way. Each is partly tenant, technician, specimen, and building material. The city makes accommodations for their bodies; their bodies lend the city scale. Together they inhabit not a finished place but an agreement still being phrased.

For now, the agreement appears to hold.
