Nothing below the salt is entirely an individual.

The first creature a visitor takes for a single animal—a black, many-legged shape crossing a red grazing flat—usually proves to be a herd seen at a distance. The second, pried from a stalk and held in the hand, proves to carry mites beneath its wing-cases, a worm threaded through its breathing folds, seeds hooked into the worm’s skin, and a pale fungal crust digesting the oils that all of them shed. Even the mites contain smaller lives. Every body is a district.

Most animals are low, broad, and sealed against thirst. Shell is common, though not always hard shell. Some wear overlapping plates of lacquered chitin; others are covered in flexible hides like oiled paper or translucent horn. Fur is rare and usually modified into quills, wicks, or pollen combs. Exposed flesh becomes useless quickly in the hot, desiccating air. Eyes, where present, sit behind clear shutters. Mouths close with interlocking lips. Joints are hooded in waxy folds.

They do not sweat. Their waste leaves them as dry pellets, fragrant resins, shed crystals, or warm puffs of nearly waterless vapor. Many never drink at all. They eat living vessels and crack the plant cells inside sealed mouth-chambers, taking moisture directly into their tissues before the air can steal it. Their blood is thick and pale, sometimes oily, sometimes almost granular. A wounded animal may plug itself with foaming fat, then stand while attendant insects plaster the injury with pollen and antiseptic gum.

The great browsers dominate the open flats.

They are six-limbed, but at first their limbs are difficult to distinguish from the feeding apparatus hanging between them. The front pair end in heavy crescent jaws that swing sideways through stalks. Behind these are grasping palps, throat combs, and stone-hard grinding plates. A large adult stands as high as a person’s chest and is twice as long, its back roofed in blue-black scutes worn smooth by passengers. Its head is low, wedge-shaped, and almost eyeless. It reads the ground through its feet and the hollow roots through a fan of whisker-rods under its jaw.

A grazing herd advances as a single cutting edge. The first ranks shear. The middle ranks crush and swallow. The rear ranks lick resin from the wounds and scrape up the tender shoots already rising in their wake. Calves travel beneath their elders, pale as grubs, feeding on softened pith dropped for them. Around the herd runs a fringe of smaller creatures: shell-cleaners, egg thieves, spore-eaters, hunters of the shell-cleaners, and bright-winged flies that drink from the browsers’ breath.

The herd’s oldest animals carry gardens. Mossy succulents root in pits along their backs, drawing salt from the shell and sunlight from the ceiling-leaves. The browsers tolerate these gardens because the plants exude a bitter oil that repels biting swarms. When one of the old animals dies, its herd does not linger. Within moments the garden splits open, scattering seeds into the seams of the corpse. By the time scavengers reach the meat, roots have already entered it.

Above the flats move the rattlers.

These are pale, long-armed animals that travel the underside of vine bridges and ceiling roots. Their skins hang in pleats between the limbs, and the folds are lined with hollow scales. A rattler in motion produces a dry, intricate music: clicks from its wrists, a rush of seedlike beads along its ribs, and deeper knocks from chambers in its throat. It has no visible eyes. The broad face is covered by a soft shield of skin that trembles at returning sound.

They live in family chains. Each animal grips the ankle tuft of the one ahead, while the eldest swings free at the front, sounding the route. If the bridge breaks, the whole chain contracts into a many-limbed ball and hangs from whichever member still has purchase. Their young are carried inside the adults’ resonant throat pouches and emerge already able to imitate the contact-rattle of their family.

Rattlers are not harmless. They raid bulb chambers by night, opening them with flakes of shell held like knives. When cornered, they become abruptly silent. Predators accustomed to tracking their noise may continue toward where they expect the animal to be, while it drops soundlessly behind them.

The winged life belongs less to species than to weather.

There are powder gnats that hatch by the billion from pollen drifts and live for a single brightening of the ceiling. There are knife-winged seed wasps that stitch eggs into fresh plant wounds. There are leathery gliders wide enough to shade a hut, their bodies little more than a mouth, a breeding sac, and long bellows-muscles for flight. They climb root towers in the darkened hours, unfold, and throw themselves into the basin when the ceiling-leaves brighten.

Many wings are not used for flight. Some creatures hold them upright to catch falling pollen. Some beat them against the ground to summon mates through the root-mat. Some have become cooling radiators through which warm body oils circulate. Others are false leaves, complete with imitation veins and bite marks, convincing enough that small grazers attempt to feed on them and are closed upon.

The principal aerial predators are called veils by the people below. A veil is nearly invisible when stretched flat against a ceiling-leaf. Its body is narrow and jointless-looking; its limbs divide again and again into hooked threads. When a migration passes beneath, it releases its grip and descends like a torn sheet. Anything touched by the threads is entangled. The veil gathers itself around the struggling mass, digests what it can, and drops a compressed brick of shell, teeth, hooks, and seed husks.

Old veil-casts are used as roofing. Fresh ones are avoided even after they appear dead. Their severed threads can close around a finger for days.

The predators of the ground are more deliberate. They hunt vibration in a place where vision is continually defeated by swarms. Some resemble walking pits: broad, soft-backed animals that press themselves into the flats, cover their bodies with clipped vegetation, and open when a herd steps over them. Others are tall and spare, with four running legs and two raised striking limbs. These follow the browsers for weeks, never attacking, until a tremor in the floor signals a coming root-swell. Then they panic the herd toward the weak ground and feed among the fallen.

Few predators roar. Noise is too plentiful to intimidate anything. They communicate by interrupting it—by producing brief pockets of silence. A hunting pack can pass through a district as a moving absence in the roar, each animal damping the vibrations around its feet. The people fear that silence more than shrieking.

Below the visible floor are the warm-mat animals.

Some are tiny and pass through capillary vessels without rupturing them. Others are longer than streets. They do not tunnel through earth; they eat paths through compressed fiber and leave the passage lined with saliva that hardens into amber corrugations. Their movement raises the floor in waves. Their breathing escapes through resin vents miles away.

The largest are almost never seen whole. A feeding head may break into a gallery, armored in plates the color of old ivory, and work for hours without exposing either neck or body. Its mouth is a revolving crown of mineralized teeth. Between the teeth grow symbiotic bulbs that secrete solvents into the wound. Smaller animals live in sockets among the plates and rush forward to harvest softened tissue before the great jaws close again.

These deep chewers may be animals, or the feeding stage of something rooted still farther below. No one knows. Sometimes a severed segment continues to excavate for years. Sometimes a root tower, wounded in a particular way, begins to grow teeth.

The people of the hollow are fewer than any of these creatures and more difficult to notice.

They make themselves narrow. Children learn early to keep elbows in, to step where a step will not be answered, and to sleep curled inside husk-lined niches. Adults are lean, deep-chested, and callused across the hands, knees, shoulders, and soles. Their skin is rubbed with wax and bitter plant dust. Hair is braided close or shaved away, because loose hair accumulates eggs. The outer ear is commonly folded and bound beneath padded caps; unprotected hearing does not last long.

They wrap themselves in overlapping strips of shell membrane, dead leaf-lacquer, and softened cork. Nothing trails. Nothing shines unnecessarily. A person walking between settlements may carry several scents: one to discourage mites, one to placate a territorial vine, one to resemble the breath of a browser herd, and one reserved for home so that the door-buds will admit them.

Names are not often spoken. Each person has a hand-sign, a drum interval, and a color pattern that can be shown on a folding paddle. Families maintain tension cords between nearby platforms and converse through sequences of pulls. A warning may travel several miles without a voice being raised: three hard tugs for a feeding swarm, a long tremor for root movement, alternating pulses for fire.

Their settlements are less like villages than temporary agreements with particular plants.

A family may spend years teaching a root fork to cradle a platform. They score one side to make it thicken, feed salt to another to slow it, and introduce larvae that hollow sleeping cells without killing the supporting tissue. Doors are grown as muscular lips. Storage jars are pinched bulbs. Walkways are trained vines with hard knobs at hand and foot intervals. When the host plant changes its growth or begins attracting dangerous pollinators, the agreement ends. The inhabitants cut loose what they can and leave the rest to be swallowed.

For this reason, ownership belongs to patterns rather than objects. A bowl shape, a method of knotting a bridge, a sequence of cuts that persuades cork to form a hinge—these can be inherited. The bowl itself cannot. It will grow shut, split, become poisonous, or walk away with the root that bears it.

Their most respected people are wound-readers. They know which plants respond to injury with food and which respond with constriction, poison, heat, or lures. A skilled wound-reader can look at the color of fresh pith, smell the first breath of latex, and decide whether a settlement has minutes or seasons before the wall closes. They carry little besides scoring teeth, packets of larvae, and strings of test beads made from metals that tarnish differently in different saps.

There are also listeners, though the name is misleading. They lie flat against the root-mat with bone rods held to the jaw and interpret pressures too deep to be heard. They predict migrations, gas bladders, splitting ridges, and the approach of the things that chew below. The oldest listeners have teeth worn flat from years of receiving the underground’s blows through bone.

The dead are not buried. There is nowhere inert to place them.

Some bodies are lacquered and hung high until the flesh dries within the skin. Some are given to a household plant in exchange for a crop of nourishing bulbs. Some are set on migration paths so that a herd will carry their salts through the basin. Important wound-readers may be pressed into a prepared gash in a root tower. Amber tissue closes over them, preserving bone, tools, ornaments, and sometimes the shape of the face. Generations later, descendants open a small window in the scar to consult the objects sealed inside.

They do not imagine this as being consumed. Consumption is too ordinary to mean extinction. A person persists as a scar pattern, a bridge design, a family rhythm, a bitter scent remembered by a doorway. To vanish completely would require leaving nothing that another life could use.

The people fear fire, deep silence, and anything without passengers.

A creature entirely free of mites, seeds, fungus, scars, or attached young is assumed to be newly emerged, violently poisonous, or not truly alive. Such things sometimes appear after a gas rupture: smooth white forms lying in the raw chamber, featureless except for a seam along the underside. They do not move while watched. By the next darkening they are gone, and the nearby plants have turned their ceiling-leaves away.

More familiar are the pilgrim colonies. These are moving congregations of people, animals, and cultivated organisms that follow newly opened hollows. They travel on broad rafts woven from living runners. Browser calves pull in front. Rattlers cling beneath. Portable gardens grow along the edges, and tame insects seal every cut as it is made. A colony can dismantle itself in an hour and stream through a crack no permanent house could survive.

They go where the deep chewers have opened space.

Behind them come pollen clouds, predators, roots, grazing herds, and all the small lives that turn an absence into habitat. The pilgrims plant ceiling-leaf tendrils upward, lash the first bridges, mark dangerous saps, and press shrines into wounds that may outlive them by centuries. Then, as the new chamber fills and becomes another crowded organ of the underworld, they move on.

No one has seen the whole of what lives beneath the salt. To do so would require a vantage outside the process, and there is no outside here. The plants are landscape until they contract. The animals are weather until one lands. The people are scars until they gesture. The floor is ground until it breathes.

And under all of them, too deep for the listeners to judge its size, the great chewing continues—not steadily, but with long thoughtful pauses, as though whatever makes the hollows is deciding where hunger should become the next world.
