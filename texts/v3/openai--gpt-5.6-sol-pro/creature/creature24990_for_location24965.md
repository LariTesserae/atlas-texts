At first they are difficult to separate from the wet architecture.

A motion beneath a stair may be a citizen descending, or only water passing behind translucent calcite. A pale arch over a channel may be a bridge until it unfolds two limbs and begins to scrape salt from the wall. They have no color that the city does not possess: milk-white plates, clay-gray joints, points of iron staining around the mouth. When they remain still, condensation covers them. Mineral dust settles into the seams of their backs. The oldest among them carry delicate ridges of calcite and can rest against a wall so perfectly that smaller inhabitants use them as handholds before realizing their mistake.

They are called, in the nearest translation, **the Kept**. The word also means *one who keeps*, *a thing held in its proper place*, and *a joint that does not slip*.

An adult would fit easily in a human palm. Its body is low and long, enclosed by overlapping plates that are not opaque shell but a cloudy, translucent armor. In the blue light, faint structures can be seen moving beneath: a dark digestive coil; the slow shutters of respiratory chambers; clusters of reddish grains stored near the heart. Water beads upon the back and runs along shallow grooves toward the tail, leaving no dry patch behind.

They possess eight limbs, though they do not assign all of them the same status. The rear four are for weight and purchase. Each ends in a fan of minute hooks capable of gripping damp limestone without piercing it. The forward four are finer, many-jointed, and almost handlike. Two are strong enough to brace a stone chip or turn a grinding plate. Two divide near the tips into soft, sensitive digits used for inspection, feeding, and speech.

The head seems unfinished by ordinary standards. There are no visible eyes. A low shield slopes toward a mouth surrounded by combs and palps. Along the underside of the jaw are paired plates thin enough to vibrate with sound conducted through stone. When one of the Kept presses its head to a wall, its whole posture changes. The limbs loosen. The back settles. Signals traveling through the city enter it not as noise but as shape and direction.

They do not hear the roar as a single overwhelming sound. To them it is geography.

The central fall is a fixed column in the mind, as solid as the pillar it strikes beside. Every channel has a tone; every cistern has a breathing resonance; every bridge interrupts some frequency and strengthens another. The Kept know where they are by the arrangement of pressures passing through their bodies. A blocked gutter changes a street’s note. A cracked retaining wall introduces a roughness beneath the wheel-beats. The approach of another citizen is perceived as a moving absence in the familiar vibration.

Their blindness is therefore not darkness. Darkness, to them, is a place where the stone has ceased to answer.

They dislike open spans, deep silt, hanging hide, and any surface that does not carry vibration cleanly. In broad chambers they lay one or more limbs against the floor as they walk. On narrow bridges they often travel beneath the roadway rather than above it, their translucent backs hanging over the water. They cross vertical walls with less hesitation than plazas.

The cold blue radiance maintained throughout the city is not principally for them. It serves their symbionts, their records, and certain minute organisms in the brine bowls. The Kept themselves can distinguish only the strongest glows through patches beneath the facial shield. Light is pressure at a distance: vague, cool, and without detail.

Their mineral feeding is so slow that it scarcely resembles eating. A citizen may occupy one seep for several years, visiting it at intervals and removing no more than a skin of deposit each time. The mouth combs sweep across the stone. Flint-hard teeth loosen grains of salt, iron, sulfur compounds, and substances for which there are no surface names. These pass into several holding chambers, where colonies of living motes dissolve and recombine them.

The motes are the Kept’s true inheritance.

Each citizen carries within itself many kinds, layered through the gut and respiratory tissues in dense films. Some make nourishment from iron. Some bind poisons. Some repair the translucent shell from dissolved calcium. Some brighten faintly when starved. Removed from the body, most die; without them, the citizen also dies. The Kept do not consider these colonies separate creatures, yet neither do they call them organs. Their term means *the older household*.

Every lineage is known by the household it carries. Since two feeding films that look identical may nourish different households, citizenship includes a detailed knowledge of chemistry expressed as taste, sting, temperature, and the color of residues under rubbed calcite. A good feeder can touch a wall with one damp palp and tell which ancestor’s interior garden would have prospered there.

They eat rarely, but they work almost without interval. Their metabolism is miserly. A long period of apparent immobility may be sleep, thought, digestion, or the performance of a task too fine to observe. One may spend months beside a collar joint, removing accretion grain by grain. Another may cross a district carrying a calcite flake no larger than a fingernail clipping, pause at forty different thresholds, and finally fit it into a roof where the loss it replaces cannot be seen.

They distinguish action from inaction by consequence, not movement. To wait beneath a forming drop and divert it at the correct moment is a form of labor. To inspect a stable wall and alter nothing is considered a completed repair.

The rarity of the Kept is not, to them, a tragedy. Crowding would be disastrous. A feeding shelf renews over decades; a chamber may gather enough useful iron for one body only after a century of seepage. Their city is built for multitudes because it is built across time. A room occupied by one citizen now may receive no other for several generations, yet it remains part of the inhabited order between those visits.

They do not conceive of empty buildings as empty. A sealed room is performing containment. A vacant stair is carrying weight. An unused workshop preserves the arrangement of tools. A burial recess holds the dead away from the water. Function is occupancy.

Even so, the disproportion between city and citizens unsettles them. They possess old counts carved into walls: sequences of marks indicating populations far larger than anything remembered. Some scholars interpret the marks as inventories of citizens. Others say they count symbiotic households, structural joints, resonant districts, or obligations. Because the oldest number-sign also means *voice*, no reading can be proved.

No juveniles are ordinarily seen because childhood occurs inside the dead.

When one of the Kept approaches the end of its life, it withdraws to a prepared chamber high above the principal spray. There it stops feeding and is washed for a long interval with water drawn from several springs. Its internal households diminish in a prescribed order. Attendants collect residues from the mouth and vents in tiny clay cups. The body gradually empties, becomes still, and hardens.

The shell is not buried. Its plates are opened along their natural seams, exposing a pale internal cradle that has been forming for perhaps centuries. Within this cradle lies neither egg nor larva, but a small soft knot assembled gradually from tissues set aside during the adult’s life. It has no limbs at first and almost no independent metabolism. The collected households are introduced into it one by one. For a period that may outlast several caretakers, it remains in the parent’s opened shell, nourished by mineral water dripping through the chamber.

Only when its inner colonies have reached balance does it divide its first joints and crawl out.

Thus each birth is also a disassembly of the dead. There is no childhood in the streets because the young do not emerge until they are nearly adult in shape. They are smaller, softer, and dangerously hungry, but they receive no special category in law. The first act expected of a new citizen is to close the cradle chamber with plates from its parent’s back.

Reproduction is infrequent and not always successful. Some citizens die without forming a viable cradle. Others carry two or three unfinished knots that never awaken. A few ancient shells in the burial galleries remain sealed because attendants still detect minute chemical changes within. The Kept will not declare these attempts dead. They maintain them, century after century, scraping the channels, renewing the cups, listening for a vibration too slight to distinguish from water in the stone.

This may explain the waiting city. Every maintained dwelling is assigned not to a living citizen, but to a possible emergence. Rooms stand ready because a cradle may open after all accepted measures of patience have failed.

The Kept do not form families around descent. The parent is mostly gone before the child moves. Kinship follows maintenance instead. One is related to the keeper of one’s cradle, to the citizen who taught one a channel’s song, to those who share responsibility for the same load-bearing wall. A group assigned to a structure becomes that structure’s household and may continue under the same name even as every member is replaced.

Individuals have several names during their lives. The first is the resonance of the chamber where they emerged, reproduced by tapping. The second is a route through the city: a sequence of bridges, turns, and stairs that identifies their earliest duty. Later names are earned from enduring work. One might be called *The Third Scraper Beneath the Split Shelf*, or *One Who Keeps the Yellow Drain from Entering the White*. These are not considered cumbersome. Their wall-tapped forms are brief and exact.

A citizen who changes duties too often acquires a long name and is regarded with mild suspicion.

Their speech at close range combines touch, vibration, and chemistry. The fine forelimbs drum on another’s jaw plates while the mouthparts release minute flavored droplets. Tapping carries structure: action, object, sequence, negation. Flavor carries condition and certainty: remembered, inferred, feared, tasted directly. Complex conversation requires physical contact. Two citizens speaking intimately may remain face to face for hours, forelimbs resting on each other’s cheeks, exchanging tiny strokes beneath the city’s roar.

Misunderstanding is described as “receiving only the tapping.”

They have no word corresponding exactly to *past*. They distinguish what is embedded, what is circulating, and what has been lost to the basin. An event preserved in a wall inscription is embedded. A duty transmitted from caretaker to caretaker is circulating. A tool dropped into black water is lost, whether the loss occurred an instant ago or before the oldest foundation.

The future is divided similarly: what is already under pressure, what must be returned, and what may enter the basin.

Their histories are architectural. A treaty is recorded by joining two channels. A census is a pattern of holes drilled through a resonant plate. A disaster is remembered by preserving the repair rather than the damage. They distrust marks that merely depict. An account should participate in the world it describes. The oldest civic records are functioning spillways whose arrangement encodes obligations in the order water passes through them. To read one is to follow the water with the hands.

Because continuous sound erodes distinction, their music is made from silence. Small ensembles gather around channels and interrupt them with fitted shutters, fingers, shell plates, or their own bodies. A performance consists of carefully shaped absences moving through the roar. The finest compositions are nearly imperceptible: a low cistern note removed for three beats; a tremor allowed to return beneath a bridge; the central cascade divided so that one harmonic vanishes and appears again in another district.

No two listeners hear exactly the same work, because each stands against different stone.

Their ceremonies are similarly restrained. They do not light lamps for celebration. Flame consumes scarce material, fouls the ceiling, and produces heat without useful pressure. A lamp is lit only when something must be witnessed by the whole range of living systems in the city: the Kept, their inner households, the brine organisms, the mineral seams that hold brightness after contact. At such times a drop of rendered body-fat is burned in a clay niche. The flame may be smaller than a seed. Citizens gather close enough that their shells touch. Those unable to attend receive the altered vibrations through the walls.

They have no rulers in the sense of command. Authority belongs to whoever is nearest the consequence and has listened longest. A channel dispute is settled by its keeper. A structural question goes to those who know the relevant tones. At the central pillar, decisions are made by the caretakers whose bodies have learned the collar’s vibration over generations of apprenticeship.

This produces neither equality nor gentleness. The most important duties can consume entire lives. A citizen assigned to the pillar may never leave the maintenance court after its first inspection. Those who tend the burial cradles are exposed to poor feeding and corrosive seepage. Silt-lifters in the lower wards accumulate dark deposits in their joints and often fail to form offspring. The Kept recognize such costs but do not frame them as injustice. Their moral failure is displacement: causing a burden to fall where it cannot be returned.

Their gravest crime is not killing. It is interruption.

To block a spring, conceal a crack, falsify a resonance, waste a nourishing film, or let silt accumulate unreported endangers balances extending beyond any single life. A murderer may have removed one citizen; a false inspector may have removed a century. Punishment usually takes the form of solitary listening in an unworked chamber, where the offender must map the stone’s unnoticed pressures. Exile is almost unknown. There is nowhere to exile anyone except the basin.

No citizen is deliberately given to the black water.

They fear it, but not because they imagine monsters there. Monsters would be inhabitants, and inhabitants would imply exchange. The basin is terrible because it does not participate. It receives every channel and returns no signal that can be understood. Lines descend inconsistently. Tapped weights answer from impossible directions. On rare occasions, a line goes slack without breaking and is drawn back coated in mineral patterns unlike those found anywhere above.

The Kept scrape these patterns away.

A minority believe the basin is not a destination but an organ: the unresponsive interior of some greater body in which the city is a thin, conscious shell. Others hold that the contradictory depths prove the water has no bottom because depth is a property created by sounding. A practical tradition rejects all such claims. The basin takes. That is sufficient knowledge.

They are more reverent toward the ceiling. They press their backs against it in narrow upper passages and feel the immense, unarticulated weight beyond. Their oldest devotion is not directed to a maker, but to pressure—the force that would close every chamber if not distributed through pillar, wall, water, and labor. Pressure is neither good nor hostile. It is what makes form necessary.

Their prayers are inspections.

A worshipper runs sensitive digits along a seam, tests each grain, and reports truthfully what it finds. To exaggerate danger is as impious as hiding it. To praise a sound joint is unnecessary. The joint continues to bear.

The pillar at the city’s center is the nearest thing they have to a sacred being, though they insist that it is not alive. Each caretaker comes to know its minute vibrations with the intimacy of another’s speech. The pillar clicks under loads too gradual for the eye. Water travels through it in threads. Its stone collar expands and contracts by amounts smaller than silt grains. Those who serve there begin to tap unconsciously in its rhythm.

Some die with their forelimbs still resting on the same joint.

There is an old unease among the caretakers that the pillar no longer needs them. The calcite deposited by water may have fused collar and stone into a stronger structure than the original. Their scraping, performed to preserve the designed gaps, might be preventing a new support from forming. Or the apparent fusion may be superficial, and leaving it untouched might conceal a fatal split. No experiment could be reversed. Thus they maintain the inherited thickness exactly, neither allowing the mineral to grow nor cutting deeper.

This uncertainty is the closest their culture comes to despair.

A few citizens reject maintenance. They are not vandals. They are called **Listeners to the Unreturned**, and they believe that perfect restoration has prevented the city from learning what it is becoming. They inhabit remote recesses where scale is allowed to thicken, channels wander, and drips find new routes. Their districts look diseased to ordinary citizens: doors sealed by crystal, stairs softened under white deposits, inscriptions obscured. Yet new resonances emerge there, and unfamiliar mineral films support households once thought extinct.

The Listeners argue that change is not always loss.

Most of the Kept answer that change which cannot be returned is only a slower name for the basin.

Still, no one destroys the altered districts. They are inspected from the margins. Their sounds are added to the city’s maps. Sometimes, discreetly, a mainstream feeder visits one of their new seams and carries a taste of it home.

There may once have been other kinds of inhabitants. Hinged shells are found in sealed silt, too large to belong to the luminous brine organisms and too small to have been Kept. Certain doorways open at heights no citizen can comfortably use. A few tools have grips arranged for five forward digits rather than four. In the oldest quarter, channels contain shallow side-pools whose purpose cannot be reconstructed; they seem designed for something that lived immersed.

The Kept maintain these features without explanation.

And there are the brine lights themselves: simple flecks suspended in salt water, brightening continuously to the vibration of the cave. They do not feed as animals feed or divide on any observed schedule. Bowls neglected for centuries remain luminous. When transferred, the brine sometimes goes dark, then brightens again only after being returned to its original niche. The Kept describe it as living because it answers, but they do not claim to know what answers from within it.

So the city’s population cannot be counted cleanly.

There are perhaps forty-three walking citizens, depending on whether the two sealed elders still changing in their burial shells are included. There are thousands of ancestral households alive inside those citizens. There are luminous colonies in the bowls, films at the meeting of waters, pale grains that gather around copper traces, and motions in the deepest cisterns that may be organisms or only precipitation. There are duties that move from body to body and are spoken of as though they themselves survive. There are buildings whose continuing functions grant them a status close to personhood.

Then there is the city entire.

The Kept do not call it alive. They say only that it must be fed stone, relieved of silt, kept open against pressure, and listened to for signs of distress. It has channels like vessels, districts that exchange materials, a hard translucent skin, and small pale creatures moving within it to preserve its balance. When one part is damaged, distant parts alter their labor. Its light responds to its noise. Its oldest substance is continually replaced while its shape endures.

Asked what lives in the hollow, a citizen would not point to itself.

It would place four hands against the wet floor, incline its head into the unceasing vibration, and answer with a word that means at once **city**, **body**, **burden**, and **that which has not yet closed**.
