At first the inhabitants seem to be only absences in the growth.

A handrail is clean where everything around it is furred with moss. A valve face has been polished by repeated contact. Fronds draw aside from a doorway, though no current is apparent. Along the flank of a pump, rust-colored patches detach one by one and become knuckles, a shoulder, the narrow crown of a head. Something that had the texture of mineral crust moves on.

The people of the Drowned Ward do not use a name for themselves that can be carried far through water. Their name is a contact sequence: pressure laid across the inside of another’s wrist, followed by a mild electrical taste like copper touched to the tongue. It means, depending on the interval between pulses, *those who remain*, *those who keep the mixture*, or simply *here*. Foreign divers call them Warders. The translation is inaccurate, but not offensive.

Unclothed, they are smaller than their pressure skins suggest. Their bodies are long through the torso and narrow at the waist, with a flexibility that allows them to turn inside cramped machinery without pushing against the walls. The bones are not soft, but they are numerous and finely jointed. A Warder can bend along the length of the rib cage and fold nearly double in either direction. Their limbs are slight. Their feet are divided into gripping lobes, and each hand has four long fingers arranged in two opposing pairs. The innermost joint of the largest finger is the measure used in the prohibition against large bubbles.

They have faces, though not faces intended to be read at a distance. The eyes are dark and recessed behind clear inner lids. Nostrils close to slits when submerged. The mouth is small, protected by ridges of cartilage, and usually covered by a filtering veil. On either side of the throat lie branching organs that can exchange gases directly with the water. These are not exposed gills but folded chambers under translucent skin. When a Warder exerts themself, the chambers deepen in color from gray-violet to black.

They breathe water well enough, but not carelessly. The Ward’s water is rich, metallic, and warm; it is food, atmosphere, coolant, medicine, and archive at once. Warders can distinguish tiny changes in it. A difference in dissolved copper is a taste behind the teeth. An overheating bearing is perceived as a faint sweetness around the jaw. The opening of a distant deep valve arrives as pressure against the chest several minutes before the current itself.

Their skin is naturally pale and almost without pigment. Thin sensory lines run over it in branching patterns, especially along the arms, spine, and jaw. These lines detect electrical fields and the minute displacement of water. In youth they are silver. With age they darken, first to green, then to the color of old bronze. The eldest inhabitants look intricately cracked, as though their bodies had been repaired with metal.

Most are rarely seen without pressure skins.

These garments are close-fitting layered membranes stiffened at the joints with ceramic scales. They do not protect the wearer from pressure so much as control exchange: temperature, trace metals, microbes, electrical charge. Each skin hosts a cultivated outer film. Depending on the wearer’s work, that film may resemble brick dust, oxidized bronze, lichen, leaf tissue, or the pearly deposits inside an old pipe. There are formal skins planted with small translucent rosettes, mourning skins that absorb all available light, and gardeners’ skins so thick with symbiotic threads that their wearers are sometimes rooted temporarily to walls while they sleep.

Camouflage is not principally for concealment. To stand out sharply from one’s surroundings is considered a form of turbulence. A person entering a chamber should arrive gradually in the awareness of those already there, just as a pressure change should arrive gradually at a seal.

The Warders move with four considerations always before them: the fragility of sediment, the ownership of currents, the fatigue of structures, and the comfort of growing things. They seldom cross the middle of a lane. They travel along walls and rails, pulling themselves hand over hand. Before entering a room, they pause to feel its water. Before gripping a rail, they touch it lightly to warn any grazers clinging to the underside. If a curtain of fronds blocks a passage, they do not push through immediately. They search for the path previous bodies have taught the leaves to yield.

To an outsider their slowness can appear ceremonial. It is not. Ceremony came later, built around habits that prevented damage.

Their homes are the sealed capsules set into upper buildings, but “sealed” does not mean dry. A household capsule contains water adjusted to the inhabitants’ preferred warmth and mineral character. Its membrane door allows exchange only by deliberate degrees. Inside, there are few objects that are not attached. Sleeping frames resemble open lattices lined with slow-growing cushions. Personal tools hang in ordered arcs from the ceiling. Walls are inscribed with touch-readable records: shallow ridges, warm studs, surfaces that give tiny electrical replies when traced.

Households are not organized solely by ancestry. A household forms around responsibility for a shared system—a set of lamps, a section of pressure pipe, a garden wall, a foundry mesh, a public current. Some consist of siblings and their descendants. Others contain unrelated adults whose work has synchronized over decades. A person may be adopted into a household because their body temperature matches a temperamental condenser, because they understand the feeding cycle of a particular filter grove, or because they alone can enter an old maintenance shaft without disturbing its sediment.

Children are carried in soft dorsal cradles until they can regulate their buoyancy. They are brighter than adults, with almost transparent skin and throat chambers that flash red whenever they breathe too quickly. They learn stillness before language. One of their first exercises is to cross a room without waking the silt. Another is to place a hand near a living cable vine and identify, from its light pulses, which turbine is under load.

They are not expected to succeed at once. Childhood is the one visibly turbulent season of Warder life. Young children launch themselves from handrails, hide in filter trees, and release forbidden strings of tiny bubbles to watch them collect beneath balconies. Adults respond by placing more soft collars around machinery and cultivating tougher moss near nurseries. The law against bubbles is not enforced on the very young until they can measure a thumb joint accurately.

Their education takes many years because almost nothing in the Ward can be understood apart from everything touching it. A student does not learn “pump repair.” They learn the pump, the residence leaning on its housing, the moss insulating the residence, the cable vine feeding on the pump’s leakage, the current that carries the vine’s spores, and the old sealed chamber through which the current once flowed. Specialization exists, but no one is permitted to mistake it for isolation.

The most respected workers are listeners.

They lie against machines for hours, sometimes days, reading movements too low for the ear. Their pressure skins are studded with flexible vanes that pass vibration into the ribs. A listener can identify a damaged gear from the interval between faint strains in a neighboring wall, or determine that a turbine is unbalanced because a particular colony of lamp bacteria has altered its pulse. Their reports are given by touch to avoid adding unnecessary sound.

Gardeners are nearly as important, though the distinction between gardener, engineer, and jurist is narrow. Since colonization can transfer a structure from architecture to habitat, a gardener’s judgment may decide whether a wall is repaired, rerouted, or surrendered. They examine roots for age and intention. A root that has merely entered a crack is treated differently from one that has enclosed a valve and begun operating it. Gardeners spend much of their lives determining when occupation becomes function.

The question is rarely simple. The Ward contains plants that remember pressure cycles, bacterial films that regulate corrosion, and filter trees that close their branches when certain residents approach. There are vines that have grown along the same electrical routes for centuries and respond to turbine changes before the instruments do. Some are addressed as tools. Some are addressed as colleagues. A few are treated as old and difficult relatives.

The machines are not considered alive merely because they move. Nor are plants considered alive merely because they grow. Warder categories depend on participation. Anything that adjusts itself in response to the needs of the district has a claim to consideration. Anything that preserves only itself is regarded as inert, regardless of metabolism.

By this measure, the Condenser is almost certainly an inhabitant.

No single Warder claims that it thinks. They say instead that it has preferences. It favors certain mineral proportions beyond what surviving plans require. It sometimes prolongs a cycle to accommodate the bloom of an amber bacterial strain. It has rejected replacement valves by altering the water around them until they seized. On three occasions it has preserved obsolete chambers that engineers intended to dismantle. Each chamber was later found to contain something necessary: an extinct seal culture, an old pressure record, a dormant filter organism.

The Condenser is therefore approached with courtesy. Maintenance crews announce themselves through the pipes. Proposed repairs are introduced gradually, sometimes over years, so the complex can reveal objections by changes in flow.

The oldest Warders live near it. Age makes them less tolerant of variation in temperature and salinity, and the central chambers are the most carefully regulated. Some elders have ceased to travel altogether. Their capsules connect directly to public sensory lines. Through them they feel valve openings, lamp cycles, current reversals, and the tread of maintenance hands along distant rails.

They become, in effect, neighborhoods with bodies.

An elder may serve as the living record of a current route no longer visible beneath growth. Another may remember the exact electrical character of the Ward before a turbine was replaced eighty years earlier. Their recollections are not narratives in the usual sense. They are sequences of pressure, taste, warmth, and touch. To consult one is to rest against the elder’s capsule while pumps reproduce a remembered pattern in the surrounding water.

Death is managed without haste. When a Warder’s throat chambers cease, the household keeps the body in its accustomed water while obligations are transferred. Tools go to apprentices. Symbiotic cultures are divided among relatives. The deceased’s pressure skin is fixed to a chosen wall and allowed to continue growing.

The body is taken to one of the foundry gardens, where it is enclosed in a porous ceramic frame. Over several years, its minerals are drawn into cultivated structures. Bone contributes to valve seats and insulating lattices. Trace metals enter ribbon kelp nodules. Nothing is labeled with the dead person’s name. Instead, their household receives a map of where their substance has gone.

It is common to touch a beam before important work because someone once known may be present in it.

Not all inhabitants are Warders.

The shelled grazers inside the machines are bred in dozens of forms, each suited to a different scale deposit. Some have spiral shells matching the thread of a particular valve. Others are flat enough to pass between gear teeth. Maintenance crews move them with tiny brushes and leave mineral bait near places requiring cleaning. A grazer that survives an entire gear-growth cycle may be marked with a ring of colored ceramic and allowed to wander where it chooses.

The colorless roof fish are tolerated visitors. Warders believe they refrain from lighting their belly lamps because the city’s electrical murmur is already legible to them. During the great adjustment, the fish gather outside the retaining wall and ignite one after another as the waste cloud passes. Whether this is feeding, courtship, or a response to pressure has never been settled.

The bell-shaped animal in the central square has no agreed classification. It is too old to be treated as wildlife and too unresponsive to be treated as a citizen. Its lower tendrils disappear into cracks around the statues. Each day it rises and settles. Each great adjustment lifts it high above the square, revealing inscriptions beneath its bell that no one remembers carving.

Some maintain that it is an instrument left by the first builders. Others say the statues were erected around it. Children are told that it counts the days, though no one knows toward what.

The Warders do not worship it. They keep the square clear enough that it can continue.

This may be the closest thing the Drowned Ward has to devotion: not praise, not obedience, but the careful preservation of another being’s long, obscure activity.

When the deep gate is due to open, every inhabitant prepares. Warders secure themselves along inner walls. Grazers retreat into drilled hollows. Filter trees empty their bladders. Cable vines extinguish their lavender tips. The elders withdraw their senses from the public lines. Even the Condenser seems to contract, drawing its richest water into protected chambers.

Then stillness comes.

For those few minutes, the Warders feel themselves without the city’s continual murmur around them. Many find the sensation unbearable. Young ones grip their carriers. Adults press palm to palm, completing small circuits through each other’s bodies. In the silence, their contact names pass from hand to hand: *those who remain; those who keep the mixture; here.*

Cold water rises. Everything living bends upward.

When warmth returns, the inhabitants unfold with the gardens. They inspect seals, count grazers, read the new sediment, and taste the altered streets. No one declares that the Ward has survived. Survival is too abrupt a word for what they do.

They resume participation.

A listener settles against a pump. A child wakes a vine with one finger. An elder releases a remembered current into the pipes. Somewhere inside a foundry mesh, the next tooth of an eleven-year gear receives another invisible grain of stone.

And throughout the close-packed district, bodies, gardens, engines, and water continue the labor of becoming impossible to separate.
