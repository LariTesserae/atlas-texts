Nothing in the sea is native. That distinction matters less with every generation.

The first inhabitants are almost too thin to possess bodies. They live as films over concrete, glass, paint, and steel: green where the lamps reach, brown in the half-light, colorless around warm drains. They occupy scratches left by construction tools and the minute shelter behind bolt heads. Bacteria feed on iron, grease, dissolved waste, sacrificial zinc. Algae spread across upward-facing surfaces until grazing mouths erase them. Together they put a skin on the reservoir’s hard young bones.

Tiny crustaceans move through this skin like livestock through pasture. They crowd wherever nutrient water escapes—a gasket breathing weakly, a cultivation pipe sweating through its seam, a grate dusted from above. Their populations flare and vanish according to maintenance cycles. A valve leak may sustain a whole province for months; its repair produces a famine no animal understands.

The small silver fish understand the sea best, though not as a structure.

They know it as brightness, vibration, and flow. The traveling front of noon draws them across districts. They turn toward newly kindled galleries and abandon sectors slipping into dusk. When adjacent power grids disagree, schools gather at the boundary, flashing between two artificial hours. Seen from a distance, each school resembles a weather formation: narrowing under threat, spreading in calm water, folding around pylons and ventilation towers.

They have learned that a low tremor in the water can mean food is coming. Screen-cleaning machinery knocks loose mussel flesh, larvae, algae, and scraps from aquaculture decks. Before the debris arrives, the fish are already facing into the current. They also know the lethal signatures: the preliminary shudder of an intake waking, the rising whine of a gate drive, the pressure change preceding a ballast discharge. They do not know what causes these things. Knowledge has entered their bodies as reflex.

Generations raised beneath lamps no longer agree perfectly with the species released from the hatcheries. Some spawn when a particular lighting district reaches full intensity, regardless of the hour elsewhere. Some keep close to warm pipes and have become smaller, quicker-breeding, tolerant of water that tastes faintly of metal. Those living in the central volume are long-muscled and tireless. They may spend their entire lives swimming without touching any solid surface.

Others are creatures of the construction.

Crabs occupy the bolt fields as if the foundations were rocky coasts. They wedge themselves among threaded rods, feeding with their claws while rust drifts down around them. Their shells are often stained orange. Individuals near painted machinery may be yellow-backed or red-flecked from mineral deposits, though beneath the stain they remain ordinary animals, belligerent and opportunistic.

Eels inherit the conduits. They enter as juveniles through gaps no inspection probe considers significant and grow in the dark among bundled cables. Some live in dead pipes; others inhabit active ones, pressed continually by warm flow. They emerge when nearby lamps dim, following service trenches from one opening to another. Workers sometimes find them kilometers inside a system thought to be sealed. An eel on a maintenance camera is treated not as a surprise but as evidence: somewhere upstream, there is a route the diagrams do not show.

Mussels turn every neglected edge into a reef. They mass along expansion joints, intake rims, ladder rungs, sensor housings, and the bars around propellers. Their shells soften engineered lines into dark, uneven ridges. Shrimp shelter within those ridges, and fish graze over them. Then a cleaning machine arrives, its brushes turning, and years of accumulated habitat become a moving cloud of shell and meat. The reef is destroyed. Downcurrent, a feast begins.

The sharks follow such feasts, but they do not depend on them.

They are cool-water animals, lean-bodied and gray, bred to remain healthy in the reservoir’s faint salinity. No ornament marks them as manufactured. They have tags beneath the skin and lineages preserved in hatchery records, but in the open water they are simply sharks. Their abundance is hidden by distance. Each patrols a volume so great that two may pass weeks without meeting.

They hunt along walls because straight surfaces deny prey one direction of escape. They hunt at power boundaries, where changing light compresses the schools. They wait beyond the turbulence behind cargo platforms, taking stunned fish from the disturbed water. Older sharks know the fenced propellers and avoid them. Younger ones sometimes follow shoals into machinery corridors and do not return.

The largest individuals carry maps made of scars: parallel scratches from cable forests, pale marks from acoustic tags, crescent wounds from gates that nearly closed. Maintenance crews recognize a few by silhouette. They give them workmanlike names—Rivet, Twelve, Westline, Lantern—but the sharks do not become tame. They pass inspection windows with the same disinterest they show a concrete wall.

At the surface live the seals, the most obvious opportunists.

They sleep on warm pump housings, crowd maintenance rafts, bark beneath the roof, and steal from conveyor systems whenever the timing allows. Alarm sirens do not disturb them. A seal born beside a turbine may sleep through vibrations that make visiting workers nauseated. Sudden silence wakes it.

They follow boats not from affection but because boats reveal openings: disturbed fish, discarded food, temporarily unguarded platforms. Colonies have divided according to infrastructure. Northern seals are thick-furred and familiar with the thin ice around refrigeration leaks. Those near the freight corridors are sleeker, accustomed to long swims in driven currents. Platform colonies defend particular warm casings as fiercely as natural seals might defend beaches.

Their pups learn the industrial day before they learn the open water. They are born beneath lamps, among painted rails and coiled hoses, to the smell of ozone, fish, hot metal, and wet concrete. Rain from the roof is the only rain they know.

Human beings live there too, though from the water they appear to be the least aquatic inhabitants.

They occupy dry pockets inside walls: pressure cylinders, suspended workshops, control galleries, dormitories behind layered seals. Their habitat is air held apart from the sea by steel, gaskets, glass, and constant attention. They hear the pumps through their floors and sleep inside vibrations that never entirely stop. New workers complain of the noise. Experienced workers fear changes in it.

They become skilled listeners. A bearing beginning to fail is a roughness beneath the usual thunder. A distant gate has a recognizable sequence of impacts. An intake crowded with animals sounds different from one clogged by insulation or shell. During shutdowns, crews speak softly, as if the whole complex has become capable of overhearing them.

Divers are the nearest thing the sea has to human wildlife. They leave the sealed rooms carrying heat, light, compressed gas, tools, and acoustic beacons. In the open reservoir they are conspicuous but very small. Fish approach their lamps. Sharks examine them and turn away. Currents slide them along walls larger than cities.

No diver trusts vision alone. The water may be clear, yet clarity only reveals how little can be seen. They navigate by transmitted machinery: western impellers behind the left shoulder, desalination grind ahead, intermittent gate-hammer below. When those references stop, the diver’s world loses its directions.

The crews are caretakers, predators, physicians, and periodic disasters. They seed plankton and cull fish. They rescue seals from cable loops, then install barriers against them. They scrape mussels from machinery and later release new filter feeders elsewhere. They regulate oxygen for the whole basin while killing every living thing in a pipe that must be reopened. Their morality is necessarily sectional. They save the sea by repeatedly destroying portions of it.

Around them work machines that are not alive, though the distinction becomes locally unhelpful.

Dredges graze sediment beds. Ballast systems breathe. Cargo platforms migrate with attendant shoals. Gates alter currents, divide populations, and carry larvae between distant sectors. Filter brushes strip reefs from steel and distribute their remains. Each machine has a territory, a rhythm, a wake, and creatures adapted to its presence. When one is retired, its ecological absence can be as significant as the death of a large animal.

Some abandoned machines cease being equipment almost immediately. Their cavities fill with eels. Mussels seal their joints. Algae take the upper planes. Fish shelter behind their blades. Sharks circle their shadows. Workers continue to call them by serial number, but the sea treats them as terrain.

Thus what lives there is not a collection of species so much as a chain of accommodations.

The microbes live on the metals. The crustaceans live on the films. Fish live on the crustaceans and the scheduled light. Sharks live on the fish and the geometry of walls. Seals live on the machinery’s warmth and the predictability of workers. Humans live inside pressure boundaries, dependent on the same pumps that dictate every current outside. The machines live only figuratively, but all literal life arranges itself around their breaths.

No creature comprehends the reservoir. A mussel knows one joint. An eel knows a conduit. A shark knows a sequence of hunting lanes. A seal knows warm platforms and boats worth following. An engineer knows diagrams, pressure districts, maintenance histories, and a few kilometers of corridor. Even the central systems possess the sea only as numbers arriving from sensors.

Yet taken together, these partial knowledges amount to something like an organism’s awareness. A change in one district travels outward as current, hunger, migration, alarm, repair order, and altered behavior. Light draws fish; fish draw sharks; sharks redirect divers; divers delay a gate cycle; the delayed gate carries larvae somewhere new.

That is what lives there most completely: not any one animal, nor the people sealed into the walls, but an industrial ecology learning itself through consequence. It has no single body. Its skeleton is concrete and trusswork, its circulation is pumped water, its nerves are cables and pressure waves, and its flesh is everything that feeds, breeds, hunts, fouls, repairs, or hides within it.

It is young. It is managed. It is already beyond obedience.
