At first the inhabitants appear to be animals only because they have feathers, hides, eyes, mouths, and the involuntary motions of living things. They breathe. They startle. Their skins tighten around working muscles. A bird resettles its weight upon a roof edge; an eel rolls a lidless eye through silver water; one of the gray six-legged beasts opens its mouth and shows a tongue dark with metal salts.

But the longer they are watched, the less certain their animality becomes.

There are no young among them and no elders. None bears a healing wound, a fresh scar, a distended belly, a shed skin. Their differences are the differences found among tools: size, fitting, placement, duty. The birds on the western roofs have broad, resonant chests and iron-colored throats. Those nearer the furnaces are smaller and stand with their beaks inserted into ceramic sockets. The beetle-things beneath the rails vary according to the pitch of the beam they strike. Even individuals that seem deformed prove exactly suited to some crooked housing, recessed flange, or misaligned length of pipe.

Perhaps they were bred for the city. Perhaps the city was built around them. More likely neither statement is old enough.

The roof-birds live in rows so dense that individuality disappears. Each is squat, ash-black, and warm beneath the breast, with feet that have clasped the same oxidized ledge for longer than the ledge can testify. Their feathers overlap from one body to the next until a flock resembles a single dark fabric laid along the architecture. When they open their beaks, there is no birdsong. A hard chord enters the bricks, and the bricks answer faintly. Their many throats seem to test the city for cracks, sounding every chamber at once.

Now and then one bird turns its head. The motion passes down the row without traveling: every head is already turned in its appointed place. Their eyes are polished brown-black and reflect not the observer but rooflines from elsewhere in the basin.

The cistern eels are nearly colorless. They knot themselves around axles and submerged copper hoops, their bodies sliding continuously without changing arrangement. Silver particles adhere to their mucus in delicate circuit patterns. Their pulses travel visibly from head to tail, blue-white beneath translucent flesh, and the dynamos below them hum in sympathy. They do not rise for air. They do not investigate anything dropped into the water. At the opaque center of each cistern, the cables of bodies become too dense to distinguish, and it is possible that all the pools contain extremities of one buried animal rather than separate populations.

The shell-backed creatures are more readily mistaken for mechanisms. They hang upside down in colonies beneath stairs, rails, and hollow galleries, gripping metal with pale jointed hands. Their carapaces are black, green, or the brown-purple of tempered steel. Inside each shell something spins fast enough to blur. They strike beams with the rounded ends of their abdomens, pause for pressure to return through the structure, then strike again. If removed from a rail—and they resist removal only by becoming impossibly heavy—they continue hammering empty air at the same interval. The rail they leave behind continues to ring.

In the alleys stand the gray herds.

They are narrow enough to fit between pipes and tall enough that their ridged backs disappear beneath conveyors. Six legs spread their weight across the cinder ground. Their hides are pleated, hairless, and powdered with the same mineral dust as the walls, so that a herd at rest looks like stacked industrial cloth. Along either flank runs a succession of eyes, each attending to a different depth: nearby valves, distant towers, the cloudy sky, perhaps something under the ground. No two eyes blink together, though the herd breathes as one chest.

Their mouths are modest and almost hidden. Their rib cages are enormous.

With each inhalation, air is drawn through vents in the belt housings above them. With each exhalation, buried fluids move, gauges climb, pistons take one more fruitless stroke. Flexible couplings descend from the machinery and rest between their ribs without piercing the skin. The animals could step away. Their feet are not chained; the alleys before them are open. Yet stepping away may be no more imaginable to them than abandoning a lung is to the breath inside it.

The largest inhabitants cannot be seen whole.

They occupy halls built as cradles: pale, yielding masses folded between columns, their surfaces marked by slow tides of color. Some have limbs, though the limbs terminate in porcelain collars and cable bundles. Some possess crowns of antlers spreading through switchyards. One is all mouth, a circular muscular opening fastened around the intake of a furnace. Another resembles a sleeping ray draped across three floors, its fins disappearing through slots in the walls. The buildings conceal so much of them that it is unclear whether the halls contain the creatures or merely expose portions of bodies extending through the city’s foundations.

Their expressions, where they have faces, are neither pained nor peaceful. They show the grave vacancy of beings for whom sensation has become infrastructure. Current passes through them. Heat flowers beneath their skin and is gone. At the city’s periodic blast, their muscles tighten; tiles crack nowhere, terminals arc, and every great body flashes with the same dim internal recognition.

Smaller lives occupy nearly every remaining scale. Needle-legged things patrol the engraved marks on gauges. Soft colonies dilate inside valve glasses. White mites cluster around red numerals and rearrange themselves whenever a digit flickers, as if they are either reading it or causing it to be read. In ventilation chambers, radial animals open and close like fleshy irises. Transparent worms inhabit insulated wire, one in each strand, carrying light along their digestive tracts. Minute winged forms circulate through relay cabinets and settle between contacts at the instant a circuit closes.

None of them eats, but many have mouths.

The mouths may be remnants, ceremonial features, or ports intended for materials that no longer exist. Some open toward blank walls. Some are fitted permanently over nozzles. Some pronounce a single pressure, click, or tone into the city’s uproar. Teeth occur where gears might have been expected, and gears turn deep in throats. The distinction between organ and component is not blurred here; it seems never to have been established.

Nor is there a clear distinction between one inhabitant and many.

A flock may be a distributed larynx. A herd may be one lung repeated in parallel. The fist-sized beetles may be the loose sensory corpuscles of the ironwork. When a white creature repeatedly crosses the square, it is impossible to decide whether the same body returns, whether a succession of identical bodies occupies adjacent portions of an arrested path, or whether flight itself has become an animal stretched from chimney to chimney.

They do not communicate in any way separable from their labor. Signals pass through rails, water, pressure lines, electrical fields, and the muscular tension of the ground. A tremor in an eel appears as a change in a high window’s blue arc. A bird’s throat tightens; far below, one of the great seated forms flexes a buried limb. The gray herds blink toward the eastern gantry, and the locomotive’s wheels acquire a brighter ring of blur. Their society, if it is a society, has no exchange of messages because sender, message, receiver, and task are parts of the same event.

Yet traces of attention remain.

Some eyes follow movement that does not belong to the city. Not all at once. First an eye in a gray flank, then the lens of a rail-beetle, then a dark roof-bird’s gaze. The attention migrates without any body turning to pass it on. It does not feel curious or hostile. It is closer to the sensation of having entered a vast sleeping creature’s dream and caused, somewhere within it, a pressure too slight to wake the dreamer.

The rooms in the administration block suggest another kind of inhabitant. The chairs face away from the desks as though their users sat backward, attending to the walls or to what moved behind them. The branching documents under glass may be writing, anatomical notation, transit schedules, genealogies, or all four. Their punched holes match the spacing of eyes along the gray animals’ sides. Their wing-vein diagrams reproduce the rail system. Their burrow-like tracks converge upon chambers corresponding to the great soft bodies in the machine halls.

It may be that the clerks, planners, or owners did not leave. They may have been redistributed.

A population can vanish by death, but it can also vanish by becoming function. The old administrators might persist as the white mites around the numerals, still compiling quantities that never change. Engineers may occupy the relay cabinets as swarms. The creatures on the loading platforms, sealed in armor and fitted perfectly to the cranes, may once have possessed names. If so, the names have worn smooth like the stair treads. What remains is placement.

And beneath all these inhabitants is the one that makes the others difficult to regard as separate lives.

It breathes under the pumping house.

Perhaps it is merely another animal, larger and older, sunk into a concrete cradle beneath the basin. Perhaps the city was assembled to keep it alive. The eels may excite its nerves, the birds sustain its sleeping tone, the herds inflate auxiliary lungs, and the furnaces preserve a low heat in blood too deep to reach the surface. The useless circulation of ingots may stand in for substances once carried through its body. The locomotive, straining forever without advancing, may be a heart arrested at the peak of contraction.

But the reverse is equally possible. The creature below may be an organ made by the inhabitants to breathe for them. It may be their collective interior, the place where all their separate motions descend and become one rhythm. Its shaft is a throat only because the city needs something to inhale through. The worn rim may have been shaped not by hands but by centuries of sound being drawn across stone.

There may never have been a creature beneath the city at all.

There may only be depth, and the breathing may belong to the basin itself.

This would explain why no animal crosses the boundary posts. Outside them, each would cease to be a bird, eel, beetle, grazer, or giant. It would become a severed action: resonance without chamber, current without circuit, pressure without vessel. Their lives are not confined in the city as animals in an enclosure. Their lives are made from confinement. Position is their anatomy. Duration is their food.

They are not immortal. Immortality requires surviving time.

These inhabitants have been spared nothing and survived nothing. Each occupies its single enduring phase: the flock forever lifting and forever settled, the eel forever turning, the great body forever receiving a pulse, the gray beast forever between inhalation and exhalation. Even their apparent multitude may be the same life encountered at different stations in an industrial instant too large to perceive whole.

So the city’s inhabitants are, finally, not the fauna alone. They are the coupled forms: bird-and-roof, eel-and-cistern, herd-and-pump, giant-and-busbar, locomotive-and-track. They are the rain held above one block, the drop under the flange, the siren divided across distance. Brick, animal, machine, and weather participate in one metabolism without appetite and one labor without product.

Something lives there, certainly.

Whether it is millions of creatures operating a city, a city operating millions of creatures, or a single buried being dreaming itself as factories cannot be determined from within the basin. Every answer is one of its organs. Every observer who remains long enough begins to notice the same mineral dampness on the skin, the same fixed interval entering the breath.

And somewhere among the tremendous noise, a place seems to be waiting for that breath to fit.
