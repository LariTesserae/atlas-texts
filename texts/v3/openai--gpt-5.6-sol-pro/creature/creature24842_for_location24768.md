At first, the inhabitants of the Still Ward seem smaller than they are.

They stoop beneath bridges, turn sideways through doorways, fold themselves onto low benches, and walk with their arms held close so that sleeves do not brush the gardens. Even in the central court no one takes a full, careless stride. Space has taught them a gathered posture. Above ground they would appear hesitant, perhaps furtive, until they found something to stand beside.

They are pale only by reflection. The Ward’s white light lies on every face, washing dark and light skin alike with the color of limestone. Their cheeks and fingertips are often reddened by cold. Older people bear fine gray scars on their hands where shell, sedge, and stone have cut them during repairs. Many have a permanent shine of mineral dust in the lines around their eyes.

Their eyes tend to be narrow against the brilliance. In the softer hours, after the blade of sky has dimmed, their expressions seem to open. Then one notices how intently they look: not only at faces, but at lips, fingers, shoulders, the slight inclination of a head. Hearing is useful in the Ward, but attention is more useful. A resident can distinguish a request from a warning by the pressure of two fingertips on the wrist.

They wear many thin layers. Closest to the body is soft felt or cloth woven from nettle fiber, over which come quilted vests, short coats of treated skin, and aprons sewn with narrow pockets. Loose garments are considered clumsy. Hair is braided flat, bound beneath close caps, or cropped so that it cannot catch moss from a wall. Children wear padded knees and elbows until they have learned the dimensions of every passage.

Nothing on them jingles. Fastenings are toggles of bone, loops of cord, polished stones captured in netting. Ornaments are quiet things: a white snail shell at the throat, a pattern pressed into felt, mica sewn beneath translucent membrane, a copper bead inherited so long that no one remembers when it was round.

Their hands are seldom still. During conversation they strip leaves, mend baskets, sort beetle cases, rub a tool edge smooth, or test the dampness of a cord. Their fingers are strong without being heavily callused, adapted to pressure and patience rather than blows. A child’s first proper tool is usually a burnisher. The customary lesson is not how hard to press, but how long.

No one in the Ward has only one occupation. The keeper of a cistern may also set broken bones, cultivate mint, and know the nesting habits of every bird along the eastern wall. A plasterer may deliver infants because both arts require warm hands and an understanding of surfaces under strain. People are identified less by trades than by the portions of the city entrusted to them: the family beneath the blue-flower bridge, the people of the western drip, the household that keeps the third stair from settling.

These responsibilities pass through generations, though not always through blood. A household may include siblings, widowed neighbors, apprentices, abandoned children, and an old person who arrived decades earlier and never disclosed from where. Kinship in the Ward is measured partly by shared warmth. Those who have slept around the same hearth through enough winters come to resemble a family in every way that matters.

Their speech is low and close. It contains many breath-soft consonants and words shortened by familiarity. Names used in public are usually one syllable. Longer names belong to formal promises and are spoken directly into the listener’s ear. Across a room, the inhabitants converse with small gestures: a palm turned outward, a finger drawn along the jaw, the knuckles briefly touched to the breastbone. A whole dispute may pass over a market table without disturbing a moth on the wall.

Silence has not made them solemn. They joke constantly, but much of their humor is physical and nearly invisible: an exaggeratedly careful hand, a fern tucked into someone’s hood, a pebble quietly traded back and forth until its original owner discovers it in their soup. Laughter is breathed through the nose or hidden against a sleeve. The rare person with a loud laugh is beloved and watched over, hurried by friends into layers of felt whenever delight overtakes them.

Children are louder than the law permits, because children everywhere begin as creatures unsuited to their surroundings. Infants are carried against the chest in thick wraps that muffle crying. Toddlers are given seed husks, felt balls, snailshell counters, and miniature channels through which droplets can be guided. Their first games teach stillness: remaining motionless while a moth crosses the face, catching water in a spoon without letting it ring, finding a hidden companion by watching which fern trembles.

They are not punished for noise so much as shown its consequences. A child who strikes a wall is taken back over the following days to feel the faint unrest in the stone. They watch displaced droplets miss their cups. They help carry water to the beds that went dry. Thus quiet becomes neither superstition nor mere obedience, but a form of care.

The oldest residents are extraordinarily slow. They know where every stair dips and can walk the Ward in darkness without touching a wall. Some are almost never heard speaking. They sit near channels with their fingertips resting on the stone, detecting changes in seepage too slight for younger hands. An elder may announce that rain will reach the cisterns tomorrow, though the plain above has been dry for weeks.

The Ward’s spiritual keepers are often chosen from among these listeners. They have no elevated shrine and wear no distinct garment. Their work is to remember what must not be altered. They recite no grand creation story. Instead, they preserve sequences: which cup fills after the north wall darkens, which root bed must be closed when the frogs rise, which crack may be packed with moss and which must be allowed to breathe.

To them, the city is not a shelter built inside dead stone. It is a slow creature whose life is distributed among water, masonry, roots, animals, and human attention. A neglected stair is therefore a kind of wound. A blocked channel is a fever. Fresh plaster is applied with the gravity of a bandage.

The dead are not carried to a cemetery; there is no room for one. Bodies are wrapped in sedge and laid for a time in the coldest chambers, while relatives sit nearby and work. Later, the bones are cleaned and separated. Some become needles, handles, measuring pins, or the small ladles used only at the central pool. Most are fitted into sealed niches behind reflective plaster. A few finger bones remain with the household, not as relics of identity but as reminders that useful care may continue after a name has ceased to be spoken.

Nothing living in the Ward is entirely tame.

The gray birds nest near people but resist handling. They know the daily dimming of the roof crack and descend to glean seeds from terraces, cocking their heads at human gestures. The velvet mice are tolerated as long as they remain in the warm channels and do not enter root stores. Some households leave them scraps; others construct elaborate silent gates to redirect them.

The eyeless frogs belong to no one. Their emergence from the lower chambers marks subtle changes in the water, and their tiny bodies are read like weather signs. A frog found on an upper stair may bring half the Ward to inspect a hidden leak. Children are forbidden to touch them until old enough to lift one without drying its skin.

The white moths are treated with particular gentleness. They settle on sleeping people, tools, fresh plaster, and the faces of the dead. No doctrine explains them. Still, when one rests on a person’s mouth, conversation stops until it leaves.

Even the gardens have temperaments. Sorrel spreads aggressively and must be bartered from terrace to terrace so no household bears too much of it. The dwarf willows remember each bend imposed upon them and resist a careless hand. Certain old moss beds are never divided because they hold water through the longest dry spells. Gardeners know individual ferns by the shape of their first spring curl.

The people themselves eat little at once: roots roasted in clay, bitter greens, mushrooms, snails, bird eggs when nesting is abundant, occasional meat from small river animals trapped far beyond the Ward. Meals are thickened into warm pastes and shared from covered bowls. Steam is caught on slanted lids and returned to the pot. Guests are offered water before food, but only a mouthful. To give someone a full cup is an extravagant declaration of trust or love.

Those born below sometimes climb to the plain. They go at dusk or under cloud, when the open light is less severe. Above ground they move close together, unsettled by the absence of walls. Wind troubles them most. It steals warmth, scatters scent, moves every plant at once, and makes sound without any visible act to cause it. Some cannot sleep afterward until they have returned beneath stone.

A few leave permanently. Their places are not kept empty. No place in the Ward is. Another bed is fitted to the hearth, another pair of hands assumes the gutter or nesting ledge, and the departed person’s name gradually shortens in use until it survives only in the name of a stair, a patched basin, or a particular way of tying willow.

Strangers sometimes imagine that such a people must be timid. They are not. They simply do not mistake force for courage. Their bravery is cumulative: placing a hand on a shifting lintel; entering a flooded channel alone; giving up a family’s share of stored water when another cistern fails; spending twenty years correcting, grain by grain, the lean of a wall one will not live to see made steady.

They possess no wish to master the Ward. Mastery, to them, would be another word for failing to listen.

So they live bent, layered, bright-faced, and close together, surrounded by creatures that have also learned not to waste motion or sound. Their city does not hide them so much as hold them in exact relation: hand to wall, roof to garden, moth to plaster, child to droplet, bone to tool, breath to cold air.

Seen from the upper steps at the hour when daylight withdraws, they seem less like a population than a single intricate life. Small lamps appear behind felt curtains. Hands close channels for the night. Birds settle into warm masonry. Frogs gather soundlessly below. Someone touches wood to wood to mark the final hour, and throughout the Still Ward, almost without a sound, the living answer by drawing nearer to one another.
