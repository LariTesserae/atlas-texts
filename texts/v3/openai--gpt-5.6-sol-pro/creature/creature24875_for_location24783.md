Nothing in the High Roar survives by standing firm.

The first inhabitants a traveler notices are the plants, because they seem fixed. This is misleading. The forests crouch, fold, stream, and tighten. Their roots enter the stone not like anchors but like fingers seeking holds. Rope grass gives before the wind and draws taut only when pulled; vines grow sacrificial outer fibers that fray away while the living core remains supple. Even the pale trees of the plateaus are less like timber than like long-lived weather vanes. Their trunks contain bands of soft, water-heavy wood between harder ribs, allowing the whole tree to bend nearly flat and rise again after a pressure change.

Many of these plants spend part of their lives elsewhere. Seeds ascend in warm shafts and may cross several valleys before descending. Cuttings torn from cliff gardens can remain alive for weeks, wrapped around their own moisture. After the great silences, entire mats of cushion plants are sometimes found adhering to new faces of stone, along with small ferns, sleeping insects, and soil held together by roots. In this way, pieces of one living province are transported intact to another.

The people say the forests migrate very slowly and by disaster.

Among the roots live gray, low-bodied browsers. They are called palmbacks in the trade language of the lower country because an adult is about the length of a forearm and its broad spine resembles the heel of a hand. Their fur is short and dense on top, nearly absent underneath. When the stone begins to hum strongly, they force themselves into root hollows and inflate loose folds along their sides, wedging their bodies in place.

Palmbacks eat leaves, fungus, bark, and the waxy skins of fallen fruit. They have flat teeth and dark, reflective eyes, but their most remarkable organs are the sensitive pads along their bellies. Through these they perceive vibration in wood and rock. A resting herd will lift its heads several moments before a mountain changes pitch. During the dangerous silences they become rigid and unresponsive. Human households sometimes keep one in a sleeping chamber, not as a pet exactly, but as an alarm. A palmback creeping under blankets and pressing itself against a person’s ribs is reason to wake everyone.

They breed in hidden communal nests. The young cling to the furless underside of any adult that will carry them. This indifference to parentage has made them symbols of hospitality, though they are ill-tempered animals and bite deeply when cornered.

Higher in the vegetation are sheath insects, leaf borers, pale moths, and the lacquer-winged creatures that pollinate hanging orchards. Most have gripping feet with opposing claws. Some trail silk safety lines continuously as they move. A fall that would kill a ground insect merely leaves one of these dangling until it climbs back.

During the wet minutes after a storm, the bark becomes crowded with life. When the surfaces dry, almost everything vanishes into seams.

The bell crawlers belong deeper in the stone.

A single crawler is a thin, many-jointed animal protected by overlapping plates of mineral shell. The shell is not grown entirely by the creature. Crawlers scrape varnish and crystalline deposits from vent walls, soften them in their mouths, and lay the material over themselves in translucent layers. The oldest are the color of smoky glass, with pale ridges where former shells remain embedded.

They feed on airborne spores, mineral films, and the minute organisms carried through the mountain by mist. Their colonies can fill chambers no human has entered. They grip the walls in moving tiers, each animal orienting its shell to pressure and flow. When the wind changes, the tiers rearrange. A vent occupied by thousands of crawlers may deepen by several notes, split one tone into two, or begin to pulse.

Whether they are merely responding to vibration or making use of it is debated only by outsiders. Those who live nearby have no doubt that the colonies communicate through the mountain.

Each large colony is said to possess a characteristic interval. People recognize the vents nearest their homes and can distinguish distress from ordinary adjustment. Before certain storms, the crawlers produce a rough metallic trembling known as shell-rain. Before a silence they often withdraw entirely from the mouths of the vents. Finding an empty throat is among the worst omens in the High Roar.

No one eats them. Their abandoned plates are gathered, however, and sewn into rain capes or hung beside sleeping niches. The shell pieces click softly under ordinary wind. If they begin to chatter without a draft, the shelter is evacuated.

The long-winged birds are seen more often than they are understood. They have narrow bodies, dark backs, and wing membranes reinforced by flexible quills. Their legs are so small that they can barely walk. They roost vertically beneath overhangs, hanging by hooked toes with their heads upright against the stone.

These birds cross the open spaces alone, but the solitude may be deceptive. They call below the threshold of human hearing, and a basin of still water will sometimes ripple as one passes. A second bird, invisible beyond a mesa, may answer in the same way. Their apparent separation may be a flock measured in tens of kilometers.

They feed on rising seeds, airborne insects, and the soft fruit exposed when hanging orchard shells burst against cliffs. During turbulent weather they disappear into the lower chasms. Afterward they emerge from vents and cave mouths far from where they entered, suggesting that some travel through the mountains themselves.

The people watch them for routes. A bird that vanishes beneath a ledge and rises steadily is marking an upcurrent. One that folds its wings abruptly has encountered shear. Their shed quills are prized for needles, tuned reeds, and the delicate springs used in pressure indicators.

There are other animals known chiefly by consequences.

Something grazes the upper canopy during storms. Its feeding marks are too broad for any visible forest creature: parallel sweeps through the treetops, bark rasped from the windward sides, whole clusters of fruit removed without broken branches below. In cloud, people sometimes glimpse a gray span moving against the direction of the rain. No head or limbs can be agreed upon. Some witnesses describe a vast, ribbed body inflated with warm air; others insist they saw several smaller shapes traveling so closely that their shadows merged.

The plateau people call these beings the Overpasturers. They say they descend only when cloud and forest touch, browsing the canopy as an ordinary animal browses grass. A few shelters keep coils of fruit cord on their roofs as offerings. By morning the cords may be gone, though in the High Roar this proves little.

More troubling are the warm hollows found in vegetation after a silence: immense shallow depressions smelling of wet stone and crushed mint. Palmbacks avoid them. Bell crawlers nearby turn their shells inward. Whether the Overpasturers land there, molt there, or are born there is unknown.

Human beings are among the least numerous inhabitants.

They have no single name for themselves. A person belongs first to a shelter, then to a route, then to the sequence of winds by which that route can be traveled. Names used with strangers are often chords: three pitches hummed, whistled, or signed by touching points on the arm. Since the voice may be useless in open country, personal names also have visible forms. A hooked finger, a closed fist against the breastbone, a stroke across the brow—these can identify someone across a roaring chamber.

Their spoken languages favor open vowels and voiced consonants that can be felt through wood or bone. In loud places, speech is transmitted by contact. Two people may converse with fingertips resting on each other’s wrists, tapping and pressing in patterns. Longer accounts are signed broadly. Children learn faces, hands, and vibration before they learn ordinary speech, and adults consider a word truly understood only when it can be expressed in all three ways.

This gives their conversation an unusual character. A statement spoken aloud may be blunt, while the same statement made by touch carries fine distinctions of certainty, affection, warning, or obligation. Songs are rarely melodic in the low-country sense. They are rhythms placed against resonant walls, performed by palms, shoulder blades, heels, and the butts of wooden poles. Some songs can be heard only by lying on the floor.

Their hearing is not supernaturally acute. Many elders are partially deaf. Scar tissue, ruptured membranes, and constant ringing are common. The culture does not treat deafness as separation, however; an elder who has lost airborne hearing may remain an exact reader of stone vibration. The most respected weather interpreters are often those who no longer hear the Roar as sound and experience it instead through their feet and ribs.

A settlement usually consists of several related households occupying one protected complex. There may be only twelve people, or forty in an unusually rich bowl. Rooms are shallow, low, and irregular, built where the rock has already made most of the enclosure. Every surface that can tremble is padded or broken into small parts. Roofs overlap rather than join rigidly. Shelves hang on braided loops. Cooking vessels rest in nests of fiber. A well-kept dwelling seems soft inside despite being made largely of stone.

Fire is precious but dangerous. Smoke can be sucked unexpectedly into sleeping chambers or blown downward from a roof vent. Most cooking is done in lidded pits with heated stones. Fruit is pounded into paste, leaves steamed in folded bark, and palmback meat—eaten rarely—is dried in thin strips. Cave water is preferred to basin water, which may contain mineral salts or the eggs of insects that wake only after swallowing.

Their gardens are not fields. They are collections of suitable cracks.

A household may tend hundreds of tiny plantings scattered over several kilometers: a cord-fruit vine under one ledge, medicinal cushions on a damp face, cup trees in a protected saddle, edible fern in the breath of a cavern. Harvest therefore resembles travel rather than farming. Gardeners spend days moving between small patches, repairing handholds and noting which surfaces have become too loud, too dry, or newly salted.

Plantings are marked but not owned. A mark records who tends a patch and when it can bear harvesting. Anyone in need may take from it, provided they leave a fiber knot showing what was taken. Concealing a harvest is a serious offense, not because it is theft of land, but because it makes the future unreadable.

The people measure wealth in readiness: dry cord, sealed food, wax, bark cloth, spare ladder rungs, knowledge of alternate shelter. Copper is valued less as ornament than as material for thin sounding plates. Hung against stone, these plates reveal changes too slight for the hand to detect. A family with many accurate plates may be poor in food and still considered fortunate.

Travelers are necessary. No settlement can produce everything it requires, and isolation becomes dangerous when routes fail. Route-keepers move between inhabited provinces with bundles strapped close to the spine. They carry seeds, shell plates, copper, medicinal sap, news, and remembered alterations to the land. Most travel alone or in pairs because a large group places too much strain on ladders and baskets.

They do not draw maps as continuous surfaces. Their route records are sequences of conditions:

*Cross the split shelf while the north throat exhales. Wait at the fern eye until the low tone divides. Descend when the three reeds answer together. Do not touch the red wall after rain.*

Such instructions may remain useful for generations, then become false in a single afternoon.

Hospitality follows the same conditional logic. A traveler arriving during a safe interval is fed, dried, and given a sleeping hollow. One arriving during a pressure fall may be tied bodily to the nearest secure beam before greetings are exchanged. No shame attaches to this. Courtesy begins after survival.

Their partnerships and kinships are distributed between shelters. Young people often leave the place of their birth, partly to prevent small communities from becoming too closely related and partly because every settlement needs people who know other winds. Courtship may involve exchanging route knowledge. To trust someone with the location of a hidden water seam, or to teach them the exact hand pattern for a dangerous crossing, is an intimate act.

Children are raised communally, like palmback young, though people insist this resemblance is coincidental. They are given soft harnesses as soon as they can walk. Games teach them to fall flat, seize rope grass, read cloud motion, and distinguish ordinary loudness from dangerous changes of pitch. One common game is played in a padded room: an adult alters the tension of cords passing through the walls, and blindfolded children identify which imaginary “mountain” has begun to speak.

Adulthood is not marked by age but by a first solitary wait. The young person spends a dusk alone at a known shelter between routes, listening through the transition into night. They are not expected to be fearless. They are expected to return with an accurate account.

Death presents practical difficulties. Soil is scarce, firewood valuable, and exposed bodies are quickly scattered. The dead are usually wrapped in bark cloth and placed in deep, outward-breathing fissures. Weighted cords lower them beyond sight. The fissure is then fitted with a reed that sounds only when air emerges from below.

These reeds are not labeled. Over time, their separate tones form a shelter’s ancestry. When one falls silent, people say the dead person has gone farther into the mountain. When an old funerary reed begins sounding again after years, no one removes it.

The inhabitants do not worship the mountains as persons, exactly. They know stone can break without intention and wind can kill without anger. Yet they regard the Roar as more than noise. It is the total expression of cavities, weather, water, roots, crawlers, and unseen distances acting upon one another. No single part speaks, but together they produce something to which attention must be paid.

Their moral vocabulary reflects this. A good person is one who “leaves room in the chord.” A liar is said to “mark a path after it has moved.” Grief is “a wind with no throat.” Someone stubborn past reason is “building a door to the west.”

Silence has its own signs. At the first broad failure of sound, people do not run. Running assumes one knows where safety lies. They sit or lie down, secure children and tools, loosen rigid fastenings, and watch the smallest things: basin water, hanging fibers, crawler shells, the hairs on a palmback’s spine. In many shelters, everyone grips the same braided line so that warnings can pass by pressure without movement or speech.

During a long silence, the oldest person present recites a list of what is not known.

They do this aloud if possible, by hand if not:

The wind has not ended.  
The mountain has not died.  
The old route is not promised.  
The next sound is not yet danger.  
The next sound is not yet safety.

Then they wait for the country to choose its new arrangement.

Afterward, they emerge cautiously. Their first task is not rebuilding but listening. Reeds are moved. Water is tasted. Cracks are touched with the cheek. Children are sent nowhere. A changed note in a familiar arch may mean only that leaves have entered a chamber, or it may mean that a stone throat has narrowed and will soon burst.

For several days, everyone becomes a route-keeper. News travels outward by bird quill, by reed chord, by gestures from visible escarpments, and finally by people carrying the exact account from shelter to shelter.

Life then resumes, though never in precisely the same place.

A garden is replanted in another crack. A sleeping niche is abandoned because its wall now trembles. A ladder becomes roof bracing. A former roof is cut into ladder rungs. A newly watered shelf receives fern spores and, a generation later, may support trees.

This is how the High Roar is inhabited: not by possession, nor even by endurance in the usual sense, but by continuous revision.

The people, the palmbacks, the vent colonies, the airborne seeds, and perhaps the vast cloud-grazers all practice versions of the same art. They hold fast without becoming rigid. They leave before departure seems necessary. They return without expecting recognition. They make homes from intervals and carry continuity in their bodies.

At sunset, when the air softens and the separate heights glow across the gaps, human figures sometimes come out onto the sheltered ledges. They stand in small groups, looking not down but outward. In that brief lull their clothes hang still, the shell plates at their shoulders cease clicking, and children can hear unamplified voices.

Then a low pressure touches the soles of their feet.

One adult raises a hand. Every face turns toward the western apertures.

Far off, beyond several empty valleys, a mesa begins its night note. Another answers from under the clouds. The bell crawlers shift in their hidden thousands. Birds tip into the darkening air. Families retreat beneath stone, drawing in their ladders but leaving the tuned reeds exposed.

The people do not say that the mountains have awakened.

Only that they have begun again.
