The first inhabitant is mistaken for the ground.

It is not one species, nor even a stable community, but a long negotiation among thousands of small lives. Mosses hold water that would otherwise escape. Rushes stitch the moss to deeper layers. Fungi enter the dead stems and preserve them as pale, resilient felt. Flat-leaved plants spread over weak places; red-rimmed water plants armor the beginnings of streams. Their roots meet underground, divide, graft, strangle, decay, and are threaded through again.

From above, this appears to be a single green skin. Within it are chambers, galleries, suspended reservoirs, drowned forests of stems, and old surfaces buried intact beneath newer ones. Some cavities are no larger than a fingernail. Others extend under several meters of vegetation and contain their own cold weather: dripping ceilings, slow drafts, and mists stirred by water moving in the dark.

The plateau’s plants do not compete chiefly for sunlight. There is more light than their low forms can use. They compete for continuity. A colony survives by remaining attached—by spreading its weight, sharing water without being drowned by it, and keeping itself bound to stone while the runoff tugs ceaselessly toward the cliffs. Upright growth is punished by wind and spray. Deep roots find little purchase in the new rock. So life clasps life. It lies down, overlaps, and becomes land.

At the surface, each colony can be recognized by touch. One is wiry and warm-colored, with stems that squeak against a glove. Another is smooth, almost waxed, and gives under pressure like soaked leather. A third trembles when stepped near because its leaves are stretched over a common basin. Maintenance workers have names for these growths, though the names differ from station to station: ladder-moss, glass sedge, widow’s cloth, green iron, sleeper’s skin. Few of the names correspond neatly to species. They describe what the plants will bear, how they fail, and what sort of water lies underneath.

The flowers are less individual than they seem. The white blooms that open into the general brilliance are linked below by cords of living tissue. A patch may blossom all at once over half a kilometer, beginning without visible signal. Each flower closes around a few pale insects before the light fades. The insects spend most of their lives inside the plant: hatching in sealed chambers, feeding on clear secretions, and crawling through hollow stems from one bloom to another. They have no need of strong eyes. Their bodies are nearly transparent except for a dark thread of gut and minute silver patches that may sense changes in light.

They are abundant, but almost never seen. When a flower is torn open, they draw their legs close and become indistinguishable from droplets.

Other small things live deeper. Threadworms graze the submerged fungal lace. Flat, pearl-colored mites collect in the warmth around buried cable conduits. Tiny crustaceans inhabit old footprints after the water in them has aged enough to grow a skin of algae. There are larvae that brace themselves inside running stems and feed from the current as if the plant were both river and house. Some chambers contain blind, soft-bodied grazers whose ancestors may have arrived in spray, on birds’ feet, or through the groundwater fractures below.

No one has made a complete census. To take the plateau apart in order to count its inhabitants would be to destroy the thing being studied. Samples from places a few steps apart may contain wholly different communities. Whole populations are born when a basin overtops and vanish when a root seam closes.

The birds are the only inhabitants that seem to belong to the open distance.

They are charcoal-dark, narrow-bodied, and disproportionately winged. On clear days they appear as cuts in the white sky. They ride the ascending air beside the escarpments, passing through spray dense enough to hide them, then emerge higher without having visibly beaten their wings. Their feathers shed water so completely that droplets fly from them in bright rings when they turn.

They rarely settle on the plateau. Their feet are small and curved for clinging to exposed root curtains under the terrace edges, where they hang beyond the falls and nest in cavities protected by moving water. The roar conceals their calls, if they call at all. When two meet in flight, they alter the angles of their wings in quick, precise gestures. Workers say the birds speak by showing each other the pale undersides of their flight feathers.

Their nests contain woven root fiber, scraps of translucent plant skin, insect cases, and occasionally a bright filament teased from an instrument cable. A dropped bolt or ceramic washer may also be found among them. Whether these are decoration, ballast, or accident is unknown.

The fish are more doubtful.

Something slender does move in the deepest inspection wells. Camera recordings show colorless forms bending against the current, then disappearing into fractures too narrow to follow. They have long fins or perhaps external gills. Their eyes, if present, do not reflect the lamps. Some engineers insist they are strips of root tissue pulled loose by pressure changes. Others have seen them turn.

The oldest turbine crews say the fish live in the mountain’s uncommitted water, before it has become any particular spring or stream. According to them, a fish may pass through stone over the course of years, entering one pool and emerging in another kilometers away. Screens are fitted over the intakes to protect equipment, officially from fibrous debris. No one admits that the mesh size was chosen with the rumored fish in mind.

Human beings do not exactly live on the plateau. They are posted to it, cross it, descend into it, and are eventually rotated away. Even those who remain for years speak of elsewhere as home.

Most arrive during the long maintenance season in low sealed crawlers whose tracks scarcely dent the mat. They emerge dressed in close-fitting waterproof layers, with hoods tight around their faces and tools strapped down to prevent vibration from walking them off platforms. Anything loose will migrate here. A wrench left on a turbine casing can travel several centimeters in a shift. A cup forgotten near an overflow may gradually fill, turn, and vanish.

The workers look deliberate because every movement must be. They step on dark growth, test pale seams, and wait for each foot to settle before transferring their weight. Sounding poles are held not like walking staffs but like instruments. The tip is pressed through the leaves; the hand reads the answer. A firm root mass gives a crowded, granular trembling. A flooded cavity answers with a slow pulse. An empty chamber sends back a clean shiver that can be felt in the wrist and teeth.

Because speech is unreliable, the crews use their bodies.

A flat palm means firm ground. Two fingers bent downward mean a concealed flow. A hand closed around the opposite wrist warns of suction beneath the mat. Pointing is done with the whole forearm so that a gesture cannot be mistaken in mist. At close range they communicate by touching helmets together and speaking into the shared shell, or by pressing a small throat transmitter against the bones behind the jaw.

Long-serving workers retain these habits after leaving. They tap walls before leaning on them. They place cups far from table edges. In quiet rooms they speak too softly, having learned that important words are exchanged mouth to ear.

A few station tenders stay through the year. Their quarters are built into the turbine houses, behind double doors and vibration joints, though nothing fully excludes the plateau’s thunder. They sleep in narrow suspended bunks. Bottles ring against one another unless wrapped in cloth. Written notices are clipped at all four corners. The roar enters dreams and remains there; during rare shutdowns, the sudden reduction of sound wakes everyone at once.

The tenders become attentive to changes too slight for visitors to notice. They know when a fall has shifted by the altered pressure in a corridor, when a bearing has roughened by the flavor of vibration in a handrail, when a basin has begun to overtop because mist forms on the wrong window. They keep records measured in decades. A red line on a wall may mark the progress of a crack since before the current keeper was born. Instruments are modern, but judgment is inherited.

They are not masters of the works. Their task is to maintain a bargain between pressure, stone, steel, and growth.

Every intervention produces consequences elsewhere. Open a gate too quickly and a root shelf may drain, shrink, and tear away from its footing. Divert a seep and a service causeway can become an unintended dam. Cut vegetation from a hinge, and the exposed surface may begin to erode faster than the mechanism can tolerate. The best workers are those willing to make the smallest correction and then wait.

They trim plants with ceramic-edged tools, never pulling by hand, because a stem no thicker than thread may be the visible end of a binding structure several meters across. They paint moving parts with bitter mineral paste to discourage moss. They clear warning lamps each morning, though by evening the glass may again be webbed in translucent filaments. When a structure is abandoned, they remove oils and reactive metals, close the deep passages, and leave the shell. Demolition would wound too much ground. The plateau will dismantle it more carefully.

The machines also acquire a quality difficult not to call life.

They breathe warm vapor through vents. They gather colonies specific to their temperatures. They pulse through the vegetation in regular frequencies, and nearby root systems grow in faint concentric patterns. Intakes clog, clear, and clog again. Gates resist after long stillness. Automatic governors make minute adjustments in response to water arriving from basins no person can see.

Yet the machinery does not spread. It cannot repair itself without visitors. Its continuity is archival and human: inspection schedules, stored parts, handwritten annotations, apprentices taught to recognize a dangerous tremor through a glove. Left alone, it would become another hard layer inside the mat.

The plateau’s true inhabitants are those that can turn disturbance into structure.

A bird’s footprint becomes a cup. A crawler track becomes a line of drainage. A shed becomes a hill. Heat from a conduit creates winter habitat for mites; rust feeds a dark bacterial stain; vibration alters the direction of root growth. Nothing remains foreign for long. The living surface does not erase intrusion so much as translate it into water, shelter, weight, and edge.

Even the people are translated. Their routes become firmer. Their discarded names attach to basins and colonies. Their warning stakes sink until only colored tips remain among the leaves. After they leave, the plateau keeps the shapes of their steps and fills them with flowers.

So the country is not empty. It is crowded in the horizontal, inhabited in layers rather than silhouettes. Its largest creature is the ground without being one creature; its deepest fauna may be rumor; its most visible animals refuse to land. Human beings pass through as careful, temporary organs of an older machine, while the machine itself is slowly admitted into the vegetation.

Everything that lives there must learn the same discipline: cling broadly, move slowly, and yield without coming loose.
