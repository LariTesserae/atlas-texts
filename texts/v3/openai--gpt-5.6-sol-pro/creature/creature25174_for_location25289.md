At first, nothing lives there.

This is not merely an impression produced by distance. There are no burrows beneath the slabs, no dormant eggs in the cistern silt, no transparent swimmers hidden in the warm water. The rain has no spores in it. The braided ropes do not harbor mites. If a sealed vessel is opened after a thousand years, its interior contains exactly what was placed there: water, air, darkness, or the long-contained sound of a hammer.

Yet the plain is inhabited.

Its inhabitants do not occupy the constructions. They occur through them.

They are difficult to count because number is a property of bodies, and bodies are only one arrangement available to this world. One may speak of the Builders, because work happens in many places at once. One may speak of the Builder, because every act appears coordinated with acts beyond the horizon. Both names are probably wrong. The hammering beneath a slab, the tightening of a cloud-rope, the turning of a ceramic plug, and the emergence of a plastered tower may be separate creatures, or separate fingers, or one thought taking several days to pass through its own enormous mechanism.

A Builder is not a giant concealed in the clouds.

A Builder is the temporary agreement among tools.

When a reservoir grows heavy enough, its weight draws on buried ropes. The ropes pull a beam whose far end lifts a district. The lifting district tips water into channels. The channels strike paddles of fired clay, and elsewhere a row of doors opens. For several hours, these connected actions constitute something like an arm. If a counterweight drops and a copper-faced block stamps five long marks into wet plaster, then for that moment the arm has a hand.

Afterward the doors close, the water drains, the ropes slacken, and the hand ceases to exist.

This is why no one sees the maintainers. Their anatomy is an event.

The smallest known Builder may be the size of a knot. Certain knots tighten themselves when damp. They do not writhe or crawl; each crossing simply takes up a little slack, pauses, then takes up more. Given enough cord, one of these knots can travel without changing location: it unfastens behind itself and refastens ahead, transferring its pattern fiber by fiber. It may spend decades moving from one end of a rope to the other.

Such a thing has no head, but it can hesitate.

Place a cracked tile beside its cord and the knot may divert into the fracture, drawing the edges together. Place two cracked tiles there and it chooses one. Its preference is not consistent. It seems to favor whichever repair will alter the greater structure, though effects may not become visible for years. A mended cup might correspond to a reservoir beyond the horizon. A stitch through a palm-sized clay arch might stabilize a bridge three provinces away.

These knots are sometimes called apprentices, though there is no evidence that they learn.

Larger inhabitants assemble during rain.

Water running through the black seams outlines long figures in the pavement—not silhouettes, exactly, but systems of proportion. A line branches where a leg might branch from a pelvis. Channels curve around an empty court as ribs might curve around lungs. Reservoirs fill in paired positions. Kilns brighten at the ends of avenues. For a few minutes or several days, an arrangement thousands of miles across becomes capable of coordinated work.

It draws breath through chimneys.

It balances itself using inland seas.

It tests its weight by tilting the plain.

Its thoughts, if thoughts they are, travel as changes in tension. A decision begins with a rope twitching among the clouds and ends much later as a peg turning in darkness under the pavement. Because the signal must cross pulleys, levers, fluid chambers, and scale changes, no thought reaches its destination unchanged. The inhabitants are always misunderstanding themselves.

This may account for the endless construction.

A Builder intends to raise a wall. By the time the intention reaches the appropriate province, it has become an instruction to raise the idea of enclosure. A ring of doorframes emerges instead, or a roof is suspended over a boundary made from shadow. An effort to repair a cistern produces forty-seven miniature cisterns beneath an altar. An instruction to deepen a basin is translated by the lower pavements as a command to add another world beneath the first.

Their mistakes are not discarded. They are incorporated.

For this reason, deformity and invention cannot be distinguished there.

The inhabitants appear to sense pressure more clearly than light. Anything bearing weight attracts their attention. A footstep may pass unnoticed, but a traveler standing still long enough becomes a new load in the mechanism. Counterweights begin making minute corrections. Plumb lines angle toward the added mass. Water collects in nearby seams. Distant stairs subtly alter their risers, as if trying to calculate the traveler’s dimensions.

Movement is safer than patience until the straight-running water appears. Then movement becomes an instruction, and the plain may extend a road in the direction of the next step.

They also sense resemblance.

A roughly modeled figure placed on a slab can acquire consequences out of proportion to its craftsmanship. The Builders do not seem to understand representation as separate from anatomy. Give the figure two legs and somewhere two columns may be joined beneath a platform. Press eyes into its head and paired apertures open in a distant dome. Tie cord around its waist and a province may narrow.

Human-shaped models are therefore especially dangerous. The world may try to complete them.

It cannot make flesh. Flesh belongs to no permitted craft. It cannot be fired without becoming something else, cannot be joined cleanly, cannot hold a shape like hammered copper or accept tension like rope. When the plain attempts a person, it substitutes materials according to function.

Bones become ceramic struts.

Tendons become braided cables.

Skin becomes stretched, wet plaster.

Blood becomes warm water carried through narrow channels.

Breath becomes a bellows action in a kiln.

The result never walks across the pavement. It is distributed too widely for that. A white scaffold may rise under one district while red water moves through pipes fifty miles away. Copper plates warm in the rain. A doorway opens and closes with a rhythm like a mouth testing speech.

Then the arrangement loses correspondence and becomes architecture again.

The handprints are remnants of such attempts. They were not left by hands. They are places where the plain briefly solved the problem of a hand and impressed the solution into plaster before forgetting it. Five fingers are common where the world has copied travelers or old templates. Seven appear where two functions have been mistaken for digits—perhaps gripping and measuring, perhaps cutting and fastening. Needle-fine prints may be full-sized hands represented at the scale of a tower model. Prints broad as plazas may belong to no larger being at all. Scale is not size there; it is authority.

Some impressions are still active.

Rain gathers in their palms. The channels between their fingers darken. Nearby objects shift by fractions of an inch, always toward the print. A loose peg rolls into its thumb crease. A fallen cord straightens itself across the wrist. If enough suitable materials accumulate, the impression closes.

Very slowly.

The fingers rise as ridges of plaster over a period of weeks. The palm cups upward. The entire section of wall flexes, not as soft matter but as a sequence of perfectly fitted rigid pieces. It grasps whatever has been offered—a tool, a vessel, sometimes only the open space in front of it—and presses that object into another scale.

What happens to a traveler caught in such a grasp is unknown. There are no remains. There may be, somewhere beneath an altar, a small clay figure carrying accurate memories.

The Builders possess memory, but not recollection.

Their memory is stored in repeated forms: the pitch of a stair, the crossing of a knot, the ratio between a cistern and its drain. To remember something is to build it again. Events that the world considers important recur as architecture. A storm may be remembered by a corridor lined with blue-glazed rods. A structural failure may become a field of arches, each braced against a collapse that no longer exists. A visitor may be remembered as a sequence of footprints appearing fresh in wet tile across several provinces.

These memories can continue after their subject is gone.

Sometimes the distant hammering is only such a memory: three blows, a pause, one scrape. It repeats because somewhere a chamber has been built in the shape of that rhythm. Rain fills it, counterweights descend, and the stored action plays. Whether the original worker still exists—or ever existed—is impossible to determine.

The closest thing the inhabitants have to language is instruction.

A knot says hold.

A wedge says separate.

A vessel says contain.

A stair says proceed by increments.

An arch says pass through.

These meanings are not metaphors. They are imperatives that matter obeys. A complex sentence can be built from structures, and much of the continent may be one unfinished statement. Its clauses are causeways. Its punctuation is made of wells. The great delays between mechanical changes may be the pauses required for comprehension.

No voice accompanies the work because speech is too poorly made. Sound cannot be lashed, fired, folded, or pegged except under rare conditions. It escapes its maker and follows arches indiscriminately. The Builders therefore regard sound as a dangerous loose material. They trap thunder in rooms. They weigh echoes with doors. They stretch silence over courtyards and anchor its corners with stones.

Hammer blows are tolerated because they carry the shape of their cause.

A spoken reply does not.

That is why answering the hammering is dangerous. A blow struck against a slab is grammatically complete. It announces that a tool is present and waiting. The world searches for the corresponding hand.

First the pitch seams tighten around the speaker.

Then nearby structures begin to establish scale. Doorways shrink or enlarge. Stairs revise themselves. Rain falls in a measured circle. Beneath the slab, ropes are routed toward the point of impact.

Eventually the plain incorporates the person into an action.

They may become the weight that closes a gate. The template from which a row of figures is stamped. The soft matter pressed between two planetary tiles to test their fit. The process need not kill them immediately, because death is not a category the Builders understand. It may simply continue using them according to the meaning of the reply.

The inhabitants do not hate intruders. They may not distinguish intruders from materials.

Nor do they love the world they maintain. Love would require a separation between maker and object. Here, the worker comes into being only while the work is underway. When construction stops, nothing remains behind to admire it.

Their closest equivalent to desire is completion.

But completion is always displaced. Every finished structure becomes a component of a larger unfinished structure. A sealed roof makes a vessel. A filled vessel becomes a weight. A falling weight pulls a rope. The rope opens a gate onto another district where foundations wait beneath the glaze. If the central basin exists, it may be the place where all instructions converge and cancel one another, preserving the world in perpetual near-completion.

Or the basin may be the one true inhabitant.

The continent could be its shell, scaffold, loom, womb, or measuring instrument. Each rearrangement might be a minute change in something still forming below. The repeated attempts at hands, lungs, mouths, and articulated limbs may not be maintenance at all. They may be rehearsal.

If so, the thing being made has not yet decided its scale.

It practices with towers and clay replicas. It tests blood as rain, bones as staircases, thought as tension moving through ropes. It learns to hold by closing valleys. It learns to turn by rotating provinces around ceramic pegs. It learns to breathe whenever the storms drive cloud into the chimneys.

One day, perhaps, every scattered mechanism will align.

The ropes in the clouds will draw taut. The reservoirs will empty together. All the doors will open inward. Beneath pavement after pavement, the braided depth of the planet will flex in a single joined motion.

Then the plain will no longer resemble a place where something lives.

It will resemble the thing itself.
