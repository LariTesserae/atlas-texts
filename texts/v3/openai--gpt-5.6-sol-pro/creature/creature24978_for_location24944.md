What lives there cannot be counted without first deciding where one life ends.

There are animals, certainly. They move, feed, mate, molt, hunt, and flee with enough urgency to appear separate. The smallest inhabit the thickness of a leaf. Clear-bodied grazers travel between its upper and lower skins, following capillaries of light and leaving lacework scars that close behind them. Warm mites sleep in knots around the luminous seeds. Pleated burrowers pass through braided roots by becoming briefly as soft as pollen, then gather themselves on the other side. Long-snouted fruit-dwellers wake from nests of fermenting pulp with their whiskers lacquered in sugar and their blood protected against the cold.

Yet few remain animals throughout their lives.

The wingless moths are born as scales on the undersides of leaves. For several winters they are indistinguishable from the plant, opening and closing with its pores. When they detach, they have no means of locomotion except negotiation. A moth strokes a leaf with its feet, producing a sequence of pressures; the leaf cups, tips, and hands it to the next. Whole migrations pass through the grove as a rumor of folding foliage. Some moths never arrive. The leaves retain them, and in spring their patterned backs appear among the plant’s new growth.

The amphibious flatlings live in water films scarcely thicker than their bodies. They have broad, transparent heads and rows of cilia that shimmer blue in the seed-light. When their pools contract, they draw their limbs inward and become drops. In that condition they possess no fixed anatomy. They are lifted through root hairs, divided at branching veins, and reassembled wherever enough of them collects. One animal may arrive as two smaller siblings. Two may descend as one creature carrying incompatible memories. Their courtship consists of exchanging the places where they have been.

The glass-shelled grazers are louder. They scrape edible frost from moss beds with black combs of teeth and strike their wing cases against hollow roots to announce territory. Their shells contain neither glass nor mineral, but layers of frozen breath laid down since hatching. Every sound they hear adds a faint ridge. Old individuals are bulky with recorded noise: root-grinding, ice-booms, spore reports, the footfalls of vanished predators. When they die, the shells continue to sound for months, releasing their lives in reverse as the layers thaw.

Among the moss move the long-snouted fruit sleepers. They are soft-footed and dark-eyed, each small enough to curl inside a single hollow berry. They feed on antifreeze nectar and the tiny black mites that gather around light organs. In cold seasons their tails branch into rootlets and drink from the fruit wall. A sleeper may remain joined too long and lose the wish to leave. The fruit then grows a pulse; the animal grows a rind. Generations later, its descendants may open the same fruit and find an ancestor still dreaming in the flesh around them.

The predators are more difficult to notice because they spend much of their existence as vegetation.

One resembles a white flower with seven fleshy petals. While waiting, it lowers its temperature, sweetens its blood, and accepts pollen. Its roots are false, but they absorb water; its leaves are jaws, but they photosynthesize. It may remain planted for decades before some grazer touches the central filament. Then the blossom closes, metabolism reverses, and what seemed rooted tears itself from the floor on a circle of pale legs.

Another predator hunts only at the prophetic basin. It is narrow and nearly colorless, with forelimbs adapted for gripping reflections. It watches the water for the image of an animal not yet present, strikes, and pulls. Sometimes the prey arrives early, shocked and wet, dragged from several minutes ahead of itself. Sometimes only a future wound is captured: a mouthful of blood, a broken claw, a tuft of fur whose owner will enter later and discover what has already been taken. The hunters decorate their nests with these fragments of unfinished encounters.

There are parasites that live in possibilities rather than bodies. They infest forked stems at the moment when both forms still exist, feeding on the difference between one branch and the other. An infected plant shivers without wind. Its two versions gradually become less alike until a landing insect forces one into solidity. The discarded branch does not always disappear. Sometimes it falls inward, into the parasite, which swells and produces a clutch of eggs shaped like tiny decisions.

There are also the dead.

They do not walk, but the grove does not allow them to become wholly absent. Roots grow through brain and ganglion with exquisite slowness, copying patterns before decay can erase them. What is preserved is not a person or an animal. It is a collection of tendencies: the turn taken at a scent, the tightening before impact, the learned stillness beneath a shadow. These pass into the vegetation as electrical weather.

Thus a mat of moss may startle because a burrower died there. A vine may recoil from a predator extinct for centuries. Fruit may ripen only when approached by descendants of the creature that once fed upon it. During certain cold spells, thousands of retained reflexes pass simultaneously through the roots. The whole chamber flinches, though nothing has touched it.

Some inhabitants exist only during those transmissions. Brief beings, assembled from borrowed nervous fragments, they last for the length of a signal crossing the grove. One may begin as hunger in a root wall, become sight in a cluster of seed-lights, acquire movement from the memory of an amphibian, and end as fear curling a distant leaf. It never occupies a single body. Nevertheless, during its passage it perceives the chamber and perhaps believes itself continuous.

The vegetation itself may be one such passage, only slower.

There is no queen root, no parent tree, no buried brain. Each bulb, moss mat, membrane, vine, and luminous organ makes local choices. Yet the choices converge. Water is sent where an unborn shoot will someday require it. A root withdraws years before a burrower needs the gap. Leaves darken in anticipation of eyes that have not opened. The grove remembers events no individual part survived and prepares for events no individual part could predict.

It does not speak. Its nearest equivalent is the long tone produced when the buried bulbs contract. Animals freeze when it begins. Pools tighten. Light travels inward through every transparent stem. The tone carries no message separable from its effect; hearing it is how the chamber adjusts the hearer. Afterward, moths change direction, sleepers wake in unfamiliar fruits, and predators abandon flowers they have impersonated for half their lives.

The grove may therefore be an organism assembling itself backward: not growing parts to enact a plan, but accumulating acts until they imply a being.

And beneath all these lives hangs the small dark absence.

At first it seems empty. Nothing swims in its cold water because the water never enters it. No root crosses its boundary. Spores veer aside. Light-bearing stems go dim when pointed toward it. Even the chamber’s stored reflexes break around that volume and rejoin beyond it without carrying any knowledge of the crossing.

But once each year, it contracts.

The beat is not forceful. It displaces no water and bends no root. Instead, every creature in the chamber briefly experiences a missing instant. Grazers find their jaws closed on frost they do not remember biting. Moths have moved to the next leaf. Hunters at the basin see circles spreading across an untouched reflection. In that lost interval, the dark shape has grown.

The animals respond to it in ways they have not learned. Pregnant burrowers turn their heads toward the lowest point. Glass-shelled beetles incorporate the silence into the innermost layer of their armor. Fruit sleepers dream of a chamber vastly larger than the one around them, with roots arching through black distances and cold green lights pulsing like constellations. The dead reflexes become calm.

The roots tend the absence without touching it. They regulate its cold, divert sound from its boundary, and bring close those capsules whose interiors hold old rain, summer warmth, and folded distance. What they are nourishing is not yet flesh, nor seed, nor organ. It has no substance to inherit. It is being made from exclusions: a place where the grove’s rules cease, held stable by the exact coordination of everything living around it.

Perhaps it will become a heart. Perhaps a mouth, an eye, a seed, or an entrance. Perhaps the chamber itself is only its egg.

For now, the deepest inhabitant is a vacancy learning to live. Each annual beat gives it another property. First boundary. Then rhythm. Then cold. Then the power to alter what surrounds it without contact. In time it may acquire hunger, memory, or direction.

When that happens, the distinction between the creature and the grove may finally become clear.

Or it may vanish altogether.
