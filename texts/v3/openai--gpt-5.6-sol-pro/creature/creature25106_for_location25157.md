What lives there is not one species but a congregation held at the threshold of itself.

At first the inhabitants seem merely adapted to darkness. They are small-eyed where sight is unnecessary, broad-footed where stone is slick, soft-furred or slick-skinned according to the kind of wetness they occupy. Their colors belong to the hollow rather than to daylight: basalt gray, liverwort green, the brown of steeped leaves, the white of roots uncovered beneath water. Even brighter markings—a frog’s yellow throat, the red seam beneath a moth’s wing, the blue skin around a primate’s eyes—have the muted intensity of pigments seen through smoke.

But concealment is not their chief adaptation. Stillness is.

The birds beneath the leaves are not asleep. Their breast feathers lift and settle around breaths so slight that condensation gathers without being disturbed. They do not sing, though their throats retain the structures for song. Within them are complete repertoires: warning notes, courtship phrases, territorial calls, the thin begging cries of nestlings. None has been forgotten. The songs remain gathered in the syrinx, always at the point of becoming sound.

They have nests, if nests can be made without having been built. Moss has thickened beneath certain bodies; twigs have come to rest in cups between branches; shed fibers cling where eggs might be held. The birds occupy these arrangements as though generations of nesting have shaped them, though no beak is ever seen placing a stem. Their eggs are present at every stage—newly laid, faintly veined, shadowed by folded young—but nothing hatches. Nor do the eggs spoil. Life remains curled inside life, beak resting against shell, the first fracture permanently intended.

The frogs inhabit the wall’s wet sockets. Each pocket contains its own assembly: adults folded over strings of eggs, tadpoles suspended in thumb-deep water, half-changed bodies with hind legs opened like delicate hinges. Their skins pulse slowly. A throat may remain rounded with a call that never leaves it. Some have tongues already extended the breadth of a raindrop toward midges that hang close enough to touch, yet neither tongue nor insect completes the distance.

The insects are the most numerous and the least separable from the place. They occupy it as punctuation occupies writing. Beetles seal cracks in rotting wood. Gnats form dim constellations above the pool, not flying so much as maintaining the shape of a swarm. Caterpillars lie along leaf veins in attitudes of travel. Ants stand in processions whose beginning and end disappear under roots. Each bears something—a shred of fungus, a pale egg, a splinter of leaf—but the colony receives no deliveries. Their burdens are retained with perfect patience.

Moths cover the trunks in overlapping generations. Some are newly emerged, their wings still pleated; some have spread them to their full powdery breadth; some seem old enough that the scales should have worn away. All endure together. Where one might ordinarily displace another, their bodies have found arrangements without conflict: wing beneath wing, antenna laid along a neighbor’s thorax, feet occupying the minute spaces between feet. Looked at from a distance, they resemble an additional bark grown by the trees—sensitive bark, furred and breathing.

The pool contains even more life than the branches. Its darkness is not emptiness but compression. Worms, larvae, threadlike fish, water mites, translucent crustaceans, and colonies of creatures too small to appear as individuals fill the brown depth. Ordinarily such abundance would turn the water foul. Here nothing blooms disastrously and nothing exhausts what it needs. Every mouth is supplied at the margin before hunger; every waste is received before it becomes poison. The pool is an organ that has finished balancing its own blood.

At its edge lie the salamanders, pale enough for their hidden vessels to show. Their limbs appear newly formed, tender and almost ornamental, yet some bear old scars and missing toes. This is one of the hollow’s quiet contradictions: its inhabitants have histories written on their bodies, though nothing in the present can continue those histories. Claws are blunted from climbing that no one sees. Teeth have been broken. Fur is parted around healed bites. One narrow cat has a torn ear and a ridge along its flank where ribs once knitted. The place did not create innocent animals. It received creatures already acquainted with injury and arrested them only after they had become entirely themselves.

The cats crouch among the roots with their attention distributed everywhere. Their muscles are neither relaxed nor strained. Whiskers lean toward nearby birds; pupils stand wide in the blue dark. A paw lifted over moss retains its weight in the shoulder, and the moss beneath seems prepared for the pressure. Yet the birds do not fear them. Fear survives only as anatomy: a rapid heart, a wide field of vision, wings designed for abrupt escape. The event that would awaken those structures never arrives.

So predator and prey have grown intimate. Snakes rest with frogs against their coils. Owls share cavities with moths. A beetle walks no farther beneath the poised claw of a shrew. They are not reconciled in any moral sense. The cat remains wholly cat, the bird wholly available to being killed. What has vanished is not violence but completion. The hollow contains every preparation for harm and no wound presently being made.

Among all these creatures, the long-limbed primates seem at first the nearest thing to custodians. They are not.

They have narrow dark faces, heavy brows beaded with moisture, and hands whose palms are almost hairless. Their gray coats have grown into small habitats of their own. Moss threads the fur along their spines. Mites gather at the warm seams of the body. Tiny white fungi rise from hairs that have fallen but remain entangled. Young cling beneath some of the adults, their fingers closed so firmly in the coat that hand and fur appear rooted together.

Their tools distinguish them, but only barely. A chosen stone differs from an unchosen one solely by being held. A bone point bears no carving. A twig has been stripped, perhaps by weather, perhaps by teeth; the evidence is too slight to say. The primates recognize usefulness without imposing it. They understand that a nut may be opened, that grubs may be drawn from wood, that fibers may be parted and joined. Their bodies contain the whole grammar of making: grip, aim, leverage, sequence, anticipation.

What they do not possess is the final verb.

The stone remains raised. The twig remains inserted only as far as the rotten wood already admits it. Fingers pinch two fibers at the beginning of a twist. These postures are not uncertainty. Their gaze is exact; their hands know what should follow. Indeed, their knowledge may be deeper for never being discharged into action. Every possible blow is preserved within the lifted arm: too hard, too soft, glancing, accurate, shattering, fruitful. To bring the stone down would choose one result and destroy the others. The hollow allows intention to become complete but not particular.

For this reason the primates are not thinkers in the way moving creatures are. Their thoughts do not replace one another. A suspicion does not yield to recognition; curiosity does not become discovery. Each mind holds its perceptions side by side, accumulating without dismissal. The nut is closed and openable. The log is inhabited and searchable. The stranger below is dangerous, harmless, familiar, unprecedented. No conclusion drives out another possibility.

Their eyes reveal this. They do not follow an entrant, because following would mean surrendering the place just looked at. Instead their attention seems already waiting wherever the entrant might stand. One black gaze meets the face; another rests upon the hand; another has always been fixed on the patch of moss where a foot will be set. Seen together, they give the impression not of many animals watching, but of a single awareness distributed through the canopy.

That impression deepens with time.

The snails’ horns point toward disturbances before they occur. Frogs face openings in the foliage that have not yet been used. Fish hang beneath the exact places where a shadow may cross the pool. The cats are poised along routes no prey currently travels. Even the plants present their unfinished gestures toward arrivals: a hooked tendril waits at wrist height; a flower keeps its petals closed around a scent prepared for a nose; fern tips bend above spaces shaped like kneeling bodies.

It becomes difficult to say that the hollow contains inhabitants. More nearly, habitation is what the hollow itself is doing.

The trees are its lifted nerves. The pool is its dark, unblinking interior. Fungi carry slow messages through the soaked wood; roots press those messages into fissures; insects continue them as lines of minute bodies; birds and primates hold the larger, warmer portions of its attention. No creature commands the whole. No creature is merely a cell. They remain individuals, each with its scars, appetites, tools, eggs, venoms, and private posture. Yet the equilibrium between them is so exact that individuality no longer requires separation.

Whether they were always so cannot be known. The stone suggests a long age of weather before the stillness. The scars suggest pursuit, mating, feeding, birth, and escape. Perhaps the hollow gradually slowed its inhabitants, lengthening the pause between impulse and act until the pause became sufficient for every need. Perhaps one creature stopped first—some frog under a leaf, some ape reconsidering a blow—and the hesitation propagated outward through gaze, touch, root, moisture, and breath. Perhaps the mountain reached completion and everything upon it was compelled, gently, to agree.

A newcomer is not attacked or welcomed. The inhabitants make no space, yet there is always exactly enough.

Snails already border the place where the foot settles. A branch already curves above the stooping head. The nearest primate holds, in addition to its stone, a small empty interval within the crook of its arm, shaped uncannily like the object a newcomer might carry. The cats’ pupils contain the entrant as a dark figure before the entrant looks at them.

Then the hollow begins its only form of assimilation.

The visitor’s next breath becomes more deliberate. A hand raised to part a fern pauses before touching it. The wish to step over a root remains stronger than the step itself. Nothing restrains the body; all movement is possible. But each possible movement appears equally present, equally consequential, and therefore impossible to choose without diminishing the completeness of the moment.

In time—or in what serves there as time—the entrant understands the common condition of the inhabitants.

They are not immortal. Immortality requires duration.

They are not frozen. Freezing is an event.

They are not peaceful. Peace is what follows the possibility of conflict.

They are the unfinished made permanent: a population of breaths not yet released, hungers that need not become wounds, births that need not break their shells, inventions spared their consequences. Each creature lives at the fullest point before life must spend itself in order to continue.

And beneath their fur, scales, feathers, skin, bark, and wet translucent tissues, they all share one vast, motionless instinct:

not the desire to remain,

but the inexhaustible readiness to begin.
