What lives in the Hanging Catchment is difficult to count.

At first there seem to be only the obvious creatures: the black shelf-lizards, the buried sacs, the rare crescent passing above. The plants, though strange, are plants. The pillars belong to some vanished maker. The pavilion is an instrument left running. Each thing can be named separately, and for a few hours the basin permits this understanding.

The lizards are the easiest to mistake for ordinary life. They hatch in pockets of warm clay beneath loose basalt plates, three or four at a time, from eggs whose shells resemble cloudy glass. Newly emerged, they are pale and nearly smooth. Over their first day they lie against a chosen stone and acquire it. Grain by grain, their hides darken; hairline fractures appear across their backs; a glint of olivine or a red mineral thread develops where the rock beneath contains one. The resemblance is exact enough that a lizard resting on a pillar takes on the pillar’s dull, yielding gray, though never for long. Something in that surface disturbs them.

They have no fixed territories. They follow heat, and they follow water when it runs uphill. A lizard will place its mouth in the path of an ascending drop and allow the water to climb over its lower jaw. After drinking, it becomes very still. For several minutes its sides cease moving. During that interval, condensation often appears elsewhere: beneath its feet, along its eyelids, or in a perfect ring around the stone on which it lies.

They eat nothing anyone has seen.

The translucent beings beneath the moss are more numerous, if each swelling is an individual. In drought they collapse to films finer than shed skin. Rain enters the ground, and they rise from it as thumb-sized bladders full of dim, granular light. Their bodies have no mouths, only layered membranes through which seepage passes. Iron, salt, and silica gather inside them in branching lines. When the water recedes, each being folds itself around the mineral pattern it has made. The soft portion disappears. The pattern remains.

Some lattices resemble baskets. Others resemble ribs, rootlets, or tiny roofless pavilions. Very old moss islands contain thousands interlocked together, the remains of generations making a single porous foundation. Water can cross these foundations without washing them away. Roots enter the empty chambers and thicken there.

It is unclear whether the sacs die after building their lattices or simply become them.

The silver palms show even less distinction between organism and structure. Their trunks are hollow down the center, but the hollow is not empty. On hot afternoons a column of colder air can be felt descending inside, though no opening is visible in the crown. Their cord-roots sometimes wrap around buried silica lattices and sometimes around narrow objects of the same material as the pillars. During storms the leaves rotate one by one, presenting their edges to the wind. The motion passes through a grove in sequence even when the gust strikes all the trees at once.

Afterward the pale undersides open to the steaming air. Their luminous pores brighten in patterns: first a line, then a ring, then several scattered points that seem to answer lights appearing beneath neighboring leaves. The exchange continues after dark, too slowly to be mistaken for signaling and too precisely to dismiss as random.

No palm seed has ever been found.

A new palm begins as a silver line rising from a wet fracture. For days it may be mistaken for a mineral seam. Then it softens, separates from the stone, and divides at its upper tip. By the time its first leaf unfolds, some other living thing nearby has become vacant.

A shrub may still stand with every waxy leaf in place, yet contain no sap. A moss cushion may retain its color but crumble at a touch into blue-green scales. Once, after a palm emerged beside a seasonal spring, a lizard was found lying perfectly matched to a basalt ledge. It did not move when approached. When lifted, it proved to be a thin, detailed shell with nothing inside—not flesh, bone, or decay, only warm saturated air.

The white flowers seem involved in these passages. Their buds form rapidly, often in places where no parent plant is visible. A swelling appears in clay, in moss, once even in a crack halfway up a pillar. By noon it has become a bloom broad enough to hold in both hands. Its petals are warm outside and cold within. When it exhales its vapor, the surrounding air smells briefly of rain falling on distant ground.

The pollen spiral is not simply carried toward another island. It hesitates at each living presence it passes. Near a palm it lengthens. Near a lizard it tilts. Around a human hand it may divide into five thin threads, one for each finger, before drawing itself together again. It proceeds only after this examination.

Flowers that open after the long-winged animal’s passage produce no pollen. Instead, a faint geometric shadow crosses the center of each bloom. By evening the petals collapse around that shadow, and by morning neither flower nor stalk remains. A shallow hollow is left in the soil, usually holding a quantity of clear water that does not reflect the sky above.

The flying creature is called singular because only one has ever been seen at a time. That may be a convenience rather than a fact.

From below it resembles a black crescent drawn between two transparent blades. From the ridge, on the rare occasions when it passes level with an observer, its body can be seen to consist of overlapping bands, each turning slowly inside the next. There is no head, but one band bears a row of dark depressions that close sequentially as it banks. Its wings are not attached in the manner of limbs. They are planes of trembling air in which rainbows, stars, and bits of the landscape flash out of order.

It circles without flapping. Clouds open around it.

Those who have tried to watch where it lands lose sight of it at the same place: above the southern crack into which the basin’s gathered mist descends. Waiting at the crack does not help. The animal fails to appear, and elsewhere in the catchment a pillar may begin repeating the watcher’s breathing.

The pillars are inhabited, although perhaps not by anything residing inside them.

They respond to nearness before touch. A surface first feels like fired clay, then like dense hide, then—if the hand remains—as if another palm presses faintly back from the opposite side. This pressure never aligns perfectly with the visitor’s hand. It is smaller, or broader, or possessed of too many long digits. Sometimes several impressions overlap.

Water climbing a pillar carries minute traces of whatever it has contacted: red clay, pollen, salt from skin, crushed moss, the metallic dust of fresh fractures. These traces disappear at the top with the droplets. Later they emerge elsewhere, sorted. Clay may appear dry beneath the pavilion. Salt may crystallize in a flower. Pollen may be found sealed inside a spring bubble. A dark line matching a visitor’s fingerprint may spread briefly through the pores of a palm leaf.

The pillars do not merely move water. They distribute contact.

Their arrangement changes, though no one has seen one walking. Measurements made in the morning are unreliable by evening. A pillar once beside the western ridge may stand after rain among the cycads, leaving behind a narrow hole filled with cold vapor. At other times the pillars remain fixed while the space between them alters: a shout crosses ten paces in an instant, but a falling stone takes several seconds to reach the ground. Droplets stop halfway. Shadows bend toward the wrong noon.

The low pavilion appears to be where these distributed impressions are compared.

Anything placed on its glass platform remains itself at first. A leaf stays fresh. A cup of water neither warms nor evaporates. A sleeping person dreams without moving. Beneath the hovering roof, the impossible night proceeds through eclipses, alignments, and star-fields that recur only after long intervals.

Then small discrepancies begin.

The leaf’s veins rearrange into the drainage pattern of the catchment. The water in the cup develops a tide. The sleeper hears, one after another, every isolated sound the basin has made that day: each drop, each pebble, each underground rush, including sounds that occurred before the sleeper arrived. On waking, they may find a red fern stem traced beneath the skin of one wrist, fading over the next hour.

Nothing is harmed immediately. The pavilion is not predatory. It seems to be asking a question for which physical resemblance is the only available language:

Are these two things separate?

The basin’s answer changes.

This is why counting its inhabitants gives inconsistent results. The lizards, sacs, palms, moss, flowers, and winged crescent possess their own cycles and appetites, but each is also a temporary concentration of something more dispersed. The same luminous geometry appears beneath leaves, inside silica lattices, across the animal’s turning bands, and in the false eclipses under the pavilion roof. The same upward motion occurs in water, pollen, vapor, roots, and sometimes sound. One form empties as another becomes present.

The catchment does not maintain a fixed number of bodies. It maintains a fixed number of presences.

A presence may occupy a lizard for years. It may pass through a noon flower in four hours. It may spread among a whole moss island, or contract into a bead of water under a stone. Some are dense enough to look back. Others express themselves only as a tendency: a stream choosing one crack over another, nine pillars leaning by imperceptible degrees toward a new spring, a circle of condensation forming around an absent foot.

Travelers entering the basin are included in this accounting.

The adjustment is not always visible. A person may feel only that the place has become quieter. Lizards stay hidden. No flower opens. One palm keeps its crown folded despite the clear sky. The newcomer has not frightened these things away. For the duration of the visit, they are the thing that the basin has made room for.

Sleep deepens the exchange. Beside a pillar, a traveler’s outline may become dry while the ground around them glistens. Their dreams enter the hydrology. A remembered childhood room appears for an instant in a vertical pool. The voice of someone long dead comes from a spring, speaking no words, only breathing. On waking, the traveler may have forgotten a face and find its expression reproduced in the folded shape of a fern.

When they depart, the place returns what it cannot keep in altered form. A flower opens in the last footprint. A fist-sized cloud follows the ridge and dissolves. Water rises through empty air. Occasionally a new black lizard appears on the sunlit shelf, its hide bearing not the basalt’s fractures but the fine lines of a human palm.

None of this means the basin is one great creature. That idea is too simple. It is not a mind spread neatly across many organs, nor an ancient machine using plants and animals as components. There are quarrels within it. Roots obstruct pillar-water. Lizards bite the luminous pores of palms. Silica beings seal fractures the mountain is trying to widen. Storms overwhelm arrangements that may have taken years. The northern wall presses inward while the southern islands build themselves outward. The flying crescent triggers flowers at the wrong season, as though introducing errors deliberately.

What lives there is closer to an agreement under constant revision.

Stone provides newness. Water provides passage. Plants provide duration. Animals provide movement and attention. The pillars allow qualities to be separated from the objects that carried them. The pavilion tests whether those qualities can be joined again. None rules the others. Together they prevent the young mountain from becoming only one thing too soon.

Perhaps the oldest inhabitant is this negotiation itself.

It has no central body. It does not speak except by exchanging voices. It has no memory except what can be held in mineral, water, growth, and recurrence. It notices pressure, warmth, arrival, departure, and being watched. It cannot create another living presence from nothing, so it transfers, divides, empties, and invites. It is cautious with novelty and extravagant with form.

When alone, it appears as weather.

When approached, it borrows the approacher.

And when the basin is seen at evening, with the real sky blue above and the pavilion’s night shining below, all its inhabitants seem briefly to occupy the space between those two heavens: not creatures in a landscape, but possible versions of one another, waiting for water to decide where each will next begin.
