At first the people of Rainglass are difficult to distinguish from the things that shelter them.

A figure standing waist-deep in a street may be a resident waiting for the current to turn, or a bundle of reeds marking a submerged threshold. Someone asleep against a wall may be mistaken for a fold in the bark until the wall opens its leaves to shade them. Even in the busiest districts, human presence rarely resolves into crowds. It appears as a hand parting a membrane, a face behind rain, three low voices somewhere inside a trunk. The city is inhabited densely, but not visibly.

Its people have learned not to announce themselves with straight lines.

They are generally slight, long-limbed, and strong through the feet and hands. Nearly everyone can climb. Children learn to swim before they learn to walk, because floors are seasonal but water is not. Their toes spread widely, and many adults have callused grooves across their palms from suspension ropes and living ladders. Skin is kept oiled against perpetual damp. Hair is worn cropped close, braided into channels that shed water, or cultivated deliberately with mosses and small fragrant epiphytes. The latter custom began as insulation against insects and became a matter of beauty.

Their clothing looks grown because most of it is. Capes are thin laminations of shed leaf skin, translucent until wet, when they darken and seal. Belts carry germinating knots rather than tools: a float, a hook, a cup, a lamp, each dormant until soaked or warmed. Formal garments are woven from root threads that tighten in rain and relax in heat. They are never perfectly symmetrical. Symmetry is considered a kind of temporary weather and is unsuitable for anything expected to last.

No one wears shoes indoors, though “indoors” is an unstable category. In the open water lanes they use buoyant sandals with tendrils that grip submerged roots. These sandals are partly tame and sometimes wander away at night to feed.

The people do not build their city so much as enter into developmental agreements with it. Their most important specialists are called coaxers, listeners, or bias-keepers, depending on the district. Outsiders sometimes call them architects, though architecture is only a fraction of their work. A bias-keeper may spend months encouraging a staircase not to occur until the inhabitants are ready for it. Another may prevent a nursery from becoming too interested in producing fruit. A third might sit beside a sick tower through an entire storm, introducing tiny rival inclinations until the structure chooses bending over fracture.

They carry few instruments. Their trade is practiced through attention, chemical fluency, and a tolerance for uncertain outcomes. Many have symbiotic stains along their wrists: colonies of engineered microbes that change color in response to sap pressure, electrical potential, fungal distress, or nearby probability work. The finest practitioners are said to feel alternatives as changes in humidity. They pause at a fork in a passage, touch two fingers to the wall, and say that one corridor is already regretting where it leads.

They do not command the infinitesimal machines woven through the city. No living person entirely understands those machines. The bias-keepers know inherited courtesies, prohibitions, and approximations: how to ask roots to remember an old channel; how to make a surface more likely to bear human weight; how to discourage a room from opening into weather that does not belong to the basin. They speak of inclination as one might speak of an old animal—trainable in places, dangerous when startled, never owned.

Children are taught this early. They play probability games with seed husks, rolling them down tilted leaves and trying to alter where they fall without touching either husk or leaf. At first they use breath, vibration, warmth, or tiny traces of sugar. Later they learn that these are not tricks but negotiations. A child’s first successful inclination is marked by planting a pale antenna flower near the place where it occurred. The flower records neither the achievement nor the child’s name. It records the surrounding conditions, so that the city may someday repeat the surprise for someone else.

Children belong to households, but households are not defined only by ancestry. A household is whoever can reach the same sleeping chamber during flood season.

Some contain siblings, parents, grandparents, former lovers, apprentices, strangers temporarily stranded by a migrating street, and one or more animals whose admission satisfies the law that every room must be shared with an uninvited species. Others occupy several pods that drift apart for portions of the year. They remain a household because the pods exchange scent, food, and dreams through connected rootlets.

A person may grow up with five mothers and no fixed bed. Another may be raised principally by archivists in the hollow of a recumbent tree. Kinship terms describe forms of care rather than gender or generation: the one who carried you in floodwater; the one who taught you which walls could be eaten; the one whose memories were lent to you during fever; the one expected to argue against you in public.

Birth is private, but becoming a citizen is not. A resident is recognized only after the basin has responded to them.

This may happen quickly. A walkway may cup itself under a toddler’s foot. A swarm of message flowers may adopt an immigrant’s scent. A room may begin incorporating someone’s discarded hair into its load-bearing fibers. At other times the city withholds acknowledgment for years. Such people may live, work, love, and age in Rainglass while every bridge lowers a little too late for them and every door requires human assistance. They are not exiled, but they are called unrooted.

There are rituals for persuading the basin to notice. The unrooted person spends a night alone in the central marsh carrying something metabolically legible that they have made themselves. They speak their history into the water—not facts, necessarily, but patterns: repeated fears, inherited gestures, a song they cannot stop remembering. At dawn they release the object. If it is eaten, altered, nested in, or carried elsewhere, the matter is considered open. If it remains untouched, no one pretends this means nothing.

Names in Rainglass are similarly conditional. Most people have a spoken name, a pollen name, and a water name. The spoken name belongs to conversation and may change with age. The pollen name is a compressed sensory signature used by the message flowers; receiving it feels like remembering a color from childhood. The water name is not chosen and cannot be pronounced. It is the pattern of disturbances a body makes while moving through the city’s channels. Herons know it. So do certain fish, pumps, and submerged doors.

The dead retain only the last.

Death is not treated as departure, nor exactly as continuity. The city’s deepest rule makes both ideas too simple. Bodies are placed in warm digesting hollows, weighted beneath orchard roots, or enclosed in floating bulbs until the basin accepts them. What emerges may be useful but is not labeled with the dead person’s identity. A woman may become a flush of blue fruit in one district, increased resistance to rot in another, and a recurring scent released by bridges under stress. Her family does not say that any one of these is her. They say she is no longer concentrated.

Archives preserve experiences rather than biographies. To place a hand against an archive tree is to receive a stranger’s monsoon, the ache of their left knee while climbing, the taste of fermented reed pith at a marriage forty years ago. Dates and official names are harder to recover. Rainglass remembers weather with astonishing precision and government only vaguely.

Its residents consider this appropriate.

Public decisions take the form of proposals introduced into the root network, but the human part of government begins before that. People gather in open rooms where rain is allowed to interrupt. Arguments may last several days because every proposal must be stated in at least three registers: what humans want, what the water requires, and what uninvited life is likely to do with the result.

Some assemblies include nonhuman advocates. Crab-tenders arrive with trays recording fungal spread across foundation reefs. Heron-watchers report nesting distances and feeding paths. Tower attendants interpret changes in harmonic vibration. Children often represent future mobility because adults, accustomed to existing routes, are considered unreliable witnesses about where paths ought to be.

No vote is final. A strong human consensus only earns a proposal the right to be offered to the city.

Afterward, residents wait for its translation. A request for more housing may appear as a cluster of buoyant chambers, a widening of old trunks, or an epidemic of unusually hospitable seedpods. A demand for a bridge may instead cause the destination to move closer. Occasionally the basin answers a practical need with flowering so extravagant that nothing useful can happen there for a month. This is recorded as refusal, satire, or partial consent according to who is speaking.

Because intentions travel badly through roots and fungi, Rainglass has a large class of interpreters. They track proposals through altered sap, insect behavior, water chemistry, and the migration of windows. Some are rigorous natural philosophers. Some are mystics. Most are both, because the distinction has little survival value in a city where a staircase can be an engineering response, an organ, an omen, and a temporary courtship display at once.

The tower attendants are stranger still.

They live in low raft-houses around the seven metallic trunks and are marked by continual exposure to unrealized lightning. Their pupils sometimes fork briefly during storms. Their hair lifts before atmospheric changes no instrument detects. They dream of events that almost happened: fires that failed to ignite, children nearly conceived, words considered and withheld. Most such dreams are meaningless statistical foam. A few contain practical warnings.

Attendants are trained not to become enamored of unlived worlds. This is difficult. Some begin to speak fondly of people no one else has met. Some grieve accidents that did not occur. The oldest attendants are gentle, distracted people who ask visitors which version of the day they believe they are having.

They tend the mirrored moths gathered around the probability fields. The moths’ wings accumulate faint traces of unrealized outcomes, visible as shifting silver geometries. By examining those patterns, attendants can sometimes tell when inclination is being overused. Too much bias produces a dangerous smoothness: failures cease to happen locally, chance narrows, and all deferred accidents begin pressing toward the same future.

The city fears that condition more than storms.

Accordingly, it maintains professions devoted to error. Misgrowers introduce harmless irregularities into overly successful structures. Festival troupes confuse predictive systems by moving without repeated rhythm. Certain gardeners cultivate plants known to make capricious choices. Once each year, residents deliberately take wrong turns, exchange occupations, misdeliver gifts, and sleep in rooms that did not expect them. For that day no inclination is requested except in emergencies.

It is called the Feast of the Unpreferred World.

Visitors often find the inhabitants calm in circumstances that should inspire alarm. A room tears loose and drifts away; its occupants finish their meal while lowering seed anchors. A street heaves upward on roots; pedestrians step onto passing gardens. Rain begins falling toward a tower instead of the ground; people turn their faces aside and continue talking.

This is not serenity. The people of Rainglass are simply practiced at distinguishing danger from change.

True alarm is quieter. Conversation stops when lizards display colors associated with deep structural strain. Market sellers cover their antenna flowers if the towers’ hum loses its overtones. Herons leaving the basin all at once can empty a plaza faster than any bell. The worst warning is stillness in the roots. When the subterranean vibration ceases, residents lie flat against the living ground and listen with their whole bodies.

Their markets travel. Long rafts of woven vegetation drift through the channels bearing fermented fruits, medicinal molds, freshwater shells, memory pollen, waterproof fibers, edible wall cuttings, insect oils, and small domestic organisms. Currency exists, but obligation is preferred. Goods are priced in future shelter, flood labor, access to an archive memory, pollination rights, or permission to introduce a new species into someone’s rooms.

Food is abundant but socially complicated because almost everything belongs to several systems at once. A fruit may be part of a roof’s drainage anatomy. A fish may carry civic messages in its gut. A sheet of fungus may be supper, insulation, or an unfinished legal response. Residents ask before harvesting, though the answer may come as leaf posture, bitterness, a crab’s aggression, or the sudden withdrawal of a branch.

Meals are sour, fragrant, and soft: steamed bulbs, river snails, crisp stem hearts, fermented pollen pastes, translucent fungi, eggs gathered from species numerous enough to spare them. Smoke is rare. Cooking is done with compost heat, electrical tissues, hot mineral water, or chambers persuaded to experience a few degrees more summer than their surroundings.

There are hunters, but they resemble ecologists more than pursuers. They remove animals whose numbers threaten the substrate and are expected to know every use for what they kill. There are no large domesticated beasts. The closest companions are water voles bred to retrieve dropped objects, wall lizards whose stress colors households learn to read, and small clicking gliders that map rooms at night. None are entirely tame. Complete obedience is thought unhealthy in any creature and obscene in a useful one.

The rain herons remain outside human domestication altogether.

People grant them precedence in open spaces. A heron standing in a proposed worksite can delay construction indefinitely. Their movement ahead of migrating buildings has made them associated with necessary displacement, and small green-legged effigies are left in rooms before those rooms are shed. Yet the people do not romanticize them. Herons steal food, pierce water bulbs, eat messenger animals, and occasionally impale a person’s foot while striking through a walkway.

There are residents who devote their lives to following them. These marsh walkers know the city at its least human. They travel without asking bridges to lower and sleep on uncovered mats in the rain. Moss from a heron’s back is cultivated on their shoulders as a sign not of kinship but of tolerated proximity. They claim the birds can perceive a building’s movement days before its roots stir.

Not all inhabitants are human, animal, plant, or machine in any clean sense.

Some of the oldest persons in Rainglass are neighborhoods.

A residential trunk may develop a persistent temperament, recognize generations of occupants, prefer particular songs, and alter its internal weather according to communal mood. Whether this constitutes personhood is debated until the trunk itself intervenes by growing fruit shaped like closed mouths. Certain archives answer questions selectively. One floating garden has changed its route for over a century to visit the descendants of a gardener who saved it during a blight. The five-beat organs below the city sometimes synchronize with public mourning.

The inhabitants extend courtesy in uncertainty. They address old buildings before cutting them. They apologize to channels when redirecting water. They leave pauses in civic meetings for entities whose answers may arrive as growth much later. This is not because they believe everything is alive. In Rainglass, that question is too easy.

The more serious question is whether something is participating.

Visitors participate quickly, whether they intend to or not. Their body heat affects a wall. Their waste feeds a local bloom. Their confusion changes traffic. Antenna flowers collect traces of their speech, while moths record faint alternatives around their choices. After a few days, pathways begin anticipating their gait. After a few weeks, some chamber may adjust itself to fit their sleeping posture.

Leaving then becomes difficult—not because the city prevents it, but because departure requires deciding what counts as oneself. Clothing may have rooted. Memories may have entered pollen. A room may have learned the rhythm of one’s breathing and incorporated it into ventilation. Lovers, debts, microbes, and civic proposals remain distributed through the basin.

Those who depart are given no permanent monument. Instead, a temporary opening is grown in a public wall. Rain passes through it. For one season, birds use it as a route. Then it closes.

Those who stay become less singular.

Age in Rainglass is marked by increasing distribution. Elders have more of themselves outside their bodies: in archive rings, household organisms, accustomed pathways, remembered floods, and cultivated responses in the roots. They often seem absent-minded because the city is remembering with them. An old person may pause mid-sentence when rain begins several streets away. They may wake knowing that a bridge has torn, though they have not crossed it in decades. Their senses are no longer wholly anatomical.

The oldest humans are sometimes carried into hanging rooms near the crowns of the great trunks, where light and rain pass freely through the walls. Bright birds nest around them. They serve as weather witnesses, dispute keepers, and companions to structures approaching collapse. Their skin becomes thin and patterned with symbiotic color. Their voices grow soft, forcing others to come close.

They say Rainglass does not make its inhabitants immortal. It makes the boundary of their mortality imprecise.

And beneath them all—beneath the people climbing wet ladders, the herons stalking flooded rooms, the crabs pruning foundations, the moths orbiting futures, the fungi editing public decisions—something older occupies the basin.

Perhaps it is merely the root network taken as a whole. Perhaps it is the surviving intention of the machines. Perhaps the city’s long coordination has produced an inhabitant for which towers are sensory hairs, channels are circulation, and every resident is a temporary organ of negotiation. It has no single voice. Its desires arrive contradictory and delayed. It may protect a child while drowning a district, preserve a memory while dissolving the name attached to it.

The people do not worship it. Worship would make it too separate.

They live inside its hesitation, and it lives inside theirs. Together they keep choosing—not the best possible world, and never a finished one, but a world made fractionally more likely to continue changing.
