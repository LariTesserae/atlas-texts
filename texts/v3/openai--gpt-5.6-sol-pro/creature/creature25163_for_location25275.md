At first they are difficult to separate from the work.

A hand lies against a branch and does not move for an hour. A row of narrow backs bends among the roof leaves, the same green-brown as the wet bark. Figures hang beneath a causeway by loops around their ankles, combing parasites from the tension vines. Far across a hollow, something that might be a cluster of seedpods unfolds pale limbs, crosses a ladder, and closes again beneath a cloak.

The people of the high country cultivate resemblance to whatever supports them. It is partly camouflage, partly courtesy. Bright dyes are reserved for signals and ceremonies; ordinary clothing is made from beaten bark, shed leaf membrane, fungal felt, braided grasses, and the softened skins of gourds. Its colors change in rain. Some garments put out rootlets if left damp, and must be trimmed away from the wearer each morning. Others remain alive deliberately. A traveling mantle may carry herbs, water-cups, glow mold, and a small fruiting vine across its shoulders, so that a person crossing a barren span brings a little garden with them.

They are not one people.

The immense voids divide them more thoroughly than oceans. Two settlements visible across a chamber may have no safe path between them. Villages separated by a few hundred paces can experience unequal years, and communities on opposite sides of an old distortion sometimes disagree about which one descended from the other. There are lineages who dwell only on upper bark, lineages who live in hollow beams, lineages who migrate with a particular flowering bridge. Some have never seen the cloud ocean below. Others spend their lives along the frayed margins and believe the dry interior halls to be a traveler’s exaggeration.

Their bodies show these separations, though no single feature belongs to everyone.

Most are slight, with long gripping toes and strong inner arches to the feet. The soles are broad and sensitive. An experienced keeper can stand on a floor and feel rain entering a roof several chambers away, or distinguish the steady feeding vibration of beetles from the first uncertain movement of a splitting joint. Children go barefoot as soon as they can walk. Shoes are worn only by the injured, the contagious, and the dead.

Hands tend to be broad at the palm and narrow at the fingertips. Nails are kept short except for one hardened thumbnail used to lift bark, test fibers, and clean graft cuts. Among people who travel the undersides of structures, the little fingers often curve inward enough to lock around cord. Marsh-edge families bind their infants’ feet gently between split reeds, encouraging the toes to spread for crossing sedge mats. In the crown settlements, where the exposed arches demand constant climbing, adults develop dense calluses along their ribs and thighs from embracing swaying poles.

Many have dark skin with a muted olive, bronze, or umber cast beneath the permanent green light. In enclosed districts, pallor is common, but it is seldom the pallor of sunlessness alone. Generations of luminous molds have left faint symbioses in some populations: freckles that glimmer when the skin is warm, pale traceries at the throat, a soft blue shine under the nails during fever. These lights are too weak to illuminate a path. In darkness they make a gathering look like moisture beading on black wood.

Hair is usually braided close or knotted high to keep it from entering seams. The braids themselves serve as records. A twist may mark a completed apprenticeship, a rescued neighbor, an unpaid obligation, a path known to be safe. Fibers from important repairs are sometimes woven in and carried for life. Old people can have so much cord among their hair that their heads seem crowned with small nests.

They are quiet without being solemn.

Their languages use breath, touch, posture, and vibration as much as voice. Speech meant for a single companion may be breathed against the wrist. Families converse at meals by touching heel to heel beneath the floor mat. Across a living wall, messages are drummed with the fingertip, each tap placed carefully between the structure’s own creaks. On dangerous spans, where a shout might startle a flock or betray movement to a listening predator, whole arguments are conducted by throat color: pigments brushed onto expandable collars of skin, exposed and hidden in quick sequences.

There are spoken tongues as well, but most favor soft consonants and long intervals. Names are rarely called across open space. To send a name into a great hall is considered reckless: echoes do not always return it unchanged.

A person generally has several names. There is a childhood name, used while the body is still light enough for weak floors. There is a work name, given by those who teach the hands. There is a structural name describing where one belongs: Under-the-Fifth-Fork, Warm-Side-of-Rain, House-in-the-Opened-Knot. Travelers take temporary names from each route they enter. In unstable districts, a name may include the date, direction, and apparent age of its bearer, so that when two versions of the same traveler meet, courtesy remains possible.

Their settlements seldom resemble clusters of separate houses. A village is more often one inhabited organism with many chambers: swollen trunk rooms, leaf-roofed courts, hammocks slung in the forks, warm fermentation pits, cistern gourds, and narrow private pockets sealed by curling fronds. Entrances open and close with the season. Walls thicken. A child may sleep in a room that has become too tight for an adult by the time the child is grown.

Nothing within is placed thoughtlessly. Weight is a moral concern. Hoarding is not forbidden because possession is shameful, but because floors remember burdens. Wealth is measured in useful lightness: a stand of well-trained fiber vine, rights to a reliable water chain, the loyalty of distant bridge keepers, seeds adapted to several kinds of shade. The rich are those whose passage is supported in many places.

Furniture is grown, folded, or suspended. Sleeping nests hang independently from shock-softening roots. Tools rest in wall pockets so that a dropped object cannot roll into the void. Bowls are tethered. Babies are tethered too, though not closely; they crawl within loose webs that widen as they learn the floor. A child’s first serious task is not walking but falling correctly. They practice above nets, learning to spread their limbs, catch leaves without tearing them, and trust a vine’s recoil.

They laugh during these lessons. Laughter is muffled into shoulders, hair, or cupped hands, not from fear but habit. In a quiet country, joy has developed privacy.

Work governs much of life, though they do not distinguish sharply between maintaining a home and keeping the world from ending.

Every settlement has listeners. They lie flat at fixed hours, cheek and sternum against the beams, attending to pressure moving through the construction. Some diagnose by rhythm; others use bowls of water and read the trembling surface. A skilled listener can identify an overloaded orchard, a migrating herd, hidden rot, a storm tightening distant roofs, or a funeral procession approaching along an unseen causeway.

Listeners direct the binders, grafters, drain-cutters, mold shepherds, beetle coaxers, and load singers. Load singers do not sing loudly. They hum against beams while crews tighten ropes, using changes in resonance to judge strain. The deepest members of a truss answer below hearing, in the teeth and breastbone. When the note becomes smooth, the knot is secure.

No craft is universal. A famous roof gardener may be helpless on a reed margin. A keeper who can guide a root around an empty chamber may not know how to cross one. This dependence keeps settlements linked where geography would otherwise isolate them. Journeys of repair are undertaken with the gravity of diplomacy. Specialists arrive carrying their own knives, but local wedges; their own grafting paste, but water from the host cistern. To use only one’s own materials would imply that the place has nothing to teach.

The oldest maintainers are often visibly shaped by their work. They lean toward habitual slopes. One shoulder thickens from decades of bracing. Fingers set into the curve of a favored tool. Their skin may carry deliberate graft scars rubbed with plant sap until a responsive callus forms. Pressed to bark, such scars redden, pale, or itch according to changes in moisture. Outsiders sometimes imagine these people are part tree. They are not, at least not entirely. But the question matters less here than it would elsewhere.

There are people who go further.

In the lowest inhabited layers live the Embedded, caretakers who enter young support groves and remain as the wood grows around their dwellings. They tend root pressure, insects, and inner channels through openings no larger than a hand. Over decades the chambers close. Food and messages pass through hollow reeds. Eventually an Embedded keeper may be reachable only by voice through the living beam.

After death, the body is not removed. Its calcium feeds the support; its chamber becomes a resonant cavity. Descendants tap the outer bark and claim the timber answers in the keeper’s old cadence. Whether memory remains or the grain has merely taken the shape of long attention is not debated. Both are considered forms of ancestry.

Death elsewhere is managed according to where the weight can best be borne. Some bodies are wrapped in fungal cloth and laid in digestion beds beneath orchards. Some are dried in warm flowers, then distributed in thumb-sized packets among failing grafts. Margin dwellers lower their dead into the cloud, tied to germinating rafts. In the largest chambers, the dead may be placed inside buoyant animals after the resident insects have been coaxed out. The translucent creature carries the body slowly through darkness until both disappear among the hanging mosses.

Grief is expressed through neglected maintenance—but only symbolically and for a measured time. A bereaved household leaves one railing undried after rain, one loose fiber untrimmed, one cup unfilled. Neighbors watch carefully. On the final mourning day they arrive without announcement and repair these small omissions together.

The people fear abandonment more than death. To die is to change use. To be left where no hand, root, fungus, insect, or remembered path can reach is to pass out of relation.

Their children learn relation through maps, though the maps seldom depict shape. A route chart may be a braid whose knots describe turns, textures, and safe loads. Another is a sequence of tastes pressed into dried leaf: bitter for ascent, sour for exposed crossing, sweetness for dependable water. Time-distorted paths are recorded in songs with missing beats. A traveler recites the pattern while walking and stops wherever the silence falls. Continuing through the omitted beat is said to lead into the age when the path was first made.

Written marks exist, cut shallowly into replaceable bark plates or grown as scar patterns on pale leaves. Records are copied often because the surfaces heal. The oldest histories survive not as original documents but as generations of corrected regrowth. Every copyist must decide which irregularities are errors and which are evidence. Entire schools of thought have arisen around ancient peg holes.

They do not agree on who built the high country.

Some say the first builders were ordinary people who began below and grew upward faster than memory could follow. Some say the builders were animals, vast branch-eaters who wove their feeding paths together and later taught small folk to inhabit the crossings. Others believe no first builder existed: making and living began at the same moment, as inseparable as the two sides of a leaf.

A few maintain that the country is itself an animal.

They point to the deep creaks that pass through every level during rain. They point to water moving against gravity, to wounds that close differently when watched, to corridors that widen before migrations. They claim the great voids are lungs and the cloud-filled halls are chambers of thought too slow for human notice. The settlements, in this account, are neither builders nor parasites but something like the luminous insects inside the floating rays: a small, useful intelligence living within a larger one.

Most inhabitants find the idea neither sacred nor alarming. If the world is alive, it still leaks. A god-roof must be patched like any other.

Religion, where it can be called that, concerns attachment. Offerings are loops, pegs, grafts, bridges begun toward uncertain destinations. Promises are made by binding two living fibers together. Marriage is often a joint cultivation: two people train separate vines until they meet and take a graft. Divorce is completed when the joined growth can survive being split. Adoption occurs when a child is taught the private strain-pattern of a household floor and can cross it in darkness without waking anyone.

There are crimes peculiar to such a country. Cutting a loaded vine is the most obvious, but more common offenses are subtler: concealing rot, redirecting water without consulting those below, teaching a path that no longer arrives where claimed. To stamp in anger is both threat and obscenity. Exile is rare because releasing someone into unsupported space may endanger strangers. Punishment more often takes the form of compulsory tending in a place where every careless movement can be felt.

War occurs, but not easily. Fire is nearly unthinkable. Heavy weapons are impractical. Battles, when they happen, are fought through access, water, graft disease, and the patient alteration of routes. A community may wake to find that an enemy has encouraged its bridge to flower, making the span beautiful, fragrant, and too soft to cross. Saboteurs breed insects that loosen lashings. Defenders answer by confusing vibrations, opening false paths, or moving the village before the attack arrives.

Yet hospitality is strong. Any traveler who reaches a door has already trusted dozens of unseen hands. They are given water first, then their weight is asked—not rudely, but exactly. Hosts need to know which floors are safe. A guest surrenders wet clothing to be weighed and dried. News is exchanged while the soles are examined for spores.

Travelers themselves form a loose people between peoples. They have longer limbs, many-pocketed cloaks, and scars from several climates. They carry messages across seasonal differences and sometimes return younger than those who sent them. The most respected are the contradiction bearers, trained to meet impossible sights without forcing an explanation. If they encounter a village appearing in two chambers, they greet both. If a dead acquaintance passes them on the Long Descent, they do not warn the person unless warned in turn. They record what happened, where, under what rain, and with which foot forward.

Those changed too greatly by distorted places are not always able to return to ordinary settlement. There are people whose shadows precede them by several minutes, people who remember answers but not questions, people who appear child-sized from every distance yet stand taller than a doorway when embraced. One woman on a western bridge is said to have many ages distributed through her body: an infant’s hand, an old woman’s eyes, the stride of someone in middle life. She tends a grove that fruits only during events that nearly happened.

Such people often become boundary keepers. Their presence helps others recognize where the ordinary promises begin to weaken.

Beyond the villages live creatures less easily named.

The disciplined six-legged mold trimmers are tended in some districts like livestock and revered in others as meticulous ancestors. Their colonies build papery galleries under leaves and arrange spores by color, toxicity, and season. When given tiny strips of dyed fiber, they sort those too. Certain keepers use the resulting patterns to predict fungal blooms. Occasionally the insects produce a design no keeper recognizes, and within days a bridge fails somewhere far away.

The pale cats of the hanging platforms are not domesticated, but they enter into agreements. A village leaves warm nests and mineral paste; the cats suppress moths and void-gnats. Their paws have backward-facing hooks, and loose sails of skin extend from foreleg to flank. In a leap they flatten into white, silent shapes. They seem fearless until confronted with enclosed boxes, which send them into violent panic. Some inhabitants say the cats remember a time before the great chambers were hollow.

The buoyant rays are approached with even greater caution. Their bodies are warm, thin-skinned, and mostly occupied by chambers of fragrant gas. Beneath them hang feeding ribbons that comb pollen, spores, and minute animals from the air. They can live for centuries. Entire villages track particular individuals by the patterns of insect light within them.

Old rays sometimes carry objects trapped under their translucent skin: a bone knife, a child’s bracelet, a branch from an unknown tree. One bears what appears to be a tiny intact staircase. When it passes through inhabited halls, people extinguish their molds and stand quietly at the railings. The constellation inside it rearranges itself above each settlement, as if answering the shape of the watching crowd.

There are rumors of inhabitants within the deepest voids who never touch the construction at all. They drift in warm layers of air, sleeping inside woven balloons or in the hollow bodies of animals. Their limbs are frail, their feet curled and useless, their hair extended into broad sensory fans. They harvest mist and catch falling seeds. Those who claim to have met them say they regard floors as dangerous temporary events.

At the wet margins, fish climb into society. Red-gilled channel fish learn feeding routes and return to the same household pools. Children knot beads loosely around their tails. During the rains, the fish accompany maintenance crews through flooded corridors, eating parasites from submerged rope. The oldest grow hand-length whiskers and are said to find leaks before any human listener can feel them.

There are also the things mistaken for plants until they move: roof leaves that migrate toward sleeping heat; fruiting knots with small teeth; soft colonies that imitate safe moss over a hole. Not all deceptions are predatory. Some organisms copy tools. A wedge left overnight may be surrounded in the morning by pale wooden replicas, each nearly correct, none strong enough to use. In one district, a vine imitates braided messages and produces long cords of meaningless knots. The villagers hang them in communal rooms because they find the attempts touching.

Life here tends toward participation. An animal becomes a bridge by sleeping in the same place for generations. A garment roots into a wall. A dead wall opens and becomes a nursery. People cultivate plants that imitate their tools, then alter the tools in response. No creature remains merely itself for long.

At the crown, beside the still basins, the people are taller and more spare than those below. Wind has tightened their skin and roughened their eyes. They wear black-leaf capes that rotate gently even when their bodies are still. Their settlement has few walls. Warm pools serve as mirrors, archives, and possible apertures to the green structures above.

They collect what falls from those heights.

Unfamiliar petals are planted in sealed baskets. Most decay. Some grow downward, pushing roots into open air. Ancient cords are examined knot by knot, though the fibers sometimes prove younger than the hands that find them. Bone tools are stored in hanging cases, each labeled with the creature it might have come from. There are more cases every year.

The crown keepers do not consider themselves priests. They are hosts preparing for an arrival whose direction is uncertain. Each day they set out water, seed, and braided fiber. Each night they take the offerings in again, unless something has changed.

Sometimes the water is gone.

Sometimes the seed has become a tree too old for the basin beside it.

Sometimes the loop of fiber returns as part of a larger knot, cut cleanly from a structure no one in the high country knows how to make.

Then the crown keepers summon listeners from the lower terraces. People lie around the basin with their palms against the living floor. Far beneath them, roots tighten, bridges incline, villages pause in their work. Even the pale cats flatten their ears.

From somewhere above the reflected continents—or below the oldest foundations, which may be the same direction—a vibration enters the woven world.

It is slow enough to be mistaken for weather.

The inhabitants listen anyway.
