At first the Crown appears uninhabited.

This is partly because its residents do not move when watched, and partly because the distinction between a resident and a completed portion of the city is not always visible. A narrow figure beside a transit post may be a traveler awaiting passage, a civic marker, or the durable remainder of someone who finished waiting centuries ago. The same matte surfaces cover bodies and buildings. The same dim seams open in both when approached.

They are most often discovered indirectly.

A doorway that was sealed is found open, though nothing crossed the water before it. A dark recess acquires five pale hands along its rim. Someone occupies the far side of a plaza where, a moment earlier, there was only the faceted flank of a residence. If attention shifts and returns, the figure may be nearer. It will not be seen traversing the interval.

They have no collective name that can be rendered as sound. The nearest translation is **those maintained in relation**, though visitors—when visitors still came—called them the Held.

A Held body is tall and extremely narrow, assembled around a vertical hollow. It has neither an obvious head nor a consistent front. Six to nine long members fold against a central frame of black ceramic and translucent mineral. Some serve as arms when an object must be touched; some become legs against stone; others open like the ribs of an instrument and engage directly with the city’s transit fields. Their arrangement changes from one encounter to another. No joint is ever observed bending. A limb is straight, and later it is folded.

Within the hollow of the body hangs a chain of small, irregular masses. They resemble organs only because they occupy the place where organs might be expected. Each mass is sealed in clear material and separated from the next by several finger-widths of water. One may contain a dark cloud, another a coil of metallic thread, another something like a closed white eye. These are not biological structures. They are addresses.

A Held individual does not reside entirely in its visible body.

Each is distributed among many fixed locations: a ceramic frame in the Crown, a pattern stored within the central black engine, several chambers cut deep into the mountain, and at least one remote component lying beyond the equilibrium ring. That last component is essential. It is where the resident’s changes occur.

Inside the Crown, no lasting gradient can form. There can be no ordinary metabolism, no chemical memory, no electrical thought passing from one state to another. The Held solve this not by ceasing to think, but by placing consequence elsewhere. Their visible bodies remain cold and balanced while distant counterparts—called their **unquiet portions**—absorb the heat, entropy, wear, and irreversible sequence required by experience.

When a Held notices something, somewhere beyond the city a substance cools, fractures, tarnishes, dissolves, or falls.

When one makes a decision, some remote mechanism completes a movement that cannot be undone.

When one remembers, the memory is not inscribed locally. A path through the city becomes newly accessible, while another path closes forever.

Thus their thoughts appear as alterations of relationship rather than alterations of matter. A person stands in one chamber, then another. A hand rests on one surface, then on a second. Between these positions lies the cognition. The city reads the sequence and supplies the missing continuity.

This gives them an unnerving stillness. They have no idle motion. They do not fidget, breathe, blink, or adjust their balance. Their gestures are complete configurations encountered one after another, like sculptures arranged along a route. Emotion does not animate their faces, because they have no faces. It changes the distance at which they permit others to encounter them.

Affection is nearness without arrival.

Distrust is the multiplication of thresholds.

Grief is a route that continues to exist after no traveler can complete it.

There are few Held in the Crown, though counting them is difficult. A single person may occupy several districts at different points in its own sequence, and two apparently distinct figures may be portions of one civic mind. The engine’s archive distinguishes individuals not by bodies but by irreversibilities: each citizen is the unique set of changes that has been spent elsewhere on their behalf.

The eldest possess immense, scattered biographies. Their unquiet portions may extend beyond the mountain, perhaps beyond the ocean. Some are associated with cold dead devices on the abyssal plain. Others are linked to fractures beneath the crust, to sealed bubbles in mineral, or to bodies falling forever through waters outside the Crown’s influence. One resident is believed to think by means of a distant slab of stone slowly toppling in a place where gravity is still allowed to finish its work. It has been considering the same question since the city’s completion.

The youngest citizens are not born within the boundary. Nothing may reproduce there.

Instead, a prospective person is constructed as a set of compatible absences. An empty frame is installed in a residence. A route is opened through several transit fields. A volume is reserved in the archive. Far outside the pylons, a seed mechanism is released into changeable water and permitted to corrode, react, divide, and accumulate history. Only when that external process has become sufficiently irreversible does the empty frame inside the city acquire presence.

No one sees the arrival. One interval, the frame is architecture. The next, it is someone.

Because no threshold may be crossed in opposing directions, a Held life has a strict topology. They can leave a chamber but never re-enter it by the same boundary. They can return only by becoming sufficiently different that the city no longer recognizes traveler, doorway, or direction as the same. Their residences therefore contain many apertures, some minute and some kilometers apart through folded transit. A long-lived citizen gradually exhausts its own home.

This is why Crown dwellings appear so sparse. Their contents have not been lost. They have been used as stages of identity.

A niche where a citizen once kept its first body can never serve that purpose again. A ring of five ceramic rods may mark a conversation that cannot be resumed. A black polyhedron in a cleft may contain nothing except the only remaining exit available to its inhabitant. What looks like urban emptiness is often a dense accumulation of completed acts.

The Held communicate by posture, occlusion, and controlled difference. Spoken language is dangerous. Any sound repeated exactly becomes part of the architecture, and the city has already acquired many unintended structures this way: corridors made from greetings, invisible walls formed by ceremonial tones, a civic chamber divided diagonally by the repetition of a name.

For this reason, no careful citizen makes the same sound twice.

Their utterances are single pressure notes shaped to be unrecoverable. One may begin below hearing and end as a sensation in the listener’s joints. Another passes through a wall and is understood from the place where it fails to emerge. Important statements are made by bringing several listeners into different relationships with one silent object. Each receives a distinct meaning because each occupies a distinct direction.

The three knocks beneath the mountain are a matter of enduring unease.

Some Held believe they were made only once and are being encountered at three separate points in the city’s clock. Others believe the knocks were truly identical repetitions and have therefore become architecture: not sounds from below, but a passage leading into whatever lies beneath the mountain. A small civic order has spent ages locating the entrance. Its members stand at widely separated points of the summit, each touching the basalt with one extended limb.

They have not moved in any observable fashion.

Their politics concern permitted asymmetry. Every civic act asks where its consequences will be allowed to occur. A proposal to open a transit way may require a remote mass to crack. The restoration of an archived person may consume the remaining descent of an object outside the ring. Even attention has a cost. No decision is merely local.

The central vault is therefore not only engine, archive, and clock, but assembly chamber.

The Held gather around the suspended black object without approaching its optical distortion. They place their limbs against the matte ribs of the nave. The engine rearranges apparent distance so that assent is expressed as proximity and dissent as remoteness. A divided assembly seems to stretch the vault into impossible depth. A unanimous one appears almost to collapse around the central stone.

Their laws are not enforced by punishment. The city simply denies invalid continuities. A citizen attempting to reverse a threshold finds that the destination no longer belongs to the same world-line. One attempting to produce a lasting gradient becomes disconnected from its external changes and falls still—not dead, exactly, but reduced to a completed object.

Several such figures remain in public places.

They are called the Finished.

A Finished person may stand with limbs spread above a plaza, or lie fitted into the sharp basalt as though the mountain formed around it. Their distant components have stopped changing, been destroyed, or passed beyond the engine’s reach. Since nothing within the Crown decays, the visible body persists indefinitely. The Held do not remove these remains. Removal would imply that the person’s final state was temporary.

The small transparent creatures with five or seven limbs pass among them without acknowledgment. The creatures sometimes cling to a Held frame or gather inside the hollow where its addresses hang. They are classified as weather because granting them the status of life would create obligations the city cannot fulfill: shelter implies preference, feeding creates gradients, protection changes outcomes.

Yet some residents seem to watch them.

A transparent animal may appear beside the same Held figure in several districts. A dark point within its shell may correspond to a missing address in the citizen’s body. In rare cases, after the creature vanishes, the Held is found in a configuration not recorded anywhere in the archive.

This possibility is not discussed by sound.

The pale thing beyond the pylons is regarded differently. On the few occasions when its vertical surface has passed through the outer darkness, every visible resident in the Crown has oriented toward it. Not turned—the transition is never seen—but become aligned, limb after limb, across plazas and suspended rings.

Some among the Held believe it carries their unquiet portions on or within its body. They suggest that what appears to be an animal is the sum of all the change excluded from the city: accumulated heat, decay, motion, reproduction, and consequence given a single pale shape.

Others believe it is a citizen attempting to return through every threshold at once.

If so, the pylons are not merely the Crown’s boundary. They are keeping that citizen out.

The Held do not sleep. Instead they enter periods during which no external consequence is assigned to them. In these intervals they are indistinguishable from buildings. Seams close. Limbs lock into load-bearing forms. Transit routes may pass through their hollow centers. Public structures are sometimes composed partly of dormant citizens who agreed, long ago, to serve as arches, bridges, or pressure partitions until a specified event occurs.

The event may be a particular pattern in the violet pylons.

It may be the final settling of a distant stone.

It may be a fourth knock.

If awakened, these architectural residents will not emerge from the structures. The structures will become their bodies.

This is perhaps the deepest fact of life in the Stillwater Crown: its inhabitants do not occupy the city in the ordinary sense. They are among the materials from which its continued instant is made. Street, citizen, archive, and law are different arrangements of the same held relation.

They persist by spending their lives elsewhere.

They think through consequences they cannot witness.

They meet one another as a sequence of finished distances.

And in the dark plazas, beneath an ocean that has forgotten motion, they wait—not for time to pass, but for some faraway change to become sufficient to let them take their next position.
