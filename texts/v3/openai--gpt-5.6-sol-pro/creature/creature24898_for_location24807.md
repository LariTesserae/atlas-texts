The first mistake is to look for bodies.

There are bodies, certainly: swifts in the bolt holes, shrimp in the gutters, geckos asleep against warm relays. But these are the chapel’s quick inhabitants. They hatch, feed, mate, and die in the time it takes a planting ledge to move from one window to the next. They live there as birds live in a tree.

What lives *as* the chapel is harder to distinguish.

Old diagrams, where they survive beneath mineral bloom, identify several **mobile inspection trestles**. None resembles the clean, many-jointed devices shown in the drawings. Centuries of repair have altered them beyond any useful taxonomy. Their hinges have been sleeved in ceramic, their cables replaced by braided roots, their sensor heads buried under translucent resin. Moss covers their backs. Ferns grow from their water sumps. Birds nest in recesses that may once have held tools.

They are called vergers, though no one remains who remembers giving them that name.

A verger at rest looks like a section of the wall that has sagged inward. It may remain folded against a rib for years, four or five long limbs nested together beneath curtains of vine. Its extremities end in clamps of differing sizes: delicate three-fingered grips for handling wire, broad magnetic pads for crossing iron plates, hollow ceramic spikes that enter maintenance sockets and drink whatever the tower provides there—current, lubricant, data, or all three.

No two vergers now have the same number of limbs.

Damage is not corrected to a pattern. A severed arm is replaced by whatever process answers first. Maintenance wire may knit a crude new joint. Resin may stiffen it. Roots enter while the resin is soft, lending tension and flexibility. Insects build chambers along its underside. Mineral scale gives it weight. After some decades, the improvised growth becomes capable of movement, though it may bend in a direction no original arm could.

Thus the older vergers are elaborate and asymmetrical. They resemble kneeling scaffolds, winter trees, collapsed cranes, or enormous animals assembled by someone who knew only the purpose of bones.

They move at the speed of the machinery.

One does not see a verger take a step. One notices that a familiar balcony has acquired an extra railing. A week later, the railing is touching a pipe. A month later, it has opened the pipe’s inspection collar and inserted a filament into the steam. By the end of the season, the whole shape has crossed the wall and folded itself around a leaking condenser bank.

Their work is not repair in the old sense. They do not know what the tower was meant to be. They respond to differences: strain, dryness, unwanted heat, a tone gone rough in the bearings. Where plates pull apart, they brace them. Where condensation goes unused, they score channels with mineral-tipped fingers. Where a gutter has become too acidic for shrimp, they lower porous pieces of old ceramic into the water until the balance changes.

Sometimes they make errors that last for generations.

One verger sealed an air duct because it interpreted the vibration as structural distress. The resulting pressure diverted cloud-rain into the eastern gardens, creating the chapel’s only grove of mature trees. Another spent eighty years polishing a dead instrument pedestal, returning to it every tower cycle despite there being no instrument left to read. Its repeated touch wore a deep oval into the metal. Moths now pupate there.

They do not speak through the air. The roar would make such speech impossible, and in any case they have no mouths.

A verger listens by contact. It lays a limb against a column, railing, or floor plate and becomes still. Vibrations pass through its entire frame. Fine rootlets tighten and loosen. Resin membranes flex over buried pickups. The faintest alteration in the tower’s note travels through it as pressure travels through the inner ear.

When several vergers touch the same structure, they share the vibration. Whether this constitutes conversation is uncertain. They may be exchanging information, or they may simply become, for a while, parts of one sensing body.

The signal lamps complicate the question.

A lamp begins pulsing green. A verger uncurls three joints. Amber follows white around the room, and another turns toward the central shaft. Sometimes the animals anticipate these responses: geckos leave their control boxes before the vergers open them; long-legged birds step aside before a limb reaches the columns. The sequence may be an old command language still issuing from buried systems. It may be something the vergers have learned to use. It may no longer be possible to separate the two.

They carry much of the chapel’s smaller life upon them.

Their backs provide stable wet ground in a room where water is otherwise transient. Drainage grooves along their limbs contain miniature streams. Pale fish eggs adhere to the mesh around their joints and remain damp through dry periods. Transparent moths lay on the warm resin over their sensor clusters. The long-legged birds stand on their shoulders when the column winds become violent.

Each mature verger has a particular ecology. One is dark with liverwort and supports a colony of red mites found nowhere else. Another is almost bare, its surfaces kept hot by an electrical fault; geckos gather on it in such numbers that their pale eggs fill its open seams. A third carries a small cork-rooted tree in the basket of its folded back. The tree’s leaves have grown narrow and metallic, and every eleven years, when its bearer reaches the western windows, it produces black fruit full of oxide dust.

The vergers do not appear to eat these growths. Nor do they remove them unless they impede movement. Vegetation has become part of their load-bearing structure, animal nests part of their insulation, fungal mats part of their chemical sensing. A verger stripped clean would probably die, if death is what happens to them.

They are most active during the flowering of the glass reed.

As the reflective vanes open over the circular pond, light passes across sensors that have not seen it for thirty-eight years. The chamber’s pitch alters. One by one, the vergers detach from the walls.

This takes several days.

They gather at the margins of the floor basin, never crossing fully into the central emptiness. Limbs extend over the iron plates. Magnetic feet settle with soft, almost ceremonial knocks. The oldest occupy the inner ring, their frames thick with accumulated repairs; younger, simpler shapes remain near the walls.

The beetles arrive among them.

These beetles spend most of the tower cycle beneath the floor, feeding on insulation and fungal threads. During the flowering they climb the vergers in shining columns. Each carries grains of conductive dust caught in hairs along its abdomen. They enter open seams, cross exposed contacts, and briefly connect circuits that have remained separate since the previous flowering.

Then the lamps burn continuously.

The vergers tremble. Not violently—the motion is scarcely visible—but droplets leap from their moss, and the long vines along their limbs draw taut. Signals pass around the gathered frames, through beetles, floor plates, pond water, roots, wire, and the three descending columns. For several days the distinction between animal, machine, garden, and building ceases to be useful.

This may be how they reproduce.

Afterward, small constructions are sometimes found in the gutter gardens: arrangements of wire, resin, beetle cement, and living root no larger than a hand. Most never move. Some become braces or nests. A few begin, over the course of years, to draw themselves along cable runs toward sources of vibration.

The young are not made whole. They recruit themselves from the chapel.

A first limb may be a root that tightens in response to current. A shell may be supplied by maintenance resin. Geckos leave bones in its cavities; insects line them with paper; mineral water deposits joints around the wire. By the time it can be called a verger, no single system can claim to have constructed it.

Nor is it clear that the vergers are separate creatures.

When one loses a limb, another may carry the severed part away and attach it to itself. A sensor cluster abandoned for a century may wake when roots from a passing verger enter it. Memories, if they possess memories, may be stored in the tower rather than in their bodies—in lamp sequences, scored channels, repeated routes, and habits of repair.

Perhaps there is only one inhabitant here, distributed across every surface that answers pressure with growth.

The swifts are its brief thoughts in the open air. The fish are impulses traveling the runoff grooves. The vergers are hands it assembles when touch is needed. The glass reed is a clock, or an eye, or the organ by which it remembers itself every thirty-eight years.

It does not rule the chapel. It has no plan for it.

It merely receives each breakage and gives it another material. It receives each absence and makes the edges of that absence habitable. It has been doing so for so long that survival and construction have become the same act.

At night, when lightning illuminates the clouds below, the folded vergers stand black against the windows. Their limbs press to the ribs. Their gardens drip. Green and amber signals travel through their leaves.

Seen in those brief flashes, they do not look like priests attending a machine.

They look like the machine, after centuries of learning how to kneel.
