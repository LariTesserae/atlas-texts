At first it seems that the High Drowned Country belongs to plants.

They occupy it with the completeness of an old civilization, though nothing they make is deliberate. The ribbon forests hold the sunward slopes. Fans and cups inherit the ravines. Rooted flotillas pass over the mountaintops, joining and parting according to encounters so slow that a union may outlast the species that began it. Even the empty water is seeded with their dormant forms.

Yet “plant” is only approximately right.

The great ribbons consume light, and their buried holdfasts draw minerals from the young stone, but between those familiar acts they do something stranger. Each blade consists of many thin, nested membranes, with clear water enclosed between them. Fine colored grains travel through these inner layers during the cycle of light. In brightness they ascend toward the gold margins; in dimness they settle back to the olive bases. A whole forest changes its center of weight as the day passes. Its morning posture is not its evening posture. The slight collective inclining of a mountainside is partly current, partly growth, and partly this daily migration within millions of leaves.

When a blade is torn, its membranes divide along predetermined seams. The outer portion becomes a broad, nearly weightless sheet. It may remain suspended for years, turned edgewise to the light, while small root buds form along the tear. Most such sheets perish. A few encounter a mineral grain whose proper altitude matches their own. They close around it and begin the first knot of a drifting meadow.

The floating gardens are therefore not a separate kind of life but the wandering generation of the rooted forests. What looks like an island is a colony carrying a great accumulation of stones, spores, water chambers, and inherited wounds. Its tangled underside contains dark older leaves, some still alive after centuries without strong light. Its upper canopy is young and almost transparent. Between these layers is a dim interior where the country’s scarce animals are most numerous.

The gardens possess no minds, but they remember contact. Where one has brushed a red cliff, new growth emerges rust-colored for many cycles. Where two meadows have interlaced, each continues to produce a few leaves bearing the branching pattern of the other. Their history is written not in rings but in variations of buoyancy. Ancient gardens are vertically deep, with generations arranged one above another at their preferred heights. They resemble forests seen upright, though they have no ground.

Some are old enough to have gathered the skeleton of a peak within themselves.

The mauve fans of the ravines are less passive. Their branching surfaces are covered in pores that open only when distant vibrations cross the water. A falling stone may cause every fan along a cliff to furl in sequence, the motion traveling outward long after the sound has become inaudible. They feed on what the disturbance dislodges: spores, mineral dust, fragments of old membranes. In still periods they open so widely that neighboring colonies overlap, making one continuous velvet wall.

The red cups live among them. The beads they hold are not simply gas but chambers for their young. Within each silver bead, minute transparent bodies turn slowly, grazing on the microorganisms that form its skin. As the bead rises, it passes through different layers of light and pressure, and its occupants change form at each one. They begin as ciliated specks. Higher up they lengthen, become many-legged, then absorb the legs and unfold a pair of clear sails.

If their bead reaches its appointed level, it halts. The young cut their way out and drift downward, contrary to the apparent direction of their bodies. They descend until they touch a red cup, where they dissolve into it and become part of the colony. Thus each cup is both an individual and the gathered remains of many generations of its own offspring.

Some beads never stop. They rise beyond the colored heights where the country’s known life can follow. On rare days, empty skins return from above, flattened and cold.

The pale grazers are the most visible animals, though they are easily mistaken for leaves until they bite.

They are long and thin, with fins extending nearly the entire length of the body. Those fins do not beat. The animals travel by passing waves through the clear tissue between their fin rays, so that the surrounding water seems to move them of its own accord. Their flesh contains little pigment. Green or gold from the forest shows through them, and when they cross bare rock they assume its black, rust, or bone-pale cast.

Each grazer carries a crescent mouth on one side of its head. The other side bears a smooth sensory hollow, sensitive to pressure, heat, and the minute changes in weight that mark the country’s invisible buoyancy boundaries. Because of this asymmetry they feed in one direction around a plant, always cutting the same-handed sequence of arcs. A ribbon leaf may preserve a grazer’s meal for decades as a ladder of pale crescents.

They do not eat the leaf itself. They open its outer membrane and drink the dense, mineral-bearing water enclosed within. The wound seals after them. A heavily grazed forest becomes more buoyant and may slowly lift its roots from the mountain, beginning the long transformation into a floating meadow.

The grazers are born in the hollow green stalks that arch across the ravines. The stalks fill with warm water from fissures at their bases. Adults enter through openings near the crowns and release chains of soft eggs, which descend inside the plant until each reaches the level suited to it. The larvae remain there, held at different heights within the same stalk. A change in the mountain’s heat releases them. Hundreds may emerge in one quiet outpouring, though few survive the first crossing to the ribbon forest.

Nothing appears to hunt them.

Nevertheless, they startle. Whole schools can disappear into vegetation before any visible danger arrives. At such times they do not flee horizontally. Each seeks its own layer and stops there, becoming a row of pale, motionless marks suspended through the forest’s depth. Only much later does the dark shape pass overhead.

The broad wanderer is not common enough to be considered part of ordinary life. There may be only one. There may be several moving at such distances and intervals that they have never entered one another’s range.

Seen from below, it has no stable outline. Sometimes it resembles a single enormous disk, black at the center and translucent around the margins. Sometimes it appears divided into lobes, like a drifting leaf with long tears. On the rare occasions when it turns through the light, ranges and forests can be seen faintly through it. Its darkness comes not from pigment but from thickness: layer upon layer of clear tissue, each bending the light a little until the combined body casts night.

It has neither visible eyes nor mouth. It does not graze, seize, filter, or root. It passes without disturbing suspended sediment, but several moments afterward the plants beneath it bend in the direction it has gone. The delay is always the same, regardless of depth.

Along its underside hang small mineral bodies embedded in transparent flesh. Green stones cluster toward the margin, red ones inward, black ones nearest the center. They are the same nodules that support the drifting gardens. Whether the wanderer gathers these stones or produces them is uncertain. Sometimes, after it crosses a floating meadow, that meadow begins to lose altitude. Sometimes a new nodule appears in open water weeks later, clothed in a film of living gold.

It may be an animal. It may be a mobile phase of the meadows, assembled when many ancient colonies exchange parts. It may be the country’s way of transporting stone from one height to another.

There are lesser creatures that never emerge into open view.

Thin, colorless borers inhabit fresh fracture faces. They move through stone more readily than through water, following mineral veins with bodies as narrow as cracks. Their passage leaves glass-smooth threads in the rock. When a cliff breaks, thousands may be exposed at once, shining briefly before they retreat into the new surfaces. The branching galleries they make are often the first places spores take hold. Every forest rooted to bare stone begins in paths opened by these hidden animals.

Inside the white fronds of lava tubes live soft bells that contract once during each passage from light to dimness. Their contractions are not synchronized nearby, but across great distances the pattern repeats. A tube on one mountain may pulse at the same instant as another beyond three submerged ranges. No connection has been found except the water itself.

There are also the dustlike lives coating the halted bubbles. Individually they are little more than films with cilia. Together they give a bubble weight, color, and eventually direction. A mature colony can lower its bubble by consuming the gas within, then raise it by releasing gas of another composition. Such bubbles migrate along level horizons, carrying spores between isolated peaks. The long silver constellations are herds of these colonies during their resting phase.

When two constellations meet, their bubbles touch and merge. For a time a single vast mirror may hang between the mountains, reflecting inverted forests and fractured summits. It slowly darkens as the colonies multiply across it. Then it separates into a thousand small spheres and disperses.

The quietest inhabitants live in the boundaries themselves.

At certain blue bands, where a seed cannot descend and a dead leaf cannot rise, there are layers only a finger’s breadth thick in which the water has a faint gelatinous coherence. They are nearly invisible until something presses against them. Spores collect there. Bubbles flatten beneath them. Small animals leave temporary furrows as they cross.

These layers are alive.

They have no fixed edge and no continuous body in the usual sense. Their substance is distributed through miles of water, condensed only at one favored altitude. They feed on differences: warm below and cool above, mineral-rich on one side and clear on the other, rising gas opposed to sinking dust. They maintain the distinctions from which they eat. The country’s law of levels may therefore be less a law than the labor of immense, diffuse organisms.

A layer can be wounded. A rockfall passing through it leaves a circular opening that takes years to close. Around such wounds, seeds cross into forbidden depths and leaves fall the wrong way. New communities appear beneath them, brief and misplaced. When the living boundary heals, these foreign groves are stranded outside their natural altitude. Most fade. A few alter themselves and remain.

Perhaps this is how the High Drowned Country becomes more various without becoming crowded.

Nothing there multiplies quickly except in response to some rare opening. Life waits in folds, in cups, in stone threads, in the sealed water of leaves. It can remain dormant longer than a mountain face remains bare. When change comes—a fracture, a migrating meadow, a wound in one of the blue layers—the waiting forms emerge almost at once, giving the impression that abundance preceded opportunity.

Deep in the newest ravines, where the rock still gives off a gentle heat, there are places even the rooted forest avoids. Clear mats cover the black stone. They contain no green pigment and make no gas. By day they are almost indistinguishable from the water. During the broad dimness, however, pale lines pass through them, joining and dividing like cracks of light beneath ice.

The lines gather around warm fissures. They persist for an hour, sometimes two, then disappear before full brightness returns.

The mats may be feeding. They may be growing. They may be exchanging some signal through the mountain. No creature approaches them while they shine, not even the hidden borers. Once the light has gone, fresh mineral crystals are often found standing on the rock, already coated with the first translucent spores.

So the country belongs neither to forests nor animals, and perhaps not even to discrete bodies.

It belongs to states of suspension: to lives that are also places, to offspring that become chambers, to colonies broad as horizons, to animals transparent enough to borrow the colors of stone. Almost everything inhabiting it spends part of its existence rooted and another part adrift. Almost everything carries a piece of the mountain or a pocket of the water within itself. Even death does not produce a simple descent. The dead separate into portions, each claimed by a different altitude.

A ribbon’s green middle settles among the cliffs. Its gold edge rises toward the gardens. Its dense holdfast sinks into a ravine. Clear inner membranes remain where they are, folding slowly into shapes the smallest creatures use as shelter.

Nothing is wasted, but neither is anything hurried back into life.

Across the warm drowned ranges, existence lingers where the water permits it: forests leaning under unfelt currents, grazers sleeping at their chosen levels, living boundaries stretched from peak to peak, and high above them the broad dark wanderer carrying its little constellation of stones.

If there is awareness in the country, it is not gathered in any one of these beings. It lies distributed among them—in the fans opening to a distant fall, in the meadows remembering cliffs by color, in the frightened grazers vanishing before the shadow arrives. The whole elevated sea appears to notice slowly.

And what it notices, it keeps for a very long time.
