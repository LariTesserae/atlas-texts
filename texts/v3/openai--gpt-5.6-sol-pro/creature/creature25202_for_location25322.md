At first, what lives there seems to be birds.

They occupy the visible world in such numbers that the islands appear to shed them. Guillemots stand shoulder to shoulder on the narrowest ledges, each facing outward as if appointed to watch a different portion of the sea. Puffins vanish into turf burrows above cliffs where no land animal could approach them. Gannets pass with the grave, deliberate speed of larger creatures, then fold themselves into white spears and strike the channels. Storm petrels emerge toward dusk, small and erratic over water immense enough to erase them.

The birds know the drowned shape of the mountains. Their colonies are not merely on cliffs but above convergences: submerged saddles where currents lift fish toward the light, narrow cuts where shoals are compressed, outer walls where deep water rises against stone. When the tide turns, whole ledges empty. The air fills, the sea stipples with impacts, and for a time the hidden topography can be read in wings.

Lower down live the seals, though “lower” changes hourly. They inhabit a border that appears and disappears: weed-slick skerries, cave mouths, slabs exposed briefly between tides. At low water they lie in gray heaps, scarred adults among pale, heavy-bodied young. At high water the resting place is gone and the seals become inhabitants of the drowned range. They pass through kelp as easily as birds pass between trees, folding themselves around boulders, hanging upright in the current, rising to inspect boats with dark, unhurried eyes.

Otters keep to more private districts. Their paths begin in wet heath and end underwater. They den beneath birch roots, in rock cavities above the highest ordinary tide, or among the softened ruins of walls. At dawn one may be seen carrying a crab into a cove, rolling onto its back where the swell is gentlest. It eats while turning slowly among the weed, as though the cove itself were revolving around it. Its trails cross old human tracks without distinction. A flooded lane, a drainage ditch and a natural channel are all simply routes between shelter and food.

But the greater population lives below sight.

Kelp is the first of it: not a fringe but a forest, rooted over entire slopes. The plants rise from ancient rock on muscular stems and spread their blades near the surface, where they make a false ground of bronze and black. In calm weather this upper canopy gleams like oil. In rough weather it heaves, opens and shuts, revealing momentary shafts into green depth.

Young fish live among the blades. Pollock flicker there in loose schools; wrasse work close to the rock; pipefish hold themselves nearly vertical and imitate fragments of weed. Snails graze the fronds. Crabs climb the stems. Small crustaceans occupy scars, folds and holdfasts, and are eaten in turn by fish that will later leave the forest for open channels. Every plant is itself a crowded place, furred with hydroids and bryozoans, carrying eggs, larvae and grazing mouths too small to notice individually.

Near the bottom, lobsters inhabit the mountain’s old wounds. They reverse into glacial cracks and wait with their claws presented toward the entrance. Conger eels use deeper fractures. Octopuses take possession of collapsed walls and discarded machinery, drawing shells into pale middens around their doors. An old turbine housing, if left long enough, ceases to look abandoned. Anemones gather along its rim. Mussels seal its seams. A crab shelters behind a bolt head, and fish pass through apertures intended for cables.

Where current strikes hardest, life grows low and stubborn. Barnacles, limpets and encrusting organisms flatten themselves against stone. In deeper ravines, away from the tearing force of the surface, bodies become softer and stranger: sponges with branching forms, pale corals, brittle stars, anemones opening into black water. They feed on what the tide brings through the drowned passes. The current is their weather and their harvest.

Fish inhabit the range as migrating nations. Herring enter in silver masses that alter the behavior of everything around them. Saithe gather beneath headlands. Mackerel cross the sounds in summer, quick and metallic, pursued from below by larger fish and from above by birds. Cod move nearer the bottom, among boulder fields and submerged foundations. Their routes are older than the piers, older than the drowned buildings, perhaps older than the present islands. They follow temperature, salinity, food and the contours of valleys no human has walked in thousands of years.

Porpoises often announce themselves only by breath: a brief black back, a soft exhalation, absence. They learn the schedules of ferries and travel the pressure ridges alongside their hulls. Dolphins are less discreet. They arrive as a disturbance, turning a quiet channel into arcs and splashes, driving fish against a submerged ridge. Whales pass farther out, though their voices enter the inner waters. Heard through a boat’s hull, a call seems less like the cry of an animal than a property of the sea itself—something the water has remembered how to say.

Then there are the people.

There are not many. The place does not support crowds easily, and those who remain tend to be distributed in small settlements turned inward from the weather. Houses gather at the heads of protected inlets, often above older foundations whose lowest courses are now reached by spring tides. Their walls are thick. Roofs are low and tied down. Doors open on the lee side. Sheds are built with the expectation that salt water will eventually enter them, and important electrical fittings are mounted higher than seems necessary.

Every inhabited island has its own arrangement of practical specialists, though one person may be several of them. The ferry operator may also serve on the rescue crew. The mechanic repairs generators, winches, outboard motors and kitchen pumps. The keeper of the weather station may record seabirds, relay medical calls and maintain the light on an outer rock. Fishers know how to splice cable; turbine crews know where to set lobster pots without losing them. Expertise overlaps because isolation punishes narrow usefulness.

They are compactly dressed people, not in body but in habit. Little hangs loose. Hoods tighten close around the face. Knives and radios are tethered. Gloves are carried even in summer. They step aboard by timing the rise of the boat rather than by trusting the pier, and they do not leap casually between wet surfaces. A visitor watches the distance; they watch the movement.

Their knowledge is full of distinctions invisible to outsiders. They know which patch of rough water is only wind against tide and which conceals a standing wave. They recognize the pale drag of sand in a channel that was clear the previous week and infer that something has fallen from a submerged slope. They can smell weather before it arrives, not mystically but from peat, diesel, kelp, cold metal and the change in the wind. They speak of currents as if they were roads with tempers: one safe only on the last of the ebb, another deceptive in calm, another capable of turning a disabled boat broadside before an anchor can bite.

Children learn the place vertically. They are taught not merely where they may go, but at what state of tide and under which wind. A rock pool is a playground until the water begins to fill the cut behind it. A path to a neighboring cove may cease to exist on the return. The first lessons are blunt: cold water steals breath; kelp catches ankles; cliffs crumble at the rim; never stand inside the loop of a working rope. Later come subtler teachings—the shape of pressure on the sea, the silence before fog, the way a line of resting gulls can reveal shelter.

Old people carry obsolete geographies. They remember jetties before storms shortened them, channels before rockfall narrowed them, houses still occupied on islands now visited only in summer. Their place-names preserve things no longer visible: the meadow beneath an eelgrass basin, the ford under the ferry route, the church field where divers now find dressed stone. Some names refer to landmarks that have been underwater for generations, yet remain useful because the water above them still behaves differently.

The working population comes and goes with machinery. Maintenance crews sleep in prefabricated quarters beside converter stations, their windows small and salt-frosted. Divers descend along cables into green dusk, carrying lamps that illuminate only fragments: a rotor blade passing with immense restraint, kelp caught on a brace, pale hands of anemones opening along a weld. They work by touch when silt closes the water. Above them, a supervisor tracks depth, air and current time, counting down the narrow interval before the pass becomes too violent.

The divers say the drowned buildings feel smaller than expected. A farm wall under twenty meters of water is not grand or haunted. It is a row of stones with fish above it. A millrace is a channel within a greater channel. Roof slates lie among shells. Doorways lead nowhere because the rooms have lost their roofs and the sea occupies all sides equally. Yet human geometry persists. Straight edges remain uncanny in a landscape rounded by ice and water. Lobsters live beneath thresholds. Shoals turn inside roofless rooms. The drowned structures have not become empty; they have changed tenancy.

Solitude also lives there, but seldom silence. A lighthouse keeper lying awake hears impact transmitted through the rock: wave, wave, wave, then a heavier concussion from some outer face. A person in a moored boat hears chains drag and tighten below. In houses above the inlets, crockery trembles when the ferry passes or when swell enters at the right angle. Even inland, if any place on the islands can be called inland, the sea continues through walls as vibration.

Nothing living there is allowed to forget attachment.

Trees grow by gripping shallow pockets of soil, their roots entering cracks and old masonry. Mussels fasten themselves with threads. Kelp holds to rock with structures like many-fingered hands. Limpets seal against stone. Birds return to exact ledges. Seals seek the same disappearing skerries. People bolt towers into gneiss, chain boats to enormous rings and run cables through armored conduits.

Those that cannot attach must keep moving. Fish travel with the tide rather than against it. Porpoises use the wakes of vessels. Birds ride the updrafts. Boats wait for slack water. Even the whales choose passages according to the moving architecture of current.

So the inhabitants are divided less into land creatures and sea creatures than into holders and travelers.

The holders cling.

The travelers learn the openings.

And nearly everything is both.
