At first, nothing that lives there appears to be in command of it.

The bright fish around the intakes are no more than slivers. They have transparent skulls and mirrored flanks, and inside each skull a small black chain of sensory knots trembles to the pressure-song of the trunk. They do not see the intake mouths so much as hear their shape. When a valve begins its mineral click, the shoal divides before it, opening a precise corridor for the expelled water. When the valve closes, they fill the corridor again. Their formation is neither learned nor decided. It is another moving part of the plant.

In the curtains of fine roots live soft-bodied grazers, flattened until light passes through them. Their mouths travel independently across their undersides, cropping bacterial films in parallel lines. They leave the root hairs clean, but never cleaner than before: while one mouth removes a film, another organ lays down the mineral and mucus from which the film reforms. Seen closely, each grazer is both eater and pasture.

The clicking crustaceans are darker and more heavily made. Iron passes through their food into their shells, plating their claws in blue-black metal. Some are small enough to inhabit the pores of the bark. Others straddle whole root cables on stilted legs and carry gardens of pale anemones along their backs. They fight constantly. Claws break, armor splits, one animal drives another from a feeding pit—and afterward the broken claw is whole, the shell closes, and both contestants return by separate routes to the places they occupied before. Their violence has no victor. Perhaps it has no purpose beyond contributing its sharp percussion to the trunk’s chord.

Higher up are the rays.

They hang beneath the crowns in immense, orderly congregations, each ray suspended at its own depth as if attached there by an invisible cord. Their wings are thin and dark enough to resemble loose leaves. They scarcely move. Water drawn toward the leaf pores passes through lattices in their mouths, feeding them a continuous abundance of plankton and mineral-rich threads.

Small animals live inside those lattices. Smaller ones live inside the small animals. The inward sequence continues almost beyond sight: chambers within gills, ciliated orchards within chambers, jewel-like parasites within the orchards. None overwhelms its host. Nothing is cured of anything. Disease exists here only as a permanent relationship.

In the open intervals drift the glass bodies. Some are bells, some ribbons, some nearly invisible spheres tens of meters across. Their organs are not gathered into a central mass. A red digestive star hangs in one portion, a ring of blue reproductive beads in another, a loose silver nervous net throughout. They look less like animals than like diagrams of animals, their functions separated and displayed.

They rise by day and sink by night, though rise and sink are misleading words. At every hour there are equal numbers traveling in either direction. They exchange depths without changing the population of any layer. Predators pass through them with mouths open, gathering thousands, while behind those mouths the same density of glass bodies remains.

Among them swim the needle whales.

Their length is difficult to judge because their pale backs vanish into the water long before their tails come into view. The adults may be hundreds of meters long, yet they are so narrow that, head-on, one seems no larger than a white scar. Their fins have become steering blades. They do not breathe at the surface. Long folded membranes within their bodies extract dissolved gases from the trunk outflows, and colonies of green cells beneath their skin feed them when they pass through light.

Each pod follows a closed route between several columns. One whale may spend years completing the circuit, calling ahead in a tone that passes through root, rock, and open sea. The answer is always waiting at the next column. Calves are born along one reach of the route; old whales die along another. No traveler has ever found either reach empty.

Their bodies do not fall when they die. They descend slowly until they meet the upward pressure of a cold vent and remain there, level in the blue. Root filaments enter them. Scavengers open them. Their bones darken with manganese and become resonant bars on which currents play. Far elsewhere on the circuit, a calf assembles in the womb from matter returned through the trunks.

But there are also inhabitants who notice.

They are easy to mistake for folds of the vegetation. They live where the vertical trunks divide into the undersides of the crowns, in the green half-light among ribs, buoyancy chambers, and streaming water. Their bodies are long and jointed, with four grasping limbs arranged around a flexible central axis. There is no permanent front to them. Any limb can lead; any side can become the side that faces the light.

Their skin is composed of overlapping translucent plates. Beneath it, dark vessels branch like writing. Along the edges of their limbs grow sensory fringes that taste salinity, voltage, strain in the leaf tissue, and the approach of distant calls. What might be called their faces are circular fields of fine apertures surrounding a pale, contractile eye. The eye sees light. The apertures see water.

They have no fixed color. A slow current of pigments travels beneath their plates: green in crown-shadow, silver in open water, rust-red beside mineral valves. These changes are not concealment alone. They are speech.

A thought passes across one of them visibly. Doubt clouds the joints violet. Recognition kindles in rings from limb to limb. Attention appears as a narrow white line turning toward its object. When several gather, their bodies answer one another in tides of color until the whole group resembles a single many-petaled creature opening and closing beneath the leaves.

They make no tools. A sharp edge cut from shell returns to the shell. A length of fiber coils back into living bark. Stones refuse the displacement required to become hammer or wall. The inhabitants can grasp, braid, stack, pierce, and carve, but the result survives only while their bodies continue to impose it. Release a knot and the strands slip backward through the motions of tying. Take a hand from a carved mark and the groove fills with unabraded surface.

For this reason, they carry their possessions as gestures.

A tool is a remembered arrangement of hands. A dwelling is a formation of bodies held around a flow of water. A door is a sequence of swimmers who turn aside for those displaying the proper colors. What another species might preserve in objects, they preserve in posture, route, and rhythm.

When they need a chamber, several hundred cling to one another under a leaf shelf, overlapping their plates until they form a living dome. Warmth and filtered water gather inside. Sleeping young are passed from limb to limb through the dome’s interior. When the sleepers wake, the chamber disperses, leaving no evidence that a shelter existed.

Their nurseries occupy the quiet pockets between gas bladders. Their dead are carried to the mineral belts of the trunks, not because burial changes what follows, but because carrying them is part of the relation between the living and the dead. The body is opened by the same pale scavengers at the same depth. Its substances enter the same ducts. At a corresponding place elsewhere, an infant is born.

No population rises. No lineage disappears.

They know this.

Their oldest knowledge is not that death comes, but that no death can make the world less full. They do not fear extinction. They fear interchangeability.

To be forgotten is the nearest thing they have to annihilation, because the sea will preserve no trace on their behalf. There are no graves, ruins, heirlooms, damaged places, accumulated bones, or altered landscapes. Nothing outside a mind can testify that a particular life occurred. Thus they attend to one another with an intensity that can seem ceremonial even in ordinary encounters.

Each individual has many names. One is a route through the crowns. One is a pattern of color. One is a pressure rhythm tapped against another’s plates. One is a sequence of positions in a group swim. To remember someone fully is to be able to reproduce all these names.

Their historians are called—if the translation can be made—those-who-do-not-release. They travel in slow circuits with the needle whales and carry genealogies in synchronized pulses of muscle. A history may require forty bodies to recite. One remembers the births, one the deaths, seven hold the cadence, others embody places by orienting themselves toward distant trunks. If a reciter dies, another takes the missing position at the same moment. The account does not pause.

Yet their histories cannot grow without limit. Memory, too, belongs to the equilibrium.

For every name taken up, another loses detail. An ancestor once recalled in the exact blue of her greeting becomes merely a turn in a route; later the turn becomes part of a general migration; at last it is indistinguishable from geography. The inhabitants debate whether this is forgetting or completion. Some say the dead are erased. Some say they become the pattern by which everyone moves.

There are no new inventions among them, but there are discoveries.

An individual may notice a relation no one near them has noticed: that a whale’s seventh call coincides with the opening of a rain territory, or that a crustacean’s struggle repeats the rhythm of a deep pump. For a while this knowledge travels. Bodies flash it to bodies. Swimmers alter their formations to express it. The discovery may cross a hundred kilometers and occupy generations of attention.

But it never becomes an institution, a device, or a permanent advantage. Eventually it is folded into an older pattern, or exchanged for some other understanding of equal weight. The total shape of their knowledge remains constant even while its contents move through it.

They do not think of the vegetation as machinery. Nor do they think of it as a collection of organisms. Their closest conception is a body too large to possess an outside.

They enter its ducts to be carried upward. They sleep against the membranes of its gas chambers and feel the compression cycles through their plates. They clean mineral valves, though the valves would remain equally clean without them. They guide fish from one channel to another, though the fish would arrive there regardless. They place their limbs against the great trunk and add minute beats to its thunder.

Whether the vegetation is conscious is a question they cannot separate from the question of whether the sea is conscious. Signals travel through the columns. Crowns exchange electrical changes through the water and the plateau’s mineral seams. A disturbance at one rim appears as a correction hundreds of kilometers away. The system responds to everything, but never with surprise.

Some inhabitants believe the whole high sea is one mind holding a single thought without beginning or conclusion. Individual creatures are, in this belief, small variations within its attention: not children or servants, but points at which the thought feels itself.

Others deny that anything incapable of change can think. To them, consciousness exists only in the brief interior between an action and its erasure. They value interruption—the instant of a displaced frond, a cloud of lifted silt, a body turned from its appointed route—even knowing the world will close behind it.

These dissenters swim farther into the empty water than others do.

They leave the crowns and columns until the industrial chord becomes smooth and directionless. There they perform elaborate acts with no witnesses: spirals, reversals, sudden stillness, colors displayed to vacant blue. They cannot leave a mark. They cannot alter a migration or begin a settlement. The water accepts every gesture and retains none.

Then they return.

Perhaps they always return by the same path. Perhaps each journey is new to the one who makes it. The place supplies no evidence either way.

When seen from afar, a gathering of these people can look like a temporary organ budding from the crown: thousands of plated bodies linked in a luminous chain, conducting messages between leaf, rain, and trunk. Up close, every one of them is distinct, watching the others, laboring against perfect restoration to remain particular.

They live in abundance without possession, safety without progress, and time without record.

Around them, rays hang beneath the leaves. Iron-clawed grazers click along the roots. Glass animals exchange the upper and lower waters. The needle whales pass on their ancient circuits. The trunks pump, the crowns drink, and the drowned mountains hold their final shapes.

Nothing the inhabitants do changes the sea.

What they do changes only who has witnessed it—and for them, that is the whole narrow territory in which a life can occur.
