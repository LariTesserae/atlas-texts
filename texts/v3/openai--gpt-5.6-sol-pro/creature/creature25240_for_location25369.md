The people of the basin are not silent by nature. Silence is something they are taught, fitted to the body as carefully as a raincloak.

Infants are carried against the chest in deep wraps of moss-felt and hollow skin, with polished ear-stones sewn between the layers. When the stones begin to tremble, the bearer lays a hand over the child’s ribs. Long before they understand danger, children learn that pressure means stillness. A palm on the breast means hold your breath. Two fingers at the throat mean swallow your cry. A touch between the shoulder blades means something is listening.

Consequently, the townspeople grow into an unusual physical composure. They place each foot completely before shifting their weight. They do not kick loose stones from their path. They catch dropped objects against a hip or thigh rather than let them strike the ground. Even surprise travels inward: widened eyes, a tightening beneath the jaw, fingers spread briefly against the chest.

Their feet are broad and usually bare. The soles become sensitive rather than calloused, trained to read the town through stone. Through them they feel the faint stamping of roof grazers, water entering a covered cistern, a neighbor approaching on the other side of a wall. They can distinguish the quick, light patter of harmless shell-creatures from the deliberate testing touch of a hollow’s limbs. Indoors, where floors are laid over ear-stones, whole households may wake at once without any audible alarm.

They are slight people on the whole, though not frail. Heavy bodies fare poorly on ladders, living roofs, and streets that may crack open overnight. Their strength lies in the hands, back, and toes. Gardeners climb suspended vines as easily as stairs. Builders brace themselves across fissures, one foot on either wall, and work for hours with hide-wrapped tools. Children move through roof trees before they are trusted near the open rim.

Their clothing blurs the distinction between garment and equipment. Short layered tunics are woven from bark fiber, silk, bird down, and hair gathered from the pale grazers. Over these they wear sleeveless cloaks made from the cured skins of hollows. The skins retain something of the animal’s talent: wet, they darken like stone; dry, they take on the muted green of moss. A person sitting still beneath hanging leaves can vanish almost as thoroughly as the predator from which the cloth was taken.

Bright color is worn on the inside. Hems, cuffs, and collars open to reveal dyes of copper green, clay white, reservoir black, flower yellow, and the rare violent red made from beetle cases. Outdoors, gestures expose these hidden colors in quick, exact flashes.

The basin’s language has two forms.

The spoken language is used in padded rooms, during hard rain, in the quarry when the mountain is groaning, and at the open platform above the cloud when scouts have declared the air clear. It is a rich language, full of imitations: the plink of cistern water, the soft collapse of moss under a knee, the felt rhythm of animals stamping through stone. Its speakers enjoy long stories and complicated jokes. After hours of restraint, they can be astonishingly talkative.

Outside, they use hands, faces, posture, and colored linings. A lifted wrist asks whether a path is safe. Fingers drawn downward mean mist is rising. A closed hand beside the ear warns of a possible hollow; the same hand opened slowly means the vibration has passed. Neighborhood signs differ slightly, and misunderstandings are common enough to be funny only once everyone is indoors.

Names are rarely spoken in the street. Each person has a motion-name, usually derived from some characteristic noticed in childhood: Fern-Unfolding, Three Drops, Bird-Under-Stair, Crooked Root, Copper Flash. Formal names are kept for contracts, mourning, and songs sung under rain.

No one lives entirely by one trade. The basin changes too often for that. A potter may also tend roof grazers, inspect drainage channels, and take a weekly watch at the bell cords. A surgeon grafts vines because the same knowledge of joining flesh applies to both. Children are apprenticed not to masters but to relationships: roof and root, insect and flower, cistern and gutter, predator and lure.

The most honored residents are not rulers but morning readers. Before the cloud lifts, they walk the town with white clay, bundles of colored fiber, and their bare feet. They inspect every shift that occurred in darkness. They note a wall displaced by half a finger, a new crack carrying warmth, a moth colony that has abandoned one eave for another. At the map platform they unbraid yesterday’s certainties.

Their authority lasts only until the next alteration.

Government is conducted by those whose responsibilities meet at a problem. If a vine threatens a roof, the people concerned may include the householder, the vine-grafter, the keeper of the cistern fed by that roof, the family whose silk-worms shelter in the vine, and the watcher responsible for keeping a hollow trail open beneath it. Ownership matters less than consequence. A person may possess a house but not the water falling from it, the roots supporting it, or the passage beneath its stairs.

They have an old legal phrase: **Nothing ends at its skin.**

It applies equally to buildings, animals, water, and people.

The human population is smaller than the town appears to require. Many apparent households are storehouses, hatcheries, drying rooms, or shelters deliberately left for other species. Some doors are built too narrow for an adult person but exactly wide enough for velvet birds. Low stone arches at garden edges belong to the pale grazers. Warm niches behind cook walls are leased, in effect, to frogs, which eat moths and announce changes in heat by changing color.

The townspeople do not think of these animals as tame. Tameness would imply that the animals had surrendered their own arrangements. Instead, they are neighbors, workers, harvests, warnings, pests, food, and sometimes citizens of a limited kind.

The pale roof grazers prune seedlings and provide hair, milk, dung, and—when one dies—translucent horn. They are gentle until their birthing season, when they claim market stalls and refuse to move. The townspeople suspend commerce above them on rope platforms.

The velvet birds consume beetles from gutters. Each flock sleeps beneath the same staircase for generations, and property disputes occasionally hinge on whether rebuilding would disturb an ancestral roost. Their courtship is copied in children’s dances: heads bowed, crests lifted one after another like a wave passing through dark grass.

The wall frogs are watched closely. Their colors form a living bulletin. Silver at the throat means cold mist. Green-black limbs mean tremors. A sudden communal paling means that something large has moved nearby. Frog-keepers know individuals by the branching vessels visible through their bellies, and can identify which moth species are hatching by what glows inside them.

The silk-worms are both wealth and calendar. Different lineages spin under different conditions—some when the stone warms, some in the first cold after rain, some only near copper-bearing walls. Their frames hang everywhere, giving the streets the appearance of unfinished weaving. Fine silk becomes surgical thread and map fiber. Coarse silk binds roofs, splints limbs, and catches condensation.

Then there are the less understood residents.

Seedpods crawl at night on pale rootlets. Many are tolerated; some are redirected with rings of ash. The many-eyed lichens are never scraped from inhabited trees. It is unclear what they eat, but gardens watched by them suffer fewer infestations. Invisible creatures pass overhead in the mist, large enough for water to outline a spine, a folded limb, once the suggestion of an enormous jaw. No one has found one dead.

Children invent names for them. Adults do not.

The hollows occupy a more exact place in the town’s understanding. They are feared without being treated as evil. Evil, the basin people say, chooses what it does. A hollow merely answers sound.

Young hollows are almost delicate: root-thin things that feed on frogs and padded insects. As they age, their limbs lengthen and their skin becomes increasingly adept at imitation. An old hollow pressed against masonry can reproduce not only the color of stone but its cracks, moss, mineral sheen, even the white measurement lines painted by the readers. This is why no one trusts a wall diagram until they have watched it through several breaths.

Hollow-watchers train by studying absence. A patch of moss with no shell trails. A vine whose leaves do not drip. Frogs facing away from what seems to be empty stone. They carry no bells themselves, only hooked knives for releasing distant cords. If a hollow enters a populated lane, the watcher signals everyone to become part of the architecture. Then a rim bell is cut loose.

The creature unfolds.

Witnesses struggle afterward to describe the motion. What seemed to be a wall acquires depth; what seemed to be roots find joints. The vertical mouth opens without revealing a head. It crosses the street faster than a thrown spear, following the bell’s clay notes toward the cloud.

Sometimes the lure fails. Every family knows what to do then, though no one rehearses with actual sound. They scatter in silence while one person creates a deliberate noise elsewhere. Those who take this role are not always warriors. Often they are older people who know the paths best. Their names are braided into the map platform afterward, not at the place of death, but along the route by which everyone else escaped.

Death in the basin is understood as a redistribution of shelter.

Bodies are not buried; there is too little soil, and too much beneath it is hungry. Nor are they burned, because timber is structural and smoke attracts high mist-scavengers. The dead are wrapped in coarse silk and laid in warm fissure chambers with cultivated beetles, fungi, and root tips. Bones are later removed. Some become tools, but most are ground into garden terraces or set beneath new trees.

A person may eventually support the roof beneath which their descendants sleep. This is not metaphorical, and therefore does not need to be said sentimentally.

Their celebrations occur during rain, when noise is safest. Doors are opened. People strike bare hands against drums, sing formal names, laugh loudly, hammer copper, and hang spoons from roof edges so the whole basin rings. Children shout simply to experience distance returning to them as echoes. Lovers quarrel. Public arguments are saved for storms, with disputants standing under separate gutters and raising their voices into the downpour.

When the rain stops, so do they—sometimes in the middle of a word.

Warm season transforms everyone. Sleep becomes a rationed resource. Bodies shine with plant sap, mud, insect scales, and white clay. Gardeners cut vines from doors in the morning and find them sealed again by evening. Frog-keepers carry animals by the basketful. Larvae are sorted between reservoir partitions with fine shell sieves. People eat constantly while working: roasted grubs, fern hearts, grazer curd wrapped in leaves, soft-bodied beetles salted with copper-green crystals.

This season is remembered as abundance only after it ends. While it is happening, it feels like siege.

Births among the people are more common just after the ground cools, when predators withdraw and food remains plentiful. Children belong first to a household, then increasingly to a stair, a cistern district, and a roof network. They learn the map by tracing colored braids before they learn to count. Their earliest games involve remaining motionless while one child, blindfolded, tries to locate the others by vibration.

Adolescents prove adulthood by making something that can fail safely. A wall with a clean sacrificial seam. A cistern lip that spills away from drinking water. A silk frame that releases rather than tears loose in high wind. Permanence is not admired. The basin mistrusts anything that claims it will never move.

This shapes their faces as much as silence does. They are attentive people, but not solemn ones. Their humor favors reversals, substitutions, and things mistaken for other things: a grandfather mistaken for a hollow; a courtship gesture accidentally made to a frog; a map braid followed faithfully until it leads into a newly opened fissure. They distrust heroic certainty. The fool in their stories is usually the one who insists that a wall is still where it was yesterday.

What they believe about the mountain differs from house to house.

Some say it is only young stone, cooling and settling according to pressures too large for people to understand. Some say the basin is the socket of a buried eye. Some say the warm seasons are breaths and the tremors are dreams. Others believe the mountain is not a single life but a congregation: stone, heat, roots, eggs, water, and all the bodies above them temporarily agreeing to rise together.

No doctrine has prevailed, perhaps because the mountain keeps interrupting.

What unites the inhabitants is a practical reverence for connection. They do not divide the town into wilderness and habitation. A house is a cliff modified to admit sleep. A garden is a migration route persuaded to linger. A cistern is mist given memory. The human beings are neither masters of this arrangement nor trespassers within it. They are one of the creatures that have learned to live on the basin’s brief surfaces.

Seen in mist, they can appear almost insubstantial: dark cloaks under leaves, hands flashing pale instructions, bare feet pausing over stone. Yet the signs of them are everywhere. A ceramic bowl placed beneath a root. A crack marked in white. A stair built around a sleeping flock. A bell cord running outward to the rim.

At dusk, when something large turns unseen above the cloud, the whole population becomes still together.

The frogs darken. The birds lower their crests. Grazers freeze among roof trees. Human hands settle over children’s ribs. Beneath the floors, hollow bones and ear-stones begin to tremble.

For several breaths there are no separate households, no trades, no tame and wild creatures—only a basin full of bodies withholding sound.

Then the vibration passes.

A drop of water falls into clay.

The town begins again.
