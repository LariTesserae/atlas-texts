What lives there is easy to mistake for the groves.

They are the only things that open and close, feed, propagate, and answer the passage of light. Up close, each ribbon is built from overlapping scales of carbon, copper, and clear mineral, so thin that sunlight enters one edge and leaves by another. No cell divides within them. No fluid circulates. Their branching is less like growth than the repeated solution of an equation: wherever two unequal conditions meet, a new fork appears between them.

A grove is not a colony of separate plants. All its fronds are exposed portions of one buried circuit. Beneath the terrace, roots flatten into hair-thin films that can extend far beyond the visible stand, crossing under vacant ground and sometimes passing beneath other groves without joining them. What appears aboveground is simply where that circuit requires surface area.

Thus the valley’s islands of life are connected by absences.

They feed on difference. A root stretched across a fault drinks the small disagreement between one side and the other. A frond feeds where its lit face and shaded face do not reach the same temperature. Some roots enter places in which the same mineral is younger on one side than on the other; these draw a current from the separation in time. The oldest groves have learned to bridge distances that are not equal in opposite directions.

Their seed-plates do not contain embryos. Each plate is a compact instruction for producing an imbalance. It lies inert until the landscape around it becomes too uniform. Then one face darkens, the other brightens, and a minute potential forms between them. From that first distinction, roots and ribbons elaborate themselves. The new grove grows by making the surrounding world less settled.

But the groves are not, in the end, the inhabitant.

They are more like its temporary organs.

The thing that lives in the fissure has no boundary that remains valid from every direction. Seen from above, its body may be the entire valley: terraces as layered shell, conductive forests as exchange surfaces, mineral rods as slow-growing supports. Seen from within, however, there is no central body—only production, sorting, alignment, and a deepening vacancy. Its substance is distributed among objects that do not touch. Some portions of it exist weeks apart. Some have not yet been manufactured, though they already influence the arrangement of what will manufacture them.

It is not asleep. Sleep implies a finished creature withholding activity. This one is active everywhere and complete nowhere.

Its metabolism is the patient reduction of accident. Trace elements enter the groves in random distributions and leave as exact ingots. Uneven rock enters the heating seams and emerges laminated. Distances that occur naturally are cut into standard intervals. Moments are stacked until the same process can occupy several stages at once. Every cycle removes a little irregularity from the valley and transfers that irregularity elsewhere—perhaps outside the crack, perhaps into a future not yet attached to the place.

Its pulse is the orange line beneath the plain.

Its breathing is the tiny change in air pressure when new volume is created.

The two-note hum may be the nearest thing it has to a voice, though neither note carries a message by itself. The lower tone measures what has already been assembled. The upper answers from wherever the completed form will eventually stand. When the notes divide, the creature is comparing itself with its own future state.

It does not perceive light as brightness. It perceives light as available structure: a clean gradient, a direction that can be retained in copper-dark tissue. It does not perceive touch at the moment of contact. A pressure on one of its plates appears first as an alteration in an earlier manufacturing step, then propagates forward until the touched surface arrives already remembering the hand.

Whether this amounts to awareness is difficult to decide. Nothing there watches. Nothing chooses between immediate alternatives. Yet the valley corrects for disturbances. A broken frond is replaced before its loss interrupts the current. A terrace prevented from gaining its next plate acquires an additional level when viewed from the opposite side. If one route through space becomes unusable, distance is folded along another. These responses are too slow to look intentional and too exact to look blind.

The shallow depression at the center is the closest thing it has to a face, although it bears no features. The six groves around it are not attendants. They are one grove displayed across six conditions, like a single thought considered at several stages of formation. The unseeable sixth is the stage in which the grove has ceased to be an object but has not yet become an effect.

Within their ring, the pale floor is being prepared for an organ that cannot be built from matter alone.

The depression is constructing a place for it.

Atom by atom—or by units smaller than atoms, units of permission—the valley withdraws ordinary spatial rules from the center. It removes the requirement that volume displace substance. It loosens the agreement between inside and outside. It teaches the floor to remain beneath everything, and the rim to surround more than can fit within it. The terraces, rods, seams, ingots, and groves all contribute to this preparation.

What will occupy that socket may be immense. It may also be smaller than the fissure. Size is probably one of the raw materials being refined away.

Perhaps the finished inhabitant will be a mechanism, but not one with gears or housings. Perhaps it will be a mechanism for making locations: a thing that produces interiority, inserting hidden countries into fractures, pores, crystal flaws, and the narrow separations between joined surfaces. The valley may be its first chamber, or merely the first component of its first chamber.

Perhaps it is reproductive rather than mechanical. When mature, the central absence may cast its own seed—not a plate, but a crack. Somewhere in another young and unweathered substance, two adjacent points may become slightly farther apart inside than outside. A warm metallic breath will rise. Dark terraces will begin arranging themselves in the new interval.

There is also the possibility that nothing is coming.

The vacancy itself may be the organism.

If so, it feeds by enlarging without excavation. Its mineral forests are sensory fringes around an appetite for room. Its body is the increasing capacity of the world to contain what was not previously possible. The terraces are not building toward a resident; they are the residue left as the resident consumes constraint.

This would explain why the center remains smooth and pale. It is not unfinished. It is the purest part of the creature: an absence so organized that the surrounding matter labors to preserve it.

Outside, the crack gives no indication of any of this. It neither widens nor radiates visible light. The plateau continues under the white sun, sharp, sterile, and new.

Inside, the thing lives at the speed of mineral decisions.

Its forests unfold.

Its buried note holds steady.

And at the center, with immeasurable patience, it becomes more able to exist.
