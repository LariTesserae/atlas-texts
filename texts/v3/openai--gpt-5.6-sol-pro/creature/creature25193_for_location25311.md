The first inhabitants one learns to recognize are not the pale cloaks but the absences they make.

A singing blade falls mute. A column loses its lowest overtone. Along a wall of black glass, droplets that had been crawling toward a white vein hesitate, then begin to creep in another direction. For a long while nothing else appears. Only when the eye has adjusted to the wet reflections does a thickness become visible against the stone: a transparent sheet folded into the crack, its edges spread farther than seemed possible.

The sheet is alive.

It has no fixed outline. At rest it may resemble spilled skin, cloudy ice, or a shallow pool adhering to a vertical surface. When it travels, it releases one margin and fastens another. The motion passes across it as a slow pleat. A crossing of three handspans may occupy an afternoon. It cannot swim in open water, but any sequence of wet surfaces is enough: stone, mineral film, the underside of a crust, even a chain of heavy droplets suspended close together.

Within it are hundreds of small grains. Some are ordinary fragments gathered from the plain. Others have been altered—rounded without abrasion, hollowed, or drawn into needles finer than hair. These circulate through branching channels in the creature’s body. Their movement is not driven by a heart. Each grain responds to the local vibration, and their collective turning pulls fluid after them. When the surrounding stone changes pitch, the circulation changes with it.

For this reason the creatures gather where several resonances cross. They fasten themselves across a seam and spread until every edge touches a different note. Then they become still enough to be mistaken for condensation.

They feed on strain.

Not sound alone, though sound betrays the process. They take in the minute flexing of mineral lattices: the compression before a crack, the relaxation after water enters it, the faint repeated bending of a pillar under the basin’s distant pressure. A settled animal can quiet a stone for years. The vibration disappears into its suspended grains, where it is retained as alignment, tension, and an almost invisible inner brightness.

The young are nearly colorless. Old ones contain elaborate mineral weather: spirals of blue-black dust, white branching figures, rust-red clouds that contract when touched by certain notes. No two carry the same collection. If moved from one district to another, they become ill until they have shed most of their grains and replaced them with local stone. Thus each is partly an animal and partly a small, mobile sample of the place where it has lived.

They do not appear to notice one another directly. Two may overlap for decades without conflict. Yet when one begins to stiffen, others slowly approach.

Stiffening is the nearest thing they have to reproduction, death, architecture, and speech.

A full creature becomes taut. Its edges seal to the surrounding rock, and its circulating grains arrange themselves in layers. Clear tissue clouds to milk-white, then darkens or pales according to the minerals it has accumulated. Over months, sometimes centuries, its body divides along straight lines. The divisions are uncannily exact. One portion thickens into a slab; another curls into a hollow cone; another contracts into a disk of glass. Water is pressed out and crawls away along chosen veins.

The approaching animals attach themselves to these hardening edges. Some add their own bodies. Others remove particular grains and carry them elsewhere. The result may be a low wall, a fitted ring, an arch, or a roofless chamber.

Whether this is deliberate cannot be answered by watching a single generation. The builders have no hands, no visible organs of planning, and no individual lives brief enough for intention to take a familiar shape. But the structures modify resonance. A ring around a dry pit gathers low vibrations at its center. A narrow arch divides one sustained note into two. Walls that do not obstruct water may nevertheless redirect pressure through the ground.

The neat accumulations inside some chambers are not offerings in any human sense. They are more like unplayed instruments, seed beds, or memories stored outside a body. Clear pebbles preserve high, clean ringing. Salt cones answer moisture with tiny changes in pitch. Dark disks absorb deeper pulses and release them only when warmed. Living sheets visit these hoards and exchange the grains they carry. Over immense spans, a chamber may become associated with one pressure cycle, one rain season, or one remote district of the basin.

In this way the inhabitants possess paths without roads. The route is a progression of compatible notes. What looks like a random scattering of structures may be a map that can only be followed by placing one’s whole body against the ground.

There are also smaller lives, if they are separate lives at all.

The silver skins on pools tighten in response to sound. Their margins form repeated angles; their centers darken; then they break into rafts of crystal petals. During rain, these petals travel through flooded channels. Most dissolve. A few lodge beneath overhangs, where upward condensation feeds them. There they produce a transparent film that gradually loosens from the stone.

Such films may be the infancy of the cloaks. Or they may be parasites, cast-off tissues, or mineral devices made by the adults. No one stage transforms quickly enough to settle the question. A petal can remain unchanged longer than many observers would live, then unfold after a pressure wave that began leagues away.

The great beings beneath the crust are harder to separate from the basin itself.

They are not simply larger versions of the surface animals, though they too alter resonance and direct water. Their passage is sensed as a broad surrender of pressure: pools rise, salt fans fracture, pillars lower their voices. Beneath a thin plate one may glimpse a pale curvature moving through darkness, followed hours later by another curvature so distant that it seems to belong to a different body.

Perhaps it does.

Some believe these deep animals are colonies: countless membranes joined under the plain, their edges carrying impulses across miles. Others imagine thick, slow bodies shaped like buried rivers, with fluid interiors and skins armored in the fragments of strata they pass through. A third possibility is that there is only one below, folded through the entire basin, and every passing “animal” is a local contraction of it.

It never surfaces entire. Where it comes near, stone warms. Water is squeezed ahead through faults and emerges as new springs. Behind it, cavities sometimes collapse into long chambers with fitted-looking sides. The smaller inhabitants arrive years later and line these spaces with their stiffened bodies.

The deep one may be feeding on the immense subterranean chord. It may be producing it. The distinction is no clearer than the distinction between the pale creatures’ deaths and their buildings.

During the long rain, surface life becomes active by its own standards. Sheets detach from pillars and spread beneath the joined pools. For weeks, the shallows fill with nearly invisible motions. Mineral grains turn inside translucent bodies like slow constellations. Walls soften at their edges. Old chambers release disks and cones that drift away under silver membranes.

When the water withdraws, many creatures are left stretched between stones. Some contract into seams. Some cannot. These remain exposed while salt grows over them. By the quiet season they have become rigid ribs, black fins, pale nodules, or angular pillars indistinguishable from the newly risen geology.

This may explain why the plain seems so crowded with fresh mineral forms. Not all the stone has come from below.

Some of it has listened, fed, gathered itself, and chosen—if choosing is the word—to stand.
