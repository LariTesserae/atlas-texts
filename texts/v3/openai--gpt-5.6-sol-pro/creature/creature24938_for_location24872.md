At first, the country appears to have almost no inhabitants.

This is partly because its creatures have taken on the manners of the place. They do not dart, forage, quarrel, or call across distances. Motion is expensive here—not in effort, but in consequence. Anything displaced will eventually return to its appointed form. A bent stalk rises. A footprint fills. A harvested fruit reappears on its stem, full and unripe. The animals have learned that the country tolerates action but refuses accomplishment.

Most therefore live by intervals.

The pale insects on the leaves are called nothing, so far as anyone can tell, but they behave like surveyors. Each stands over a junction of veins on six attenuated legs. After hours or perhaps years, one lifts a forelimb and sets it down on the next vein. It may take an age to cross a single leaf. They do not feed by biting. Their mouthparts rest against the green surface and receive the minute sweetness exhaled through the pores. When disturbed, they do not flee. They become rigid, and the leaf slowly folds a shallow chamber around them until the danger has gone.

The glassy snails are even less separable from their stations. Their shells are transparent, showing a coil of silver organs within, though these organs never pulse. Each snail inhabits the curved margin of one pool or the rim of one water-filled leaf. It advances only when a condensation bead touches its face. Then its whole body flows forward the width of the bead and stops. Long chains of them mark the paths of recurring droplets. Since every drop forms at the same point, each snail possesses, in effect, a single road and a single event.

There are soft quadrupeds among the lower terraces. They are the size of hares but built more like unfinished deer: long front legs, folded hindquarters, narrow bodies covered in colorless down. Their faces bear no visible eyes. Instead, the down along their cheeks turns green or gold according to the light falling through nearby leaves. They seem to perceive by these changes.

They eat fallen petals. The petals remain fresh in their mouths and appear again where they first fell, so the animals are never seen to swallow. Perhaps nourishment passes through flavor alone. Perhaps eating is simply how they examine the world. When alarmed, they step behind a partition of foliage and are impossible to find, though the leaves do not stir. Occasionally one emerges from behind a plant too narrow to have concealed it.

These are not the builders.

The builders—if that word can be used—are seldom noticed until one has lived among the rootways long enough to recognize an upright stillness among all the vegetal kinds.

They are small, most no taller than the five-step stair beside the pool. Their bodies are slender and jointed, wrapped in overlapping tissues that resemble the sheaths of young stems. Their colors are not camouflage in the ordinary sense. One may be citron near the shoulders, violet at the hands, and deep green along the ribs, as though several kinds of leaf had agreed temporarily to make a person.

Their heads are smooth and slightly elongated. What first appear to be closed eyelids are translucent membranes over large, dark eyes. They have no hair, but fine tendrils grow from the scalp and lie along the neck. These tendrils curl toward nearby supports during sleep. Their mouths are small, vertical seams. When they breathe, additional seams open quietly along their sides.

They are warm to the touch.

This surprises visitors more than their appearance. They seem as if they should be cool, sappy things, but heat gathers in them. A resting group under a shelter warms the enclosed air until condensation forms on the living walls. They collect these droplets in their cupped hands and pass them from one to another, though none appears to drink.

They have no enduring name for themselves. A permanent word would imply that the speaker remained the same creature from one meeting to the next. Instead, they identify one another by brief arrangements of touch: two fingers against the wrist, a palm laid over the breast, the tracing of a particular leaf vein on the shoulder. The arrangement changes whenever they meet. Recognition is not declared but renewed.

They do speak, although their voices scarcely carry. Their language consists of soft clicks made behind the teeth, breaths released through the flank, and pressure applied to the plants around them. A hand laid on a ribbed root can produce a low fiber-hum audible only to someone touching the same root. Thus a conversation may extend through an entire grove while remaining silent to anyone standing between its participants.

Nothing they say can be written. Marks close. Cut fibers rejoin. Piles of stones resume their former positions. Even knots loosen into the shapes the vines held before. Their memory therefore lives in routes, postures, and living bodies.

An old one is not visibly aged. Age shows as complexity. The oldest carry many repeated gestures within ordinary movement. They may turn their head in a way first used by a companion long absent, or fold their hands in the manner of someone who entered a pool and did not return. Each survivor becomes crowded with inherited mannerisms. A very old person may appear indecisive when walking, but in truth several remembered walkers are passing through the same body.

Their shelters are not houses. They offer neither security nor permanence. The inhabitants bend rooted branches together and wait—not for the branches to grow, since growth never completes, but for the country to accept the bend as one of its allowable forms. Most attempts are quietly undone. Once in a great while, two stems remain inclined after the hands release them. Mats hung between such stems root themselves and become walls. A shelter is therefore considered less a construction than a successful request.

The rings of slabs and roofless rooms are older requests.

No one now living claims to know who made them. The inhabitants visit these places and stand in the divisions between the blocks, matching their bodies to the proportions of the rooms. Some chambers are too narrow for them; others seem made for creatures twice their height. At the ring of upright slabs, each person chooses a vine-wrapped stone and rests their forehead against it. They remain until a droplet falls somewhere in the clearing. Then they depart in different directions.

Their lives are devoted to acts that need not be performed.

They carry pollen between flowers already fertile. They support tendrils that will never fall. They place cushions of moss beneath the regular rain. They lift trapped leaves from pools, though the leaves return to the water later. When they find a bud, they may sit around it for a long while, hands open, accompanying an opening that never occurs.

This is not labor as surface creatures understand it. They do not expect improvement. They seem to believe that unfinished acts require witnesses.

There are no children in the usual sense. Sometimes a new figure is found standing waist-deep in one of the black-green pools. It has the stature of an adult but no color and no practiced gestures. Its tendrils float on the water. Its eyes are open, yet it does not climb out until someone on the bank offers it a movement to imitate.

The first gesture given to such a being matters greatly. A hand extended too quickly may leave it fearful of hands for centuries. A bowed head may produce a contemplative wanderer. For this reason, several inhabitants gather when a new one appears and deliberate in silence before welcoming it.

No one sees the figure rise from below. It is simply absent, and then present.

The smooth forms visible beneath the deepest pools may be those not yet arrived. At times they resemble curled bodies. At other angles they look like bowls, masks, seeds, or pale stones. The inhabitants do not attempt to retrieve them. They kneel beside the water and show them gestures across the surface.

Departure also occurs at the pools.

Eventually, an inhabitant may become too crowded with remembrance. Their movements accumulate hesitations; their hands begin a dozen greetings at once. Colors darken across their tissues into olive, copper, and purple, matching the preserved foliage in the lowest layers of the root mat. Such a person goes alone to a pool, although others follow at a respectful distance.

They descend the five steps.

The water does not rise above their ankles, but with each step they appear farther away. On the last step they may be visible deep below, standing among the smooth objects. Then reflection covers them. No ripple remains.

Afterward, those who knew them take one of their gestures into their own bodies.

Whether this is death, return, or merely another kind of residence is uncertain. Sometimes, long after a departure, a new figure at another pool will perform an old gesture without having been taught. The others do not proclaim recognition. They approach and renew it.

There may also be something larger living in the country.

The inhabitants never refer to it directly. In certain vast clearings, all leaves incline by a fraction toward the same empty point. Root bridges curve around spaces where nothing visible stands. Regular droplets delay their fall when a group passes beneath, then resume afterward in altered sequence. The pale quadrupeds stop eating and turn their blind faces toward the young stone.

At such times, the whole vegetation seems less like a forest than a single organ attending to itself.

The small people place both hands on the root mat. Their flank-pores open. Across miles of layered growth comes the dry, nearly inaudible whisper of millions of leaves doing the same.

Perhaps the country is their dwelling.

Perhaps they are its thoughts.

Or perhaps everything below—the insects poised over veins, the snails awaiting their one drop, the silent animals behind the leaves, the bright unfinished people, the pools with their unreachable forms—is one inhabitant held at the instant before it decides what kind of life it will become.
