Nothing on the Low Roaring Plain lives alone.

At first its inhabitants seem divisible into kinds. There are the flat ones and the tall ones, the shelled processions, the airborne rings, the pale hunters that knot themselves around herds. Given time, these distinctions become less certain. Every large body carries a smaller migration. Every mouth is a pool with its own scavengers. Eggs travel inside grazers that did not eat them deliberately; parasites shelter parasites; the dead continue moving because their occupants have not yet learned that the host is gone. An animal here is less an individual than a temporary arrangement of hungers.

The creatures nearest the rock are called pressers, though no two colonies produce quite the same shape. Young pressers are translucent disks no wider than a nail, with a fringe of gripping cilia and a dark digestive knot at the center. They settle wherever chemical films have spread across damp stone. As they feed, mineral grains accumulate within their tissues. Older animals become opaque and heavy, patterned according to the bed beneath them: black on basalt, milk-pale on limestone, rust-streaked around iron seepage. Their bodies are soft above and toothed below. Thousands of microscopic rasps travel in waves across the underside, shaving film, salts, and sometimes the stone itself.

A presser does not crawl in the usual sense. It releases its rear edge, squeezes fluid forward, adheres again, and draws the rest of itself after. In a mass this movement passes from body to body. A hillside of them may advance without any one animal seeming to move. Their slow contractions are among the plain’s deepest rhythms, felt through buried rock long after rain has ceased.

They reproduce by layering. When well fed, a presser grows a second underside between itself and the ground. The lower layer thickens, develops its own rasping surface, and eventually remains behind when the parent shifts onward. For a while the two are joined by fine vessels through which water and dissolved nutrients pass. Needle-legged pickers seek these nurseries, piercing the soft new layer to drink from it. The pressers respond by contracting around the intruder. Sometimes the picker is crushed. Sometimes it remains trapped and feeds safely for days inside the fold.

The pickers are almost all limb. Their central bodies are small, blind beads suspended among six, eight, or twelve jointed legs, the number changing between molts. Each foot ends in a fan sensitive to minute differences in vibration. They test the stone continuously, reading feeding rasps, heart pulses, egg movements, and the reflected tremors of distant herds. When threatened, they pull their legs inward and become hard seeds. Grazers swallow them by the thousand. Many survive the passage.

Their eggs survive even more readily. They are soft when laid but harden in digestive fluids, so the safest nursery for a picker is often inside something much larger. Afterward the eggs emerge in warm slurry, already supplied with microbes and powdered mineral. This is why pickers gather along migration grooves and behind the filter-feeding herds, stepping delicately through waste that would drown lighter animals.

The most visible travelers are the shellbacks. A shellback is roughly fist-sized when mature, though famine produces smaller adults and rich seepage produces individuals large enough to fill both hands. Its shell consists of overlapping cups rather than a single enclosure. The cups knock together with every step, creating the dry, continuous clatter that occupies the middle range of the plain’s roar.

Beneath them, the animal is little more than a muscular strap. It can reverse direction without turning by extending the opposite set of limbs and raising what had been its rear feeding mouth. This gives shellback streams their peculiar indifference to collision. When two migrations meet, neither yields. Animals climb face-first over the shells of the opposing current, turn themselves inside their armor, or wait compressed beneath the crossing for hours. Many are cracked. The injured are consumed while still walking, yet their detached shells continue along the route, carried on the backs and gripping feet of others.

Shellbacks navigate by inherited tremor. Before hatching, a brood is cemented beneath the parent’s cups and exposed to the rhythm of its migration. The developing young feel the repeated ground-signature of particular ridges, hollows, and cavities. Once freed, they follow familiar sequences of vibration without requiring the parent. A route can therefore outlive every animal that learned it firsthand. Even when floods alter the basin, shellbacks attempt the older course, piling against new obstacles until abrasion, death, and weight make the path passable again.

Above them move the sediment grazers, vast blind funnels borne on rings of short legs. Their heads are not distinct from their throats. The forward third of the body opens, scoops, and closes around whatever lies ahead: mud, pressers, shells, eggs, carrion, water, and smaller members of their own species. Internal combs sort the mouthful chiefly by size. Nothing is identified. Anything fine enough is sent into branching digestive sacs; anything coarse enough passes through lateral slits in milky fans.

These discharged fans support whole communities. Pickers comb them. Shellbacks chew the soft edges. Balloon-bodied brooders attach egg cords to them before they collapse. Some small hunters live permanently beside a grazer’s waste slits, feeding on what emerges and biting away anything that might block the opening. They defend their particular host from others of their own kind, though the grazer itself shows no sign of knowing they exist.

Old grazers become landscapes. Their backs flatten under accumulated shell plates and mineral deposits. Grooves deepen between the breathing folds. Pressers adhere to them, mistaking the slow bodies for warm stone, and airborne drinkers descend to collect condensation from their hides. Some carry so much life that when they stop, the plain continues flowing over them. Only after the body cools do its passengers begin to depart.

Among the grazers travel the egg-bearing giants. They are taller than most life on the plain, though “tall” here means that their lower surfaces clear the ground. Their bodies hang from arched mineral braces on many pillar-like limbs. Along the underside are hundreds of sealed brood chambers, each holding a different stage of development. The chambers open one at a time as the giant crosses suitable conditions: fresh water awakens one clutch, heat another, sulfurous air a third. Thus a single giant may shed young belonging to several seasons over the course of years.

The young do not resemble the adult. They hatch as thin swimming ribbons in transient pools, feeding on blood-light microbes and one another until the water withdraws. The survivors bury their heads in damp sediment and digest their own tails. Limbs bud around the buried portion. Mineral braces precipitate inside them. When at last they pull free, they are small walking arches carrying the scars of the vanished pool on their backs.

Adults announce themselves through the ground. Their calls are made by flexing the braces of the chest against one another. The lowest notes travel farther through stone than through air, arriving as pressure before sound. Other animals interrupt feeding when such a call passes. Shellback streams slow. Coiled hunters loosen. Even winged swarms alter height.

This pause may protect the giants’ broods from being trampled. It may be an ancient reflex from a time when something larger hunted the callers. No creature on the present plain appears to know the reason. The response persists because those that fail to stop are often crushed beneath the migration.

The common predators are not swift. Speed has little use where prey already presses against the mouth.

Splitters are long muscular animals with a hard wedge along one side and jaws along the other. They fling the wedge into moving crowds, dividing a many-directioned flow into a brief surge. Then they turn their feeding side toward the pressure. Their jaws do not bite down. They remain open while the herd packs prey into them. A successful splitter may be dragged for a considerable distance, belly swollen, before it can seal its mouth.

Its greatest danger is excess. A splitter that creates too strong a surge can be buried under shellbacks or forced into a gutter where the moving mass folds it against stone. Old migration trenches contain rows of splitter spines, all aligned with the flow that killed them.

The circular hunters begin life as pairs. Each is a narrow arc with a mouth at one end and a clasp at the other. Two clasp together, then four, then dozens, forming a feeding ring around a patch of crowded ground. Once closed, the composite animal contracts. Blood and digestive fluid circulate through the joined bodies, allowing damaged members to draw nourishment from successful ones. The ring tightens over hours, compressing what it encloses.

If food is plentiful, more arcs join until the hunter is broad enough to surround a pool. If food fails, the ring opens and separates. Former members may later become parts of rival circles. There is no permanent center and no individual that directs the tightening. Coordination passes as pressure from clasp to clasp.

Some rings never separate. Their members fuse, calcify, and continue growing as ridged wheels that roll slowly under the push of migrations. Their inner mouths face the hub. Anything falling through the spokes is eaten. These old wheels are rare and conspicuous, their passage marked by repeating scars in the polished sediment.

Smaller predators descend from the air in living veils. They are called joint-gleaners because they feed where armor plates meet, where limbs enter sockets, and where breathing folds cannot harden. Each is scarcely more than a hooked mouth attached to wings. They detect the heat of exertion and fall upon animals struggling through dense crowds. A healthy host may shake them free. A weakened one acquires a luminous fringe as the gleaners open hundreds of tiny wounds and the exposed blood organisms flash.

The flashing is not the wounded animal’s signal. Its blood is alive with symbionts that spend their dormant stage circulating through vessels. Air wakes them. They emit one hard white pulse, grow adhesive filaments, and attach to whatever mouthpart or limb has broken the skin. In this way they pass into scavengers and predators. Large deaths shine because they are also mass departures.

In the air, the simplest fliers are mist needles. They have clear wings, a threadlike gut, and no resting stage. Their entire lives are spent in flight. They drink suspended moisture and the fine organic dust beaten upward by the crowds below. Mating occurs when swarms cross: individuals collide, exchange cells, and continue. Eggs develop in the forward body and are released behind as minute floating bladders. Most never reach the ground. They hatch in cloud, consume the bladder from within, and enter the swarm already flying.

Mist needles live briefly, but the swarm is old. Its paths are maintained through pressure and electric charge. Each animal follows the wingbeat disturbances of its neighbors. No one knows the route, yet the whole aggregation turns from poisonous pans and descends toward newly wet stone.

The great ring-fliers are stranger. They have no permanent face. Their bodies are circular and pleated, with several mouths spaced around the inner rim and sensory pits around the outer. Muscular waves travel along the ring, tightening one section and expanding another. This pushes air through the center and carries the creature forward. To reverse direction, it does not turn; it reverses the traveling wave and begins feeding through the opposite mouth.

Young rings are small and nearly symmetrical. With age, repeated injury makes them uneven. Torn sections heal thicker. Parasitized mouths close. One arc may grow heavy with eggs while another becomes a resonating chamber. The oldest fly crookedly, beating the air with slow corrections, each body a record of collisions and storms.

They drink from other animals because open water is dangerous in a crowd. At rest they settle over a grazer, giant, or dense shellback stream and spread absorbent filaments across hundreds of backs. Condensation runs along these threads into the ring’s mouths. The host is cooled and sometimes cleaned of mites, though neither service appears intentional. If the terrestrial mass begins to panic, the ring sheds sections of its own body to rise quickly. Those severed arcs may survive on the ground, clasp one another, and become circular hunters. The division between aerial drinker and terrestrial predator is therefore only a division between intact and broken forms of the same creature.

Many lives on the plain have such alternate shapes.

The warm-lures, for example, resemble no animal while buried. They are broad sacs sunk beneath sediment, feeding through threads that enter carcasses and dung beds. At night they pump warm fluid into chambers near the surface, reproducing the heat pattern of a sleeping colony. Cold animals gather on top. When enough weight accumulates, the sac collapses inward, dropping them into a digestive hollow. After feeding, it inflates and raises the cleaned shells back to the surface.

Yet during rain, warm-lures abandon predation. Their upper membrane opens into cups that collect water, and small animals shelter safely among them. Eggs are laid there. Pickers clean their pores. For a few hours the false colony becomes a real nursery, and attacking it would destroy the next generation of creatures the lure itself requires.

The burrowers are seldom seen whole. Their drumming limbs appear from holes, or pale feeding tubes emerge between stones, but the bulk of each animal lies distributed through cavities beneath the plain. What seems to be a colony may be one body extending across kilometers; what seems to be one body may be thousands joined at hardened valves. They drink seepage, filter microbes from underground water, and tap on hollow strata to map changes in pressure.

Their signals fill the basin from below. Rapid strokes warn of flooding. Uneven triplets follow roof collapses. Long rolling sequences occur before mass hatchings and may help soften egg cases through vibration. Surface animals have incorporated these subterranean rhythms into their own behavior. A false flood-drum can redirect a migration. Some predators have learned to produce it. Burrowers answer such imitations by changing the pattern, and the deception begins again.

No animal willingly enters the Mirror Pans. The mineral there interferes with the electrical gradients by which nerves and muscles coordinate. A shellback pushed onto the metallic lining loses the sequence of its limbs and walks in several directions at once. Fliers above it experience their own bodies as conflicting orientations. Those that do not escape quickly fold, spiral down, and become still.

At the pans’ edges live the only creatures that exploit this emptiness: narrow, motionless things rooted not in soil but in the discarded armor of their ancestors. They are animals despite their resemblance to stakes. Their tissues remain mostly outside the poisoned ground, elevated on mineral stilts. Long threads extend over the pan and collect fallen fliers. Because they cannot cross to mate, they cast packets of living tissue through the air when deep calls shake the basin. Most packets miss the opposite rim. A few adhere, unfold, and begin building new stilts from the bodies of the dead.

In the breathing fields dwell the levelers. They are soft, hand-sized creatures with fluid chambers along their flanks. Alone, each is easily overturned by the slow rise of gas beneath the ground. In crowds they link their chambers through temporary pores. Pressure entering one passes through thousands, flattening some bodies and lifting others until the colony rests as a continuous sheet over the swelling earth.

Other animals climb onto this sheet. Eggs are laid in its warm seams. Predators cross without attacking. The levelers gain scraps, heat, and protection from being peeled away, while the larger life of the plain gains stable ground. When the earth subsides, the connections close and the sheet dissolves into separate animals again. For a few moments afterward they retain traces of one another’s internal chemistry. Then even that communal body is gone.

The plain’s rarest inhabitants may be the hush-makers.

No one sees them arrive. Their presence is known when the continuous roar thins around a moving point. Wingbeats falter. Shell plates stop knocking. Burrowers hold their drums above the stone. The quiet spreads not because the creatures flee, but because they flatten, fold, seal their vents, and wait.

Within the resulting stillness something passes—a displacement low against the ground, colder than rainwater and broad enough to disturb several migration streams at once. It leaves no prints. Dead animals are not found behind it. Sometimes eggs hatch prematurely along its route. Sometimes every blood symbiont in a nearby grazer flashes through unbroken skin, outlining the animal from within.

Whether the hush-maker is a predator, a weather event given biological shape, a subterranean organ briefly pressing against the surface, or merely the plain’s collective response to a vibration too deep to perceive cannot be separated from the behavior it causes. Here, a creature need not possess a single body. It need only create a reliable change in other bodies.

This is true of nearly everything that lives in the basin.

A migration is an animal made of shellbacks and inherited rhythm. A feeding ring is an animal assembled from hungry arcs. A giant is an animal carrying pools, nurseries, scavengers, and remembered rain beneath its ribs. A carcass is alive first with itself, then with its blood, then with its consumers, then with the eggs inside those consumers. Even the roar behaves like life: it feeds on movement, breeds echoes in buried hollows, warns, deceives, gathers, and directs.

The inhabitants do not master the Low Roaring Plain. They constitute it. Their routes are its channels, their armor its sediment, their breath its cloud, their pressure its polish. When millions shift to admit millions more, no creature understands the whole adjustment. Each responds only to heat, touch, thirst, tremor, and the bodies immediately against it.

Yet the change crosses the basin like a decision.
