No single creature can be said to live in the city alone. Life there occurs in assemblies: herd and pavement, colony and exhaust, swimmer and sluice, worker and machine. Even a solitary predator waiting beneath a dripping stair depends on a chain of pumps, microbial films, grazing bodies, and warm runoff extending far beyond its gutter.

The citizens understand themselves in much the same way.

They are broad-footed, heavy-limbed people, built less for climbing than for keeping their balance on wet stone. Generations of mineral-rich food have given many of them dense bones and darkened teeth. Their skin is commonly marked by the city: pale scars from caustic spray, green traces around copper jewelry, black crescents beneath nails that never quite wash clean. Few dress against cold. They dress against wetness, abrasion, hot pipes, falling scale, and the exploratory mouthparts of animals. Aprons of lacquered cloth overlap at the thigh. Work coats are short so they do not drag through standing water. Sandals and boots have divided, gripping soles, and nearly everyone carries a hood even beneath a clear sky, because condensate may descend without warning from bridges far overhead.

They walk slowly. Visitors sometimes mistake this for weariness, but haste is considered a poor urban instinct. Streets change use from one hour to the next. A dry arcade may become a runoff channel. A flight of steps may fill with sleeping grazers. Steam may obscure a crossing where freight cages descend. People learn to pause at thresholds and feel for vibration through their soles. Before entering a narrow passage, they listen for the bottle-deep calls of tower colonies, the scrape of herd bodies, or the almost inaudible movement of a gutter hunter shifting its jaw.

Children learn these distinctions before they learn letters.

They are taught which pools are merely warm and which conceal thin crusts over boiling cavities; which filter animals can be stroked closed without harm; which armored swimmers feign death; which three-beat tower calls belong to the compression works and which indicate distress. They learn not to step over a motionless six-legged hunter, however old or rain-polished it appears. They learn to leave strips of wall uncleansed after rainfall so the grazing herds continue along safe routes instead of entering houses.

Their games imitate maintenance. Children build little canals from broken tile, dam them with shell fragments, then release dyed water to see whose district floods. They mark slow grazers with chalk and wager on which will reach the end of a square first. On laundry bridges they clap their hands beneath the hanging filter colonies, trying to send a wave of folding bodies farther than their companions can. Adults discourage this, though many remember doing it themselves.

Most citizens belong to some keeping profession. There are keepers of gates, pressures, passages, herds, heat, settlement, resonance, silt, and forgotten motion. A person who tends a machine may know only one section of it: the sound of a bearing, the proper warmth of a casing, the precise interval between two drops from a relief valve. This is not regarded as incomplete knowledge. Complete knowledge is viewed with suspicion, because nothing important in the city is simple enough to be held by one mind.

Maintenance guilds preserve instructions in chants fitted to the rhythm of the mechanisms they serve. A pump sequence may be remembered as a twelve-line work song whose pauses correspond to valve changes. Directions through flooded foundations are encoded in accounts of ancestral voyages. Children of the western works can reproduce the three-beat piston rhythm by striking knuckles against their ribs. In some districts, diagrams are considered less reliable than trained bodies: paper molds, plaques corrode, walls shift, but a sequence performed every day can survive for centuries.

The people do not often claim to repair the oldest machines. They “persuade” them, “quiet” them, or “give them another interval.” When a mechanism can no longer perform its intended work, it is rarely dismantled. Its movement is redirected. A failed ore lift may drive ventilation fans. A seized fan shaft may become the anchor for a footbridge. A reciprocating arm whose original chamber has flooded may agitate a breeding pool for filter larvae. Machinery passes gradually from industry into architecture, from architecture into habitat, and from habitat into something like geology.

This makes ownership difficult. A building may belong to one family, its roof heat to a nesting colony, its drainwater to a neighboring workshop, its lower chamber to the canal authority, and the mineral crust forming on its eastern wall to a licensed herd route. Rights are layered vertically and seasonally. Disputes are settled less by deciding who possesses a place than by determining whose use can bend without breaking the others.

The city’s judges are therefore often engineers, naturalists, and elderly route-keepers. They hear cases in open halls where small grazers wander beneath the benches. Their decisions may specify pipe diameters, nesting intervals, acceptable pressure loss, hours of passage, or the thickness of sacrificial wall facing. A famous ruling can be remembered not for declaring anyone right, but for moving a stairway half a meter so that a household, a freight line, and a breeding colony could all continue undisturbed.

Food comes chiefly from the wet industries of the plateau. The citizens cultivate microbial films in heated galleries, growing them in sheets of rust-red, cream, violet, and black. Some are dried and ground into dense flour; others are fermented in clay vats until they become sharp pastes or dark, mineral-tasting broths. Filter animals are harvested selectively, usually after spawning, and their ribbed fans are eaten steamed. Small canal swimmers are boiled in their shells. Larger ones are cut apart in waterside markets, their armor sold for roofing plates, tool handles, drainage baffles, and children’s toys.

The broad-backed grazers are not commonly slaughtered. Their meat is bitter and their bodies carry concentrated minerals, but their eggs are collected from warm culverts, and secretions scraped from their mouthparts are used in polishing compounds. Some households keep a single grazer in an entrance court to clean the walls. Such animals are never quite tame. They recognize routes, warmth, and feeding surfaces rather than owners. When one joins a passing herd, the household accepts the departure and leaves the side arch open in case it returns years later.

Nothing edible is called fresh merely because it was recently alive. Freshness means chemically balanced, safely settled, properly leeched. Most cooking is a form of purification. Pots have multiple chambers. Broths pass through shell, charcoal, porous stone, and filter tissue before reaching the table. Families inherit cultures of useful microbes as carefully as other societies might inherit orchards or livestock. A household ferment can be hundreds of years old, divided among descendants and fed with condensate from a particular pipe whose metallic character is thought essential to its flavor.

The wealthiest citizens do not live in the driest districts. Dryness suggests dead conduits, failed heat, or separation from public flow. Prestige belongs to controlled wetness: a home beside a reliable warm channel, with clean condensation, stable foundations, and a roof colony whose calls harmonize with the neighborhood engines. The most desirable rooms are those where the floor remains merely damp and the walls bloom in predictable colors after rain.

Their furniture is low, heavy, and usually fixed to the masonry. Cabinets stand on stone feet in shallow runnels. Beds are suspended just above floor level on bronze links, enough to escape minor flooding but not enough to invite nesting underneath. Doors swell, floors sink, walls perspire; no one expects straightness to persist. Carpenters and masons build adjustment into everything. Hinges have several future positions. Stairs are laid with extra depth so they can be recut as they sag. Family records include not only births and deaths but changes in floor level.

The citizens measure age by settlement.

An old person may say that in childhood the southern sill stood two fingers higher than the northern one, or that the canal could then be heard only during rain. Generations are associated with changes in animal movement: the years before the shell crossing, the first nesting of the high stacks, the period when violet film entered the eastern wards. Political eras are remembered by pump tones. There are citizens who cannot state the date of a law but can sing the machinery rhythm under which it was passed.

Their dead remain within the city’s cycles, though practices differ by district. Some bodies are sealed in porous mineral coffins and lowered into hot cisterns, where heat and microbial action gradually reduce them. The resulting mineral deposit is recovered years later and fired into commemorative tiles. Elsewhere the dead are placed in high rookery vaults, protected from large scavengers but open to pale filter colonies and minute drifting organisms. Names are cut into replaceable stone, with the expectation that rain will soften them. Permanent inscription is considered boastful. Continued use is the preferred memorial.

A deceased pressure keeper may become part of the floor of a valve house. A canal pilot’s mineral tile may line a lock where armored swimmers knock against it for centuries. Families visit such places, but they do not insist that the material remain untouched. To be worn smooth by traffic is an honorable end.

The animals, meanwhile, are not arranged around the citizens’ meanings.

The grazing herds know the city as a succession of edible surfaces. Their world begins with rain. Dormant films awaken across plazas and retaining walls, and the herds rise from drainage hollows where they have stood for days in a state resembling sleep. They move toward chemical traces too faint for citizens to detect, flowing around buildings with the slow cohesion of floodwater. Each animal presses close to the one ahead, slate back against slate flank, until boulevards seem paved with moving bodies.

They have poor vision and exquisite mouths. With their blunt scraping plates they can distinguish iron-feeding colonies from sulfur-feeding ones, fresh stone from sacrificial facing, harmless glaze from toxic precipitate. Old herd leaders remember routes through districts transformed by subsidence. When a familiar passage is blocked, hundreds may stand against the obstruction in silence, applying no force, simply waiting for the city to become passable again. Often the city yields first.

The filter animals live a more aerial existence. A colony may cover the underside of a bridge so completely that no stone remains visible. Their bodies are pallid, translucent, and soft as folded cloth, but their attachment limbs can grip iron through decades of vibration. They feed on what the city sheds: skin, spores, oils, vaporized metals, microbial dust, fragments of shell, the powdered residue of industry. Their drifting young are nearly invisible. They enter ventilation systems, settle in warm joints, and either die by the millions or found a colony that outlives the building.

They respond to danger collectively, though no one has found a leader among them. One body folds, then its neighbors, and the motion runs outward in a percussive wave. Citizens use these waves as warnings. A clap traveling against the wind may announce a pressure leak, a predator climbing the supports, or a vibration in the bridge long before instruments register it.

In the canals, the armored swimmers live in crowded darkness beneath the moving backs visible from above. Locks, reservoirs, foundation vaults, and flooded cellars form one connected territory to them. Small species graze the inner walls of pipes. Flat-headed varieties cling near pump mouths and feed in the inflow. The largest patrol old channels with shell ridges scarred by masonry. They are nearly blind, sensing pressure and electrical gradients through pits along their armor.

Some carry entire communities. Filter buds attach beneath their plates. Coin-sized scavengers shelter in their joints. Mineral bacteria make colored maps across their backs. When an old swimmer dies, its shell may remain lodged in a tunnel and become a reef, a blockage, a shrine, or a municipal problem depending on where the current leaves it.

The tower animals know another city again: one made of vertical warmth, resonant cavities, rising vapor, and narrow stone faces. They cling head-up or head-down with equal ease. Their hook feet continually score the masonry, and generations of climbing carve shallow tracks around chimneys. They feed on airborne young, filter fragments, and soft creatures drawn to exhaust heat. Their low calls travel through pipes as readily as through air.

Each colony acquires the rhythm of its district. The imitation is not exact. Over generations the mechanical pulse is elaborated into something organic—hesitations, overtones, replies between stacks. Engineers can identify failing equipment by changes in nearby calls. A bearing that begins to drag alters the vibration of a tower; within days the colony’s drone roughens. Skilled listeners are sometimes sent to hear the animals before anyone opens the machinery.

Predators inhabit the gaps in these arrangements.

The long-jawed gutter creatures are patient enough to become part of the streetscape. Dust, mineral film, and rain color their hides. Small drainpipes may be mistaken for their snouts, and their snouts for drainpipes. They feed infrequently, striking at coin-swimmers, juvenile grazers, or careless hands. Residents mark known individuals with bright ceramic studs driven into nearby mortar, but the predators sometimes relocate, leaving behind a warning for an empty gutter.

The six-legged hunters are more visible and, in some ways, less feared. They follow herds without concealment, placing each heavy foot carefully among them. They do not charge. Instead they select a weak grazer and remain behind it for hours, then days, preventing it from resting, feeding, or rejoining the densest part of the herd. Their hunt is an exertion of inevitability. Citizens give them room. A hunter interrupted repeatedly may abandon its chosen prey and begin examining slower things.

Far above, translucent gliders turn in thermal columns. Their bodies are little more than stretched membranes around dark digestive cords. Rain passes through fine openings in their wings. They descend rarely, usually to collect tower young or scraps from refinery roofs. Their shadows are so faint that people notice them first in reflections, sliding across flooded courtyards like clouds beneath the water.

In the deepest basins live the creatures few citizens see whole. They are called silt mothers, sump backs, bottom binders, or simply the old ones. Their visible surfaces resemble ridges of pale stone beneath opaque water. They move perhaps a body length in a year. Intake divers describe broad feeding folds opening along their undersides and forests of sensory filaments waving around them. Contaminated mud passes through these creatures and emerges layered into dense mineral shell.

Some settling basins have depended on a single individual for longer than surviving records. The city builds around it, adjusts discharge chemistry to its health, and excavates rooms when its growing shell narrows the basin. Whether such a creature is livestock, infrastructure, citizen, or landscape has never been resolved. It continues ingesting silt while councils debate.

There are smaller lives beyond counting: heat worms braided around pipes; translucent mites grazing salt from windows; bell-shaped organisms pulsing in cisterns; shell borers that tunnel into armor and fired clay alike; threadlike parasites that alter the routes of grazers; airborne specks spending their adult lives within a single droplet beneath an eave. Whole ecologies occupy the damp lining of a worker’s hood or the warm felt around a conduit.

The people are not disgusted by this intimacy, though neither are they sentimental about it. They cull, harvest, poison, divert, protect, breed, and occasionally exterminate. They know that coexistence is not gentleness. It is pressure made survivable by distribution.

Their deepest fear is not predation, flood, subsidence, or mechanical failure in isolation. It is interruption spreading unchecked: one stopped pump drying a nesting wall, one displaced colony clogging a vent, one altered runoff channel starving a herd route, one absent herd allowing corrosive film to thicken across a foundation. Catastrophe is imagined as a chain of things no longer accommodating one another.

For this reason, watchfulness is the city’s central virtue. Not vigilance in the sense of alarm, but sustained attention. Citizens stop to hear whether a pipe’s note has changed. They touch walls to judge warmth. They notice when swimmers surface at the wrong hour or when a tower colony misses a beat. They remember where water stood last year. They leave room around what is growing.

At dusk, when wet surfaces catch the gray-gold light, the distinction between inhabitants becomes briefly difficult to maintain. Shells shine like windows. Windows appear scaled. Workers in lacquered coats move among slate herds. Gliders pass through chimney plumes; filter fans open beneath bridges; machines breathe through animal calls. The city does not seem occupied so much as composed of occupations, each life holding a place open for another.

Then the lamps ignite in rows. The canals stir. Night crews descend into the flooded foundations by barge. Tower colonies lower their calls to match the slower engines. Grazers continue along boulevards polished by their ancestors, and deep under the streets the oldest basin creatures draw another thin layer of poisoned silt into themselves.

Nothing sleeps all at once.
