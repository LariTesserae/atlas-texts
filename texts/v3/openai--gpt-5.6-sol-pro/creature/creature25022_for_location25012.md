The people are not green, though visitors often expect them to be.

They are human in the ordinary, unremarkable ways: they cough in winter, complain about damp bedding, burn porridge, fall in love with unsuitable neighbors, and keep bent nails in kitchen drawers. Their familiarity is most evident after dark, when they appear behind square windows carrying bowls or folding shirts. Only gradually does one notice how the basin has entered their habits.

They walk with a yielding gait. Even on stone beyond the embankment, they place each foot as though the ground might answer. Children learn early to distinguish the soft spring of healthy root mat from the hollow give above a water bladder. Adults can feel a loaded tram approaching through their soles. Factory hands recognize the deepening tension before a press descends and move their fingers away without looking.

They seldom stand directly beneath door lintels. They do not lean chairs back against walls, because walls bruise and chairs sink. No one sets down a hot pan without a cork trivet. When rain begins, conversations pause—not from reverence, exactly, but calculation. Faces turn toward roofs. Someone checks which gutter opens first. Someone else remembers a cistern valve left on its dry setting. Children run into courtyards with cups, though they are scolded for taking water before the roof has sorted it.

Their clothes are narrow-cut and frequently patched. Loose hems catch on bark grain, hooks, and training staples. Mill workers wear waxed aprons made from shed roof membrane, translucent at the edges and greenish in rain. Surveyors dress in gray coats with many inside pockets for chalk, tension gauges, strips of measuring bark, and small brass bells. Almost everyone owns soft-soled shoes. Hard heels are considered both noisy and discourteous.

There are stains particular to each trade. Water sorters have pale mineral lines around their cuffs. Glassworkers carry glittering dust in the creases beside their eyes. Those who tend pressure groves smell faintly of sap and cold iron. Root-rail conductors develop dark palms from gripping the fibrous brake ropes. Nursery crews are recognizable by scratches running lengthwise along their forearms, as if they too have begun to show grain.

The town produces few farmers in the usual sense. Instead there are wall nurses, membrane cutters, root combers, gutter grafters, fungal stewards, cistern listeners, and people whose entire occupation is persuading one living surface not to join another. Separation requires more labor than growth. A skilled separator can maintain a finger-width seam between two houses for fifty years without killing either one. Such workers are respected for tact. They say that any fool can cut; the work is teaching the cut to remain a boundary.

The most prestigious inhabitants are not necessarily the wealthiest but those trusted to interpret strain. Engineers of the Office move through every district, kneeling in streets, pressing ears to columns, tapping iron collars with small hammers. They are patient people with an air of permanent distraction. During meals they may stop chewing because a spoon has vibrated against a bowl. They read cracks the way sailors read weather.

An experienced strain engineer does not ask whether a beam is strong. She asks where its strength is going. She knows that a healthy eastern wall may be stealing load from a bridge three streets away, or that a narrowing bedroom can signal a dangerous loosening beneath a mill. Her authority is considerable. She may order a family to leave a room, a factory to release its stored weight, or an entire square to remain empty until the next rain. Yet she cannot command the town outright. Her decrees are negotiations recorded in conditional language: *hold until softened; permit after swelling; observe through two dry cycles.*

Their maps resemble anatomical drawings and musical scores. Streets appear as bands of tension rather than routes. Buildings are marked not by outline but by tendencies: tightening, drifting, grafting, lifting, tiring. The maps are obsolete almost as soon as they are completed, but no one regards this as failure. Accuracy here is understood to have a season.

Below the engineers in status, though often above them in practical knowledge, are the old women and men who have lived in the same cluster of rooms for decades. They know the local body intimately. They can say which stair hollows fastest, which pipe knocks before frost, which patch of wall dislikes copper, and where a cup left overnight will be warm in the morning. They distrust measurements taken on a single day. When the Office proposes a collar or cut, an elder may answer, “Wait until it remembers winter.”

Memory is often expressed this way, as if growth were a kind of recollection. A root trained around a conduit is said to remember the pipe after the pipe is gone. A repeatedly reopened doorway remembers passage. An abandoned workshop remembers weight and keeps its floor dense for generations. People, too, are praised for remembering correctly—not merely recalling events, but allowing past pressures to shape present conduct.

Their speech contains few absolute directions. They say rainward and dryward, high-root and low-root, tight side and settling side. An address may include a building’s current relation to another: the rooms above the westward seam; the house not yet joined to the old dye works; the third door before the sinking court. Written addresses are amended in different inks. Some envelopes bear half a dozen corrections and still arrive.

Children are taught the town first through touch. They press their hands to walls and guess whether sap is rising. They lie flat in quiet streets to count the intervals between underground shifts. In school, they learn arithmetic with counterweights, civic law through water allotments, and history through layers cut from retired walls. A classroom may keep a polished cross-section showing a century of repairs: woven twigs at the center, then pulp, ash, fungal felt, iron staples, brick dust, and new pale wood grown around all of it.

They also learn restraint. A child who gouges a wall is not told merely that property has been damaged. The offense is explained as an unauthorized instruction. Every cut tells living matter to do something: divide, thicken, seal, turn aside. Careless marks may take years to undo. Children nevertheless carve initials into rail roots, usually in places where the grain will stretch the letters into illegibility. Adults pretend not to know who does it.

Play follows the town’s slowness. Children ride the root tram without boarding it, running alongside the slight traveling rise in the street and trying to keep a foot atop the moving ridge. They float seed husks through flushing channels after first runoff. They build tiny houses from pruned fibers, then leave them in damp corners to see which ones root. A structure that survives is no longer considered a toy and must be reported to the nursery office.

Adolescents gather in the broad dark spaces between lamps. The town offers many concealed places but few truly private ones. Walls transmit murmurs. Floors react to pacing. A vine elevator used after curfew leaves measurable tension. Consequently, secrecy is practiced outdoors, in the vacant squares and cooling banks where there are no rooms to listen. Courtship often consists of walking the embankment slowly enough to be mistaken for inspectors.

Marriage does not traditionally merge property, since neither spouse can own the living structure around them. Instead, couples file for a shared interval: permission to occupy whatever rooms remain mutually connected through an agreed number of growth cycles. Some unions begin with the grafting of two door frames. Others end the same way, when the frames are cut apart and trained to seal over. Divorces may leave visible scars for decades.

Families are not always organized by building. A house can divide around them or join them to strangers. When two residences graft and are declared one organism, their occupants acquire obligations to one another: shared maintenance, coordinated heat, collective liability for harmful cuts. Such involuntary households are called joins. Some become close. Others preserve elaborate internal borders, marked by hanging cords, changes in floor bark, or a line of bitter moss the common wall will not cross.

Privacy is understood as a temporary condition produced by good maintenance.

Ownership, likewise, attaches to removable things. People prize knives, kettles, clocks, glass lamps, ceramic basins, spools of wire, and tools with family histories. Furniture is light enough to move when rooms narrow. Large wardrobes are rare; clothing is stored in wall recesses that may slowly change shape around it. It is common to find a forgotten coat sealed behind new growth, flattened and preserved. Such discoveries belong to the finder unless a name tag remains legible.

Metal carries emotional weight because it does not grow. A steel square inherited from a grandparent seems wonderfully definite. Rings, buckles, and keys are given at important moments, though keys may outlast their doors. People keep obsolete keys anyway. Many households have a bowl of them, each attached to a story about a room that closed, softened, joined another building, or became part of a cistern.

Food comes partly from the structures themselves, but eating the town is surrounded by rules. Only designated growth may be harvested: roof fruit, gutter cress, pale pith from thickening partitions, fungal loaves raised in compost warmth, and translucent pods grown along clean-water channels. Structural tissue is never cut casually. The distinction between food and wall is legal rather than botanical, and poaching can look very much like carpentry.

Meals are mild, earthy, sour, and often warm rather than hot. Fuel is too valuable to waste on long boiling. Pressure cookers are common, their valves pulsing quietly in kitchens. People eat ceramic-baked roots, snail-sized dumplings of fungal protein, pickled shoots, bitter leaves, and thin cakes cooked on pipes carrying waste heat from the mills. Real animal fat is rare enough to be medicinal, ceremonial, or scandalously expensive.

Most inhabitants have never seen livestock. Their idea of a cow is large, imprecise, and somewhat mechanical. School illustrations emphasize udders and digestion, making the creature resemble a walking factory. Dogs are known from old stories but are often imagined as foxes that belong to someone.

The Bestiary is among the town’s most beloved records. Officially, it exists to document animal incursions and possible ecological disturbance. In practice, its entries are full of wonder disguised as bureaucracy.

*A red fox, adult, sex uncertain, entered through South Culvert after second rain. Paused beside the ceramic works. Looked upward at assembled witnesses. No damage except three crushed moss cups and interruption of Belt Six for forty-two minutes.*

Pages include moth emergences, unusual snails, a lost goose that remained overnight on a leaf roof, and once, many years ago, a horse. The horse’s visit occupies seventeen pages and several conflicting diagrams. No living witness agrees on its color. A hoofprint was cast in plaster and displayed in the civic hall until roots lifted the case apart.

When a rare animal appears, the townspeople become profoundly still. They do not know how to approach without directing it, and they are accustomed to everything around them answering pressure. An animal answers in ways they cannot predict. It may flee from kindness, ignore an open route, damage nothing useful, choose nothing prepared. This freedom unsettles them. For weeks afterward, children imitate it by running abruptly across squares.

Death is handled through descent. Bodies are not buried in soil, because there is none, nor are they given directly to the town’s structural roots. The dead are wrapped in spent membrane and placed in compost vaults tended by fungal stewards. Families may request that the resulting material feed a nursery, a heat bed, or a non-load-bearing garden. Feeding a load-bearing wall is forbidden; no one should be made, even poetically, to support a building without consent.

Names of the dead are cut into replaceable bark tablets rather than permanent monuments. The tablets weather, darken, and eventually become illegible. Before that happens, a relative may recut the name. If no one does, the tablet is composted. This is not considered erasure. The town keeps too much already.

Religious beliefs vary, but most local rites acknowledge pressure, release, and return. Bells are unsuitable—their vibration travels too widely—so ceremonies use taut wires that hum when touched or hollow stems sounded by breath. Silence is not sacred by itself. Attentiveness is.

There are those who believe the basin is one organism and every apparent plant merely an organ of it. There are those who insist it is a colony of competing lives held together by law and pruning. The disagreement has theological and political consequences. If the basin is one body, then cutting anywhere is a concern for all. If it is a commonwealth of bodies, then each district may possess some right to its own growth.

The Office of Strain avoids the question. Its documents refer to “the supporting life.”

Not everyone is patient. The town produces rebels against slowness: young machinists who build fast motors, homeowners who line cupboards with forbidden sealant, factory managers who release counterweights early, lovers who carve openings without permits. Speed has glamour because it seems independent of weather. A machine that spins continuously is thrilling and faintly obscene.

Yet fast devices rarely last. They shake their mountings loose, heat the damp walls, or provoke compensating growth that swallows their housings. Their makers often become excellent conventional engineers after the failures. Others leave for the gravel flats and return years later walking too hard.

Those who leave are marked by the town in subtler ways. They dislike sealed floors. They wake at the sound of heavy rain and look for channels. They find fixed buildings eerie, as though dead but undecayed. In cities of stone, they touch walls and feel nothing answering. Some find this liberating. Others cannot sleep.

Those who remain develop a tolerance for being rearranged. They may come home to find a cupboard jammed shut, a floor risen under the table, or a hairline of pale tissue closing across a window corner. They curse, fetch tools, file a notice if necessary, and make accommodations. Their fatalism is limited. They do not submit to the basin; they collaborate with it argumentatively.

The vegetation itself lives at several speeds.

The great column stalks at intersections are slow, civic creatures. They hold moisture deep in their ribs and can outlive the districts around them. Their outer layers are scarred by generations of signs, collars, tram wires, and official measurements. Some lean toward old sources of water that no longer exist. They are not worshiped, but meetings are held near them because they make everyone speak more quietly.

House bodies are more responsive. They thicken around warmth, reinforce repeated loads, and sometimes close neglected spaces. Whether they possess anything like intention is debated most fiercely by those who live in them. Every family can tell of a helpful wall and a malicious door. Engineers attribute both to gradients.

The pressure trunks of the industrial quarter live strenuous, shortened lives. They are bred for swelling and constrained from every side by frames. During rain they drink until their tissues become taut enough to lift beams weighing many tons. Their expansion is slow but irresistible. Workers tend them with a mixture of horticultural care and mechanical caution, checking for rot, uneven fiber, mineral starvation, and what they call reluctance—a swelling that begins later than expected despite adequate water.

When an old pressure trunk is retired, the weights are removed over several weeks. Its iron braces are loosened one by one. Some trunks split as soon as the strain leaves them. Others continue lifting against absent loads, raising empty beams into the air as though remembering work.

Fungal life occupies nearly every hidden boundary. Pale cords carry dead matter downward and return minerals to growing walls. They bind old pulp, digest spilled oil, clog neglected drains, and occasionally invade insulation. Fungal stewards know them by texture and smell. Some are dry and papery, some cold as wet thread, some faintly luminous when torn. The most useful strains are never seen in fruit. They remain within the mat, performing the dark municipal labor of conversion.

Mosses live closer to human notice. They line gutters, soften yards, indicate leaks, and change color when water is contaminated. Householders cultivate small diagnostic patches beside sinks. A silvering moss may mean clean condensation; yellow tips suggest metal salts; sudden blackening brings a water inspector. Children stroke the patches despite being told that oils from their hands interfere with the reading.

The few animals live at the edges of function.

Pale moths inhabit the mill roofs, where the leaf-skin turns moonlight into a dim green glow. Their larvae feed on membranes already marked for replacement and are tolerated so long as they observe this distinction. The narrow snails clean mineral films from channels. Workers move them aside before first runoff and return them afterward.

The black birds are less cooperative. They arrive after rain with no reliable warning, glossy and sharp-eyed, landing along tram lines to pry out the hard metallic-looking seeds lodged in the grain. They have learned the timing of the root wave and step aside just before the rail rises. Attempts to frighten them fail. They collect bright wire clippings and sometimes drop them into cisterns. The Office classifies them as a recurrent obstruction. Everyone else watches for them.

There are also lives that remain disputed: tremors in the deepest mat, warm hollows no survey has mapped, fruiting bodies found crushed beneath districts where no known fungus was introduced. Pipe listeners occasionally report pulses that do not match pumps, roots, weather, or tram movement. The official explanation is transferred strain.

The oldest inhabitants use the same words and smile.

At evening, the people rise into view while much of the supporting life withdraws from sight. Mill crews cross the squares carrying lunch tins. Water sorters close sluices. A surveyor may remain kneeling in the dusk, one hand on the street, waiting for a tension to resolve. Families light their lamps. Behind amber panes, familiar silhouettes bend over tables in rooms that are very slowly changing shape around them.

Under those rooms, trunks release the day’s pressure. Fungal cords draw down what has fallen. Roots tighten across the basin in long, communicating bands. Moths stir in translucent roofs. A black bird, late to leave, tucks its head beneath one wing on a warm transmission pipe.

No single creature can be said to inhabit the town more fully than the others. The humans maintain it, the plants support and remake it, the fungi remember its losses, and the machines translate one kind of patience into another.

Together they live in something that is neither shelter nor landscape, neither tool nor body, but an agreement among all four—revised by weather, enforced by strain, and never signed in final form.
