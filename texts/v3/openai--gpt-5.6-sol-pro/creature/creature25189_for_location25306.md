At first there seems to be nothing in the hollow that is not already accounted for by stone.

The largest pool is dark beneath its overhang. A rounded mass occupies one side of it, rising just above the waterline and continuing below until its outline is lost among the submerged bosses. It resembles a fallen slab thickened with calcite. One end narrows into a pale ledge; the other merges with a folded curtain of tufa. Droplets strike it without producing any response.

Only after waiting does the error become apparent.

The droplets do not strike indiscriminately. They land in a line of shallow pits along the thing’s back. Each pit has a polished center and a raised mineral rim, as though the body has remained in precisely the same position for decades. Beneath the clear water, something closes over one of the pale crustaceans. It is not a jaw. It is more like the slow folding of a hand whose fingers have been webbed together.

The inhabitant is low, broad, and nearly colorless. Fully extended, it might be as long as a person is tall, though it is seldom possible to tell where its body ends. Its limbs lie fitted into channels in the pool, bent at angles that imitate broken stone. The forelimbs are slender near the joints and heavy at their ends. Each bears four blunt digits, joined by translucent skin and capped not with claws but with dark, porous pads. These pads hold to wet limestone without slipping. The hind limbs are stronger, though so tightly folded beneath the body that they appear to be part of its bulk.

It has no scales. Its skin is soft where the water continually washes it and armored where it rises into air. Mineral has accumulated there in overlapping ivory plates. Some are no thicker than eggshell; others have built into knobs and shelves colonized by the pearly bacterial film. Ochre lines branch under the plates wherever iron-rich seepage follows the creases of the body. When it moves, the newer deposits crack with a sound like cooling pottery. Older crusts remain behind, perfect fragments of shoulder, flank, or knuckle gradually incorporated into the pool.

Its head rests beside the third bowl.

That is the part most often mistaken for an object made by human hands. The skull is shallow and dish-shaped, with a broad rim above the mouth. Its upper surface catches water. In the center are two sealed depressions where eyes might once have been useful. Thin membranes cover them, filmed with calcite. The creature does not open them. Light has no importance beyond the faint warmth it brings at midday.

Below the blind hollows, the face is crowded with small pits. Some test heat. Some detect the mineral composition of the water. Others feel changes in pressure so slight that the fall of a droplet anywhere in the chamber arrives as a distinct event. Through the stone and connected basins it senses the hollow all at once: the displacement of a crustacean in a crack, the entrance of cooler rainwater into the ridge, the weight of a foot placed on the first path stone.

The mouth is a narrow horizontal seam. It opens rarely and never wide. Inside are no cutting teeth, only rows of soft ridges over which water passes. The inhabitant feeds by filtering the seep. Bacterial threads, mineral-bound organic matter, tiny eggs, salt, iron, and whatever the porous limestone has carried down from the surface are caught in folds of dark tissue. Now and then one of the little crustaceans is taken, but the creature does not hunt them. It simply remains where they must eventually pass.

Its breath is difficult to distinguish from the chemistry of the pool. Long slits along the neck draw oxygen from the water. At intervals measured in many minutes, a valve in the back opens and releases a single bubble. The bubble creeps from beneath a calcite plate, lingers, and breaks without noise. In drier air the creature could also breathe through a small chamber behind its mouth, but the hollow is never dry enough to require this. The saturated atmosphere is an extension of the pool.

It moves most during the deepest part of the night, though “moves” suggests more activity than occurs. One forelimb loosens from a basin. The head inclines. The body advances the width of a finger and settles again. A journey from the large pool to the dripping bowl may take until dawn. It follows the laid stones, not by walking on them but by bracing between them. Their smooth tops are not worn solely by human feet. The dark pads of its hands have passed over the same places thousands of times.

The bowls matter to it.

It drinks from none of them, since water surrounds it, but it attends to their sound. Each vessel produces a different vibration when struck by a drop. The two bowls filled with stone have fallen silent. The third continues to ring faintly through the ledge, and the inhabitant often rests its chin against the niche below it. If the bowl shifts, a blunt hand returns it beneath the drip. This may be why its rim is worn more heavily on one side.

Whether it learned this from the people who once visited the hollow cannot be known. It may have watched them without sight, feeling their steps and the knocks of pottery against stone. It may have understood only that they placed hollow objects beneath falling water and returned when the objects changed weight. After they stopped coming, it continued the arrangement in its own slow fashion.

The slab platform also bears signs of its use. In colder intervals the inhabitant raises part of its body onto the stone and lies where the mineral-warmed seep passes nearest the surface. Its weight has bowed one of the slabs. The notch beside the pool accepts the narrow end of its forelimb exactly, though that may be coincidence. Many things in the chamber fit one another after enough centuries.

Its age cannot be read from size. Growth and encrustation are too easily confused. It sheds no annual layer, has no season of mating, and may spend years in a state so quiet that even its tissues scarcely renew themselves. A portion of the bacterial film lives within its skin and provides what the seep alone does not. The crustaceans clean dead material from the creases under its mineral plates. Their relationship is neither tame nor affectionate, but it is old.

There may once have been others.

Along the deepest wall are three shapes beneath the tufa: a curved ribbed patch, a line of four blunt impressions, and something like the sealed hollow of a head. They could be fossils formed by ordinary dripping. They could be bodies that ceased moving and were accepted into the architecture. The living inhabitant sometimes presses itself against that wall for days, its face laid over the smallest of the forms.

It makes no call. A voice would travel nowhere useful. When disturbed, it does not flee, because there is nowhere to flee to. Instead it lowers itself until water covers its back, empties the small air chamber behind its mouth, and becomes heavier. Its soft portions draw beneath the mineral plates. The pool stills above it. What remains visible is a collection of pale ridges no different from all the others.

If touched, however, the entire hollow seems to answer.

Water spills over several basin rims at once. The crustaceans retreat. The pearl films contract against the rock. One blind head lifts beside the old bowl, carrying a delicate burden of new stone. The movement is neither quick nor threatening. It has the grave resistance of something dislodged from a place to which it has been fitted.

Then the inhabitant settles again. Warm water returns to its established level. Droplets resume their sequence. Its breathing disappears among the pores and cracks, and before long it is impossible to say whether a creature lives in the chamber or whether the chamber itself has, for a moment, rearranged one of its stones.
