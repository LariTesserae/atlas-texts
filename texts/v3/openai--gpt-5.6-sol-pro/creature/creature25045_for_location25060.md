The first inhabitants are not the animals.

They are the pale cells inside the rock, living where rainwater enters hairline fractures and carries traces of iron, sulfur, and old volcanic glass. They grow as stains too thin to see individually. Some darken stone and warm it slightly in sunlight; others live beyond light, feeding on chemical differences between one mineral layer and the next. Their generations leave microscopic partitions behind them, narrowing pores into chambers and opening other pores with weak acids. Much of what appears to be weathered stone is, in fact, inhabited stone.

Above them lies a second, more continuous creaturehood: films, crusts, cords, and mats. Algae seal exposed surfaces in green-black skins. Lichens stand in minute branching forests. Fungal threads pass through soil, rotten wood, root tissue, mineral deposits, and the bodies of dormant spores. No single fungal body spans the plateau, but adjoining networks meet, exchange compounds, invade one another, retreat, and reconnect. Together they make a shifting subterranean mosaic whose borders may persist for centuries while the living filaments composing them are replaced every season.

These networks are not a mind beneath the land. They do not know the forests they join. Yet they remember in simpler ways. A route repeatedly enriched by the passage of a herd becomes thick with transport cords. A poisoned seep is walled off. The chemical signature of drought remains in hardened knots long after rain returns. Signals travel outward from damaged roots, sometimes farther than any animal’s scent. At night, accumulated electrical differences discharge through wet membranes, and pale pulses pass from mound to mound like heat lightning buried under moss.

The trees above are among the oldest visible inhabitants, though age does not make them tall. Their trunks thicken sideways, buttressing themselves against saturated ground and loading winds. A mature tree may divide into twenty or thirty low crowns, each appearing from a distance to belong to a separate individual. Where a branch touches moss, it roots. Where a rooted limb is buried by peat, it becomes a new trunk. Whole groves may therefore be one ancient life repeatedly laid down, lifted, divided, and joined again.

Their small leaves turn silver-side upward under changes in humidity. This is not merely an effect of wind. The leaves alter their angle as pressure falls, reducing the force of incoming rain and exposing pores that absorb cloud droplets. The brightening that passes across a watershed is the forest preparing itself.

Nearly every tree carries several other forests. Curtains of lichen hang from shaded forks. Cup plants hold cold water in their leaves, and within each cup live transparent larvae, grazing mites, coiled worms, and flat-bodied scavengers no larger than flakes of skin. Mosses climb the bark in generations, lower growth dying into peat while fresh growth rises over it. Some trunks are wrapped so deeply that the tree’s true surface lies an arm’s length inside its green covering.

The ribbed vent columns are more difficult to call plants.

They begin as low, soft crowns around warm seeps. Their outer cells harvest the pale daylight, but most of their substance is sustained by symbiotic colonies within. Fungal tissue provides the walls. Bacteria precipitate mineral rings. Rootlike threads draw in dead vegetation and contaminated water. Over centuries the crown lifts, leaving beneath it a hollow, chambered stalk.

A mature column is a congregation held in one shape. Its cool outer skin, warm core, nitrogen-fixing chambers, metal-binding films, decomposer sacs, and pressure bladders are genetically unrelated occupants that cannot live alone in the same form. If one partner fails, neighboring chambers may seal it behind ceramic partitions and grow around the loss. Old columns contain entire buried histories of former arrangements: blocked vents, abandoned nurseries, fossil membranes, and bands of nickel, iron, and silica extracted from waters that no longer flow there.

Small animals live in their walls. Mineral-shelled grazers pass through pore galleries, scraping excess microbial film. Blind valve-keepers cluster around flexible openings and feed on anything that might prevent them from closing. Slender heat-seeking predators patrol the inner chambers, guided by gradients rather than sight. When gas pressure rises, these animals withdraw into side pockets moments before the entire column exhales.

Across a vent field, the releases spread from tower to tower. They are not perfectly synchronized. The first soft breath lowers pressure in the shared gravel bed; distant columns respond, then others beyond them. A white band rises across the basin. Spores, aromatic waxes, bacterial capsules, and tiny dormant animals travel in it. What looks like waste vapor is also migration.

The tarns have their own great populations. Their submerged lawns are grown by low, ribbon-leaved organisms rooted into mineral crust. Thousands of amphibious grazers crop them. These grazers are broad, smooth-backed animals with six short limbs held close against the body while swimming. Most of their head is occupied by a grinding plate. Their eyes are small and directed upward, useful mainly for detecting the change that occurs when a shadow crosses cloudlight.

They breathe through narrow tubes along the spine. A herd can remain submerged with only these tubes breaking the surface, so that a crowded tarn appears empty except for rainlike rings. Individuals maintain contact through pressure flicks made with the tail. When one detects danger, no alarm cry follows. A single pulse passes through the water, then another; the whole herd presses itself into the dark lawn, changing the tarn’s color.

Their young are soft, threadlike, and scarcely resemble the adults. They spend their first season inside the floating wax layers of warm bogs, eating microbes trapped at the water’s surface. As ultraviolet light breaks the wax into plates, the young descend with the fragments. Thus the bog’s chemistry determines when a generation enters the tarns.

On firmer ground live the armored scrapers. Some are small enough to graze the film from a single leaf; others are the size of a hand and travel in files along wet stone. Their shells are built from layered mineral pellets, each new plate incorporating local metals. A colony from an amber spring may be yellow-brown, while one only a valley away is green-black and faintly magnetic. Predators can taste where they came from.

The broad-footed browsers are larger, though “large” is concealed here. Their bodies are low and deep, carried on six legs. Each foot opens on contact into six fleshy divisions that spread across the root mat, then folds tight when lifted. They do not step over the ground so much as settle upon it and withdraw.

Their mouths work sideways. Paired jaw combs strip moss, bark scales, waxed leaves, and the softened surfaces of fallen trunks. Woody food ferments in a chain of warm internal chambers, and a full meal may take days to process. During that time an old browser stands nearly motionless, legs locked under its weight, head sunk between raised shoulders.

Its back is not merely dirty or overgrown. It bears a developed garden.

The skin forms ridges that trap peat without allowing it to press against breathing pores. Along these ridges grow mosses and shallow-rooted ferns. Cup-shaped plants retain rainwater. Small armored insects clean fungal growth from the skin and eat parasites around the folds of the legs. Their waste feeds the garden; the garden insulates the animal; its roots wick excess moisture away from lesions. Some back plants occur nowhere else on the plateau. Their spores germinate only after passing through the mouthparts of the cleaning insects, and those insects spend their whole lives on one host or its offspring.

Young browsers begin almost bare. They acquire their first moss by pressing beneath adults during rain. Family membership can be read in the composition of their gardens.

The oldest individuals are difficult to distinguish from root mounds. Ferns soften the outline of their flanks, and pools collect between the shoulders. Saplings occasionally germinate on them, though most are shaken loose before their roots penetrate far. When one of these animals finally moves after a long rest, water spills from its back and small gliding creatures scatter from the foliage.

The gliders are delicate only in flight. Folded among leaves, they resemble dark, many-jointed seed pods. Their membranes extend between four lateral limbs and the long rear body, translucent except for a branching network of blood vessels. They do not launch into open air. They drop through narrow interior gaps, spread for a heartbeat, alter direction with the tail, and vanish into the next crown. Generations may pass without one crossing above the canopy.

They communicate by drumming their hooked feet against branches. The sound scarcely enters the air. It runs through wood and can be answered by an animal hidden several trees away. Courtship may continue for hours as alternating patterns traveling through interconnected trunks, while to an observer the forest remains silent.

The birdlike animals are clothed in soft, divided feathers that shed mist without producing the hiss of ordinary flight. Many have broad wings and very little breast muscle. They move by short descents from branch to branch, climbing afterward with beak and hooked feet. Their colors are strongest where their bodies are least often seen: ultraviolet bars beneath wings, cold blue patches inside throat folds, silver flashes along the tail. Display is brief and close-range because nothing here can count on being visible from far away.

Their two-note calls are felt as much as heard. The lower note passes through inflated chest sacs and enters trunks through the feet. A prospective mate may answer from the other side of a dense grove without revealing its position to a hunter listening in the air.

The long-bodied predators listen elsewhere.

They lie against fallen wood with the lower jaw resting on bark or soil. Sensitive plates along the jaw detect the alternating pressure of feet, the gnawing of internal insects, the contraction of roots drawing water, and the faint repeated signals of gliders. Their nervous systems are adapted less to sudden sound than to separation: which vibration belongs to rain, which to transported water, which to a six-footed browser, and which to another predator imitating one.

Their bodies are flexible enough to follow the channels between roots. Short limbs can fold into grooves along the flanks. The skin bears broken stripes of moss-gray, amber, and wet black, but concealment depends more on stillness than color. Some carry patches of living lichen in shallow pits along the spine. Unlike the browsers’ gardens, these growths seem to function chiefly as scent masks.

They seldom chase. A predator selects a place where several root pathways narrow, presses its jaw to the ground, and waits. It may allow dozens of animals to pass before striking at the one whose gait reveals injury, pregnancy, or the weight of a mineral-rich shell. The attack is rapid and almost soundless. The struggle is brief because prolonged violence would advertise the body through every connected root.

Death summons an assembly.

Burrowing scavengers arrive first, guided by fluids entering the soil. They open the body from below. Canopy scavengers descend along lianas and feed at exposed joints. Tiny specialists enter the fermenting chambers of browsers; others take only eyes, marrow, garden peat, shell, blood salts, or the mineralized pads of the feet. Fungal cords rise through the ground and penetrate what remains. Certain plants germinate only in this interval, their roots adapted to follow the brief outward pulse of nitrogen.

No scavenger consumes the whole. The body is portioned by the order in which its tissues change. Within days its shape is lost. Within weeks its harder elements have been carried into burrows, nests, vent walls, eggshells, and living bark. The back garden often outlives the animal briefly as a green mound, then divides among the ground and the hides of those that fed there.

At the escarpments, other forms occupy the falling water. Rooted wheels hang in persistent cascades, each composed of many flexible vanes around a tough living hub. The current turns them slowly. Their motion crushes drifting husks against rough internal plates, releasing seeds and pulp into sheltered basins behind the wheel. Larvae develop there, protected from the full force of the fall. The wheel gains nutrients from their waste; the larvae feed on the ground seed; the plant’s own spores adhere to the emerging adults.

In the spray beyond the ledges live nearly weightless filterers, wide as hands and thin as wet paper. They cling to mineral curtains when the falls are strong and release when humidity rises, sailing within the cliff cloud. Their translucent bodies carry symbiotic algae, so they feed from both light and mist. Some never touch the plateau interior. Their world is a vertical chain of wet ledges descending toward lowlands they cannot survive in.

None of these beings sees the whole country.

A browser may know a migration circuit three basins long, remembered through water taste and root vibration. A glider may spend its life in one interconnected grove. A vent inhabitant may never encounter unheated air. A fungal border may advance for centuries toward another watershed without reaching it. Even the oldest clonal trees possess only the weather and chemistry that pass through their own tissues.

Yet each carries some distant part of the plateau. Salts climb in animal bodies. Spores ride vapor from the towers. Minerals pass from rock cells into grazers, from grazers into predators, from predators into flowering mounds. Water drawn by one forest enters cloud and rains upon another. The inhabitants do not coordinate this exchange, but neither do they exist apart from it.

They are quiet not because little happens among them, but because air is only one of their mediums, and often the least useful one. Their messages travel in wet wood, pressured water, electric membranes, disturbed wax, altered scent, turned leaves, and tremors beneath the moss.

To stand among them would be to mistake silence for secrecy. Nothing is hidden by intention. It is simply occurring in forms too distributed, too slow, too small, or too deeply joined to be witnessed all at once.
