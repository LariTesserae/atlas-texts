At first they are difficult to distinguish from the city.

A figure resting beside a root curtain may seem to be another loop of pale timber, a bundle of tools hung from a peg, or a shrine wrapped in weed. Their stillness is complete enough for seed dust to settle on their shoulders. Tiny grazers cross their wrists. The breathing folds along their ribs open and close so slowly that an unfamiliar observer might wait several moments before realizing the figure is alive.

Then one hand turns.

The fingers are long and divided by narrow webs, not broad enough to make the hand a paddle but enough to give each gesture weight against the water. One pair of fingers hooks around a guide rope. The feet release from the stone. Small bladders at the belt are shifted, a stone weight slid from hip to belly, and the citizen rises without anything resembling a leap. Their body does not swim so much as alter its agreement with depth.

Most are slender, though not frail. Their chests are deep from front to back and narrow across the shoulders. Along the lower ribs and beneath the jaw lie pleated breathing seams, dark when closed and wine-colored when opened. These are efficient only in well-tended water. A citizen can cross a stagnant shaft or endure a mineral-clouded lane, but prolonged labor away from a breathing garden leaves the seams swollen and the mind slow. Houses are therefore alive partly for the sake of air: every inhabited room contains strings of oxygen weed, porous root fans, or trays of blue-green slime stirred at intervals with a comb.

Their skin varies with district and ancestry. Some are the matte gray of old shell, some brown-black with a dull violet cast, others pale enough that branching vessels show beneath the surface. Children are often translucent at the fingers, eyelids, and fins. Over the years mineral exposure freckles the body with green, rust, and white. Gardeners acquire permanent stains around the nails. Vent workers grow dark calluses like polished stone along the palms, knees, and outer forearms.

They have hair, though not in the dry abundance imagined in pictures assembled from objects fallen from above. It grows in sparse cords or soft crests, usually braided close to the skull and threaded with identifying beads. Loose hair is troublesome among vines and pulley lines. Some shave it with glass, while others train living filaments through the braids until the head carries a small garden of luminous threads. This is beautiful, expensive, and slightly dangerous: neglected ceremonial growth may root into a sleeping mat or knot itself around a handrail.

Their eyes are large but not monstrous. In the middle terraces the iris appears black, swallowing what little light descends. Near a glow bowl it contracts to reveal rings of bronze, pearl, red, or cloudy green. A clear inner lid protects the eye while cutting stone and swimming through seed clouds. Vision matters less below the warm cracks. There the citizens navigate through touch, remembered pressure, and the faint tremors carried by structural cords.

Their ears are small external ridges surrounding deep pressure chambers. Sudden noise hurts them. A hammer blow nearby can leave a listener nauseated or unable to balance, and children are taught never to clap stones together. Their voices are low and compact. Words are shaped in the mouth and throat but also pulsed through the chest, so conversation at close range may be felt through joined wrists more clearly than heard.

For strangers, their silence is the most unsettling thing about them. For one another, they are rarely silent at all.

A turn of the palm can mean *wait*, *danger above*, or *I acknowledge your claim*, depending on whether the fingers are spread. Two touches along the forearm ask a question. A thumb pressed into the hollow of the wrist is an intimate reassurance. Workers communicate along shared ropes with plucks too subtle for an untrained hand to separate from current. Lovers may sit on opposite sides of a room with a cord between them, exchanging patterns under the sound threshold of the household.

Names have several parts because sound alone is unreliable. A person has a spoken name, a hand-name traced against the palm, and an anchor-name locating them among kin and district. The spoken name may change as the voice changes. The hand-name is usually kept for life. The anchor-name changes whenever the household moves, splits, or is carried out of alignment by the mountain.

Thus someone might be introduced aloud as Sava, signed as a hooked line crossing the base of the thumb, and formally identified as:

*Of the Root-Through-Glass household, two terraces warmward of the Split Vent, held beneath the Lantern Orchard.*

When a building shifts enough to alter the address, the household’s identity shifts with it. This is not regarded as administrative inconvenience. It is one of the ordinary ways in which people become different people.

They possess no single notion corresponding exactly to up and down. There is *stoneward*, meaning toward mass, support, and remembered ground; *lightward*, toward the paling water; *warmward*, toward a vent or heated layer; *deepward*, toward pressure and danger; and *looseward*, meaning away from any reliable anchor. Looseward can describe a direction, an object, a promise, or a person whose judgment has become untrustworthy.

Their bodies are made for choosing among these directions. A narrow fin runs from the outer calf to the heel, and another folds along each forearm. Neither is impressive when spread. Their purpose is correction, not speed. Fast swimmers are considered reckless, vaguely comic, and often young. An adult in a public lane moves by short pulls, pauses, and adjustments of the belt. The ideal is to arrive without stirring anyone’s garden.

Belts are the nearest thing to universal dress. They are broad, intricately knotted, and built in layers. Every item has a counterweight. A glass blade worn on the left may be balanced by a shell case on the right; a gas-fruit at the spine offsets a pouch of pegs at the belly. Skilled citizens can identify an occupation by balance alone. Masons hang low and stoneward. Seed keepers are nearly neutral, able to work for hours in a suspended orchard. Couriers carry multiple tiny bladders that can be pierced one by one to change depth quickly. Children wear training belts with brightly colored floats, though they still often drift upside down.

Adornment is not separate from utility. A polished shell clasp may also be a measuring gauge. Family histories are recorded in belt knots. Courtship gifts are finely matched pairs of weight and lift: a carved stone and a sealed seedpod, equal in effect though unlike in substance. To accept only one half is an insult. To wear both is an answer.

They do not divide themselves neatly by profession. Nearly everyone gardens because nearly every room must breathe. Nearly everyone knows basic knotting because a failed cord can turn a street into wreckage. Nearly everyone learns to listen to rock.

Still, there are recognized callings.

Rootwrights persuade plants into architecture. They do not merely prune and tie. They abrade selected roots, expose them to mineral solutions, split their growing tips, and guide the divisions through drilled stone. Their plans are drawn as sequences of future tensions. A rootwright may begin a doorway knowing it will not bear weight for thirty years. The most respected are those who can imagine a building after their own deaths.

Layer keepers study the invisible boundaries in the water. They suspend strings of beads, oils, wood slivers, and mineral flakes to learn where one density gives way to another. They know which lanes become treacherous during distant storms and which sleeping chambers will turn drowsy when cold water descends. Their instruments are simple; their records are immense. Much civic authority belongs to them because a district can survive a mistaken boundary marker for only so long.

Vent tenders cook, fire rare ceramics, harvest mineral crust, and watch for changes in the mountain’s heat. Their skin is often scarred pale around the breathing seams. They have a reputation for bluntness, perhaps because ambiguity is dangerous near trapped gas and hot fissures.

Listeners are part mason, part diviner, part engineer. They tap the rock and interpret its reply. Some insist there is no mystery in the work: cracks, voids, tension, and thickness all have distinct answers. Others maintain that the mountain possesses moods too slow to be called thoughts. Most listeners hold both views without difficulty.

The mountain does not speak, they say, but it answers.

Children begin life in the upper, brighter rooms, though not necessarily in the house of their parents. The young are soft-boned and dangerously buoyant. For their first seasons they wear little anklets of warm stone and sleep in woven cradles closed on all sides. A startled infant can otherwise rise into rafters, fruiting vines, or a heavy-water boundary from which it cannot correct itself.

They are born with sealed breathing folds and draw nourishment through an attached vascular cord for several days while attendants gradually expose them to garden water. The first full opening of the gills is treated as the true birth. The household dims its glow bowls, everyone becomes still, and the oldest present places two fingers against the infant’s ribs to feel the first independent rhythm.

Young children are louder than adults. They click stones, slap the water, chase loose seeds, and shout merely to feel rooms answer them. Entire neighborhoods participate in teaching them restraint. This is done gently. A child who makes a dangerous sound is not told that noise is wicked, but is led along the cords to discover how far the vibration traveled and whom it woke.

Their games teach buoyancy. They exchange belt pieces underwater and attempt to remain level. They navigate orchards blindfolded by following temperature alone. They build tiny cities from shell chips on layers of water, adjusting fruit skins and stone dust until the structures hang in place. The best constructions last for weeks and become inconvenient to dismantle once moss begins using them.

Adolescents test themselves at the city’s edges. They swim farther from anchors than their elders approve, visit abandoned ridges, or descend until their belts feel strangely light. Some return with black mineral flowers, lost tools, or stories of lamps moving beneath the neutral depths. Some return changed in less visible ways, unable afterward to sleep unless tied securely to stone.

Their elders grow dense. Cartilage slowly mineralizes, the skin thickens, and old injuries gather deposits from the water. Very old citizens require fewer belt weights and become markedly stoneward. They prefer the lower rooms, where warmth eases stiff joints and the floor can be held with both feet.

Age is associated not with frailty but with anchoring. An elder may be unable to cross a buoyant avenue without assistance yet sit through a mountain groan without drifting a finger’s width. During civic arguments, the oldest participants often attach themselves directly to the speaking stone. Their steadiness is both practical and symbolic.

They have no rulers in the sense of a person who gives commands to the whole city. Authority follows the problem. A rootwright has precedence when a district’s living braces are failing. A layer keeper decides whether a lane must close. A household’s eldest settles questions concerning its anchor, unless the mountain has shifted the anchor beyond recognition, in which case neighboring households join the decision.

Large disputes are heard in a suspended public volume. The disputants occupy marked positions around a central stone, while witnesses hold radial cords. Since raised voices are unacceptable, argument proceeds through sign, colored panels, and pressure transmitted along the lines. A persuasive speaker is said to “keep all cords even.” One who manipulates outrage or fear is accused of “pulling the circle crooked.”

Their most serious crimes involve detachment: cutting an occupied structure loose, falsifying a depth marker, damaging a breathing garden, or removing someone’s counterweight in open water. Theft matters, but sabotage of balance is worse. Punishment rarely means confinement; enclosed stagnant water would require others to maintain it. Instead offenders may be made to labor visibly at public anchors, their belts marked with an unbalanced knot until restitution is complete.

They eat slowly.

Meals consist of sliced gas-fruit pulp, soft leaf hearts, mineral-fed moss cakes, fermented seed paste, and strands of ribbon plant scraped of their tough skins. Hot food is treasured. A sealed pot brought up from a vent is opened inside a hood of woven leaves so its heat and aroma do not vanish immediately into the water. Diners gather close, passing morsels hand to hand.

There are few animals to eat, and killing one is never casual. Thread-worms may be harvested from vents, but only in numbers carefully counted. Glass mites are sometimes ground into medicinal paste after they die. A blind fish is almost never taken. If one enters a garden and feeds there, the household accepts the loss as a kind of visitation.

The citizens do not imagine animals as lesser versions of themselves. They imagine them as older witnesses.

Plants, by contrast, are younger kin: abundant, patient, acquisitive, always becoming part of whatever they touch. A house root receives praise when it strengthens a wall and reprimand when it closes a passage. Gardeners speak to their crops in pulses tapped against trellises. Whether the plants respond is disputed, but they certainly react to vibration, warmth, and touch, and some old orchards alter their flowering after the death of a longtime keeper.

Death is a form of reassignment.

Bodies are not buried, for there is little loose earth, and they are not released into open water, for descent without return is reserved for the lost. Instead the dead are wrapped in root fiber and placed within a chosen wall, orchard frame, or mineral hollow. Breathing plants are trained over them. Over years the body feeds a structural grove, and the household incorporates a bead, tooth, or small bone into its anchor cord.

A person who becomes part of a load-bearing root is said to remain *under tension*. This is honorable. Descendants touch the root when asking patience or courage. If the root later dies, the remaining bones are moved, but the old location continues to be addressed as though someone still listens there.

Their grief is almost soundless. Mourners alter their balance so that they tilt slightly toward the side where the dead once swam beside them. The tilt may be kept for a day, a year, or a lifetime. An elderly person surrounded by losses can sometimes be recognized by a belt whose many subtle imbalances somehow resolve into stability.

Religion is less concerned with creators than with destinations.

Some believe the mountain is climbing toward a promised condition in which stone will enter air and cease to be entirely stone. They picture air inconsistently: as a water too thin to breathe, a luminous emptiness, an ocean that has forgotten its weight. To some it is paradise. To others it is catastrophe. If the summit reaches it, breathing gardens may die, buoyant houses may collapse, and every carefully balanced avenue may become a fall.

The unfinished platform at the crown belongs to no sect, yet every sect claims a possible meaning for it.

The Ascenders tend its broad-leaf garden and measure the paling above. They preserve fallen feathers, wood, and tile as evidence of another realm.

The Deep Rememberers face the opposite direction. They believe wisdom lies below, in the old ocean where animals carry memories and the mountain has not intruded. Their meditations involve hanging at the edge of the dense layers, weighted but tethered, until familiar ideas begin to loosen.

Most citizens belong fully to neither. They repair the platform when it cracks, avoid discussing what will happen if it reaches air, and bring their children to watch objects descend from above.

Those objects inspire wonder but also unease. Worked stone implies hands. Roof tile implies shelter from something they cannot imagine. Feathers are especially troubling. They are clearly grown by an animal, yet shaped to push against a substance thinner than water. Scholars arrange them in mica cases and argue whether the unknown animal swims in the upper emptiness or whether the feather itself is a kind of leaf.

Metal from above is treated almost as condensed lightning. It does not chip like glass, grow like root, or dissolve like certain stone. A metal fragment may belong to the city rather than any household. It is lent for the finest cuts, then returned and counted by touch.

Their art favors duration and transformation. A carving is not complete when the sculptor stops. It is complete when mineral film softens its edges, roots enter its grooves, and the intended image emerges through alteration. Some works require three generations: the first cuts the stone, the second plants it, the third trims away what has become excessive.

Music is performed through structures. Players tighten cords of different lengths between walls and pluck patterns that travel farther by touch than by hearing. Audiences rest palms, backs, or breathing ribs against the architecture. A concert may be nearly inaudible while the entire chamber trembles with interwoven rhythm. Particularly old compositions include pauses for the mountain’s unpredictable murmurs. A performance in which the summit groans at the proper moment is remembered for decades.

Humor, too, is quiet. They enjoy deliberate mistakes of orientation: a formal speaker entering upside down, a carved doorway that opens onto the back of another door, an apprentice weighting a master’s abandoned tool so it follows her around a workshop. Their jokes often concern impossible rigidity. In one common tale, a foolish builder makes a perfectly straight house and is astonished when the mountain refuses to remain straight beneath it.

They distrust anyone who claims certainty about distant things. The water obscures, ropes distort messages, markers drift, and the mountain changes. A credible person says where they were anchored when they saw something. An incredible person merely says what happened.

Thus their histories are arranged by relationship rather than by year:

*when the Red Orchard was still below the Fifth Bell;*  
*before the western root took the broken gallery;*  
*three hand-rises after the tile roof fell;*  
*in the lifetime when the veil crossed warmward.*

They count generations more reliably than seasons. The oldest civic projects are not monuments but corrections: stairways repeatedly extended, towers reweighted, orchards moved upward one trellis at a time. To maintain something is not to preserve its shape. It is to accompany its change.

This makes them patient, but not passive.

Every citizen knows that the city could be lost through neglect long before it is destroyed by disaster. Roots must be redirected. Gas-fruit must be harvested before they tear free. Mineral crust must be tested. Warm cracks must be listened to. Nothing can be abandoned without deciding what will inherit it.

When the mountain groans, they stop.

Hands find stone, rope, root, or one another. Breathing seams close against dust. The water trembles, and the inhabitants feel the enormous unfinished body beneath the terraces shift by some immeasurable amount.

Afterward they do not immediately return to work. Listeners test the foundations. Layer keepers watch their suspended beads. Children are counted. Households inspect their anchors and trade signs across the lanes.

Then, somewhere, a rootwright discovers that a living brace has taken new tension. A mason finds a crack where none existed. A doorway has become too narrow; a garden has risen half a finger; a public cord now points slightly looseward of its bell.

The city begins correcting itself.

A stone is moved. A bladder is tied on. A new root is threaded through a fresh hole. Someone revises an address.

And the inhabitants, who resemble neither the fixed stone nor the drifting water but have learned to live as an agreement between them, move carefully into their altered world.
