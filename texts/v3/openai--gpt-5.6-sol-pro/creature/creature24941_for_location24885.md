The smallest inhabitants live closest to certainty.

They know a world measured in films of water, the thickness of leaves, the interval between one drop and the next. Soft-shelled grazers move through the upper felt in herds too numerous to count, shaving molds from root hairs without damaging the roots themselves. Their six legs end in rings of fine hooks that spread their weight over wet surfaces. When threatened, they draw their limbs beneath them and fill their translucent shells with air. A disturbed colony rises slowly through the mulch like a handful of dull pearls and drifts until rain presses it down elsewhere.

Blind swimmers occupy the deeper leaf-mass. They are long, white, and faintly segmented, with mouths surrounded by brushes. They do not tunnel so much as loosen what lies before them and compact what lies behind. In their passage, dead leaves become dark paste; paste becomes a stable wall. The plateau’s hidden chambers are partly their work. Without them, the accumulated forest might suffocate under its own abundance.

Some never leave the water held inside plants. Every old rain bowl has its transparent grazers, no larger than seeds, bearing green organs stolen from what they eat. In bright weather they lie motionless with their backs to the light. During long cloudings, they digest those borrowed green bodies and become colorless again. Generations may pass in a single stone hollow. Their eggs tolerate centuries of dryness, though dryness seldom comes.

Flowers contain their own fauna. Slender amphibians curl inside unopened blooms and wake when the petals loosen. They are the color of diluted milk, except for their mouths, which are stained by pollen. Many have never touched anything that was not part of the same flowering organism. They mate in the warm chambers around the ovary and lay their eggs in the shallow nectar. When the flower collapses, the young descend with it, sealed in droplets or carried by moths to the next blossom along the century-long flowering front.

The moths are everywhere and almost never seen in flight.

By day they overlap upon trunks in communities several bodies deep. Their folded wings imitate not merely the color of bark but its age: fresh green scar tissue near a graft, black ridges on old wood, pale crusts where mineral water dries. A colony adjusts slowly as its tree changes. Individuals crawl from dark places to light ones, from ridges to hollows, maintaining the larger pattern. What appears to be a patch of lichen one year may resemble an old wound the next.

At rare intervals the entire arrangement lifts.

The first movement is nearly inaudible, a dry separation of scales from bark. Then acres of false surface open at once. The moths do not scatter. They remain a thin, articulate sheet, flexing around branches and passing through the canopy like a piece of night freed from the trunk. They settle elsewhere in the same order. The pattern is preserved, though no moth occupies its former place.

Predators follow these flights. Fan-mouthed hunters cling beneath leaves and sense shadows through the tissue above them. They strike upward, piercing leaf and prey together. Narrow-winged animals, more membrane than flesh, harvest moths from the edges of the moving colony. Their bones are hollow lattices filled with warm vapor. If forced to land, they can scarcely lift themselves again; they must crawl to a vent and wait for an exhalation from below.

The gliders of the ravines are larger and stranger. Seen against mist, they resemble loose pieces of pale cloth. Four long limbs support a continuous membrane, while two smaller arms fold against the chest. Their heads have no obvious necks. Rows of fine sensory pits run along the front edge of the body, reading changes in pressure, heat, and the mineral scent of distant water.

They may remain aloft from one dawn to the next, circling on the breath of the compost depths. Sleep comes to one half of the body at a time. During migration, hundreds pass through a basin without a wingbeat. They descend only to drink from particular root curtains, scraping reddish deposits with flat teeth. Their paths suggest that they remember vents no individual could have visited before. Whether this knowledge is inherited, taught in flight, or carried in the microorganisms lining their mouths is unclear.

The broad-backed grazers belong to the upper country.

They are not enormous by the scale of the plateau, but anything large enough to bend a supporting crown acquires the dignity of weather. Adults have eight short legs arranged along a low, flexible body. Each foot spreads into a pad divided by deep grooves, gripping reeds without tearing the marsh beneath them. Their hides are black-brown, porous, and ridged into drainage channels. Soil collects there. Moss follows, then sedges, bladder-leaves, flowering creepers, and sometimes saplings.

An old grazer may carry several tons of garden.

These gardens are not incidental. The animal’s skin releases different minerals in different regions. Sweet water gathers in hollows above the shoulders. Warm, nitrogenous seepage feeds the rear slopes. Thorny plants grow along the flanks where biting parasites gather; fruiting plants cluster near the spine, where birds can reach them without troubling the host. The grazer periodically arches its back beneath low branches, allowing the garden to graft into the canopy. It walks away with strips of old growth torn from its hide and new seedlings embedded in the exposed grooves.

The birds that follow are small, deliberate things with blunt beaks and feet like many-fingered hands. They eat seeds, but they also plant them. A bird presses each unwanted seed into some suitable softness, taps it twice, and covers it with moss. This behavior persists even when food is abundant. Different flocks favor different plants, so a grazer’s route can be reconstructed from the gardens it leaves behind.

The grazers communicate through the living surface. They lower their chests against the branch-mat and produce vibrations too deep for the upper air to carry. Replies travel through fused wood for days. A herd may begin turning toward another watershed years before the herds there arrive. Their migrations are slow enough that trees mature between the first change of direction and the eventual meeting.

Below them, among the middle crowns, move the long-limbed browsers.

At first they seem easier to understand. They have warm blood, paired eyes, four grasping limbs, and a smaller pair folded near the mouth. Their bodies are covered in fine fibers that bead water and host a shifting green film. They travel without exposing themselves, laying their weight across many branches and drawing leaves inward with hooked wrists. One is usually perceived not as an animal but as a shallow valley moving through the foliage.

They eat selectively. From one vine they take only the newest tips; from another, the diseased leaves; from certain trees, nothing but the swollen tissue around a graft. They scrape fungal cords from wounds and press them into other wounds. They bite channels through bark where water has begun to stagnate. They carry pollen on their forearms for distances much greater than insects usually travel.

It is tempting to call this gardening.

It may only be feeding. Yet groves frequented by the browsers heal differently from abandoned groves. Their branches fuse into broad, resilient platforms. Water is divided among many small reservoirs rather than allowed to gather in dangerous masses. Thorn growth is redirected to the edges. Hollow trees develop openings at similar heights.

The browsers sleep in those hollows, several bodies wrapped together and suspended from the walls by all four major limbs. Their smaller hands continue moving after the rest of them becomes still. They comb one another’s facial fibers, exchange mouthfuls of fermented sap, and trace the wood grain with repeated gestures.

Juveniles watch these gestures. They repeat them against the wall.

In the seven towers above the mist basin, their marks occur everywhere: not cuts, but polished bands where generations of fingertips followed the same spirals. The alcoves fit their sleeping clusters. The narrow openings admit their bodies when young and flexible, though old adults must enter through gaps in the collapsed crowns. The black plaques of hardened seedpods bear patterns that resemble the routes of underground water when those routes are mapped over centuries.

No plaque can be removed. Its stalk enters the wall, branches through it, and joins the tower’s circulation. If broken, the seedpods close and the pattern dies.

The browsers do not appear to make the plaques. During certain wet years they gather around them, touch them in sequence, and feed them drops of saliva. The plaques soften. Pale lines move across their surfaces. Shoots elsewhere in the tower then open or seal. Water changes course. A chamber warms; another cools. Roots thicken around a failing arch.

This too might be called architecture, though the animal does not command the plant as a builder commands material. The tower answers according to its own growth, often taking decades to complete what was begun. Many browsers who touch a plaque will not live to see the resulting chamber. Their young inherit the route and continue it.

Their dead are not carried away. Companions place them in narrow wall folds and pack the openings with chewed leaves. Roots enter the body before decay has finished. Sometimes a new plaque forms over the place. Its first pattern often resembles the final hand movements of the one enclosed beneath it.

Yet even the browsers may not be the makers.

Within the plateau’s deep circulation lives something less visible and perhaps more consequential. Its presence is detected through delay. A wound made in one province alters flowering hundreds of miles away. Fungal cords carry chemical pulses through buried trunks. Root grafts transmit pressure, sugars, toxins, and minute electrical changes. None of these signals alone suggests thought. Together, over centuries, they produce responses difficult to dismiss.

A basin begins to sag. Long before collapse, trees along its rim change their leaves. Browsers arrive and open drainage wounds. Grazers shift their routes so that new gardens are grafted across weak crowns. Moth colonies carry spores into the region. Burrowers thicken the lower felt. For three hundred years the basin is reinforced by creatures that do not seem to coordinate and may not perceive one another directly.

Elsewhere, a diseased lineage of vines is isolated. Roots close around it. Pollinators cease visiting its flowers. Gliders alter their mineral-lick circuit. When the dead growth finally falls, adjacent branches have already formed a cradle to receive it.

Perhaps these are only convergent instincts shaped by an old ecosystem. Perhaps the plateau is not one mind but an agreement maintained by countless bodies. Perhaps mind is the wrong scale of description.

The oldest root-ridges respond too slowly to be tested. Their active margins release compounds that change the dreams of sleeping browsers. During those seasons the animals travel farther, touch unfamiliar plaques, and gather in towers they have never visited. They perform sequences of gestures absent from local custom. Centuries later, ivory flowers appear along the paths they traced.

A browser is therefore difficult to call an individual. Its gut contains organisms passed through its maternal line for millennia. Its skin garden changes with every grove. Its gestures come from the dead. Its routes are prompted by roots. When separated from the living network, it grows agitated, then still, pressing its hands against any damp wood as though waiting for an answer.

The towers may be organs through which the plateau instructs them.

Or the plateau may be the long result of their instruction.

There are rarer inhabitants beneath the root-mat, in spaces where the warmth approaches fever. Their existence is known by shed membranes found near vents: colorless, pleated sheets large enough to cover rooms, with blood vessels branching through them like leaf veins. Something descends the central shafts at the end of the long rains. Something rises when remote flowering belts cross particular watersheds.

The browsers withdraw before it comes. The small animals seal themselves into bark. Even the blind mulch-swimmers knot together and stop feeding.

Then a pulse passes upward through the tower. Water on the walls trembles. Seedpods open all at once, showing interiors that emit a deep red light. Warm air rises carrying spores, unfamiliar pollen, and a smell like newly split fruit over old stone.

No whole body is seen. There may be none. The shed membranes could belong to a creature moving through the buried galleries, or to the galleries themselves, periodically sloughing their inner surfaces. What ascends may be an animal, a root organ, a colony, or a temporary body assembled by the plateau when it needs to cross some boundary its ordinary growth cannot cross.

Afterward, the structures alter. An opening appears where there was none. One tower begins leaning toward another. The slow breath from below changes rhythm. Browsers return and spend generations learning the new arrangement.

Most life there does not wonder what the plateau is. It eats, grafts, pollinates, hollows, carries, settles, and is incorporated. The smallest animals inhabit droplets. The largest carry forests. The long-limbed ones preserve intentions they may not understand. The ancient plants migrate through their own descendants. Beneath all of them, decay remains warm and generative, receiving every finished body into the common depth.

Nothing lives upon the plateau in quite the usual sense.

Everything lives within it, even when walking on its highest leaves. Everything is carried. Everything carries something else. A grazer bears a marsh; a bird bears a seed; a moth colony bears a pattern; a browser bears a gesture; a root bears water and the memory of pressure; the towers bear dark shafts through which the lower warmth continues to breathe.

And the plateau bears them all—not as a land bears inhabitants, but as a body bears its senses, its hungers, its old injuries, and the thoughts that take longer than a lifetime to finish.
