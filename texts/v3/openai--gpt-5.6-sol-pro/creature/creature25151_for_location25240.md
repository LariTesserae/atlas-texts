At first it seems that everything living there is small.

The shallow water is thick with transparent bodies: threads with pulsing centers, flat swimmers no larger than fingernails, bead-eyed fry, larvae carrying fans around their mouths. They occupy different depths in water scarcely deeper than a hand. Some hang from the surface film upside down, breathing the air through tubes. Some graze the illuminated skins of submerged leaves. Others lie buried in the silt with only a ring of feathery gills exposed. When shadow passes overhead, whole layers vanish at once—not by fleeing, but by becoming clear.

Among them move the quick silver schools. Each fish is slight, nearly without color, but the school has shape and apparent intention. It narrows around stems, opens around a reptile’s foot, folds upon itself when a hunter enters the water. Thousands turn together so precisely that the school resembles a single sheet of metal being crumpled and smoothed. At intervals, individuals rise to swallow insects from the surface. Their mouths make soft, innumerable kisses.

The amphibians are more various than they first appear. The long mud-colored ones scarcely move and may be mistaken for fallen stems until they open their mouths. Smaller climbing kinds cover the reeds. Their toes end in wet discs; their skins carry greens, yellows, bruised purples, and the pale blue of mineral deposits. They do not call one by one. A whole stemful inflates together, throats swelling into translucent lanterns, and releases a pulse that travels outward through neighboring colonies. The answering pulse returns altered. In this way the frogs continuously measure the occupied world.

Many bear their young upon themselves. Eggs lie in rows beneath loose back skin, visible as pale moons. Tadpoles ride in pouches at the thigh or cling around the parent’s neck in glistening wreaths. One heavy species carries a basin of water in a saddle of folded flesh, allowing its larvae to feed and grow without ever leaving its body. There is little distinction here between animal and nursery.

The insects fill every interval left between larger lives. Long-legged skaters embroider the water. Beetles in lacquered shells descend with silver air held beneath their wings. Soft white moths sleep in layers under broad leaves, all facing the same direction, so that a disturbed colony peels away like a piece of fabric. There are pollinators with thick, slow wings and bodies furred in greenish filaments; needle-bodied hunters that seize them in flight; gnats whose columns stand in fixed positions for generation after generation, though every member of the column is replaced.

Some flying creatures never land on solid growth. They mate in the mist, catch food from the air, and release their young into the water. Their legs have diminished into hooks used only to hold one another. When they finally weaken, they descend into waiting mouths.

The smallest inhabitants cover the larger ones. Mites graze hides clean of fungus. Pale worms inhabit folds around eyes and nostrils without harming their hosts, taking salt from tears and mucus. Tiny crabs live beneath the marginal plates of the shelled reptiles. Every animal carries a lesser marsh upon itself: feeders, cleaners, parasites, eggs, spores, and predators of parasites. A grazer entering open water may appear alone, but it transports thousands.

The grazers move in broad, dark companies. They are low-bodied, thick-necked animals whose feet spread widely when they set them down. Flexible toes distribute their weight over moss and submerged roots, and the mud seals so closely around their legs that they seem to wade through warm flesh. Their hides are nearly hairless. Condensation and plant oils keep them shining.

They feed continuously, taking the soft ends of reeds with sideways pulls of the jaw. A herd does not leave a trail. The first animals bend the vegetation; the last feed among stems already lifting upright. Calves walk beneath the bellies of adults, guided by smell and by low contact grunts that pass through the herd like the frog pulses through the reeds. When danger comes, the adults do not run. They compress around their young until the group becomes a single many-backed obstruction.

Their principal hunters are low, loose-bodied cats adapted less for speed than for passage. Their shoulders turn narrowly between trunks; their paws open across root mats; their tails are broad and muscular enough to push them through water. Moss-colored markings blur their outlines, but the camouflage is never perfect, because minute flies follow the moisture at their eyes. A watcher often detects the cloud before detecting the cat.

These predators take prey often, but seldom violently enough to interrupt the plain’s greater sound. A cat rises from the greenery, fastens beneath a jaw, and sinks back with its victim. The herd closes. Frogs continue calling from the reeds bent under the struggle. Beetles arrive at the first blood before the body has stopped moving.

The crocodilian hunters are narrower still. Their limbs are long enough to carry them through water too shallow for swimming, but they lower themselves when stalking and advance by pressure of tail and toes. Algae grow on the ridges around their eyes. Small frogs sit upon their heads, and fish enter their open mouths to remove scraps from between the teeth. Even the ambush predator cannot keep an empty surface around itself.

Broad-shelled reptiles occupy the mineral swells and warm basins. Their shells are not dry armor but living gardens, grooved with channels in which water, moss, mites, and flowering plants collect. The oldest individuals can be identified by the complexity of these growths, though none appears ancient. They browse submerged vegetation and rest in groups so tightly fitted that their shells form temporary islands. Birds stand upon them. Vines catch on them. Seeds sprout and are carried until the reptiles submerge again.

Above these move the long-necked browsers. Their bodies are compact and heavy, their necks muscular rather than graceful. They feed by inserting narrow heads into the entangled canopy and stripping vines with comb-like teeth. Skin fringes along the throat protect the blood vessels from biting insects and help shed excess heat. Each fringe carries colonies of red mites, giving the animals the appearance of wearing damp, living embroidery.

They advance carefully because a panicked movement would bring down half the growth around them. Their slowness is therefore not gentleness but practiced negotiation. Vines slide over their backs. Frogs leap from leaf to shoulder and shoulder to leaf. Small birds follow their heads into the canopy to seize insects exposed by their feeding. When a browser pauses, one cannot easily tell where the animal ends and the vegetation resumes.

The birds are loud, wet creatures. Their feathers lie in heavy, oil-dark layers, often incomplete around the throat and face. Many have bare resonating sacs in colors brighter than flowers. They shout paired notes from the upper crowns, and their territories overlap so densely that the calls form interlocking patterns rather than separate declarations.

Few construct nests. Some cement eggs to the undersides of leaves with secretions that harden in moisture. Others place them within the cups of living plants, which tighten around the clutch when touched. One kind entrusts each egg to a different broad-shelled reptile, wedging it among the shell growth where warmth remains constant. The hatchling stays with its moving island until its wings strengthen.

There are also climbers less easily placed. They have grasping feet, slick scaled flanks, and faces softened by mobile folds around the mouth. They travel through the communal canopy on all fours, pausing to press their narrow snouts into flowers or split swollen stems for grubs. Loose skin stretches between their limbs. They cannot truly fly, but in the dense vegetation they rarely need to: a short glide carries them from one leaning crown into another.

They are alert, social, and dexterous. They groom each other, steal food, recognize individuals, and give different cries for cats, snakes, and aerial hunters. Yet they do not gather objects. A grub is extracted with a claw grown to the necessary curve; a fruit is opened against the animal’s own ridged teeth; a sleeping hollow is supplied by a folded leaf. Their intelligence flows directly into posture, memory, alliance, and escape. Nothing of it remains behind after they pass.

The climbing serpents prey upon them. These hang along vines in loose descending coils, their bodies flattened where they rest against leaves. Moisture beads upon their scales. Some carry leaf-shaped projections along the spine, and algae lodge between them. Small moths shelter on these false leaves until the serpent moves.

Not all serpents kill what they catch. One small species enters the throat sacs of calling frogs to eat parasites and unfertilized eggs. The frog tolerates its intrusion, sitting motionless with mouth open while the serpent’s tail remains curled around the stem. Another lives among the shells of the great reptiles and feeds entirely on mites. Forms that would elsewhere be enemies repeatedly become habitats for one another.

The same is true below the surface. Roots are crowded with pale crustaceans, blind fish, filter-feeding mouths, and colonies that might be mistaken for flowers. The colonies open when mineral-rich seepage rises and close when a grazing animal stirs the silt. Their branches share a digestive cavity. Individual members catch food, reproduce, defend, or pump water, but no member performs all these acts. Whether the colony is one creature or several depends only on how closely it is examined.

This uncertainty spreads.

The vines that connect neighboring crowns exchange water and sugars through grafts wherever they touch. Frogs transfer spores on their feet. Browsers prune one plant and fertilize another. Insects carry pollen, parasites, yeasts, and eggs. Fish feed beneath the grazers; birds feed above them; mites feed upon them. The inhabitants are recognizable as separate bodies, yet separation is brief and incomplete. Each life enters others before death, often before birth.

Even parenthood is distributed. An egg may be laid by one creature, warmed by another, defended by a third, and fed after hatching by secretions from the plant that holds it. Young do not always resemble the bodies that produced them. Some larvae pass through several hosts and several shapes before becoming winged adults. Some never mature at all unless swallowed. Some adults dissolve after breeding, their tissues nourishing the plant cup in which their offspring develop.

There are scavengers, but no creature has scavenging to itself. Death summons the whole plain. Flies arrive first, though they seem to have been waiting already. Beetles enter through the eyes and mouth. Pale amphibians rasp the softer skin. Fungi bloom along warm seams. Roots release digestive films. Larger scavengers pull the body open and are, in turn, relieved of scraps by birds and fish.

Within a short time, a carcass loses the shape that made it singular. Its salts brighten a colony of frogs. Its fat appears in beetle larvae. Its calcium whitens a mineral swell. Its last warmth passes into eggs. Nothing marks the location afterward except a temporary intensity of flowering, and even that is grazed level.

No one rules this abundance. No species clears enough room to become dominant, and none can withdraw far enough to become independent. The nearest thing to a sovereign is the exchange itself: eating and being eaten, sheltering and carrying, opening a wound and closing it with mouths.

Yet there are moments when the plain appears to notice.

A distant disturbance—a large body falling, perhaps, or a herd entering a pool—passes outward by many channels. Fish turn. Water skaters lift. Frogs alter their pulse. Birds repeat a harsher pair of notes. Grazers raise their wet heads. The climbers freeze against trunks. Even flowers contract as vibrations enter their stems. No individual knows the extent of the event, but the response travels farther than sight.

Then, just as gradually, it is absorbed. Calls resume their former spacing. Fish reform their flowing sheet. Leaves reopen. Predators lower their heads. The countless local awarenesses settle back into the continuous animal weather.

This is what lives there: not a single hidden intelligence, and not merely a multitude. It is a population so complete that solitude has never become an available condition. Every hide is a country, every body a passage, every birth an occupation of space already alive. The inhabitants do not stand against the land or move across it as visitors. They are its temporary mouths, limbs, throats, wings, and watching eyes.

The plain has no empty place from which one of them might look back and call the rest of it world.
