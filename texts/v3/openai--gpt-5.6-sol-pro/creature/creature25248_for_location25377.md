The first inhabitants one notices are not the largest. They are the ones whose separateness is easiest to understand.

Along the warm seams sometimes walks a small white grazer. It has six short legs, each ending in a divided pad that distributes its weight across the fibers. Its body is no larger than a clenched hand and is covered in hollow, colorless quills. These trap both heat and the valley’s ordinary green-blue reflections, so that from a distance the animal resembles a piece of old snow moving where snow cannot remain.

It eats almost nothing substantial. Its narrow mouth scrapes yeasts, salt crusts, and fungal films from the sides of roots. Even this meager feeding is cautious. It takes a little from one place and moves on before the surrounding tissues begin to tighten. When it rests, it presses itself into a fork in the mat and slows until condensation forms on its quills. Females carry a single soft-shelled egg in a warm fold beneath the abdomen. The young hatch with their mouths already stained by the dark microbes on which they will live.

Their survival depends on leaving no wound worth closing.

The black-winged pollinators are smaller and stranger. For most of the year they are not visibly animals at all. They remain folded inside the insulated pods of certain high-crown plants, their wings wrapped around them and their metabolism reduced to an occasional tremor. The pod supplies moisture and a trace of sugar. In return, when the crowns open, warmth and chemical pressure wake the insects together.

They emerge by the millions but never form a lasting cloud. Their season may be only an afternoon.

Each has four wings as thin as soot on glass and a body heavily furred around the thorax. They do not fly from blossom to blossom at random. Bands of flowers open in sequence across the slopes, and the pollinators follow these openings like sparks following a hidden fuse. By dusk their wings are abraded. They crawl into newly formed pods, carrying pollen with them, and seal themselves inside. Many never emerge again. Their bodies are absorbed alongside the pollen they delivered. The valley makes little distinction between guest, partner, and nutrient.

There are other brief animals: transparent mites in water-filled stems, flat worms inhabiting the warm undersides of leaf cisterns, eyeless things that move through fermenting chambers and feed on bacteria before being digested by the chambers themselves. Most spend their entire lives inside one plant structure. Whether they are parasites or organs is often impossible to decide. Their ancestors may once have lived freely. Here, freedom is an expensive trait.

The largest animal is known mostly by displacement.

It travels through submerged channels below the mat, where root vaults enclose black water under pressure. Its body is long, pale, and muscular, perhaps twenty meters in an old individual, though no one sees enough of it at once to measure. A blunt head parts the braided roots without biting them. Behind it follow many paired fins, or limbs reduced until they serve the same purpose. Leaves rise in a narrow procession overhead as it passes. Pools cloud briefly with shed skin and then clear.

It appears to feed on swollen root nodules, but only those already marked for dissolution. Chemical traces on its face may allow it to distinguish living conduits from tissues the valley has begun to reclaim. In turn, its passage keeps the deep channels open. Seeds and mineral grains adhere to its skin and are carried between basins beneath the ridges.

If stranded by a contracting channel, it does not thrash. It curls into a chamber, coats itself in mucus, and waits. Roots lace over its body without penetrating. Months or years later, when water returns, the roots loosen and release it. Very old individuals bear a mesh of scars corresponding to former root patterns: obsolete maps embossed in white tissue.

Yet the animals are only annotations. The principal inhabitants are the growths that compose the place.

The great columns are colonies rather than trees. Each begins as a dense knot of warm root tips gathered around a cavity in the mat. From this knot rise dozens or hundreds of stems. They fuse, separate, and fuse again, leaving vertical passages between them. The overlapping scales on the exterior are dead only at their exposed edges. Beneath each scale is a shallow chamber where new tissue, fungal threads, dormant buds, and saline fluid are held against the cold.

A column has no permanent center. Its active tissues migrate around its circumference according to light, wind, and the pressure of neighboring growths. The shaded side may be dismantled while the sunward side thickens. Over centuries the whole structure can walk many times its own width without falling, continually abandoning itself behind itself.

High in the crowns grow the pressure pods. These are thick-walled seed chambers connected to long vascular muscles. During Casting, they rotate toward gaps in the ridges. The parent colony loads them with seeds, desiccated nutrient paste, and tiny cultures of partner organisms. When internal pressure reaches its threshold, a seam parts. The seeds leave almost silently, narrow as hairs and heavy at one end, traveling in streams too fine to see except when they cross a patch of sun.

Lower down are the jointed carriers. They look like vines but behave more like moving tracks. Their segments contract in waves, passing capsules, mineral nodules, or packets of living tissue from one rooted district to another. No single vine crosses the basin. Loads are transferred at junctions by changes in moisture and tension. A seed capsule may travel for months through many unrelated bodies before reaching the slope on which it opens.

Some carriers cheat. They accept packets and digest them. Others have evolved clasps that will release only when they encounter the correct wax signature. Still others carry nothing themselves but grow beside productive routes, drawing warmth from the repeated contractions. The valley’s order is full of theft, refusal, and failed exchange. Its coordination is not harmony. It is a durable congestion of competing needs.

The soft black filaments are among its most pervasive inhabitants. In exposed air they seem to stir without wind, but most of each organism lies below. They enter dead fibers almost as soon as circulation ceases, separating waxes, lignins, salts, and metals into different streams. Their visible hairs are sensory and reproductive structures. They taste pressure, temperature, and airborne chemistry. When a column is damaged, nearby filament fields lean toward it before any other visible response begins.

They are also the valley’s archivists.

As they digest old tissue, they preserve useful vascular shapes by lining them with resistant black resin. New roots later enter these hollow molds. Thus a successful channel can outlive the species that formed it. Deep in the mat are conduits established by plants that have been extinct for thousands of seasons, still directing water and growth. The filaments do not remember what made those forms. They preserve only what continues to carry something.

The glass-bladed plants along the river are more independent. Each anchors itself to the smooth root bed with a disk of cartilage-like tissue. Their pale blades lean upstream and comb mineral particles from water that already appears clean. Inside the blades, silt is sorted grain by grain. Iron is sent to dark nodules; phosphorus to soft inner pith; silica to the blade edges. When a plant becomes overloaded, its anchor releases. It rolls downstream until caught by a root gate, where other growths disassemble it and distribute its concentrated minerals.

Far below these visible forms live the heat-makers. They occupy broad, sealed chambers inside the oldest layers of the mat. Some are fungi, some colonial bacteria, and some have become so interdependent with roots that no useful boundary remains. They ferment buried wood slowly, conserving heat rather than releasing it in bursts. Their chambers swell and subside over decades. Warm vapor leaves through towers whose openings are disguised among ordinary bark scales.

Around these furnaces cluster the valley’s most delicate tissues: pale roots without antifreeze, translucent sacs in which germ cells are stored, and soft provisional growths that could never survive near the surface. During the unstable intervals between seasons, these chambers open. Their contents rise.

Most of what rises is unsuccessful.

There are trunks that build enormous vanes but cannot keep them from freezing; cups that gather water and then poison themselves with concentrated salts; root clusters that sever every neighboring connection and starve in isolation. There are flowers shaped for pollinators that no longer exist. Some release scents into a valley with almost no animals able to smell them. Others open downward against the warm mat, fertilizing only the black filaments beneath.

For a few days such forms may occupy an entire hollow. Then carriers bypass them, communal roots withdraw heat, and dismantling creepers arrive. Their substance returns inward. Their arrangement does not.

Occasionally one persists. A useless sail catches wind in a way that pumps fluid. A downward flower proves capable of exchanging cells with a fungus. An isolated root cluster survives a disease moving through the shared network. What began as an error becomes a new participant in the basin’s exchanges, and centuries later its descendants may form a slope.

No inhabitant governs this process.

There is communication everywhere: changes in hydraulic pressure, traveling electrical pulses, airborne compounds, mineral signatures, the angles of leaves, the tightening of roots. A disturbance can cross kilometers. But there is no hidden brain beneath the valley and no single vegetal will deciding where a walker should go. Paths close because many local tissues respond to repeated pressure. Groves migrate because light, warmth, and transport pull growth in one direction while old structures are reclaimed behind. The result resembles intention because intention is another way of coordinating many needs through time.

The basin nevertheless behaves as something more than a collection. Its inhabitants have lived against and within one another for so long that none possesses a complete life alone. The columns require heat made from their dead predecessors. The heat-makers require water delivered by crowns they never encounter. The river blades depend on root gates downstream. Pollinators hatch inside structures that will consume them. The long channel animal opens routes for seeds that may later close around its descendants.

Even the white grazers carry films of microbes between distant root seams. Their negligible mouths join parts of the valley that would otherwise remain separate.

So what lives there is not one creature, though from the ridges it can look like one. Nor is it simply a forest. It is a multitude that has replaced the ground beneath itself, making habitat out of accumulated bodies and making new bodies according to the pressures of that habitat. Individuals appear, divide, fuse, migrate, and are reclaimed. Species endure, then blur into partnerships or vanish while their useful shapes remain.

In the coldest part of Compression, the distinction becomes hardest to see. Animals are sealed in pods or deep channels. Columns lower their crowns. Carrier vines stop with their loads held between segments. The outer mat darkens and the heat-makers draw the viable tissues inward. For a time the basin seems inhabited only by frost.

Underneath, circulation continues.

A white grazer sleeps with its egg held against its abdomen. Black-winged adults dissolve around next season’s pollen. The long animal waits in a water chamber beneath the ridge. Old trunks are sorted into salts, sugars, waxes, and empty passageways. Root tips gather around the preserved shapes of vanished roots.

Then somewhere in the depth, a provisional bud begins to divide. No inhabitant knows what it will become. The valley makes room for it by moving everything else.
