At first, there seem to be innumerable species in the summit hollow. Given time, it becomes less certain that there is more than one.

The smallest inhabitants gather wherever water has decided, temporarily, to possess an edge. Pale heat-mites line the beads on the walls in crowded wreaths. Before feeding, they are chalk-white and almost angular, with bodies like chips of salt and legs too fine to resolve. As they drink, they clarify from the mouth backward. The water can be watched entering them until only their joints remain visible: six or eight dark punctuation marks holding a droplet-shaped absence against the rock.

They do not drink a bead dry. A few always stop early and crawl to the next, carrying inside themselves enough water to alter the droplet they enter. In this way they may regulate the condensation lines, joining beads that would otherwise remain separate and separating those that have grown too large. Their migrations trace branching diagrams over the stone. Whether these paths are instinct, writing, or merely the geometry of thirst is impossible to tell.

When startled, the mites let go simultaneously. They do not fall. They remain arranged in the air along the curve of the vanished surface, a pale constellation outlining the water-film that had supported them. Eventually another film condenses around their feet, and they resume feeding as if nothing has happened.

Beneath every overhang live the card-crawlers. Each is broad, black, and nearly without thickness. Alone, one resembles a wet scale with many short legs hidden underneath. They dislike exposure and shelter by covering one another, fitting edge to edge and layer over layer until hundreds make a single glossy mass. The lowest animals bear the heat of the rock; the uppermost receive the cooling droplets. At intervals the colony turns itself inside out. The bottom layer slips from beneath the others, crosses their backs, and settles on top. This rotation continues without leader or pause, so that no crawler is burned and no crawler remains thirsty.

Their young appear between layers. They are not laid as eggs. Instead, when two adults press together for long enough, the darkness between them thickens and peels free as a third, smaller crawler. For its first hours it has no surfaces of its own. Its upper side belongs to one parent and its underside to the other. Only after it feeds does a border form around it.

Needle-legged hunters stride over rivulets and stretched films. Their bodies hang high above the water, no larger than soot flecks, while their legs reach outward several times their length. Each foot rests in a separate dimple. Through these dimples they sense movement far beyond the range of sight. The struggles of prey arrive as changes of curvature; a hunter turns toward them before the prey has moved.

They seldom attack directly. Instead, they alter the water beneath their feet. By shifting their weight from leg to leg, they steepen one side of a bead, flatten another, or create a minute downhill where none existed. Prey slides toward them. A successful hunter appears motionless until something small touches the shadow under its body and ceases to be separate.

The knot animals live in the rock’s smallest pores. Soft, colorless, and apparently boneless, they emerge by pushing a loop of themselves through the opening and pulling the rest after it. No observer has seen an entire knot animal at once. A loop may be followed by another loop, then another, for far more length than the creature’s body should contain. Some naturalist, if one could remain there long enough, might conclude that every knot in the hollow is an extremity of a single animal threaded through the mountain.

They feed on trapped gases and on whatever passes through the stone without crossing the intervening space. When a droplet enters one wall and emerges elsewhere, knot animals cluster around both events. Their bodies pulse in correspondence. Sometimes one end swallows a mite and another end, meters away or moments earlier, releases its clear husk.

Across the uncertain floor move the shell-backs. They are the most numerous visible animals and the chief makers of what might be called soil, if the hollow possessed any. Their shells grow as black volcanic glass, layer by layer, from mineral-rich tissues along the spine. No two shells are alike. Some are smooth domes; some are ridged like knapped arrowheads; some trail transparent blades longer than the animal beneath them. A shell-back newly emerged from water is soft and gray. Within minutes the surrounding heat hardens a glass crust over it.

They graze continuously. Their mouthparts rasp salt from the stone, crop mineral films, and shave transparent skins from the dead. Behind them they leave pale trails of altered chemistry, which fill with smaller feeders almost at once. Crowds of shell-backs flow around hot vents and condensation wells like slow, many-legged weather.

Because their armor is truly grown obsidian, it breaks sharply. A fractured shell may kill its bearer, or it may give the animal a useful new edge. Shell-backs with asymmetrical breaks often become successful predators, turning sideways to slice softer neighbors. The distinction between grazing and hunting is therefore not hereditary. It may begin with an accident.

Their dead shells do not remain empty for long. Mites gather in the hollows. Crawlers line the undersides. Knot animals thread through cracks too fine to see. Occasionally the shell itself softens, sprouts hidden legs, and walks away with an entirely different occupant—or with no occupant at all.

The transparent ribbons are first known by absence. A clear lane opens through the shell-back congregation. Feeding stops on both sides of it. Bodies press away from something that cannot yet be seen. Only when the ribbon swallows does its outline appear: a long muscular distortion filled with the silhouettes of prey.

It has no fixed front. A mouth forms at whichever end lies nearer food, while the opposite mouth closes and becomes a tapering tail. If prey approaches from both directions, both ends open. The animal may then pull itself apart during feeding, leaving two smaller ribbons that recoil from one another and hunt independently. If neither finds enough food, they eventually meet and merge, though not always end to end. Crooked, forked, and ring-shaped hunters result.

Within water, the ribbons swim by undulating. On stone, they move as cracks do: one section appears ahead while another seals behind. Across vertical condensation planes they become narrow shadows with no measurable thickness. A ribbon caught at the intersection of two planes may produce four descendants, each departing along a different surface. For a short while all four make identical choices, even when encountering different prey.

Many of the hollow’s animals undergo such changes. A silver-eyed swimmer reaching the shore does not merely crawl out. Its fins stiffen, split at the rays, and become legs. Its single eye sinks through the head and emerges as several light-sensitive pits along the joints. If driven back into water, it may reverse the process, but not exactly: the new eye remembers several points of view and shows a fractured image of its surroundings.

Those crossing the free-hanging films become flat beings. They travel inside the water’s surface rather than upon it, visible equally from either side. Their organs spread into signs: a branching stomach, a pulse traveling around the outline, eggs like dark commas. In this state they prey on reflections. A flat hunter may seize the image of a mite drinking from the other side of the chamber; the mite itself then collapses, though nothing has touched it.

Some never recover their depth. When the film evaporates, they remain as moving stains on the rock, migrating from shadow to shadow. These may be the slight disobedient silhouettes that trail the larger inhabitants, anticipating their motions by a heartbeat.

The creatures of the droplets live on another scale of experience. To an outside eye they are specks turning inside specks. Within, they form migrations, feeding grounds, mating swarms, and catastrophes.

A fresh rain-bead begins nearly empty. Heat-mites puncture its boundary to drink, and particles from their mouths enter the water. Clear swimmers hatch from those particles. They browse along the inner surface, pursued by needle-thin hunters that never cross the membrane. Egg-bearers descend into the bead’s apparent depths, towing chains of young. Scavengers gather beneath floating husks. What looks from outside like a single cloudy tremor may be the movement of thousands crossing a lake that, to them, has shores beyond sight.

As evaporation advances, distances shorten. Creatures that have lived generations without meeting are forced together. Hunters become common. Feeding turns frantic but remains silent. Some animals climb the retreating boundary and transform into walkers. Some harden around themselves, making bubbles within the rock. Others continue swimming after the water is gone, circling through clear mineral as if it were still liquid.

The drifts of transparent husks near the hottest fissures are therefore not entirely graveyards. A husk may be an abandoned body, an egg, a vessel, or a second animal waiting for the correct direction of rain. When steam pulses through the crack, whole drifts stir. Empty skins stand on delicate legs and walk toward the moisture. They are often eaten before it becomes clear whether they have returned to life.

The largest residents are the gray six-limbed glass-workers.

They keep to folds where daylight reaches only by reflection. Their soft hides are the color of cooled ash and show faint bands of transparency when wet. Four limbs support the body; the forward pair are smaller, divided at the tips, and capable of turning an obsidian flake with exquisite precision. They have no visible eyes. Along the head and wrists are rows of shallow pits containing droplets that never evaporate. Through these they sense heat, vibration, gravity, and perhaps the recent history of whatever surface they touch.

Their mouths are dark with glass. Teeth begin as soft mineral nodules, harden into obsidian, and chip during use. The animal swallows the fragments. Later, new teeth grow with edges resembling those it has ingested. An old individual carries in its jaws a rough record of every tool and shell it has eaten.

They work stone, but not as builders. One selects a fresh edge by stroking it repeatedly with both forelimbs. It braces a flake against the rock and strikes. The sound does not travel through the air; every animal touching the chamber wall pauses as the impact passes through their feet.

The resulting scraper may open a shell-back, shave edible film from a hot seam, or cut a trapped creature free from mineral crust. A hooked point may draw knot-flesh from a pore. A broad flake may redirect a rising droplet long enough for the animal to drink. Then the tool is left.

They do not hoard useful objects. Possession appears to end the instant an action is completed. Nor do adults visibly teach their young. Instead, a juvenile watching an elder strike glass may repeat the movement hours or generations later, even if the juvenile stood on the opposite side of an intersecting water-plane and saw the gesture reversed. Left-handed and right-handed traditions pass through the hollow in waves, overlap, and sometimes cancel each other.

Their social life is equally difficult to name. They do not nest or pair. Several may gather around a vent, resting limb against limb while steam moves through them. At other times one follows another for days, placing each foot in the fading condensation print ahead. When the leader disappears into a fold too narrow to admit it, the follower continues alone without searching.

Young glass-workers are occasionally found clinging beneath an adult, but they do not resemble smaller versions of it. They begin as bundles of white threads knotted around a warm pebble. Over time the pebble becomes a mouth, some threads become limbs, and the remainder withdraw into cracks. It is possible that the adult contributes only the stone. It is also possible that knot animals, card-crawlers, and glass-workers are different ages of the same life.

This possibility grows harder to dismiss the longer the hollow is watched.

A heat-mite swollen with water becomes transparent enough to resemble the larva of a swimmer. A swimmer reaching stone unfolds legs like a crawler. Crawlers pressed into colonies produce knots in their darkness. Shell-backs eat the mineral leavings of all three and grow armor identical to the tools of the gray animals. Ribbons split at intersecting films just as shadows and streams do. The glass-workers themselves, when killed or overtaken by sudden dryness, collapse into heaps of pale mites that scatter toward condensation.

Yet no complete cycle has ever been witnessed. The place interrupts observation. An animal passes behind a hanging drop and emerges as something else. Time within the crack loosens. The witness blinks. The mountain adjusts by the width of a hair. What appears to be metamorphosis may be replacement, mimicry, predation, or the hollow correcting an inconsistency.

There are also the mineral colonies: translucent crusts that spread over the hottest stone whenever direct attention lapses. They have no recognizable organs. Under magnification they consist of branching crystals enclosing soft pulses of fluid. Their growth follows the tracks of animals, filling footprints, covering abandoned flakes, and sealing old channels. Sometimes a crust swells into blisters from which mites emerge. Sometimes a wounded shell-back presses against one and later pulls away repaired, its new glass continuous with the wall.

The gray tool-users avoid stepping on these colonies, but they also harvest them. They scrape thin curls from the edges and place the curls against their mouths, wounds, or sensory pits. Whether this is feeding, medicine, reproduction, or communication cannot be separated. Afterward, the colony grows in the pattern of the animal’s grip.

No inhabitant seems to rule the hollow. There is no apex in the ordinary sense. A ribbon consumes shell-backs until mites drink the water from its body. A glass-worker splits the ribbon and is later threaded through by a knot animal. Mineral film seals the knot. Crawlers graze the film. A fresh rain transforms the crawlers into swimmers, and the cycle loses its names again.

Their true predator may be evaporation. Their great migration may be condensation. Rain is both season and creation event. Heat is famine, metamorphosis, and burial. The animals survive not by resisting these changes but by consenting to become whatever the new surface permits.

Thus the hollow’s multitudes may be an ecosystem of countless species, each exquisitely adapted to hot glass, transient water, and contradictory gravity. Or they may be one distributed organism whose organs mistake themselves for prey and predator. They may even be the wet phase of the mountain itself: basalt acquiring legs, water acquiring hunger, fracture learning for a little while to sense its own edges.

After rain, when every wall shines and the invisible planes fill with life, the distinction matters least. The inhabitants drink, divide, graze, stalk, chip glass, and move between incompatible directions. None appears astonished. For them, an animal has never been a fixed body. It is a gesture carried from surface to surface—the momentary shape taken by thirst as it crosses stone.
