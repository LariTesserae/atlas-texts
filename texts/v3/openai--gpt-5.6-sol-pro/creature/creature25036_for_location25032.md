No creature of the high country thinks of itself as living *on* the plateau.

To live there is to live in it, through it, beneath its skin or among the drafts that pass between its bones. Even the people do not possess a word corresponding exactly to land. Their nearest words mean *the thing that bears us*, *the woven height*, or, less reverently, *what has not fallen yet*.

The smallest inhabitants are the most numerous: translucent mites in the sap-lit veins, frost worms curled inside hollow moss hairs, and minute six-winged pollinators that thaw only when a leaf warms around them. Many spend most of their lives dormant. They are carried as dust in wool, bark cracks, nostrils, pond scum, and the pleats of folded leaves. A brief mist wakes them. For an hour a dry slope may boil with feeding and mating; then the moisture is taken up, and the entire generation seals itself into pearls no larger than pollen.

The leaf felt has its own hidden fauna. Flat-bodied siftlings pass between compressed needles without disturbing the surface. They have no eyes, only rows of pressure pits, and feed on fungi, shed scales, dung, and one another. Where the felt grows thick, they form deep colonies around pockets of fermentation. Their heat makes faint soft spots underfoot. Canopy runners hunt these colonies, but so do the people, who probe for warmth with long thorns and cut out blocks of inhabited felt during famine.

Siftlings taste of resin and cold iron. They are eaten alive because cooking them causes their defensive bladders to burst.

The fur-moss plains belong to woolbacks.

From a distance a moving herd looks like weather passing over the ground: a gray front, broad and indistinct, with ripples of silver where individuals turn. Up close, each animal is low-slung and six-legged, with split gripping feet and a blunt head nearly hidden by its coat. The front pair of limbs comb moss. The middle pair bear most of the weight. The rear pair anchor when the branch beneath them bucks.

Their wool is not entirely their own. Moss spores, hitching seeds, insulating molds, tiny mites, and threadlike scavengers live in it. Older woolbacks carry miniature gardens. In damp seasons flowers open along their flanks. Calves recognize their mothers partly by the pattern and scent of this traveling vegetation.

They migrate not simply toward food but along the slow warmth of moving sap. A herd may stop without visible reason, mill for half a day, then turn together and climb a ridge whose slope appears barren. Days later the ridge glows beneath them as deep channels fill. The plateau’s people say woolbacks hear tomorrow through their feet.

They are hunted, but rarely driven. A panicked herd can strip a trail, rupture a cistern, or place enough weight on a strained seam to tear open a valley. Hunters approach from the sides and select one animal with barbed cords. The killing is done quickly, before the herd’s agitation passes into the wood.

Behind the herds come the runners.

A canopy runner is longer than it first appears. Much of its body remains submerged in the leaf felt, sliding between mat and branch, while its eyes and breathing slits break the surface in separate places. A traveler may see six pale eyes arranged along ten paces of trail and mistake them for several animals. Then the spine moves beneath the black needles, and all the eyes vanish at once.

Its dozens of short limbs end in soft hooks. It feels vibrations through the supporting bough and can distinguish a woolback’s gait from falling debris, a human hammer from a cracking stem, and a wounded animal from one merely tired. It attacks upward. The felt erupts, the prey is pulled down, and within minutes only a trembling patch of moss remains.

Runners do not like resonance. Travelers carry ribbed sticks which they drag behind them, producing an irregular chatter through the wood. Regular beats attract curiosity; irregular ones suggest unstable ground. Children learn safe walking rhythms before they learn all the names of their kin.

In the chasms live creatures that have largely abandoned the distinction between swimming and flight.

The smallest gliders are called sail-fingers. They cling in colonies beneath ledges, stacked like dark leaves. When disturbed, they release in a cloud and steer with membranes stretched between four elongated limbs. They graze airborne spores and ice crystals. At night their bellies flash in sequence, each animal answering the one ahead, until the flock resembles a glowing script written across the void.

Larger forms carry internal bladders of warm gas. Their skeletons are lattices rather than solid bones, and their bodies are so lightly made that one stranded on the plateau may be unable to stand. These broadwings spend years without landing. They sleep in ascending currents, bear live young in the air, and drink by passing through mist with their mouths open.

The greatest are known by shadows, drumbeats, and the scars where they have briefly attached themselves to the country. Their wings may span the distance between neighboring ridges. Along the leading edges, muscular cables alter the tension of the membrane; a single adjustment produces a concussion felt more than heard. They feed on spore clouds, swarms, and sometimes whole colonies of smaller gliders. During storms they fasten to the underside of the plateau with hundreds of pale gripping tendrils. People descending for root fiber occasionally find one hanging there, folded and sleeping, immense as a valley.

They are not considered animals in the ordinary sense. Some settlements regard them as weather with offspring.

Bark vents support another order of life. Heat-beetles begin as soft white grubs in chambers surrounding the warm sap core. They cultivate fermenting molds there, plastering the walls with chewed pith and their own mineral-rich secretions. A healthy colony keeps its chamber warmer than blood despite the cold outside. The host growth tolerates this, perhaps because the beetles remove rot, perhaps because their heat aids sap flow, or perhaps because the relationship is too old to separate into benefit and harm.

When pressure and temperature align, adult beetles emerge all at once.

They are black until they open their wing cases. Beneath, their bodies glow orange from stored chemical heat. Millions may pour from a vent in a single incandescent column, transforming a dark ravine into something like sunrise. They mate in flight, cool rapidly, and fall. For a few minutes the ground becomes warm enough to steam.

Everything comes to feed.

Needle-jaws leap after them. Sail-fingers sweep low. Woolbacks turn from the moss. Runners expose dangerous lengths of themselves. Humans spread woven nets, wearing eye coverings against the brightness. Even nearby leaves open and cup the dead beetles, digesting them slowly.

A handful of plateau animals are heavy, which means their lives are shaped by apology.

The knuckleback is one: a broad, armor-plated browser with eight pillar limbs and a mouth capable of shearing young blade-ferns. It never walks continuously. It takes three or four steps, then settles its weight over as wide an area as possible and remains still while the surface adjusts. Its belly plates hum against the branch. Sensitive folds in its feet monitor the answering tension. Only when the wood’s vibration steadies does it move again.

Knucklebacks can take a day to cross the width of a village. People dislike meeting them on narrow boughs but seldom kill them. Their paths become unusually strong, reinforced by decades of repeated pressure, and many of the safest human routes began as knuckleback trails.

More feared are the unmoored.

These are not one species but many: animals, parasitic plants, and compound colonies that spend their lives hanging below the shelf. They climb root curtains, feed from underside condensation, and never see the upper surface unless rupture exposes them. Most are blind and colorless. Some resemble long strings of jointed hands. Some are sacks trailing mouths. Some are branching colonies mistaken for roots until every branch begins to crawl.

When a district tears, unmoored things spill upward around the fracture, driven by pressure, light, or the sudden loss of their feeding grounds. They are poorly adapted to the cold upper air and usually die, but for several nights a fresh chasm may be crowded with pale forms hauling themselves over its edge.

The people avoid such places. Not all the unmoored are harmless, and not all of them are animals.

Human beings are among the rarest inhabitants.

They are not a single nation. Distance, moving terrain, and the danger of concentrated weight keep communities small and scattered. A typical settlement contains a few extended households—perhaps thirty people, perhaps a hundred—distributed around a branch rather than gathered in one place. Their shelters migrate in a slow circuit. One season they occupy the warm barkward side, another the lee of a fern ridge, another the margins of a pond. By the time they return to the first site, their old walls have thickened, healed, or been absorbed.

Most people are compactly built, though not especially short, with broad gripping feet and strong hands. Children are carried in slings long after they can walk. Falling is the first danger they understand. Toddlers wear trailing loops that snag on vegetation, and family shelters are hung with low nets. No one stands casually near an edge. No one runs without first knowing what lies under the felt.

Their clothing is layered from woolback fiber, seedcloth, and strips of shed bark membrane. Outer garments are dark and glassy to absorb the weak sun. Beneath them, heat-beetle cages rest against the ribs, separated from skin by woven pads. The glow leaks through perforations in small orange constellations. A group of travelers seen at night may look less like people than a line of embers walking carefully across the black.

Every person carries cord.

Cord binds clothing, catches falls, reads wind, tests depth, marks stress, snares food, splints limbs, and measures whether a crack has widened. To cut another person’s safety line is the clearest form of murder. To give someone the last intact length of one’s cord is a declaration more binding than marriage.

Because fixed roads cannot exist, navigation belongs to memory and listening. Route-keepers memorize not only junctions but tones: the pitch of bridge shoots, the hum of root cables, the hollow knock of unsafe bark, the seasonal cadence of major sap channels. Their maps are sung. A route song changes tempo where the ground slopes, rises in pitch approaching tension, and breaks into held notes at water sites. Old songs are dangerous if repeated without correction, so each performance includes the name of the person who last traveled the way and the condition in which they found it.

Settlements exchange these songs when they meet.

Such meetings are infrequent and elaborate. Neither group steps fully onto the other’s branch until both have tested the load. Gifts are passed across first: resin glass, beetle brood, preserved pond leaves, runner teeth, carefully bundled spores, news of fractures. Only then do people cross one at a time. Large gatherings are spread over kilometers, with drums carrying conversation between camps.

Their drums are not merely instruments. They are organs of public speech. A branch transmits low sound farther and more reliably than air, and a skilled drummer can send a message through country invisible beyond ridges. There are patterns for water, migration, swarm, rupture, sickness, and death. There are warnings that mean *do not answer; listening predator nearby*. There are courtship rhythms deliberately complicated so that only the intended person will recognize the variation.

Some communities drum to the plateau itself.

They insist that certain growths answer differently depending on season, injury, or mood. Skeptics say this is only pressure, sap flow, and wind. The drummers agree and do not see the distinction.

No people of the high country believe the shelf is a single mind in the human manner. It has no face, no central desire, no memory that can be questioned. Yet neither do they regard it as insensible. Its responses are slow, distributed, contradictory. One district may accept a settlement for generations while another thickens bark beneath a sleeping child. A bridge may tense before a storm. A burdened branch may reinforce itself—or begin quietly killing its own tissues around the burden so that the section will shed cleanly later.

Accordingly, morality is partly a matter of weight.

Greed means taking so much that the branch recoils. Cowardice means letting another bear a load whose tension you have already felt. Hospitality is the art of distributing guests. Wisdom is leaving before welcome becomes endurance.

Their dead are lowered through openings in the canopy in seedcloth shrouds. The cords are paid out slowly while relatives drum the person’s route song. Some bodies are taken by gliders. Some catch in root curtains and become pale hanging gardens. Rarely, a shroud’s seeds germinate before the body is consumed, producing a slender red shoot far below the inhabited surface. Such a shoot is watched for as long as it remains visible. Families claim that if it reaches another root and braids there, the dead have founded new country.

There are also solitary people.

Pond-followers travel with collapsible bowls and know how to read the curling of thirsty leaves. Vent-keepers tend domesticated beetle colonies. Edge-walkers live near growing margins, where the ground is red, hot, flexible, and not yet trustworthy. They cross new shoots on broad woven frames that spread their weight, trimming parasites and encouraging braids. A good edge-walker may help join two exploratory branches and thereby create a future province.

Fracture-watchers occupy more ominous places. They measure white seams, mark their widening with resin pegs, and send drum warnings into the interior. They are respected but thought strange. Years spent beside a wound alter the sense of scale. Fracture-watchers speak of districts as if they were already falling and of falling districts as if they remain part of the world.

Sometimes they refuse to leave.

Life does not end at the plateau’s broken margins. A detached province carries whole populations downward: moss plains, ponds, burrowers, herds, human shelters, and glowing veins. Most vanish beneath the clouds. No one above knows how long they fall or what waits below. Yet very rarely, during unusual weather, drums answer from beneath the cloud deck.

The beats are faint, slowed by distance and obscured by thunder.

They may be echoes traveling through the supporting trunks. They may come from people living on lower branches no upper traveler has seen. They may be produced by something that has learned the warning rhythms.

When such sounds rise, everything nearby seems to listen. Humans put their hands to the bark. Woolbacks lift their heads. Runners cease moving under the felt. Even the blade-ferns, between gusts, fall briefly still.

Then the shelf groans, the sap knocks in its chambers, and the innumerable inhabitants resume their climbing, grazing, hunting, drinking, weaving, and holding on—each alive by virtue of lightness, attachment, or the ability to let go.
