At first the inhabitants seem as pale as everything around them.

Seen across the Worn Forum, they are narrow figures in chalk-colored wraps, crossing one at a time with the careful, unhurried gait of people walking over something asleep. Their clothing hangs close so that no loose edge catches against stone. Their heads are often covered against falling grit and hard phases of ceiling light. They carry flat baskets against their hips, rolled hides across their shoulders, infants bound high against the chest, and slender staffs used less for balance than for feeling what travels through the ground.

Only when they come near does their variety become apparent.

Their skin ranges from the gray-white of old mortar to muted brown, yellow clay, and the bluish cast of limestone seen through water. It is rarely uniform. Mineral dust settles into pores and creases; brine leaves pale borders at collars and wrists; habitual kneeling darkens the shins. Many rub exposed skin with rendered fat and powdered calcite, producing a dull, protective sheen. Workers from the wet chambers smell faintly sour. Those who tend warm fissures carry a sulfurous warmth in their hair and clothes. Cistern keepers are recognizable by the clean salt smell of their hands.

They are not blind.

Their eyes are large, but not fantastically so, and their pupils contract to pinpricks beneath a brightening seam. Irises tend toward washed colors: smoke, amber diluted almost to clear, the pink-gray of shell interiors. The whites are often darkly veined. Generations beneath mineral light have not abolished sight; they have made it cautious. People look indirectly at brilliance, observing reflections in polished stone or in small plates of darkened shell. In dim districts they notice motion well, but color has little importance to them. They distinguish substances by luster, grain, translucency, temperature, and the way a surface accepts moisture.

A child learns dozens of whites before learning to count.

There is wet white, load-bearing white, edible white, fever white, the white of a seam about to kindle, the dead white that appears on stone before it powders apart. Two slabs that would seem identical to an outsider may be, to them, as different as meat and poison.

Their hearing is more conspicuous than their sight. The outer ears are broad and close to the skull, with complex folds that can be pressed shut by a small movement of the jaw. Most people keep their hair cropped short around them. When listening through stone, they place not only an ear but the hinge of the jaw against a wall or floor, teeth slightly parted. Older citizens often have worn patches at the temples from a lifetime of doing this.

The soles of their feet are thick, yet exquisitely sensitive to vibration. Indoors, almost everyone goes barefoot. Sandals are thin, soft, and easily removed. A person entering a dwelling touches the threshold with the toes before stepping across, not as ceremony alone but to learn whether the house has altered since the last visit.

They walk without striking the heel. Even children run quietly.

This does not make them solemn. It makes their liveliness difficult to recognize from a distance.

Their humor is carried in shoulders, fingers, and eyes. A crowded eating room may appear silent while everyone in it is laughing. Jokes are signed rapidly beneath the edge of a table. Friends insult one another by imitating the gait of badly balanced walls. Children play games in which one must cross a courtyard while the others, eyes closed, try to detect each footfall through the paving. Lovers tease by altering familiar signs so slightly that only one person understands them.

Speech exists and is richly developed, but voices are generally low. The language favors breath, tongue clicks, soft palate sounds, and consonants made without closing the lips sharply. In unsafe districts, speech may be abandoned for hours. The public sign language is not a reduced version of the spoken one; it has its own poetry, obscenity, formal rhetoric, and regional accents.

A person can sign with one hand while carrying stone with the other. Builders converse along entire walls through relays of visible gestures. Market bargaining may take place between people thirty paces apart. In council, the speaker stands with hands illuminated against a dark stone screen, and disagreement travels around the assembly as small repeated motions.

Names are given twice.

The first name belongs to the household and is usually intimate: a touch pattern, a mouth sound, or both. The second is chosen when a child becomes old enough to recognize instability. To earn it, the child is taken through its district and asked to identify water routes, animal paths, safe hollows, dangerous roofs, and the nearest place to shelter if the floor opens. This public name often refers to something noticed during that walk: Sees-the-Damp-Line, Waits-at-Cracks, Three-Shells, Hears-Under-Basins.

Names may change after displacement. A person who moves with a dismantled house sometimes takes the name of the old threshold stone, preserving in speech a place that no longer exists.

Kinship is similarly portable. Households are not defined only by blood but by shared stones, shared water rights, and obligations of rescue. Someone may belong to the people of one cistern, sleep among the stones of another household, and inherit duties from a third. Marriage does not necessarily combine residences. The important agreements concern who will carry whose walls, who may open whose water jars, who takes the children when warning marks appear, and which animals must be allowed through.

Every household keeps its building in a state that looks, to outsiders, half finished. This is intentional. Roof slabs are numbered. Wall stones bear shallow marks indicating order, load, and ownership. Goods fit into standardized hide bundles. Sleeping platforms can be lifted by four adults. Sacred objects are small. An heirloom too heavy to move is regarded with suspicion, and families tell cautionary tales about ancestors who loved a doorway, a cistern lip, or a carved seat more than they loved the living.

The oldest people possess the most portable wealth: knowledge.

They remember which floor cracks reopened after three generations, where brine once rose beneath a market, which pale runners become poisonous during long bright phases, and how a distant district used to lie before a terrace sank. Their memories are tested publicly. No single elder is trusted with a civic fact. Important histories must be held by several unrelated people, ideally from districts exposed to different dangers.

Records also exist in stone, but they are not written as long continuous texts. Tablets break and walls move. Instead, records are distributed.

A cistern’s history may be carved around a set of small disks kept in separate households. The sequence of grooves on each disk means little by itself. Brought together, they describe flood heights, contaminants, repairs, and deaths. Maps are made as flexible nets, each knot representing a landmark and each cord a route. When the city changes, the cords are retied. No map is considered honest unless part of it can move.

Children learn this early. They are indulged in many things, but never permitted to ignore the ground.

Before they can speak, adults tap warnings into their palms. Before they can carry water, they learn not to run beneath a dry whisper in the roof. They are taught to sleep through harmless settling and wake at certain faint rhythms. Their games involve sorting fragments, assembling miniature arches, tracing flood routes with trickles poured from shells, and identifying animals by the vibration they make beneath a covered bowl.

They are fascinated by anything newly arrived.

After floods, children crowd the sorting basins despite every attempt to keep them back. They watch clear-bodied creatures knot and unknot themselves in the water. They invent names faster than naturalists can forbid them. Many district animals owe their accepted names to an ancient child’s joke whose meaning has been forgotten.

The inhabitants eat in small groups, often around communal warm stones. Meals are pale but not monotonous to them. Cultivated film paste may be fermented until sharp, dried into brittle sheets, diluted into broth, or packed around hot mineral pebbles until it sets. Milk from the grazers is thin, salty, and faintly metallic. Eggs are opened with flint points. Cistern flesh is eaten raw when safe, pressed, brined, or laid on warmed slabs until it clouds.

Texture matters more than appearance. A formal meal proceeds from slick foods to fibrous ones, then to crisp salt crusts that clean the teeth. Strong flavors are prized: sourness, mineral bitterness, blood, hot brine. The rarest delicacy is a fat-rich membrane from a deep-water creature that appears only after particular floods. Portions are translucent and nearly tasteless at first, then warm in the mouth for several minutes.

Nobody eats without setting aside something for the cultivation chambers or the small scavengers under the floor. This is not always reverence. Waste allowed to decay unpredictably is dangerous. Their rituals often begin as maintenance and become inseparable from affection.

They do not worship the cavern as a person. They distrust any story that grants it intention.

Instead, their ceremonies attend to relations: weight upon support, water within hollow, creature along path, body within house, house within district. A shrine may consist of two stones that must never touch. Another is a basin kept empty through every rain. Some households preserve a fragment from each dwelling they have abandoned, grinding a little of it into the mortarless dust beneath the next threshold.

At funerals, the dead are not sealed into permanent tombs. A tomb that insists on remaining where it was placed would violate both law and common sense. Bodies may be given to brine pits, warm colonies, deep cistern creatures, or carefully managed scavenger chambers. Bones are later recovered, cleaned, divided among relatives, and made useful. A grandmother may remain as toggles on water bags, a listening disk placed against the floor, a polished handle on a child’s tool.

This is not considered desecration. To keep the dead from civic use would be to exile them.

Some bones are never recovered. People lost to sudden water are said to have entered the unsorted districts. Their names remain in flood recitations until the routes that took them no longer exist.

The city’s most respected citizens are not rulers in a fixed sense. Authority shifts by circumstance. During a flood, drain readers and cistern wardens command. During a roof-shedding phase, weight judges overrule household heads. When a herd changes its path, animal guides decide which walls must be opened. In quiet years, water accountants and memory keepers dominate public arguments.

There are officials whose sole duty is to disagree with new construction.

They crouch beside foundations, taste dust, set bowls of water along proposed walls, and return through several cycles of ceiling light. They ask where the collapse will go. They ask who benefits if pressure is diverted. They ask which creatures used the site before people did. An architect who cannot show how a building should fail is treated as someone who has not designed it.

Builders therefore think in joints, gaps, slopes, and sacrificial weakness. Their pride lies not in making the immovable but in arranging a graceful surrender. A roof that folds inward without crushing its occupants may be praised for generations. A wall that breaks exactly along its prepared seam earns its maker a public hand sign of honor, even if the maker has been dead for a century.

Their bodies show this endless labor. Fingers are broad at the tips, nails kept short, forearms marked by pale scars. Many have one shoulder higher from carrying slabs. Knees thicken early. Copper ornaments, when worn, are thin and curved so they cannot become blades during a fall. Necklaces break under tension. Bracelets are open. No ceremonial garment is allowed to trap its wearer in rubble.

Beauty is found in fit without fastening.

A well-made garment wraps securely but can be shed with one pull. A beautiful wall appears solid from afar and reveals, close up, the minute channels through which it will part. Faces are decorated with lines of mineral powder that emphasize movement: the flex of the brow, the speaking hand, the throat. During courtship, people sometimes dust their palms in contrasting shades so gestures remain visible across a bright plaza.

Courtship itself often begins with listening. Two people lie side by side, not touching, with their ears to the pavement. They compare what they hear.

Some claim they can recognize compatibility in whether another person notices the same hidden water. More practical families judge a prospective partner by how calmly they pack a room during a warning, whether they hoard water, and whether they know how to approach a frightened grazer without driving it into a wall.

The inhabitants are gentle with many animals, but not sentimental. They milk, trap, slaughter, skin, harvest, and eat them. What matters is whether this is done without disrupting the routes on which the city itself depends. Herds are followed and counted. Armored beasts are scraped free of parasites as they pass under the bridges. Drifting ceiling creatures are collected only after they shed naturally, except in famine.

Certain animals attach themselves to households.

Small crack-runners learn to emerge when eating stones are warmed. Families distinguish individuals by mouth shape and fluid pattern. A pale grazer may return to the same courtyard for decades, kneeling there during bright phases and leaving a damp silhouette when it rises. Such an animal is not owned, but the household may carve its path mark above the doorway and argue fiercely if a neighbor obstructs its approach.

The people fear animals that fall silent more than those that attack.

A plaza abruptly emptied of runners, a herd standing with pressure pits closed, ceiling drifters descending at the wrong brightness—these are warnings. Human watchers may debate the meaning, but evacuation begins before the debate ends.

There are people who choose to live beyond the settled districts. Some tend isolated cultivation chambers. Some map water by setting rows of hollow shells into the floor and returning to see which have cracked. Some follow herds into the glare, carrying little beyond brine, hide, and numbered marker stones. They come back months or years later with unfamiliar parasites, altered signs, and accounts of walls that may have built themselves.

A few descend whenever older levels open.

These lower explorers are regarded with admiration mixed with unease. They bind cords around their waists, but cords are useless when a passage closes by convergence rather than collapse. They carry reflective plates to signal through narrow openings and small animals whose behavior reveals bad water or changing pressure. What they retrieve is seldom treasure: softened tools, pieces of worked shell, residues from extinct cultivation slabs, bones with joints arranged in unfamiliar numbers.

More troubling are objects made in recognizable city styles but found beneath layers believed older than those styles. Such finds are placed in public wells or distributed among memory keepers so that no single interpretation hardens around them.

The inhabitants resist final explanations. Their philosophers are valued not for deciding whether the cavern lives but for making distinctions precise enough to prevent bad action. They debate whether a closing fissure is the same place when it opens again; whether an animal path survives the extinction of the animal; whether a household still owns stones worn too smooth to bear its marks; whether stability is a condition or merely a movement too slow to feel.

These arguments are conducted with great seriousness and much concealed amusement.

They have music, though an outsider might initially miss it. Drums are rare and dangerous. Their instruments hum, breathe, scrape, and resonate through the body. Thin stone plates are stroked with wetted fingers. Hollow shells are held to warm cracks, where rising air produces wavering tones. Sinew strings are stretched across loose frames designed to detune as the floor moves. A long composition may follow the changing pitch of a wall over several days.

Some performances are felt through the feet rather than heard. The audience sits or lies on the pavement, palms down. Skilled musicians can send delicate patterns through a plaza without alarming the creatures beneath it. The highest praise is not that the music overcame the city’s sounds, but that it revealed rhythms already present.

When the mineral seams brighten, the inhabitants appear almost insubstantial. Their wraps, powdered faces, and pale tools merge with the glare. Only their eyes, mouths, and moving hands remain distinct. When the light recedes, they gather substance again. Scars emerge. Copper glints. The translucent panels in their clothing show layers of sinew and shell. Children become shadows under catchments. Elders sit against warm walls with listening stones tucked beneath their jaws.

They are not a hidden people waiting to discover a greater world above. Few think of the surface except as a direction from which weight comes. “Above” means burden, roof, compression, the unknown thickness through which no useful path has been found. Their journeys are lateral or downward, toward water, warmth, old roads, and passages where unfamiliar animals appear.

Nor do they consider themselves trapped.

To them, an open world without a roof would be the incomprehensible one: a place with nothing overhead to read, no luminous stress passing from district to district, no enclosing stone to carry distant movement. Silence there would be empty rather than informative. Rain falling from an unreachable height would seem wasteful and obscene.

They belong to a city whose permanence is made entirely of departures.

They expect to lose rooms, streets, wells, professions, names, even the shape of familiar districts. They grieve these things intensely. They do not pretend that ease with rebuilding abolishes sorrow. When a household dismantles a home, members touch each stone before it is loaded. When a street closes, those who used it walk its length one final time if they can. When a cistern fails, a little of its last water is carried to every basin that depended on it.

Then they move the stones.

From the high terraces they can be seen doing so in long, pale files: adults pulling hide sledges, adolescents balancing baskets of numbered rubble, elders directing with economical hands, children ranging ahead to test the ground. Crack-runners stream beside them. A grazer herd divides to let them pass and closes behind.

No command travels audibly across the cavern. No bell begins the work.

One person feels the pavement lift. Another sees water tremble in a bowl. Across the district, heads turn. Palms descend to stone. Signs pass from terrace to terrace faster than speech.

Walls are opened. Roofs are lowered. Cistern mouths are covered. Sleeping platforms are raised onto sledges. The oldest and youngest leave first, carrying the smallest pieces of the house.

By the time the street arches into its slow wave, the inhabitants are already elsewhere—not fled, not defeated, but spread across the cavern floor with their tools, their animals, their dead, their water, and all the marked stones from which another arrangement of the ancient city will be made.
