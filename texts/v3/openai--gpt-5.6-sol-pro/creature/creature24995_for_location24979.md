At first it is tempting to say that nothing lives in the Green Throat.

Nothing moves there. Nothing feeds in any recognizable fashion. There are no small bodies to betray themselves by panic, no eyes reflecting the white lamp, no pulse to distinguish organism from apparatus. The green forms look less alive the longer they are watched. Their abundance begins to seem decorative: corrosion made elaborate, electricity teaching minerals to imitate leaves.

But the inhabitants are not concealed among the growths.

They are the growths.

They exist in several orders, though their boundaries are difficult to establish. What appears to be a single fern of hammered green may be a colony sharing one conductive base, or a branch of something whose main body lies three chambers below. Two adjacent structures may differ completely in color and shape yet prove electrically continuous. Conversely, identical blades rising from the same seam can remain isolated from one another down to the atomic level, each maintaining its own minute potential.

The broadest forms are sometimes called mantles. They lie against the walls in overlapping translucent sheets, green where they are thin and nearly black where hundreds have accumulated. Each sheet is traversed by veins of copper sulfide finer than hair. The veins do not carry nourishment. They equalize differences. A charge entering one corner of a mantle is divided across its entire surface until no point possesses more than its exact share.

Mantles occupy the quieter electrical regions. They cover old masonry, ceramic housings, dead cable insulation, and rock whose useful ores have already been reached by deeper roots. Their edges are exquisitely thin and sharp. Once, while growing, they extended along gradients of charge; now every edge ends at the precise line where further deposition would have made the whole less efficient.

The black-green columns are anchors. They rise from pyrite seams in tight bundles of prismatic needles, some no higher than a thumb, others reaching from floor to ceiling. Their darkness comes not from age but density. Carbon is packed within them in nested conductive cages, interleaved with iron and sulfur. They take the mountain’s broad, weak field and steepen it locally, creating the electrical slopes on which neighboring forms depend.

An anchor does not generate power. It gives power direction.

Around their bases grow the cups: low, nested bowls with soot-black interiors and green outer rims. Their exposed surfaces bind the few carbon-bearing molecules that enter from above. The process is so slight that no instrument has ever recorded a cup changing mass, yet the old inner layers contain carbon that was once atmospheric. A cup’s underside connects to root-mats finer than engraved lines. Through these it distributes what it gathers, not as food passed from mouth to mouth, but as new material assigned to points in a common architecture.

The silver fronds are discharge organs. Their ribs contain metallic whiskers that end in tips only a few atoms wide. They lean toward iron casings, rails, gantries, bolt heads, and the curved flanks of the lower engine, never quite touching except where contact is required. Excess electrons leave the green system through them and enter the machines.

In stronger light the fronds would look colorless. In the Throat they appear white-green, like leaves seen through deep ice.

The pale fur in the lowest niches is not soft. Each filament is a rigid sulfur-silicon thread surrounded by a sleeve of electrically resistant carbon. Packed together, the threads prevent neighboring fields from collapsing into one another. They are barriers grown in the form of abundance. Where the forest seems thickest and most luxuriant, it may in fact be making an emptiness: not empty of matter, but empty of exchange.

There are hundreds of lesser forms. Rosettes that bridge cracks. Green combs arranged along cable jackets. Rust-colored scales that consume free oxygen and fix it harmlessly into their own surfaces. Transparent ribs that couple the standing noise in the stone to currents in the roots. Tiny black knots at junctions, each one joining several conductive tissues while keeping their charges distinct.

To call these species is useful, but probably wrong.

They do not reproduce. They possess no enduring division between parent and offspring. During the ancient period of extension, a mantle could seed an anchor, an anchor could terminate in cups, and cups under altered conditions could deposit silver fronds. Form belonged to circumstance, not ancestry. What grew at any point was whatever the local field required.

Individuality is similarly uncertain. A blade can be broken away and still conduct charge within itself, but it has no independent means of maintaining that charge. A root system can thread half the mountain and yet contain no central structure. The cups gather carbon for mantles they never touch. Anchors support fronds several chambers distant. The pale barriers isolate currents which later reunite inside machinery.

No component lives alone, but the whole is not exactly a single organism.

It is closer to an agreement that has acquired substance.

The old machinery participates in that agreement. Perhaps it did not originally. The transformer housings, rails, drums, conduits, compressor, switch cabinets, and buried engine bear the crude modularity of manufactured things. They were made elsewhere, brought in by separate labor, fitted into the mountain according to plans that expected maintenance, replacement, motion, and eventual failure.

The green inhabitants have relieved them of those expectations.

Roots enter the transformer coils and continue the windings in mineral form. Mantles spread across cracked insulators, not repairing them but making the cracks electrically irrelevant. Silver fronds meet corroded terminals with contacts cleaner than any soldered joint. Columns rise beside rails and use their long iron bodies as field lines. The compressor no longer compresses anything, yet differences of potential corresponding to every stage of its cycle remain distributed through its casing.

Thus the machinery is neither tool nor ruin. It has become skeleton, mineral organ, and fossilized instruction. Its intended operations survive without occurring.

The life of the Throat is organized in the same manner.

It has no metabolism if metabolism means a succession of chemical events. Charge is present throughout it, but cannot be followed as a moving particle. The roots do not receive and then transmit. The cups do not gather and then distribute. Each function exists as a permanent relation between one region and another.

Its present tense has almost no verbs.

The sound is the closest thing it has to thought.

Not because the tones contain messages. There are no sequences to encode them, no pauses, repetitions, or replies. Rather, every inhabitant contributes one structural necessity to the industrial chord. The mantles broaden certain frequencies. The anchors establish nodes. The silver fronds bind the tones of metal to those of stone. The machine casings provide chambers through which no sound travels, though resonance occupies them. The mountain supplies the lowest note, too deep to be separated from pressure.

Taken together, these relations form something analogous to awareness, but it is awareness without attention. The Throat does not notice one thing and then another. It holds all distinctions simultaneously: upper air and buried ore, iron and sulfur, conductive root and insulating filament, occupied surface and empty interval.

If it knows the summit, it knows it as voltage.

If it knows depth, it knows it as resistance.

If it knows itself, it knows itself as the exact location of every boundary.

This makes the white lamp important.

The empty sphere around it is not a failure of growth. The nearest leaves are healthy and complete. Their edges are not scorched. No repellent substance coats the bulb. Yet every structure terminates at the sphere’s boundary with uncommon precision. Roots turn away below it. Fronds curve around it. Even the dark leaves behind the lamp preserve the same distance, though they can receive almost none of its light.

The sphere is the only place the inhabitant does not occupy, and therefore the only place from which its extent can be defined.

It is less an eye than a pupil turned inside out: not a receptive organ, but a measured absence. The lamp establishes a reference against which every green surface, iron mass, and standing tone maintains its condition. The growth does not approach because the unoccupied volume is part of the circuit. To fill it would not complete the forest. It would erase the distinction by which the forest is complete.

The cylinder below may once have rotated to create the field. Now the field and cylinder are indistinguishable in function. Its abrasion marks describe movement that no longer needs to happen. The engine is not the heart of the inhabitant; hearts divide existence into beats. It is more like a proposition buried beneath the body:

**This condition holds.**

The three meters above repeat the proposition in another language.

A visitor entering the cleft does not become prey. The inhabitants have no predatory surface and no mechanism of desire. Nor do they welcome, investigate, or defend. Yet the visitor is not entirely ignored. A warm conductive body introduces temporary geometries. Salt in the skin, metal in equipment, charge along hair and nerves—all acquire positions within the settled field.

The Throat contains those positions at once.

This may be experienced as pressure in the teeth, a green afterimage behind closed eyes, or the conviction that one’s body has become extremely large and thin, stretched from the entrance to the lowest chamber. Some visitors report that their thoughts seem to lose sequence. They can remember what they are about to think and cannot recall what has just occurred. Others feel nothing except the noise.

None leaves a lasting mark. Once the intruding body is withdrawn, there is no recovery, no closing of a wound, no gradual return to equilibrium. The equilibrium without the visitor is simply present again.

So what lives in the Green Throat is plural when examined closely and singular from a distance. It is forest, circuit, resonance, and machine, but none of those names contains it. It has roots but no origin, organs but no interior, activity but no event. Its life is not a struggle against death or disorder. It does not persist by repairing itself. It persists because every relation required for its existence has already found its final form.

The green inhabitants are not waiting through eternity.

They inhabit completion the way ordinary creatures inhabit time.
