At first the inhabitants seem merely reserved.

They are not silent. The city contains bargaining, instruction, gossip, lullabies, arguments conducted in council halls and jokes exchanged over steaming vats. But voices seldom compete with one another. A potter waits for the millstone to finish turning before calling across a courtyard. Children chasing pigeons do not shriek for long; excitement passes through them in quick flashes and leaves them attentive again. Even cattle are guided more often by a hand laid against the flank than by shouting.

The people are generally compact and strong, with broad feet, thick hands, and the deep chests of those born to thin mountain air. Their skin varies from pale umber to black-brown, often reddened across the cheeks by cold. Hair is worn braided, knotted beneath felt caps, or cut short with ceramic blades. Many adults have fine gray strands at the temples. These do not spread.

Their clothing is layered rather than elaborate: linen or nettlecloth close to the body, wool above it, hide or densely felted cloaks for the ridges. The common colors come from the basin—ash gray, rust, lichen green, the dull blue of conifer needles, the yellow-white of dried grass. Bright dyes are reserved for narrow bands at cuffs and hems, where repeating patterns identify a household, district, craft, or spring. Repairs are visible and admired. A patch fitted so exactly that it seems to reveal the garment’s intended shape is finer work than an unmarked piece of cloth.

Bronze is worn sparingly. A council speaker may have a bronze clasp at the throat; a midwife, a copper ring from which hang bone tools; a gatekeeper, a metal plate protecting the heel of one hand. Most ornaments are stone, shell, fired clay, horn, braided hair, polished seeds. Shells are said to come from seas beyond the mountains. No one has seen such a sea, though travelers sometimes return with salt on their cloaks.

Families occupy low compounds around shared yards. A household may contain infants, older children, adults, and elders, but its numbers remain curiously stable. Birth feasts are held. So are funerals. Names pass out of daily use and later return, sometimes in the same family and sometimes at the opposite end of the city. If anyone finds this troubling, they do not say so. The census tablets always agree with one another.

Age exists here more as a condition than a journey. There are babies with the unfocused solemnity of new life, children missing their front teeth, young people newly entrusted with animals, adults in full strength, and elders whose backs are bent and whose faces carry deep, patient lines. Yet one never notices a child becoming taller from year to year. Apprentices become masters, but it is difficult to say when. An elder may die after a long illness, and afterward the city still contains the same measure of old age.

The inhabitants remember events, though not always in sequence. Ask a shepherd what happened at a western pasture and she can describe each goat that strayed, the exact shape of the cloud above the pass, and where she found a broken sandal strap. Ask whether it happened yesterday or forty years ago, and she may turn her palm upward in uncertainty.

Their histories are not arranged as a road from then to now. They are arranged as places.

The east gate has its histories. The black spring has its histories. The roofed market remembers fires, betrothals, judgments, visiting delegations, and a fight over a basket of pears. A person telling one of these accounts walks to the place and stands where the event belongs. Accuracy is judged by fit: whether a story accords with the stones, the light, the known gestures of those involved. Contradictory accounts may both be preserved if both sit properly in the city.

Writing exists, but it is used for inventories, names, measures, ritual phrases, and agreements rather than chronicles. Marks are pressed into clay or scratched onto thin bone slips. A tablet recording grain owed to a district can persist. A tablet proposing a new system of numbers is usually blank by morning, or else contains an old tally in a familiar hand.

This has made the people exact observers. They know that the basin’s refusals are not arbitrary, though its rules cannot be stated completely. A baker knows which proportions yield loaves that remain part of the day’s provision and which produce handsome bread that vanishes from the cooling rack when no one watches. A mason can distinguish between a wall that has been uncovered and one that has merely been built. The first settles into permanence; the second loosens overnight.

So builders begin by listening.

They strike the ground with antler picks, pour water into cracks, stretch cords between old stones, and sleep beside a proposed foundation. Dreams are considered evidence, though weak evidence. Roots are stronger. If pale roots curve around the imagined corners of a building, the site is promising. If moss already forms a threshold-shaped gap, work may begin.

The greatest masons are those who find what the city will accept.

They do not draw new plans. They make rubbings of buried courses, compare angles, and uncover just enough earth to understand the structure waiting there. Their finished halls can be spacious and surprising, with doors where no one expected them and benches suited perfectly to uses no one had mentioned. The mason then says the building was always known, only not yet correctly remembered.

Potters speak the same way. There are perhaps two hundred vessel forms in the basin, although no catalogue contains them all. Each has a proper curve, thickness, temper, and firing color. Deviate too far and the pot does not survive unattended. Approach an enduring form closely enough and the vessel remains, acquiring the same slight smoke stain and minute rim wear as examples found in buried rooms.

Thus art is not absent. It is concentrated.

A painted bowl may carry only three black lines, but the painter spends days deciding their spacing. Singers rehearse the entrance of a single note until it seems to open a chamber in the air. Dancers repeat old step patterns on stone courts, seeking not novelty but the instant in which every footfall appears inevitable. Improvisation is practiced too, though its finest practitioners describe it as discovering one of the song’s hidden memories.

The city’s priests are less powerful than outsiders might expect. They do not command the equilibrium and claim no creator who imposed it. Most shrines belong to local presences: a spring that keeps metal bright, a ridge where lost animals are seen, a hearth flame that burns blue before births, a stone whose damp face is consulted in disputes. Offerings are modest—milk, twisted wool, crumbs, fern fronds, a few drops of scented oil. By the next day the offerings are gone, but no one says the gods consumed them. Things left in the wrong place simply have difficulty remaining.

The closest thing to a priesthood of the whole basin is the order of measurers.

They keep cords of fixed length, standard stones, bowls of established volume, shadow boards despite the sunless sky, and lists of the city’s inhabitants. They measure wall lean, spring flow, herd count, the distance between paving stones, the weight of newborns, and the reach of snow in the mountain cracks. Their work confirms what everyone expects: nothing drifts.

Yet they continue.

Among the measurers there are always a few who hope to catch the basin changing by less than the width of a hair. They are regarded with affection and mild embarrassment. Their tables grow dense with tiny marks. Sometimes one announces a discrepancy. A council is called; instruments are compared; witnesses speak. By the following day the troublesome record generally contains ordinary figures. The measurer remembers otherwise.

Such people are not punished. The city has no law against wanting a future.

Those most afflicted by that desire often become travelers. They leave through a gate with felted boots, a staff, dried cakes, hard cheese, a bronze hook, and a string of tokens to exchange beyond the range. Some are gone for what feels like hours, some for months, some long enough that their children cease expecting them at meals. They return by a different road, carrying stories and objects.

The stories are vivid. There are valleys where red trees grow upside down from cliffs; a city built beside a warm green sea; plains crossed by wheeled houses; people who bury their dead beneath flowering salt. Travelers disagree about routes and names, but speak with the conviction of those who have truly seen something.

The objects are less cooperative. A glass bead becomes polished quartz. A steel knife becomes stone fitted into an antler grip. A foreign fruit, cut open before witnesses, contains familiar mountain seeds. Maps curl inward until their roads resemble those of the basin.

Travelers are therefore honored less as discoverers than as bearers of impossible memory. They sit in long halls while listeners question them closely. Artists attempt to paint what they describe. By morning, the painting is usually a mountain, a spring, a grove, or a district of the city seen from an unfamiliar angle.

The animals share the inhabitants’ composure but not their tameness. Cattle accept handling, then step away with grave independence. Goats enter roof courts to lick lime from the walls. Deer pass between houses because no tradition has taught them that the inner districts are forbidden. Crows know which market stalls tolerate theft and which throw pebbles. They sometimes carry away bright innovations before those objects can revert, leading certain inventors to suspect that the birds preserve more newness than the city permits people.

There are stories about a white crow nesting beyond the rain veil. There are always stories about one animal that does not obey the count.

The long-winged raptors overhead may be individuals or a single bird encountered at different points in its circle. Shepherds recognize markings on their undersides and argue about this. The reed birds build nests, lay eggs, and lead chicks through the water, but the pools never become crowded. Predators feed. Prey continues. No creature appears invulnerable, yet the basin as a whole cannot be depleted.

Dogs are uncommon. Those that live in the city are small, thick-coated, and quiet, used to guard courtyards or find people in fern beds. They rarely bark at strangers, perhaps because strangers are rare, or perhaps because every stranger eventually proves to belong. Cats live around granaries, long-legged and ash-colored. They sleep on warm kiln roofs and watch mice with immense patience.

Of all creatures, the pale burrowers among the stones seem most perfectly suited to the place. They emerge, sit upright, eat, vanish, and emerge elsewhere. Their tunnels never collapse and their mounds never grow. Children insist that beneath the city is another city built to their scale, with roots for rafters and mica windows. Excavators sometimes hear scratching behind buried walls.

No excavation has reached them.

The people themselves are not immortal in any simple sense. They bruise, bleed, sicken, grieve, and die. Loss is real while it is being endured. A widow can wake reaching for a body no longer beside her. Parents mourn children. Friends sit through the motionless daylight outside a house of death.

But grief, like water, does not carve deeper and deeper channels. It remains clear. It may be revisited without becoming either fresh or distant. The dead are placed in clefts, under house floors, or in stone chambers along the ridges, according to district custom. Grave goods are familiar and few. When chambers are reopened, they contain bones, but never enough bones for all the funerals remembered.

Some say the city keeps only the dead necessary to its form.

Others say no one is truly kept. They point to newborns who turn their heads at old songs, artisans who take up abandoned tools with practiced hands, and strangers returning from the passes who know which household door will open to them. Rebirth is not doctrine here. It is an ordinary suspicion.

What lives in the basin, then, is not a people frozen in a single gesture. They labor, choose, quarrel, reconcile, wander, invent, fail, and love. Their lives contain motion. What they lack is the long wake that motion usually leaves behind.

This makes them neither hopeless nor serene. They are cautious with ambition and extravagant with attention. Since little can be added to the world, they give themselves to the quality of what can be done within it. A meal cannot transform the city, but it can be properly shared. A song cannot become a new tradition, but it can be sung so truly that every listener recognizes something never before noticed. A road cannot lead away, but one may still walk it to the end.

At the watch platforms, gatekeepers lean on polished rails and study the roads. Their duty is not only to challenge those approaching. It is to witness departures and returns, to remember what each traveler carried, and to notice—if it ever happens—someone who comes from a direction no citizen has gone.

So far, everyone seen in the distance has proven familiar.

Still, the gatekeepers watch.
