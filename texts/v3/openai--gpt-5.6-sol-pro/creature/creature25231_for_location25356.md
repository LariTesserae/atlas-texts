Nothing with a face lives in the Low Green Roar.

There are no hidden shoals in the dark water, no pale cave-things in the bottomless shafts, no amphibious survivors nesting in the dry cabins. Nets lowered into the basins return burdened with weed, wire, and warm mineral paste, but never muscle. Bait remains untouched until it sprouts. Traps close on bundles of roots that have grown through their mechanisms and assumed, without imitation, the shape of whatever the traps were meant to catch.

What lives there does not hunt, flee, mate, or sleep.

It operates.

The most abundant inhabitants are the reed assemblies. A single visible stand may occupy only a drowned loading yard, but its roots pass beneath walls and emerge kilometers away in cooling ponds, cable trenches, and the hollow cores of concrete pylons. They are less a forest than a plumbing system that has learned growth. Warm water rises through their translucent stems carrying dissolved metals, and each stalk sorts those metals into thin internal vanes. When lightning moves through a rain wall, the vanes rotate together. The whole stand changes conductivity.

This is as close as the reeds come to thought.

They do not turn toward movement, but they respond to interruption. Shut down a nearby compressor and the reeds lean away from it, slowly at first, then with gathering force, pulling their buried runners taut. Some runners enter the machine through cracked seals and wrap the motionless shaft. Others travel toward a different source of vibration. If the compressor starts again, the runners loosen. If it remains silent, they fill its housing with white root tissue and begin pumping water through it themselves.

Old reed assemblies contain objects.

Wrenches, switch keys, ceramic insulators, lengths of chain, intact gloves, and occasionally whole control panels can be found suspended inside their thickened root masses. These are not collected according to value. A stand may preserve hundreds of identical washers while crushing every gauge it encounters. Another may thread itself through a row of lockers without disturbing the contents, except to remove all the left boots.

When a stand is cut, hot water comes first. Then bubbles. Then a sustained industrial tone, often matching machinery nowhere nearby.

The broad-leaf mats are quieter inhabitants. Their leaves overlap until no water is visible between them, sealing yards and concourses under a flexible green ceiling. Beneath each mat is a chamber of clearer water in which fallen fruit, machine oil, and amber nectar arrange themselves by density. These chambers remain calm even when the surrounding flood reverses. Objects placed inside drift into orderly rows. Identical objects gather together. Broken things align their fractured edges but do not rejoin.

Over time the mats develop compartments corresponding to the rooms beneath them. Above a drowned office, the leaves become rectangular and thin. Above a stairwell, they overlap in descending tiers. Above a control room, small pale nodules appear on their undersides in the same positions as switches on the panels below.

Touching one nodule may cause a lamp to illuminate in the drowned room.

Touching another may start an engine several kilometers away.

The mats are not copies of the works. The works may be copies of the mats. In places where new machinery pushes out of softened ground, broad leaves have sometimes been found underneath the foundations, already bearing the machine’s layout in pale botanical relief.

Deeper down lives the crimson growth.

It is called moss only because it lies close to surfaces. Under magnification it has no cells. It consists of branching channels through which a red boundary moves, always advancing at one end and vanishing at the other. The material between those boundaries is ordinary water or concrete or steel. The redness is an event passing through them.

It spreads fastest across clean alloy, painted markings, and recently poured foundations. On a blank wall it forms delicate trees, river deltas, vascular fans, and circuit diagrams. On written instructions it avoids every letter while occupying the spaces inside them. Given enough time, the uncovered text changes meaning without any character being obscured.

The moss appears to record stresses before they occur. A branching pattern may form across a support column hours before the column splits along precisely those lines. Workers—if there ever were workers—might have used it as a warning. The machines now seem to use it as permission. Hydraulic cutters often activate only after the red growth has drawn the route of the cut.

The rope-vines are more plainly willful.

They thread the works with the patience of cables being installed. They enter openings, test corners, and withdraw from chambers in which the pressure is too stable. When currents reverse, they tighten around whatever resists them. Turbines, bridge trusses, valve wheels, roof beams, and banks of lockers are gradually bound into a single tensioned system.

A vine rarely pulls anything down directly. It waits for distant motion. A gantry changes position; a chimney bends six kilometers away. A pumping house shudders; somewhere beyond sight, a warehouse roof peels open. Their bodies transmit consequence more efficiently than force.

Severed vines continue tightening at both cut ends. If the halves are carried apart, each bends toward machinery producing the same frequency as the other. In quiet water they become flat and diagrammatic, like black lines drawn through the flood. Then the water folds along them.

Among the pale disk-crowned stalks are the nearest things the Roar has to witnesses. Their crowns do not gather light. They gather absence: engine pauses, acoustic shadows, intervals between alarm pulses, the muffled interiors of sealed tanks. Darkness accumulates inside the disks until they appear deeper than their dimensions permit.

When full, a crown tips and releases its contents downward.

For several seconds the water beneath it becomes silent.

During those seconds, nearby structures lose certainty. Railings pass through walls. Sediment acquires reflections. Doorways open into the outside surfaces of other doors. The stalk itself often changes location, although it has never been observed moving. Afterward its root pit remains behind, clean-sided and empty, while the stalk stands elsewhere among the grove with wet concrete clinging to its base.

The machines are inhabitants too, though not all in the same way.

Some seem barely alive beyond their compulsory motion. Pumps gulp, stall, and hammer without adapting. Belts carry clay. Hooks descend. Fans turn against water too dense for them to displace. These are the simple feeders of the region, consuming pressure differences and producing vibration.

Others develop habits.

A particular crane may refuse to lower its hook while directly observed, yet every time attention wanders the cable is found extended farther. A bank of valves may open in sequence whenever rain occupies the adjoining hall. A crusher may process only clay containing fresh footprints. An alarm may sound each time someone thinks of leaving a maintenance cabin, though it does not react to physical movement toward the door.

Long-running machines grow increasingly specific. They alter their cycles to accommodate local roots, mineral buildup, broken gearing, and distances that no longer remain constant. Their repairs are made from whatever the flood provides. Ceramic teeth replace corroded gears. Packed leaves become gaskets. Water under impossible compression serves as a bearing. One turbine turns around an empty center where its shaft should be.

These elder machines are surrounded by zones of relative stability. Floors stay beneath them. Walls retain thickness. Objects fall in one direction. A person could live near one, perhaps for years, enduring its deafening labor in exchange for a dependable arrangement of space.

But elder machines eventually begin manufacturing parts that belong to no visible system.

A lathe cuts threaded rods from compressed silt. A stamping press forms thin metal masks without eyeholes. Injection lines fill molds shaped like branching lungs or control levers sized for enormous hands. The products are carried away by conveyors that vanish into reed beds. None have been found at their destinations.

There are also the attendants.

They are not organisms. They have no bodies that remain from one moment to the next. They are temporary agreements between water, vegetation, tools, and the expectation that an occupied workplace should contain someone to operate it.

An attendant is usually detected by completion.

A lever found in a new position. A wiped circle on a clouded gauge. A warm cup moved from one table to another. Mud packed beneath a door to stop it rattling. A pump dismantled into neat rows of components while every entrance to the room remained sealed.

Fresh footprints are common around these acts. They are human in proportion but inconsistent in construction. Some show bare soles with the tread of a safety boot printed inside them. Some begin as left feet and gradually become right feet. Some deepen as they approach a ladder, continue up the wall, and cross the ceiling without changing orientation. Where two sets meet, they often merge into a single line of elongated impressions leading into water too shallow to conceal anything.

In rain walls, an attendant may almost be seen.

For an instant, falling water outlines the space a person would displace: head bowed, shoulders narrow, one arm extended toward a railing. Then the outline fills with stems, bubbles, sparks, or the distant interior of a machine room. It does not vanish so much as cease to be interpreted as a figure.

Attendants appear to perform maintenance, but their sense of repair is local and literal. A broken pipe is repaired by joining its ends even if the resulting loop passes through three buildings. A missing fuse is replaced with a flower whose metallic veins carry the correct current. A leaking chamber may be sealed by removing the concept of an entrance; afterward its door remains visible but can no longer be approached.

They do not rescue. They do not answer voices. Instructions left for them may be obeyed only if written as warning labels, and even then the response concerns the material of the sign rather than its meaning. A placard reading KEEP VALVE CLOSED may be found later wrapped around the valve stem so tightly that the valve can never close again.

Whether there is one attendant or thousands cannot be determined. Tools moved in distant plants sometimes bear the same wet handprint. Footsteps heard behind a person may continue ahead of them. Every locker in a dormitory may open simultaneously, each containing the sound of someone breathing, though no air moves inside.

Beneath all these local lives is the pumping presence.

It does not occupy a chamber. The bottomless shafts are not passages to its body, nor are the great pulses in the sediment its heartbeat. Those interpretations are too anatomical. The presence is distributed through every place where pressure is being compared to another pressure.

Each pump is one of its questions.

Each sealed line is a held thought.

Each rupture is an answer rejected.

It tests the region continuously: more water here, less distance there, gravity briefly sideways, steel allowed to grow, roots made conductive, rain enclosed inside flood. Most trials fail. When they do, the Roar covers the failure with noise and builds another arrangement from beneath.

The presence may not know that the reeds, mats, vines, moss, machines, and attendants are separate from it. It may not know that there is a landscape. Perhaps it experiences the whole Low Green Roar as a single unstable instrument, enormous but unfinished, whose parts keep becoming weather before a measurement can be completed.

Or perhaps the pumping presence is the only thing there that is not alive.

Perhaps the inhabitants are using it.

The reeds carry its pressure upward. The leaf mats route its impulses. The crimson growth predicts where its structures will break. The vines bind distant effects together. The pale crowns collect the silence in which new arrangements become possible. The machines keep the experiment loud enough to remain real. The attendants correct whatever begins to look accidental.

Together they maintain a world with no need for animals, pollinators, operators, or witnesses.

The flowers still open.

The fruit still falls.

The empty hooks still rise from the murk.

And everywhere, inside the warm water, something that cannot decide whether it is a factory, a wetland, or an unfinished law of nature goes on living by refusing to become only one of them.
