The inhabitants are not separate from the structure in the way animals elsewhere are separate from landscape. They live in it, feed it, wear it, polish it, and eventually become part of it. The distinction between resident, parasite, gardener, and architectural element is useful only to an outsider.

Most are too small to possess any idea of the whole.

The blue terrace-lights, for instance, are animals no larger than coarse grains. Each consists of a transparent shell containing a folded thread of luminous tissue. They settle where moving water meets still water: parapet rims, pore-mouths, the lips of shafts. Their lights are not continuous. One brightens, then those nearest answer, and a line of cold blue travels along an edge until a whole gallery is momentarily drawn against the dark.

They feed on particles trapped by the bark. They also clean the plant’s breathing surfaces. When a colony grows too dense, the structure alters the taste of its outer tissues, and half the lights release their hold and drift away like sparks. Most are eaten before they find another ledge. Enough survive that every inhabited route acquires a broken blue border.

The seam-worms live deeper. They are pale, flat creatures fitted between plates of wood so closely that removing one would tear it. Their glow is normally hidden. Pressure awakens it. A foot, fin, mouth, or falling seed passes over them, and the touched seam shines from within for several breaths.

Thus movement writes itself briefly upon the city.

A grazing shoal appears as a storm of fine green lines. A heavy foundation-walker leaves a chain of broad white impressions that can take hours to fade. Sometimes a vacant chamber begins glowing along a wall where nothing visible has passed. This is usually the movement of sap through a reopened vessel. The inhabitants do not always distinguish such events from the passage of an animal.

Among the suspended forests move the wing-finned browsers. Their bodies are broad and very thin, dark on the upper side and silver-gray beneath. From a distance they resemble leaves that have detached and decided not to fall. Four fins extend around them in a continuous rippling margin. The ripples are slight; a browser can climb through the water without stirring the fronds beside it.

Their mouths open downward into rows of flexible combs. They graze by settling over a wall of foliage and drawing themselves across it. Old tissue disappears beneath them in smooth parallel bands. Young buds and load-bearing ribs contain bitter salts that the combs refuse. The animals do not know what must be spared. Their mouths know.

Each browser follows a route inherited in the shape of its nervous system. It turns at an arch before the arch is visible. It circles a column at the same height as its parent. It pauses in courtyards that have been closed for thousands of years, hovering against the seamless bark where an entrance once stood. If a passage changes, several generations continue attempting the old course. Their bodies wear shallow hollows into the obstruction. Sometimes the plant responds by opening the route again.

The silver fish of the wells are scarcely more individual. A school may contain millions, but it maintains one form: a loose helix extending farther upward and downward than any fish can travel in its lifetime. Those near the upper end feed, grow heavy, and descend. Those below spawn and die. Their young rise through the center. Seen by the flashes of their sides, the school resembles a screw of light turning inside the dark shaft, though its rotation may require a century.

Predators live in the empty center of the helix.

They are long, nearly transparent animals whose jaws are set not at the front but along the underside of the body. Their bones are thin rings. When they call, the rings vibrate one after another, producing a descending note that makes the school contract. The contraction presents exactly the number of fish the hunter requires. It takes one or two and withdraws. A hunter that continues striking is answered by a deeper vibration from the walls, which disorients its ring-bones and drives it away.

No one governs this proportion. It persists because hunters that feed beyond it lose the resonant shelter in which they breed.

Lower down are the many-footed walkers.

They are immense by the measure of the upper provinces, though small beside the foundations they traverse. Their bodies are low, soft, and segmented, with a gripping foot beneath every segment and another along the back. They have no permanent underside. When a root turns over, they turn with it. When two supports cross, they may transfer themselves from one to the other without ever entering open water.

Their skin is colorless and deeply grooved. Seeds lodge in those grooves. Filaments spread, leaves emerge, and eventually each old walker carries a garden dense enough to conceal the animal itself. Small shell-creatures settle among the plants. Grazers follow. Breeding swimmers deposit their young in the sheltered creases. A mature walker bears ponds of trapped brine, miniature groves, and colonies that may never touch the mountain-city directly.

The walker feeds by pressing its mouth against old wood and digesting only the softened outer layer. Its passage leaves broad polished roads. Because it may spend decades crossing a single vault, the plants on one end of its body can belong to a different pressure province from those on the other. When it finally dies, its feet remain closed around the support. Roots enter its body. Its garden becomes a hanging terrace, recognizable for several millennia by a faint segmented curve.

There are also inhabitants who speak.

They are rare enough that a visitor might cross many provinces without meeting one. They have no cities within the city, no permanent rooms, no objects, and nothing that could be called clothing. They build nothing. Nonetheless, it is from them that the place receives the nearest thing it has to a name.

Their bodies are long and softly jointed, usually carried upright against a trunk. Around the narrow central body are six grasping limbs, arranged in pairs suited to different scales of wood: the lower pair broad enough to embrace a pillar, the middle pair divided into delicate gripping branches, the upper pair ending in fans of sensory threads. A membrane runs from shoulder to hip and opens when they cross water, turning the entire body into a slow steering surface.

They have no eyes.

Across the front of the head lies a chambered plate, curved like the face of a drum. It detects vibration in wood and water with extraordinary precision. Through it they perceive columns as vertical chords, chambers as volumes of returning sound, animals as disruptions in the city’s continuous voice. A seamless wall is not blank to them. It may be crowded with sap channels, sleeping larvae, old fractures, and distant footsteps.

Light is, to them, a minor pressure felt by patches of skin. They notice the blue edge-animals chiefly as warmth.

Their mouths are small and used for feeding. Speech comes from resonant sacs placed along the ribs. By compressing these in sequence, they send low pulses through whatever surface they hold. Their simplest utterances are not heard in the water at all. They pass into bark, divide at branches, deepen in thick supports, and return altered. Every statement includes the structure through which it travels.

For this reason, they do not have a single word for the mountain-city. The nearest translation would be:

**that which continues the saying**

—or perhaps:

**the held answer**

No utterance is repeatable in another place. A greeting spoken against a young gallery differs from the same greeting made on an ancient buttress, because the gallery answers sharply and the buttress answers from great depth. Identity includes location. One of these beings gives its personal call, then the column completes it.

Their names change when they move.

They possess memory, kinship, grief, expectation, and long disputes, but these do not form histories in the usual sense. No individual can travel from crown to root. A body adapted to one pressure band sickens after ascending or descending too far. Knowledge of the whole therefore passes as relays.

A summit-dweller speaks into a descending mast. Far below, another feels the weakened phrase and repeats it into denser wood, adjusting its pitch. The message continues downward, generation after generation. By the time it reaches the foundations, centuries may have passed and none of its original speakers remain alive. Replies rise in the same manner.

What the upper provinces know of the roots is ancient, translated, and full of resonant distortions. What the roots know of the crown has the quality of myth: thin water, transparent leaves, a remote brightness that changes without any creature touching it.

They do not imagine that one person could know both. To them, such knowledge would be shapeless and therefore meaningless. Truth must have a pressure.

Their families gather around resonant structures rather than places of shelter. A deep-toned trunk may support a lineage for hundreds of generations. Young cling to its smoother hollows and learn to distinguish the calls carried through it. Adults feed in surrounding gardens but return at intervals to press their plates against the family wood.

They eat seed-flesh, transparent grazers, and soft colonies scraped directly into the mouth. They store nothing. The city produces different foods in different chambers, and hunger is answered by movement along remembered routes. A closed route may produce famine, but famine is local and slow. Those who survive follow other vibrations. The abandoned gallery seals over behind them.

They use no tools because their world contains no detached matter suitable for one. Anything broken from the structure is alive, dying, edible, or already being reclaimed. They do not cut wood. They do not shape it. At certain seams, however, they press ripe seed-bodies into receptive tissue. At certain wounds, they gather and remain for years, vibrating against the bark in repeated patterns.

The seedlings respond.

Not to meaning, perhaps, but to frequency, pressure, and touch. Roots thicken around sustained low tones. Leaf membranes align themselves with recurring pulses. Hollow stems grow toward the source of certain harmonics. The inhabitants cannot command a wall to rise or an arch to open. They can only make conditions to which inherited growth may answer.

They call this neither gardening nor construction. It is closer to courtesy.

Whether their songs have influenced the architecture is impossible to determine. The structural forms are older than their species. Yet some galleries match the spacing of their bodies. Some chambers return their speech with unnatural clarity. There are vertical chains of resonant hollows that carry calls across pressure boundaries better than any accidental trunk should.

The speakers believe the city learned them before they learned it.

They do not worship the structure. Worship would require a separation they do not feel. Nor do they think it alive in the same way they are alive. It is slower than life and more continuous than an individual. They understand that it grows, heals, feeds, and reproduces its forms. They also understand that most of it is dead wood. These facts cause them no contradiction.

Their dead are given to the vascular seams.

A body is placed against a pale region of new growth and held there by kin until fine roots fasten around it. The soft tissues are absorbed over many years. The chambered facial plate remains longer. Sap enters it, salts harden it, and its hollows become part of the wall’s resonance.

For generations, descendants may hear a familiar overtone in the column.

Eventually even that distinction fades. The plate fills. The pitch deepens. The dead no longer answer as themselves, but the structure’s voice has changed by an amount too small for any outsider to detect.

In the upper crown, the speaking inhabitants are slight and translucent, with wide membranes and large resonant plates suited to thin water. They cling to the branching masts and attend to the distant brightening caused by surface storms. They interpret these oscillations as vast, silent calls from beyond the upper dark. Some spend their lives composing answers too low ever to reach the surface.

In the middle provinces, they are stronger, darker, and more mobile. Here the population is greatest. Families travel between leaf walls and open wells, carrying messages vertically, tending seed seams, and listening for alterations in the city’s equilibrium. Their gatherings can last decades. Dozens arrange themselves along a single buttress, each holding a different branch, and pass a layered chord through the wood until nearby animals alter their routes.

In the foundations, they are compact and pale. Their limbs are short, their sensory plates thick, and their voices so low that upper dwellers would experience them only as nausea. They rarely cross open water. They crawl among nested vaults, reading the pressure locked in ancient supports.

They know chambers that have not opened in ages. They know where old wood lies inside older wood. They can detect a break occurring miles above by the minute change it sends through a root.

Of all inhabitants, they come closest to asking what supports the whole.

Their oldest accounts tell of expeditions downward, each beginning where the previous one ended. These were not journeys by single travelers but chains of births. A group would descend until the pressure approached the limit of their bodies, settle, bear young suited slightly more closely to the depth, and send those children farther. Such descents may have continued for tens of thousands of years.

No final report ever returned.

There are, however, very deep calls that rise at intervals through the foundations. They are too regular to be fracture, too complex to be ordinary animal song, and too powerful to have originated nearby. Root-dwellers press themselves flat when these calls arrive. Gardens close their leaves. Shell-creatures stop clicking. For a time the entire lower structure seems to listen downward.

Some say the descendants of the expeditions are still there, transformed by pressures no upper body could survive, continuing to relay a question toward a bottom that recedes as quickly as life can follow.

Others say the calls belong to the city itself.

The distinction matters less than it might elsewhere.

For every inhabitant, from the seam-worm to the speaking ones, lives inside an answer already in progress. Each cleans a pore, grazes a measured strip, carries a seed, opens a channel, dies into a support. None understands the completed system. None needs to.

The tiny animals illuminate its edges.

The shoals give shape to its emptiness.

The browsers preserve its gardens.

The walkers carry new terraces across the old.

The speakers listen to its weight and add their brief notes.

And beneath them all, alive in some layers and dead in others, the oldest inhabitant continues its imperceptible labor: growing into what has worn away, wearing into what has grown, holding every creature without enclosing it, and making from their innumerable small lives the single, unending body of the place.
