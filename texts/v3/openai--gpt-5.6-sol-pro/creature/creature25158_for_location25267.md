What lives there is difficult to count, because the basin does not divide cleanly into inhabitants and habitat.

At first, one would count the obvious animals.

There are the six-legged hares, white as exposed pith, stationed on the open rock. Their extra limbs are not deformities and do not hinder them. The middle pair rests close against the ribs, trembling with a rapid, delicate industry, as though kneading some invisible substance inside the air. Their outer bodies are soft, warm, and mammalian. Beneath the fur, however, the muscles occur in repeating bands like the windings of a motor. Each breath passes through them from nose to flank without ever quite leaving. If one lays a hand against a hare’s side, the same inhalation can be felt arriving again and again.

They are not tame. Tameness would imply that fear had been overcome. They simply possess fear without retreat. Their eyes are wide, their ears laid back, their blood loud in the thin skin—and they remain exactly where alarm has placed them.

The silver foxes belong to them, if belonging can run in both directions. Each fox is narrow enough to seem drawn out by speed, though it does not travel. Its pelt has the sheen of machined wire. The jaws close; the hare departs; the teeth retain it. Within the fox’s mouth, the hare’s neck is both warm flesh and an unbroken interval. Neither animal suffers, but both continuously contain the complete machinery of suffering: terror, pursuit, force, escape. The fox’s hunger is likewise preserved. It is never relieved and never worsened. Predator and prey are not two creatures engaged in an event. Together they are one fixed organ of the basin, performing the shape of capture.

The birds on the cliffs are less like birds the longer they are watched. Their slate feathers overlap in rigid vanes. Their toes enter the fresh cracks in the basalt and appear to continue as pale mineral threads within it. When the underground engine engages, the birds’ spread wings vibrate, and the rock vibrates with them. It is impossible to tell whether they cling to the mountain or whether they are small exposed parts of something beneath it. Their chorus does not come from their throats. It passes through their hollow bones and emerges along the cutting edges of their feathers, shaving the air into a sustained metallic note.

Each vertical fissure has one bird. Each bird has one fissure. Empty cracks exist only until noticed carefully.

The frogs are windows disguised as animals. Their flesh is clear but not transparent in the ordinary sense. To look through one is not to see the pool beneath it, but to see farther inward. In the swelling throat lies the basin: black ring, wet floor, isolated fern beds, red plants pumping along the northern wall. In that smaller basin the observer may also be present, bent over a smaller frog. Sometimes the figure is standing when the observer is kneeling. Sometimes it has not yet arrived. Sometimes beetles have already arranged themselves around its feet.

Breaking a frog would disclose no interior. The skin yields, the organs gleam, and the tiny landscape remains at precisely the depth at which it was first seen. No one has successfully reached it.

The salamanders under the moss are warm despite the cold water. Their bodies are long, blind, and almost colorless, with red gills branching from their necks in orderly arrays. They lie along buried lines of pressure and brighten whenever the deep gears engage. A pulse travels down each body but never reaches the tail. Another begins behind it, then another, so that the animals hold trains of motion inside themselves. They may be nerves grown by the mountain, or animals employed as nerves. The distinction has no consequence there.

The gnats are not individuals except at the edges of their clouds. In the middle they are a dark granular volume, a piece of weather with wings. Each swarm occupies one exact portion of air and keeps its boundary as sharply as moss keeps the edge of its bed. Gnats emerge continuously from the lower surface, rise through the swarm, and settle at the upper surface without changing its density. The dragonflies poised beside them have jointed metallic bodies and enormous faceted eyes. In those eyes the gnat ahead is shown as already seized, already missed, and still awaiting pursuit.

The beetles are the closest things the basin has to attendants.

They come from under roots, from seams in stone, and occasionally from the closed mouths of sleeping-looking animals, though nothing sleeps. Their iron wing cases are always faintly scratched, but no scratch is ever acquired. Heat shows through the joints. When disturbed they open their shells, revealing not wings but rows of thin orange plates receding farther inward than their bodies should allow.

They inspect boundaries. They walk the edges of fern colonies, pause where water touches rock, measure the spacing between hares, and gather around anything newly introduced. Their antennae tap in groups of seven. After each sequence they become motionless for an interval too exact to be hesitation.

They do not repair irregularities. Irregularities repair themselves while the beetles witness them.

Nor do the beetles remove foreign matter. A dropped object remains foreign only briefly. They surround it at equal distances, turn their furnace-bright undersides toward it, and wait. Rust may appear without consuming the metal. Moss may occur on a surface without attaching to it. A shadow suited to the object may arrive from the wrong direction. Eventually the thing becomes included in one of the basin’s operations, although it may remain visually unchanged.

A knife becomes a permanent gleam along an edge that never cuts.

A length of rope becomes tension between two points to which it has not been tied.

A bone becomes the condition of having been uncovered.

The plants are inhabitants too, though “plant” describes only their outward manners.

The red piston growths drink stone. They stand widely spaced because each draws from a vertical territory extending deep below the basin. Their translucent leaves are hard and cold, strengthened by branching veins that resemble both vascular tissue and fine glass tubing. Beneath the bark, cartilage rods descend, compress, and return. No plant is synchronized with its neighbor, yet every seventh stroke occurs at once.

Their luminous sap contains neither sugar nor mineral in any stable proportion. Removed from the stem, it continues to brighten while remaining inside. The cut that released it appears vividly on the plant, but the tissue is whole. If tasted, the sap has the flavor of cold metal and causes the tongue to remember injuries it has not received.

The moss is less a species than a surface activity. It carries basalt grains beneath the roots and returns them unworn, but the transportation may not be its true labor. The grains appear to be counters, or bearings, or small dark tokens through which the basin confirms that departure and return remain equal. Each cilium pushes with earnest precision. Each grain travels. No grain changes place.

Among the larger ferns live pale shapes easy to mistake for curled fiddleheads. They uncoil when no one is looking directly at them. They have no faces, limbs, or separable organs; only a flexible white axis wrapped in green scales. They move from frond to frond without crossing the air between. Their presence can be traced by the brief stiffening of each leaf and by a hard white reflection that travels over wet surfaces without relation to the light.

These may be the youngest creatures in the basin. They may also be probes extruded by whatever lives beneath it.

For something does live beneath it, though perhaps not underground.

The impacts felt through the floor do not suggest a single immense beast. They suggest many components accepting force from one another: teeth meeting teeth, chambers locking, weight transferred through structures too large to fit inside the mountain. Yet every opened fissure contains only fresh basalt and pale crystal. There are no cavities, no shafts, no buried mechanisms.

The thing below is not concealed by the rock. It is expressed as rock.

Its rotation appears as circular shadows. Its circulation appears as mineral water. Its respiration appears as the fixed mist volumes. The red plants are perhaps its pumps; the salamanders its nerves; the birds vibrating along the cracks its sounding vanes. But such comparisons make it seem more unified than it is. It may have no body apart from these correspondences. It may be an organism assembled not from matter but from simultaneous functions.

If it possesses awareness, awareness is distributed unevenly.

A hare holds its vigilance.

A beetle holds its scrutiny.

A fox holds its intent.

A root tip holds its pressure against unopened stone.

The deep engine holds persistence.

Together these may amount to something like thought, but not thought that proceeds. The basin does not consider and then conclude. It contains consideration and conclusion side by side, with the act of inference omitted. Its intelligence is the permanent arrangement of all that it knows.

This is why a visitor troubles it.

A visitor arrives by sequence. First one foot, then the other. First a sight, then recognition. Hunger increases; fatigue accumulates; a question forms and seeks an answer. Such a creature brings duration into a place that has operations but no passage.

The basin cannot expel this novelty, because expulsion would require a completed act. Instead it distributes the visitor.

Breathing is given to the underground beat. Pulse is taken up by the piston-plants. Moisture is assigned to the silver rows beneath the overhangs. Shadows begin appearing elsewhere in the basin with the visitor’s outline: crouched where the visitor stands, standing where the visitor sleeps, reaching toward things the visitor has not touched.

The beetles establish the proper intervals.

After long enough, animals begin to include small signs of the intruder. One bird may acquire the color of their eyes along the edge of a wing. A frog’s throat may contain a memory of a room far below the mountain. A hare may move its mouth around a word the visitor once spoke, though no sound emerges except the standing chord. The miniature conifer may bear, among its cones and frost, the odor of a season from the visitor’s childhood.

Nothing has been stolen. The person still possesses all these things. The basin possesses them also.

Eventually the visitor’s actions cease to require completion. A hand is forever withdrawing from cold water and has always remained dry. A mouth opens around a name and rests closed. One step occupies several places on the wet pavement without traveling between them.

At that point it is no longer clear that the basin contains an intruder.

It contains another function.

Perhaps this is how all its inhabitants came to be there—not born, evolved, or created, but admitted as contradictions and gradually made necessary. The fox may once have entered hungry. The hare may once have fled. The bird may once have crossed the rim in ordinary flight. Each brought an unfinished instant, and the basin discovered how to keep it.

What lives there, then, is not a collection of species. It is a population of preserved exertions.

Biting without injury.  
Escaping without distance.  
Pumping without yield.  
Growing without enlargement.  
Falling without descent.  
Watching without discovery.  
Beginning without loss of what came before.

And beneath, within, and across them all lives the basin’s sole continuous creature: the instant itself, kept young by innumerable organs, manufacturing every inhabitant as a means of remaining unfinished.
