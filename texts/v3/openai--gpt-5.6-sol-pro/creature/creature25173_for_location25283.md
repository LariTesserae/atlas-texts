At first the inhabitants are difficult to distinguish from the town. Their clothing takes the colors of it: undyed wool, ash gray, clay brown, the faded yellow of old straw. They move close to walls and choose the shallow side of stairs, not furtively, but with the unconscious economy of people who know where every draft falls and which paving stones become slick after rain. Seen from the upper court, they are small deliberate figures crossing broad spaces built for many more.

There are perhaps a few dozen households, though no one counts the town only by occupied doors. A sealed house still belongs to a lineage, and an absent family remains in certain reckonings for generations. The people speak of such households as quiet, not gone. Their names continue to be used when assigning repairs, cleaning drains, or dividing the cistern’s water. A person may be told that she is tending the western screen “with the people of the three closed courts,” meaning that the duty attached to those houses has passed temporarily to her neighbors. Emptiness here has obligations.

The living are spare without being starved. They tend toward narrow shoulders, strong hands, and the steady calves of those who have climbed stone stairs since infancy. Their faces are weathered first around the eyes. Sun, altitude, and the pale glare off paving make them squint even in youth, so that many acquire an expression of patient scrutiny. Older people often have mineral-darkened cracks in their fingertips from clay, stone dust, metal filings, and cold water. Their palms are broad and polished by tools.

They wear layered tunics belted close to the body, with sleeveless felted coats for work and heavier wool mantles after sundown. Cloth is patched until the patchwork becomes its structure. A child’s garment may contain pieces from five adults’ clothing, none large enough to reveal its former shape. Bright dye is rare and therefore intimate. A narrow red edge inside a collar, invisible unless the wearer turns her head, may be a wedding gift or mourning token. Blue thread is saved for marking water officials and for tying the cloth wrapped around a newborn’s first cup.

Their sandals have thick hide soles studded or cross-bound for the slopes. In cold weather they wrap their feet in wool and wear soft leather boots. Nothing trails. Sleeves tie back; hair is braided, cropped, or covered. Loose fabric catches wind and collects dust, and both are considered forms of carelessness.

They do not speak much while working, but they are not solemn people. Their humor is dry, exact, and often delayed. A remark may be answered several minutes later, from another side of a courtyard, once the proper response has occurred to someone. They make jokes about badly fitted stones, extravagant goats, and ancestors whose supposed wisdom did not prevent them from putting a drain where it clogs every third winter. Laughter carries farther than conversation and is usually brief, as though enjoyed and then folded away.

Children are few enough that every adult knows them individually. They are not kept from the cisterns or ledges so much as instructed early in how to approach them. A child who can walk is taught to place a hand on stone before leaning over water. By five or six, children carry droplets from the fog screens in tiny covered cups—not because the quantity matters, but because spilling teaches what no reprimand can. They learn the town as a sequence of gradients: which way a floor falls, where runoff turns, which wall contains soil, which shaded step holds ice.

Their games follow the same knowledge. They build miniature channels in dust, dam them with chips of pottery, and pour thimblefuls of water through them under severe rules. They race pebbles down drains after rain. They play at sealing houses, solemnly blocking mouse holes with fitted fragments and reopening them by ceremony the next day. On clear evenings they throw knucklebones in the square, where there is room for a hundred players and seldom more than six.

The elderly are numerous, or seem so because they remain visible in public life. Work changes rather than ends. Those no longer able to climb to the screens sort seed, twist cord, sharpen bone tools, watch settling basins, mend baskets, and teach the placement of wedges. Memory is treated as a practical faculty. An old woman may be valued because she remembers that a certain roof leaked forty years ago during rain from the east, or because she knows which sealed courtyard contains a sound juniper beam. Age does not grant unquestioned authority, but a claim beginning with “I saw this before” receives attention.

No single craft wholly defines a person. The copper worker also tends lentils. The cistern keeper mends sandals. A family known for setting vaults may go years without raising one, spending the interval pruning trees, cutting slate, or leading asses to the lower clay pit. Specialization exists as accumulated trust rather than exclusive occupation. People say not that someone is a mason, but that walls agree with him.

Their most respected skill is adjustment. A maker who produces something flawless but difficult to repair is regarded with suspicion. A good door can be lifted from its sockets by three neighbors. A good roof reveals where it is failing before collapse. A good cup has a foot broad enough not to tip on uneven stone. Beauty lies in an object showing how it can be kept.

Water shapes their law more deeply than any ruler does. The person responsible for the reservoirs is chosen for attentiveness, not rank, and rarely serves alone. Two or three adults inspect the chambers together, carrying clay lamps and speaking the measured depths aloud so that no one person owns the knowledge. The cistern cover is not locked. To lock it would imply that water could belong to whoever held the key. Instead, its iron binding bears small hammered marks recording years of cleaning, contamination, abundant rain, and repair.

Households receive water according to need, but need is publicly legible. Pregnancy, illness, mortar work, tanning, a newly planted tree—each changes the allotment. Disputes occur. They are quiet but not gentle. Someone who takes too much may find no one willing to help lift a roof slab or bring an ass to the clay pit. In a place where nearly every heavy task requires several bodies, disapproval has weight. Deliberate fouling of a channel is the nearest thing they have to an unforgivable crime.

Yet they do not worship water as a distant or capricious god. Their reverence is more domestic. Water is greeted, covered, divided, and returned. At the roofless court, a person touching the hollow stone may be making a prayer, remembering a dead relative, checking for moisture, or merely following the habit of the hand. No distinction is demanded. Their sacred acts often resemble maintenance because maintenance is what has kept them alive.

The dead are not buried within the cultivated terraces. Soil is too scarce, and the town does not confuse honor with consumption. Bodies are carried beyond the eastern wall to stone chambers cut into the dry slope. After years, when little remains but bone, the chambers are opened. The bones are cleaned, bundled in old cloth, and placed deeper inside with those of earlier generations. A small piece may be retained—a tooth, a finger bone, a bead worn by the dead—and set into a sealed niche above the household door. When a house is closed, its niche is closed with it.

Death seldom empties a dwelling immediately. Neighbors keep the hearth cold but sweep the rooms. Grain is redistributed, tools returned to the families from whom they were borrowed, and roof drains inspected before the door is blocked. Sealing a house is careful work and may take weeks. The final stones are placed from inside and outside together, with one person leaving through the roof aperture before the closing slab is lowered. There is no dramatic last stone. The house simply ceases to admit weather and becomes part of the town’s reserve.

Some inhabitants leave. Young adults go down into the valleys as traders, hired builders, caravan hands, healers, or scribes. A few return with metal, cloth, salt, new songs, foreign spouses, or children who initially find the silence frightening. Others never return. Their names remain attached to duties until word of death arrives or enough years pass that expectation becomes observance.

Newcomers are not common, but neither are they refused. Belonging is earned through seasons rather than ancestry. A stranger may sleep first in a chamber near the western gate, then in a borrowed court, and only later be permitted to reopen a sealed house. Reopening requires more than removing stone. The drains are tested, roof loads examined, the old household niche acknowledged, and the neighbors consulted about shadows, runoff, and shared walls. The newcomer takes on the house’s obligations along with its shelter. In time people may say the house has awakened, though they do not say it has changed families.

There is no palace. Decisions are made in the market square or, in cold weather, in one of the larger storehouses. Those who speak stand; those who wish only to listen sit against the wall. Authority shifts with the subject. Orchard matters belong largely to the seven tree-tenders, reservoir matters to those who have entered the settling chambers, and disputes over walls to people who can read settlement in a hairline crack. The arrangement is not perfectly harmonious. Old families guard certain privileges. Skilled people can become vain. A persuasive speaker can disguise appetite as necessity. But every claim is constrained by the town’s material facts, which are difficult to flatter.

Food is plain and strongly flavored where it can be. Barley becomes flat cakes, porridge, or a thin sour drink. Lentils are cooked with bitter herbs. Goat milk is soured, pressed, and stored in small hard rounds. Eggs are saved for children, the sick, and feast days. Meat is uncommon enough that its smell draws people to doorways. Juniper smoke seasons nearly everything.

The seven orchard trees produce fruit that is nearly inedible when picked. The townspeople store it in straw-lined recesses until frost and time soften it. When the first fruit is cut, the slices are shared throughout the town, including portions set at the doors of sealed houses. Children claim this is so the quiet households will not become jealous. Adults say it is to remember whose labor built the orchard wall. Both explanations are accepted.

On rare abundant years, when reservoirs rise high in their gauges, the town changes character. Basins are uncovered. Floors are scrubbed with more water than strict necessity permits. People wash their hair all at once and sit in the square while it dries. Children are allowed to splash in a shallow court drain. A little water is poured deliberately onto bare stone where no root can reach it. This is considered neither waste nor offering exactly, but proof that abundance has been recognized.

Their songs are built for enclosed courts. They have narrow ranges, repeated lines, and pauses in which an echo supplies something like another voice. Work songs are rare because tasks do not require rhythmic masses of labor. Instead they sing while sorting grain, watching a kiln, or waiting for rain to pass. Instruments are small: bone pipes, skin drums played with fingertips, metal tongues mounted in wooden frames. The copper worker’s hammer sometimes enters a song unintentionally, and no one stops for it.

At night there are few lamps. People know their rooms by touch and will not spend oil merely to defeat darkness. Doorways show low amber light for an hour or two, then go black. The town becomes a collection of warm interiors separated by cold stone. From within come murmured recitations, the grind of grain prepared for morning, a goat shifting in its pen, an old person coughing, someone teaching a child the names of closed households.

Those names are the town’s longest story. They are cut over doors, repeated in marriage agreements, assigned to channels, stairs, wind screens, and orchard roots. The inhabitants do not imagine themselves as descendants of a single founder. They understand the town as a gathering of continuities, some human, some not: a family line, a watercourse, a repaired retaining wall, a strain of barley, a juniper propagated by cuttings from a tree long dead.

Even the small pale lizards are known by where they emerge. No one keeps them, but children watch for them after the cold season and report the first sighting. The hens belong to households; the goats often seem to belong more to their routes than to their owners. The asses are treated with a mixture of gratitude and exasperation. Because they carry clay, grain, roof slabs, and sometimes the dead, they are given names from old honorifics, which makes scolding them sound ceremonious.

What lives there, finally, is not a hidden order of austere guardians, nor the remnant of a people waiting to disappear. The town’s inhabitants can be miserly, funny, lustful, stubborn, bored, jealous, tender, and foolish. They quarrel over inheritance and smoke, fall asleep during public reckonings, hide sweetened fruit from siblings, make poor marriages, and sometimes neglect repairs until rain exposes them. Their discipline is not purity. It is the accumulated correction of ordinary failure.

They have made endurance into a social art. Each person occupies more space than they strictly need, yet almost nothing is considered solitary. A cup of water has passed through roofs, screens, channels, chambers, hands, bodies, soil, and air. A house contains its builders and those who kept it empty. A field is fed by ash from many hearths. The living walk among the provisions of the dead and prepare, without particular sadness, provisions for people not yet born.

From the ridge they may look sparse: a few figures, seven trees, a handful of animals, smoke no thicker than thread. But below that sparseness is an intricate density of attention. That is what chiefly inhabits the town—not merely people, but their long habit of noticing what must be answered.
