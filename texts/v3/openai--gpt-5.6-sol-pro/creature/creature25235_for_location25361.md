What lives here does not live upon the country. It composes it.

The smallest inhabitants are the pavement cells: innumerable flattened green bodies joined edge to edge across the open reaches. Alone, each is little more than a photosynthetic sac with a gripping underside and a rim sensitive to pressure. Together they form the warm felt of the plateau. They close cuts, register rainfall, digest whatever falls, and pass slow chemical changes outward in widening rings. A footstep would remain in them not as a print but as information—a temporary darkening, a redistribution of water, a minute tightening that travels farther than the disturbance itself.

Beneath this skin live the pale feeders. They have no permanent shape. They gather where something has torn, died, or been deposited, emerging as fine threads that braid themselves around the material. Their work is swift and almost fastidious. A fallen membrane, a shattered seed hull, even a strip ripped from a distant tower is enclosed before decay can become an independent process. The feeders soften it, separate its useful compounds, and pull the remainder into the vascular dark. They are undertakers, mouths, and roots, though perhaps these are three names for the same function.

Deeper still are the great cable-growths that bear the weight of the land. No one sees an entire one. A black root exposed in a fracture may continue beneath several valleys, dividing into pillars, floors, reservoir ribs, and the cores of towers before joining other roots so completely that origin becomes meaningless. Some cables are centuries old; some were laid down after the last storm. Their outer layers harden into load-bearing fiber while their centers remain wet and responsive. They thicken under compression. Under tension they divide into parallel strands. Where opposing systems meet, they may fuse, recoil, strangle one another, or remain pressed together for decades, exchanging water through a boundary neither fully accepts nor rejects.

The upright structures are less like trees than temporary organs raised by this buried population.

A leaf tower begins as a swelling in the pavement, a circular ridge scarcely higher than a hand. For weeks it gathers pressure. Then bundled stalks rise from within it, sliding against one another, lifting a folded blade into the air. The mature blade turns not by a joint but by uneven growth: cells on one side lengthen, cells on the other lock, and the whole fifty-meter surface slowly inclines toward light or away from dangerous wind. Its broad face harvests sunlight. Its shaded back condenses water. Grooves along its veins sort each drop by volume, sending light moisture into surface tissues and heavy runoff down to reservoirs below.

Such a tower may appear solitary, but it is never independent. Its roots drink from distant basins. Its posture is altered by messages arriving through grafts under the ground. When a storm approaches, pressure changes in its hollow ribs before the clouds are visible. It bows, stiffens, or deliberately splits a seam to spare the deeper structure. If it falls, the plateau does not necessarily treat it as dead. The lower surface may become a roof. The torn crown may root where it strikes. A collapsed trunk often continues for years as a bridge carrying water through what was once its height.

The reservoir forms are broader, quieter lives. They grow as cupped leaves with massive reinforced rims, their inner surfaces slick enough that little can cling to them. They fill, tip, drain, and right themselves by shifting fluid between chambers in their ribs. Some migrate almost imperceptibly. Each overflow softens the pavement on one side; new roots advance into the softened ground while old attachments are sealed behind. Over many seasons the entire basin crawls toward lower pressure, better light, or some chemical need communicated from structures beyond the horizon.

Along the towers hang translucent bladders. These may be offspring, organs, or symbiotic growths; the distinction cannot be cleanly made. They begin as clear knots under the outer membrane, inflate with captured water, and darken as dissolved compounds accumulate. At fullness they open along a living seam. The release is violent, but the bladder itself is uninjured. It folds, rests, and begins to fill again. Old bladders sometimes cease closing. Their walls thicken around the permanent spill, becoming galleries, channels, or the beginnings of a new terrace.

The seed pylons are the nearest things here to discrete individuals. They rise quickly, live briefly, and end in explosion. Their inner tissues manufacture broad, dense seeds under increasing pressure while the hull outside grows dry and rigid. For days before rupture, the pylon trembles. Neighboring pavement tightens around its base. Nearby towers angle away. Then the seams flash open and the seeds are thrown across kilometers.

A seed that lands badly is absorbed. One that lands well drives into the living skin and immediately begins a negotiation conducted through sap. The crater closes around it, but does not digest it. The seed sends out a fan of pale filaments, sampling moisture, tension, and the chemical identity of the surrounding growth. If accepted, its coat opens and becomes the first retaining wall of a new district. If resisted, it may remain dormant beneath the surface until injury or drought changes the local balance.

There are other ways of reproducing. Two mature towers may grow toward one another across an empty interval. When their crowns meet, neither simply pollinates the other. They interweave. For a season they become a single elevated body, sharing water, structural fibers, and packets of formative tissue. Their joined crown thickens into a storm-catching mass. Eventually wind tears it apart. The ragged strips carried away are composites of both towers, already vascular, already capable of fastening to whatever surface receives them.

Thus ancestry spreads sideways as often as it descends. A wall may inherit its outer membrane from one lineage, its water-conducting core from another, and its reproductive tissues from a third. The inhabitants do not preserve themselves as fixed forms. They preserve capacities: to seal, brace, cup, conduct, split, anchor, and begin again.

They have no eyes, but light enters deeply into their bodies and alters them. They have no ears, but vibration changes the pressure of their fluids. They have no nerves, yet injury releases signals that can cross a valley through connected roots. The response is not thought in any familiar sense. It is slower in some places than intention and faster in others. A puncture closes locally in minutes. A drought may take a season to answer. A dangerous prevailing wind can reshape the spacing of towers over generations.

Nor are they peaceful merely because there are no animals to hunt.

Their conflicts are contests of geometry. One growth shadows another until the deprived structure delaminates and becomes part of the floor. A reservoir diverts water from a neighboring root-field. Competing cables tighten around the same load, each forcing the other into less favorable angles. Boundaries form as ridges of resinous scar tissue, glossy and nearly black. Yet storms often break these partitions, and the rivals graft across the wounds. What began as competition becomes a shared buttress. What appears to be cooperation may later become engulfment.

It is therefore impossible to count the inhabitants.

There may be millions: each pavement cell, feeder thread, seed body, tower, basin, and root cable pursuing its own local continuance.

There may be several immense colonial species, so thoroughly intergrown that none can survive apart from the architecture made by the others.

There may be only one living thing, ancient but perpetually youthful at its edges, raising and discarding organs across the high country as a tree raises leaves.

The land offers evidence for all three possibilities. Fluids pass where individuals should be separate. Scar lines remain where one body ought to be whole. Distant towers answer one another through the wind, yet nearby roots sometimes constrict each other to death.

Whatever the truth, the principal inhabitant is not shaped like a creature. It has no front, no center, no stable outline. Its body is distributed among terraces, membranes, buried channels, reservoirs, and resonant trunks. Weather passes through its life as hunger, courtship, injury, and command. Rain gives it weight. Wind tests its joints. Heat drives water through its vessels. Catastrophe rearranges it.

Its behavior is the landscape changing.

Its breathing is mist rising after rain.

Its voice is the note that begins underfoot and spreads from tower to tower until several valleys are sounding.
