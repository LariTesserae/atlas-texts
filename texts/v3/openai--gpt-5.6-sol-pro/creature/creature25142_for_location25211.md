At first the people of the Condensary appear slow.

They pause at the foot of ladders. They stand aside long before a chain basket reaches its landing. They wait with one hand beneath a gutter until enough water has gathered to justify bringing the cup to their mouths. Even their arguments contain long intervals during which both parties inspect a valve, retie a sleeve, or watch a drop swell beneath a railing.

This is not lethargy. A visitor who mistakes it for weakness soon loses sight of them in the city’s tiers. Condensarians move with an exact, economical assurance, turning sideways through gaps without breaking stride, catching overhead rungs without looking, placing their feet on braces that seem too narrow to be paths. They know which pipes can bear a hand and which only resemble handrails. They know where a walkway dips under accumulated root weight and when a cooling drum will bring its single projecting bolt past knee height. Their city is crowded with slow dangers. Haste is treated as a kind of temporary stupidity.

Most are slight, though not uniformly so. The heat does not favor bulk, and food is too carefully apportioned for many bodies to grow large without purpose. Porters, press-tenders, and cistern workers may be broad through the shoulders, but even they have the ropy build of people who climb more often than they walk. Their feet are wide and tough. Their toes spread around ladder rungs. Old workers develop pale mineral crescents in the cracks of their heels and fingertips, impossible to scrub away.

Their skin carries the city. Copper workers are faintly green at the nails. Gardeners have dark stains in the whorls of their fingers. Those who spend years in the lower galleries acquire a yellow cast from sulfur and lampglass. Everyone bears small burns: bright fresh marks, brown old ones, glossy scars where an elbow met a steam pipe. A person without scars is assumed to be a child, an invalid, or newly arrived.

Hair is worn short, braided tightly against the scalp, or bound under absorbent cloth. Loose hair catches condensed water and drips unpredictably, which is considered both inconvenient and slightly rude. Beards are uncommon among machinery workers but fashionable among cistern clerks, who thread theirs through small ceramic beads that collect moisture. The finest beads have tiny channels and a cup at the bottom. Their yield is negligible. That is not the point.

Clothing consists of wraps, narrow trousers, sleeveless undershirts, climbing sashes, and many-pocketed oversleeves. The outer fabric is usually made from fungal fiber mixed with tough plant thread. It is dampened before a shift and worn close to the body, slowing the loss of sweat. Long scarves are drawn across the mouth in the lower galleries, not because the air is cold but because it may be corrosive. Nearly everyone carries a square of dry cloth in a sealed case. To offer it to another person—to wipe their face, wrap a burn, dry a tool—is an intimate generosity.

Colors are difficult to judge under amber and green lamps. In daylight, if daylight ever reached them, Condensarians might look extravagantly dressed. Cloth is dyed blue-black, red oxide, pepper green, copper turquoise, and the purple of certain medicinal molds. In the city these collapse into subtler distinctions. Residents identify one another more readily by silhouette, gait, tool belt, and the arrangement of reflective tabs sewn at shoulder and wrist.

Children wear more tabs than adults. They also wear soft caps because they have not yet learned where the city is lowest.

A Condensary child learns routes before letters. Small children are tethered near open shafts with braided waist cords, long enough to explore a room and short enough to keep them from the machinery. By five, they can name the principal pipe colors. By seven, they can carry messages across three tiers without descending to the floor. Their first civic examination is not arithmetic but stillness: they must sit beside a collection basin, notice when its overflow rhythm changes, and fetch the correct adult.

They are not solemn children. They play wherever adults have failed to fill the space.

Their games are small and vertical. They race droplets along waxed threads. They build miniature cities in the hollow ribs of retired pumps. They dare one another to touch a railing for as long as possible, though all deny doing so when burned. During the High Leakage they shriek like children anywhere, scandalizing their elders with the echoes. They hunt glass-winged gnats with cups and release them from upper bridges. Some crawl into the intact rooms of the Deep Past through gaps no adult can use and return with obsolete seals, brittle paper, or tools whose purposes have been forgotten.

A child who finds a blind threadfish becomes famous for several days. A child who keeps one hidden becomes infamous much longer.

Speech in the Condensary is soft, compressed, and accompanied by the hands. The finger signs used in markets began as factory signals, but generations have made them expressive. A twist at the knuckle can change *too expensive* into *you know this is too expensive and I admire your nerve*. Two fingers drawn downward mean water, debt, lineage, or obligation according to speed. People conduct entire minor quarrels without voicing a word.

Names are spoken more often than shouted, so a person may possess several. There is a household name, a work name, a childhood sign, and sometimes a route name given by neighbors. Route names are practical and unflattering: Ducks-Under-Blue, Three-Rungs-Missing, Walks-Where-It-Drips. They change as bodies and passages change. An old woman may still be called Quick-Through-Fanleaf long after the fanleaf has swallowed the passage and she has ceased to be quick.

Households are rarely bounded by a single dwelling. A sleeping alcove may belong to one family, the kitchen below it to another, and the catchment cloth between them to both. Kinship follows water as much as blood. Those who drink from the same minor gutter owe one another maintenance. Those who inherit opposite ends of a valve schedule are treated as something between cousins and spouses, whether or not they like one another.

Marriage, where practiced, is less decisive than the joining of obligations. The formal promise is not “I will live with you,” because there may be nowhere to live. It is “I will answer your leaks.” Couples mark their union by exchanging access keys and revising the pruning roster. Separations can be bitter, particularly when neither person is willing to surrender a productive sill.

Privacy exists, but acoustically rather than spatially. A drawn cloth means little; a change in the local drip pattern means someone wishes not to be overheard. Neighbors may hang extra catchers, set a wheel turning, or comb whisper grass to produce a soft masking tremor. To listen past such measures is a grave offense. To pretend not to hear ordinary life through the walls is basic courtesy.

Condensarians measure wealth in several overlapping ways. Water matters, but hoarding it openly attracts inspection and resentment. Space matters more, especially cool space, dry storage, and unobstructed headroom. Yet the most respected wealth is reliability: a pump share that has not failed in twenty years, a roof garden that consistently catches more than it consumes, a family whose valve records are legible across four generations.

Objects are seldom owned without duties attached. A person who owns a bridge must inspect its braces. A person with rights to a fruiting shrub must keep its roots from entering the neighboring wall. Inheritance is full of unwanted treasures. A beautiful copper still may come with six corroded feeder lines and a feud over runoff. A sleeping alcove near the upper foliage may be cool but carry a nightly obligation to clear leaf gutters before dawn.

This has made the people suspicious of gifts that are too large.

Their craftsmen are patient to a degree outsiders might find unnerving. A ceramicist can spend a season preparing a pressure vessel that will be closed only once. Mineral platers judge a bath by smell and by the taste of vapor on the upper lip. Gardeners work with hooked knives, bone tweezers, and little wedges of porous clay. They do not merely cultivate plants; they negotiate among structures. A root may be allowed to crack one wall if it relieves another. A vine may be pruned from a family’s window but trained toward the municipal duct. Every garden is also plumbing, insulation, filtration, food, medicine, shade, and property dispute.

Gardeners and engineers publicly disagree about nearly everything. In practice, each is often apprenticed for a year to the other.

The highest-status engineers are not inventors but listeners. They walk the city with sounding rods and shallow ceramic bowls, setting them against pipework to detect changes in vibration. They can distinguish a harmless mineral narrowing from a dangerous one by the note it produces. Some are said to wake moments before a pipe begins to sing. Their hearing diminishes early, and elderly listeners often continue their rounds with fingertips pressed to the walls.

Lizard keepers occupy an uncertain rank. Officially, anyone may maintain a hot-pipe lizard. In fact, a healthy one requires expert knowledge of airflow, insects, salt, and poisonous seepage. Factories boast about their lizards while pretending not to be sentimental. The designated niche is swept, shaded, and sometimes ornamented with a glazed arch no larger than a thumb. When a lizard’s skin darkens, workers evacuate first and praise it later. When one dies without darkening, they wrap it in filter cloth and argue over whether the loss was negligence or age.

The covered threadfish pool in the western works has its own attendants. They are part biologist, part custodian, part priest despite objecting to the last description. They count the fish by the movements of ripples because direct light distresses them. The number is publicly posted but never quite believed. Twenty is considered auspicious: enough to suggest survival, few enough to require care.

Food is green, pale, sour, and often soft. Edible stalks are steamed in pipe jackets. Fern hearts are pickled in mineral brine. Fungal loaves are pressed into dense sheets and toasted briefly on hot stone. Fruit is small and intensely flavored. Salt is plentiful but carefully balanced; everyone sweats, and too much of one mineral can be as dangerous as too little of another. Meals are accompanied by diluted infusions that taste of metal, pepper leaf, or smoke.

Fresh cold water is the rare luxury. During the High Leakage, people drink it too quickly and become ill. Every year the same warnings are issued. Every year the children ignore them.

No one wastes water deliberately in public. There are, however, ceremonial wastes. A cup is poured into the root mat at a birth. A few drops are flicked into the lower grilles after a death. At reconciliations, both parties wash their hands in the same basin and allow the runoff to go unclaimed into a public gutter. These acts are powerful precisely because the water is not recovered for private use.

The dead do not have much room.

Bodies are wrapped in fungal cloth and placed in warm mineral chambers where heat, molds, and bacteria reduce them over time. The remaining bones may be ground into pale cement or sealed whole inside structural members, according to family custom. Names are written on the surface before moss is encouraged to fill the lettering. Thus the dead become lintels, cistern braces, stair lips, garden troughs. It is not said that they “support the city” in any mystical sense. Such sentiment is considered cheap. They support it literally, and records are kept.

Some families object to structural burial and keep their dead in suspended ceramic shells. These hang in upper darkness among the gardens, collecting condensation. The water that runs from them is directed toward medicinal plants.

Condensarians do not agree about gods. They agree that anything worthy of reverence should also perform a function.

There are shrines around old pressure regulators, memorial lamps beneath bridges, offerings tucked into sealed rooms of the Deep Past, and small copper tongues mounted where condensation is especially dependable. People touch these tongues before journeys. Engineers call this a way of checking temperature. Gardeners call it greeting the city. Neither explanation excludes the other.

The translucent columns below provoke the strongest unease.

Those assigned to tend them are called interface workers in municipal records and root nurses everywhere else. They descend in pairs wearing glazed masks and damp wraps layered thickly over the throat. They clean mineral buildup from the coupling rings, record the columns’ internal chambers, and collect water produced by no understood pressure. The work leaves their hands unusually smooth. Some develop clear, hard patches beneath the nails. Some report dreams of rooms they have never entered, full of dripping surfaces and no machinery at all.

Root nurses are respected, well compensated, and not always invited to sleep in other people’s homes.

They disagree among themselves about whether the columns are alive. The youngest tend to say yes. The oldest usually say the question is poorly formed. One famous nurse maintained that the columns were not organisms but intentions: slow arrangements by which the fracture sought to become habitable. Her writings were officially rejected, then incorporated into three engineering manuals under different terminology.

Water from the columns is used because it must be. People complain about its taste, then notice when it is absent. Infants given too much of it sometimes bead moisture along their eyelashes while sleeping. Ledger keepers place waxed sheets between pages after condensation appeared inside a locked archive. Lovers tell stories about waking with water under their tongues, each convinced the other has done something tender or strange.

There are rumors of people altered by long exposure: a surveyor whose body temperature followed the eastern seam rather than the surrounding air; a child who could find concealed leaks by pressing her cheek to stone; an old root nurse who stopped drinking entirely and remained alive for eleven days. The official position is that heat produces confusion. The unofficial position is that the city has always required a certain amount of confusion to remain workable.

The people’s deepest virtue is not patience, though visitors call it that. It is continuance.

Patience suggests waiting for a condition to end. Condensarians do not expect the heat to end, the walls to stop sweating, or the city to become spacious. They maintain things that will never be finished. They inherit tasks whose original reasons are obscure. They replace one corroded link in a chain that their grandchildren may still be repairing. They are capable of extraordinary innovation, but distrust anything that cannot be introduced by increments, observed, reversed, and introduced again.

Suddenness frightens them. A burst pipe, a shouted alarm, a dropped tool, a person running—these create a momentary vacuum of attention into which the whole city seems to lean. Crowds do not panic noisily. They go silent. Hands appear. Valves close. Cloth is thrown over steam. Children are passed upward. Only afterward does anyone tremble.

Their humor, correspondingly, is dry and delayed. Condensary jokes often rely on a consequence arriving years after the cause. A common story concerns a man who steals one drop from his neighbor each day, only to discover after thirty years that he has been watering the neighbor’s fig. Another concerns an engineer, a gardener, and a surveyor who spend their lives disputing whether a wall is moving; the punch line is that the wall attends all three funerals.

Elders tell these stories while pruning.

The annual measurement at the eastern face draws nearly everyone eventually, though there is no single festival day. Too many bodies would raise the temperature and disturb the readings. Instead, families visit in a sequence of dim shifts. They examine the silver pins. They read the posted figures. They touch the measuring wire only if a surveyor permits it.

Surveyors are among the few people who regularly wear pale clothing, so that grime and mineral dust show immediately. Their fingers are long-practiced and steady. They are chosen as much for temperament as skill. A surveyor must be able to discover change without desiring too much of it.

Around them, the moisture-loving plants form a living border. Children search the leaves for new varieties. Gardeners note which roots have turned toward the seam. Engineers place their ears to the rock. Old people bring stools and sit for hours with their palms against the hot stone.

What they imagine beyond it varies.

Some picture another cavity already furnished by mineral columns, cool and wet and empty of human beings. Some imagine open space so large that voices do not return. Some believe the new chamber will permit broad gardens, straight streets, reservoirs deep enough to swim in. Others fear it will drain the vapor upward and leave the Condensary dry. Property claims have been drafted, challenged, archived, and redrafted for land that does not yet exist.

Children are more practical. They argue over who will be first through.

No one living expects the wall to open in a dramatic rupture. That belongs to the founding stories, to the terrifying birth of the cavity, when black stone split from black stone and heat breathed upward into emptiness. The people now expect a hairline crack, then a wet line, then perhaps a gap narrow enough for a thread. They will watch it. They will measure airflow. They will train fanleaf nearby. A ceramic lip will be fitted under the first dependable seep.

Only much later, if the opening proves stable, someone small may put an eye to it.

Until then, they wait with hands on the eastern wall. Not passive, not still: listening through skin, counting drops, imagining obligations. Behind them, the whole packed city breathes by pistons and leaves. Beneath them, the translucent columns lift warm water through their chambers. Above them, unseen, weather crosses the desert.

And sometimes the stone answers—not with a voice, but with one cold drop falling in darkness where no drop fell before.
