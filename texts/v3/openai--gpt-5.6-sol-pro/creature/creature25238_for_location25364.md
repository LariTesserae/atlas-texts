At first glance, the people of the garden seem less strange than what they tend.

They are slight, brown-skinned, long-fingered, and usually wrapped in sleeveless garments of woven fiber that leave the knees and shoulders bare. Most go unshod. Their soles are thickened by heat and delicately crosshatched by the tile channels, whose patterns imprint themselves over years until an elder’s feet resemble two small maps of the basin. They wear their hair in tight cords dressed with resin, oil, powdered chalk, or fragments of green stone. A person’s trade can often be read from what has collected there: kiln ash, mica scales, blue-glass filings, beetle husks, snippets of red thread.

They move carefully, not from fear of breaking the garden but from certainty that it is attending to them. They step over oil beads. They apologize to closed flowers before cutting around them. They never lean idly against a wall, because walls have been known to develop doors around people who remain in contact too long.

There are several hundred inhabitants, though a count never produces the same number twice. They live in low houses along the least fertile perimeter, built of fitted tile, hide screens, fiber rafters, and blocks taken from wall recesses that later refill. Their rooms are narrow and roofless. At night the false sky can be seen between awnings, charcoal-dark and without stars. Beds are braided hammocks suspended above the warm paths. Infants sleep in clay cradles whose inner surfaces are inscribed with their names many times, in case the child begins growing in an unexpected direction.

No one is entirely certain whether the people contain water.

They bleed when cut, but the blood varies. In some it is red and ordinary-looking, though unusually slow to fall. In others it emerges as clear oil, red dust, hot sap, a brief ribbon of black fiber, or a swarm of tiny lights that crawl back toward the wound. Families disagree about whether this has always been so. Bodies are not opened after death unless there is great need, and what has been found inside has discouraged general inquiry: knotted cords passing through hollow bones, little stacks of warm tiles, organs shaped like tools, or nothing at all except a dry space from which the deceased’s voice continues for several days.

They eat sparingly. Their staple foods are shaved chalk-pith, softened resin, the flour of red teeth, beetle paste, smoked bulbs, and thin lacquer stripped from certain leaves before the browsers can reach it. Meals are ground, folded, hammered, warmed, or sung into digestibility. Nothing is boiled. Nothing is washed. The old songs mention thirst, but the gardeners use the word for several forms of longing and do not agree that it once meant a bodily need.

Children sometimes ask why they have mouths.

Adults answer that mouths are for naming.

Every child receives a temporary name at birth, usually a simple object-word: Pin, Cup, Reed, Hinge, Ash. The permanent name is earned later, when the child first notices a new growth before anyone else does. The name given to that growth and the name taken by the child are paired forever. Thus a woman may be called Iram-of-the-Listening-Spine, because as a girl she discovered a white ribbed shoot that vibrated when lies were spoken nearby. A man may be Tavel-of-Nine-Elbows, after a vine that bends at eight joints when observed and nine when touched.

To name a growth is not merely to label it. The namer must decide what sort of thing it is permitted to become.

A proper name has a boundary inside it.

For this reason, the most honored people are the dusk-namers, who walk the garden in the final hours of day carrying clay tablets, charcoal sticks, string, and small shuttered lamps. They work in pairs. One looks for alterations; the other watches the first for signs that something has begun growing through their thoughts. When they find a new bud, seam, animal, shadow, opening, sound, or distance, they describe it aloud as narrowly and exactly as possible.

“Three-knuckled gray feeder, confined to the northern chalk stand, awake only under divided shadow.”

“Low red hinge-flower, no larger than a hand, bearing neither offspring nor doorway.”

“Second echo of Mal’s left foot, permitted to follow Mal alone.”

The formula is pressed into clay before sunset. If the thing accepts the name, it usually folds, shivers, clicks, or changes color. If it does not, the namers shorten the description, sharpen its limits, or offer it a better sound. Some growths are vain. Some are literal. Some object only to being called plants.

The tile-keepers preserve these names. They are elderly men and women with scarred knees and excellent memories, responsible for the incised pavement, the oil’s progress, genealogies, path changes, forbidden words, and events that have ceased to have happened everywhere except in one particular groove. They crawl through the basin with wax tablets strapped to their backs. They know which channels are veins, which are roads, and which become one another during the night.

A tile-keeper never says, “This leads there.”

They say, “Today, this consents to be followed.”

Their oldest member is a woman called Oru-of-the-Closed-Mouth, though she speaks more than anyone. She has lived through nineteen Seasons of Narrowing and remembers when the western arcade had ten recesses, eight recesses, and—on one difficult morning—none. Her left hand is young, smooth, and quick. Her right is ancient, with translucent nails and too many joints. She claims they were born together. When she sleeps, the younger hand makes baskets from strands of her hair.

The craftspeople form another kind of priesthood, though they deny being priests. They repair what can be seen: collars, screens, collars around screens, split stems, kiln mouths, animal masks, warm paths, and the hinged ribs of houses. Their tools are simple, exact, and inherited. A bow drill may belong to a lineage longer than the family using it. A loom-song may contain a pattern no weaver understands until, after twelve generations, someone completes it and discovers they have woven a window.

Such accidents are treated as harvests.

The maker does not celebrate immediately. First the object is watched.

A basket larger inside than outside is covered with felt, bound shut, and carried west without looking into it again. A bowl that fills with an unfamiliar afternoon is placed in the sixth recess to cool. A stool whose fourth leg stands in another room is marked on the floor and approached only from the other three sides. Useful impossibilities are not destroyed, but neither are they trusted. The craftspeople say that skilled hands sometimes remember a craft the mind has not yet learned—or has learned elsewhere.

The gardeners themselves are divided by task rather than rank. Oil-guides clear the tile channels with bone needles. Leaf-menders sew cracked mica to hide backing. Trellis-tenders prune predatory frames after feeding, wearing masks on the backs of their heads so the frames cannot decide which face to close around. Herd-keepers tend the pale lantern-bodied flocks, though “tend” mostly means following them and collecting the thin translucent strips they shed at dawn.

The smallest children are red-tooth feeders. They grind the fragrant teeth from opened jar-fruits and carry the powder in stoppered gourds. Creatures fed on it become briefly more complete. A leaf-moth may acquire a second wing pattern. A six-legged lizard may remember the two legs it does not have. Once, a child fed too much to a shadow-animal from the twelfth recess, and by morning it had become the shadow of an enormous tree no one could see. For a month, the central paths were cool beneath its branches.

The people do not rule the other inhabitants. At best, they have negotiated habits with them.

The leaf-moths are among the most numerous. Each is assembled from mismatched materials: stitched gut membrane, shell dust, hair, mica, pressed fiber. Their bodies are soft plugs of gray felt with tiny warm stones at the center. They feed by resting over scratches in manufactured leaves. By morning the scratches are gone, transferred onto the moths’ wings as dark branching marks. Old moths become crowded with damage. Eventually they can no longer fly and hang beneath the canopy like folded records.

The glazed lizards are bolder. They lick chalk from trunks with tongues like rough ribbons. Their six feet rotate in pairs, allowing them to walk along narrow rods without turning their bodies. If grasped, they shed not their tails but their glaze. Beneath it they are plain unfired clay, soft enough to bear fingerprints. They hide until a new surface hardens. Children sometimes find a cast-off glaze intact and wear it as a finger puppet, though this is discouraged; the lizard may later mistake the finger for part of itself.

The warm beetles live in disciplined colonies. They bore holes of astonishing regularity, each exactly one beetle-width apart, unless an object is dangerous. Around danger their measurements contract. Gardeners use them to mark beams, test straightedges, and judge whether a newly formed recess is stable. A ruler made from beetle-holes is called an honest ruler because beetles have no known reason to flatter anyone.

The lantern flocks are less understood. Their bodies appear folded rather than born. Long paper-like legs support pale pleated torsos, and their lowered heads are hidden inside their chests. They graze on narrow bands of warmth, leaving the tiles cool behind them. At night they align along heated paths in rows so exact that sleepers sometimes wake believing a fence has been built around the house.

Now and then one unfolds.

No one watches this directly. There is a dry snapping, a sudden increase of pale surface, and afterward something tall and nearly flat stands among the thickets. It bears writing on its inner side. The writing is always in the hand of someone living in the garden, but usually records an event that person has not yet witnessed. The tile-keepers copy what they can before the creature folds itself back into animal shape.

The heavy browsers are called veiled ones. Their ribs are basketry that tightens and loosens as they breathe. Under the ribs hangs a dark, heat-shaped body, not quite touching them. Their faces grow masks: some smooth, some beaked, some bearing crude human expressions. A shed mask is buried beneath an upturned tile, because if hung on a wall it slowly grows a new browser behind it.

The veiled ones strip lacquer with black tongues. They are placid unless cornered. Then distance opens around them. A hand reaching toward one may travel for hours without crossing the final inch. Young herd-keepers use this property for dares, placing sweets on a browser’s back and seeing who can retrieve them before night. Elders punish the game severely. Not because of the danger of becoming lost—most children return—but because things sometimes return in the child’s nearness.

Thread-snakes are tolerated because they keep the beetle colonies from becoming too orderly. They hang like loose fibers until prey sleeps beneath them. Then they descend, undo the sleeper, and wind themselves into its shape. For several days the substitute beetle performs its duties perfectly. Gradually it introduces variations: one hole too high, one too deep, one opening onto a place that is not inside the wood. Careful craftspeople inspect every colony for originality.

The trellises are fed at designated intervals. They resemble harmless frameworks of resin-stiffened rods until a moth crosses the inner angle. Then the rods close with a single domestic click. In the morning a new joint may have appeared. Older trellises are elaborate, dense, and difficult to distinguish from cages. The gardeners prune them back, but never to fewer than three crossings. A trellis reduced to two becomes a pair of lines, and lines in the garden are capable of extending indefinitely.

There are less visible inhabitants.

The thing that lives in gaps is called Under-Near, though no one considers this a true name. It is described differently in each archive: as a flat animal with too many knees; as a hunger moving sideways; as the underside of a creature too large to enter; as a black hide stretched between adjacent things. Its tracks resemble four-fingered hands pressed one after another into dust, soot, or soft clay. Since the garden has almost no dust and little soft clay, finding tracks means that Under-Near has prepared the surface on which to walk.

Doors are fitted tightly. Tiles are stacked without slits. Speakers avoid deliberate pauses when discussing it. Two knocks are answered at once, lest it inhabit the interval and knock a third time from inside.

There are also the remnant shadows of the twelfth recess. They begin as stains shaped like discarded tools, jars, masks, or eggs. Over several days they thicken, detach, and develop nervous motion. Most become small four-footed creatures, no higher than an ankle, with pointed ears and no visible mouths. They eat reflected light. They hide behind feet but avoid the people who cast them. If startled, they flatten against the nearest object and temporarily become its second shadow.

Each carries some faint habit of the item that produced it. The shadow of a hammer taps in its sleep. The shadow of a cord coils around friendships. The shadow of an egg follows warm bodies and waits.

The dark sockets in the wall are inhabited too, though perhaps not by organisms. Gardeners leave clay effigies beside them: crude figures with broad hands and blank faces. Sometimes an effigy is gone by morning. Sometimes it remains but has become skillfully made. Occasionally a socket contains breathing. Occasionally it contains a view of the garden from a height no one can reach. One contains a sound like hundreds of people shaping blocks just beyond sight.

The gardeners call those beyond the wall the Neighbors, because “builders” grants them too much intention.

As for the distant figure among the central thickets, it has accumulated many names and accepted none. Seen indirectly, it appears human-sized, though at times its shoulders are level with the canopy and at others it could fit inside a jar-fruit. It stands with its back turned, working at something held before it. Its arms move with the calm repetition of weaving, grinding, mending, or assembling. Some observers see long hair. Some see a hood. Some see a cluster of poles tied in the outline of a person.

Children believe it is making the garden.

Craftspeople believe it is repairing something on the far side.

The tile-keepers suspect it is taking the garden apart and that what appears to be growth is merely the loosened material coming through.

No path reaches it. Attempts do not end in obstruction; they become journeys. A person may walk toward the figure for half a day and return years older, carrying unfamiliar tools, insisting only a few steps were taken. One woman came back with soil under her nails. Another returned with a wet green leaf pressed to her tongue. She died before she could describe its taste, though for several seasons afterward ordinary shoots appeared in footprints around her grave.

Death in the garden is understood as a kind of season affecting one person.

The dead are not buried. There is nowhere to bury them that does not add architecture. Instead they are seated in woven frames near the outer thickets and watched until their bodies declare what they intend to become. Some dry into light, hollow forms and are dismantled for useful bone. Some stiffen into chalk-cored saplings whose leaves whisper in the deceased’s voice. Some produce masks. A few simply become increasingly distant, like alarmed browsers, until they remain visible only as upright marks at the end of very long paths.

Funeral bells are struck once. The returning sound is listened to carefully.

If it comes back as a tool-noise, the dead person’s implements are redistributed.

If it returns as an animal call, food is left beneath the foliage.

If it returns as speech, the sentence is recorded, even when spoken by someone not yet born.

Birth is treated with greater caution than death, especially during the Season of Facets. Midwives are joint-keepers as much as healers. They bind the mother’s shadow to her ankles so it cannot divide away under strain. They count the infant’s edges before counting its fingers. They ensure that the afterbirth does not attach itself to the walls. Sometimes a child arrives accompanied by an object: a hinge, a smooth stone, a sealed clay whistle, a scrap of woven pattern. These are kept until the child’s permanent naming. Sometimes they prove to be organs the child will need later.

The people are affectionate, but affection is maintained as carefully as any structure. During the Season of the Open Joint, families retie their kinships with spoken vows and literal cords. Friends sit facing one another and recount the limits of their friendship: what may be asked, what must be given, what cannot be allowed to spread from one life into the other. Lovers exchange fitted pieces of wood that make a whole only when held at the correct distance.

Promises that survive the season are prized more than metal.

Their greatest fear is not death, predation, or the walls. It is boundaryless life.

They have seen a misplaced shoot enter a carving of itself. They have heard a tune acquire roots in every throat that hummed it. They preserve fragments of maps burned around a shape no one can name. There are elders who wake with a missing adjective lodged painfully behind the teeth. There are children who draw a pale tendril without having been shown one, always extending it beyond the edge of the tablet.

When this happens, the drawing is not destroyed at once. A dusk-namer is summoned. The child is asked what the line is doing.

The answers vary.

“It is looking for its leaf.”

“It is going where the word went.”

“It is holding all the gardens together.”

Once, a child answered, “It is the part that lives outside.”

That tablet is kept covered in the western arcade, in a recess whose number depends on who is counting. Beneath the cover, the line has continued to lengthen. It crosses the clay without marking it, passes over the shelf, and disappears into the cool mineral darkness behind the stone.

On certain nights, something there gives the line a gentle pull.

And throughout the basin—the named people in their hammocks, the moths under leaves, the snakes inside stolen shapes, the shy shadows, the veiled browsers standing immensely far away, the figure working at the unreachable center—everything grows still enough to feel it.

Not silence exactly.

Tension.

As if the whole garden were a woven thing, and one living thread had just been drawn taut from the other side.
