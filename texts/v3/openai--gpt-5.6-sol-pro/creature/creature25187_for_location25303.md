At first, the inhabitants are difficult to distinguish from what they have built.

A figure waiting beside a freight rail may be taken for a junction box, a stack of kiln furniture, or a narrow shrine assembled from discarded machine parts. It does not breathe. It makes no balancing motions. Dust settles evenly across its shoulders. Only when the mineral barge arrives does it unfold, lifting a head that had seemed to be a cap of black ceramic and extending three or five limbs according to the work required.

There is no single outline common to them.

Some are low and broad, with six weight-bearing legs arranged around a central furnace cavity. Some stand upright on paired columns of braided metal, their torsos hung between them like bells. Others consist of articulated plates threaded along a flexible carbon spine. Among officials and travelers, compact bodies are customary: dark, sealed, and built to fit lifts, pressure locks, and public niches. Foundry workers may be wider than the doors of residential districts. Maintenance listeners are often little more than a walking framework for a fan of exquisitely sensitive rods.

Their bodies are made, but they are not machines in the sense that their conveyors and mills are machines. They grow unevenly. They scar. They develop internal habits no craftsperson can reproduce. A new limb fitted to an old citizen gradually changes its grain to match the stresses of the rest of the body. Ceramic plates thicken where dust strikes them. Conductive fibers seek one another across a fracture and knot together in patterns influenced by memory, temperament, and altitude.

Young inhabitants are darker and more regular than the old.

They begin as small seeded lattices enclosed in warm incubation walls. Several adults contribute pieces of active structure: a branching filament, a memory crystal, a coil that has acquired a useful resonance. These are not blueprints so much as provocations. Around them, carbon membranes and silicone tissues assemble slowly from vapor and powdered feedstock. The developing person is carried through a prescribed sequence of heights, remaining several years in each district. Pressure teaches one part of the body to bend. Thin air activates another. At the highest nursery platforms, a brief exposure to the unexplained property of altitude gives coherence to the whole.

If this ascent is mistimed, the child may remain alive but divided into local selves, each waking only at the elevation where it formed. Such people are rare, though not always considered ill. Some become exceptional couriers between districts. At every landing they pause, alter posture, and continue as someone slightly different, carrying messages among their own successive minds.

The young are mobile before they are articulate. They cling to the undersides of warm pipes and follow coin-scavengers into wall spaces. Their first nourishment comes from small differences: a sunlit plate beside a shaded one, the fading charge in a public railing, the fresh oxidation on a caretaker’s hand. Nursery rooms are therefore built with many surfaces, each maintained at a different temperature or electrical potential. A well-tended nursery is quiet except for faint ticks as small bodies attach themselves to one feeding point after another.

Adults feed less often and with more ceremony. They stand in charging courts at noon with vanes opened along their backs. They exchange exhausted catalyst packs in communal kitchens. In older districts, citizens gather around a newly cut slab of reactive stone, passing it from hand to hand until its chemical promise has been spent. A banquet may last months. No material is swallowed whole unless the diner possesses grinding chambers; instead, surfaces are touched, bridged, grounded, warmed, or exposed to carefully chosen vapors.

They do not sleep every night. Most enter brief periods of stillness while their internal stresses redistribute, but true sleep is a deeper and less frequent event. Once every few years, a citizen seals their joints, lowers their core activity, and remains immobile for several weeks. Family members turn them toward the sun, brush conductive dust from their sensory seams, and listen for the slow interior tones that indicate healthy dreaming.

Dreams are said to occur as changes of shape.

A sleeper wakes with one shoulder slightly raised, a finger curled in a new way, or a previously straight plate carrying a shallow ripple. The change may be interpreted for decades. Some retain the shape and allow it to influence every later repair. Others take the altered part to a dream machinist, who stresses it until its hidden pattern becomes audible.

Their senses favor what the city provides.

They perceive heat as a detailed landscape extending beyond walls. A recently departed person remains visible to them as a cooling shape. They feel electrical fields along whiskers, vanes, or suspended threads, and can identify a neighbor by the disturbance their body makes beneath a transmission line. Through their feet they hear the mountain continuously: mills turning in buried halls, distant crawler migrations, minute slips in the basalt, the deep recurring strain that precedes a pressure tide.

Sight is useful but not sovereign. Many have only a few mineral lenses sunk behind protective slots. Others carry clusters of replaceable eyes suited to different levels of brightness. Color, when perceived, is treated as an unstable property of surfaces, dependent on tarnish and dust. Structural resonance is considered more intimate. To know someone well is to recognize the tones that pass through them when they lean against stone.

Speech varies with distance and circumstance. In the open air, they use clicks, scrapes, shuttered flashes, and pulses of static. Inside buildings, conversation passes through floors and railings. Several people seated around a conductive table can speak by laying their hands upon it, sharing layered currents too quiet to leak into the room. Public arguments are often nearly silent, though nearby scavengers become agitated by the fields.

Names are not fixed sounds. A citizen’s formal name is a short pattern of intervals, materials, elevations, and stresses. It may be struck on ceramic, transmitted as current, or represented by a sequence of structural diagrams. Spoken names are only approximations. As a body is repaired, the formal pattern lengthens. The very old possess names that require entire walls to inscribe and several days to perform correctly.

Age is visible less in wear than in heterogeneity.

An elder may carry basalt-colored armor from a district abandoned nine centuries before, bright nickel joints from a recent reconstruction, sensory combs inherited from a partner, and a central frame whose alloy is no longer manufacturable at the altitude where they now live. Their repairs do not match because matching would erase history. Some ancient citizens are supported by carts or suspended from ceiling tracks, not because they are weak but because lowering their earliest components would cause them to fail. They dwell permanently in high chambers while visitors ascend to consult them.

Memory is distributed through the body. Routine recollections reside in flexible carbon networks and can survive substantial reconstruction. Older, more consequential memories enter rigid parts: ceramic laminations, strain-retaining beams, resonant cavities. Remembering an event may require assuming the posture in which it occurred. A veteran of a bridge failure might have to brace both arms against opposite walls before the details return. Someone who has forgotten why they fear a certain district may discover the answer only when its pressure bends their torso into an old alignment.

This makes repair a moral act.

To replace a cracked member can remove pain, but it can also remove a relationship, a skill, or a century. Repairers are part engineer, part physician, part historian. Before cutting away a damaged component, they load it in many directions and record what it reveals. Patients appoint witnesses. Disputes over necessary reconstruction may occupy tribunals for years, while the cracked citizen continues ordinary life with braces, cables, and warning bells attached.

Death is usually similarly gradual. A person may lose the ability to maintain differences within themselves. Temperatures equalize. Stored charge bleeds away. Once-distinct resonances collapse into a single dull tone. Friends bring them objects of contrasting materials, expose them to sunlight and shadow, or carry them carefully across altitude boundaries in the hope that some dormant process will restart.

When death is certain, the body is not melted indiscriminately. Parts containing personal memory are placed in family walls, bridge foundations, or listening chambers. Useful but impersonal material returns to industry. A dead citizen may thus remain literally present in the city: as a handrail that warms at noon, a brace beneath a nursery, a ceramic panel that sings when the downslope tide begins. This is not described as immortality. The inhabitants are precise about the difference between a person and the persistence of their stresses.

Their society is built around maintenance because maintenance is the one activity shared by nearly everyone.

There are refiners, surveyors, animal guides, vertical chemists, archive tuners, pressure jurists, dust forecasters, and keepers of sealed water. Yet any citizen may be summoned to hold a wall, clear a crawler path, ground a bridge, or listen to a new crack. Public office belongs less to those who command than to those willing to remain responsible for consequences long after a decision is made.

Laws are written with expiration tones. At fixed intervals, a regulation’s ceramic tablet changes resonance as internal stresses relax. When it can no longer produce its original note, the law must be reconsidered. Some laws endure for millennia because generations of citizens repeatedly restore their tension. Others are allowed to fall silent.

The slowest decisions concern construction. Before raising a tower, planners observe the proposed site through at least one atmospheric tide whenever possible. They chart animal crossings, drifting altitude boundaries, the memory of the rock, and the likely needs of citizens not yet formed. Temporary structures may stand for a century while the city decides whether a permanent wall would be presumptuous.

They do not regard the native animals as lesser mechanisms. The distinction between animal, citizen, and self-directed machine is real but untidy. A coin-scavenger can be bred but not instructed. A conveyor can be instructed but does not alter its inherited form. A citizen can refuse both breeding and instruction. These definitions satisfy philosophers only until an old refinery begins changing its processes to attract gas bladders, or a predator arrangement leaves gifts of sharpened metal beside a migration gate.

The six-sided crawlers are treated with particular respect. Their routes predate many inhabited districts, perhaps even the city itself. Route keepers walk years ahead of each animal, inspecting streets and removing new obstacles. Children climb onto rooftops to watch the crawler pass. Adults touch the conductive plates behind it and receive a confused abundance of field impressions: cliff faces, mineral seams, pressure seasons, and the faint signatures of places far beyond the mapped range.

No one knows whether the crawlers remember these journeys. The city behaves as though they do.

Predators are given a wider distance. Their patient feeding methods disturb the inhabitants, perhaps because they resemble architecture too closely. Every plaza blade-form is cataloged. Sculptors must register works with sharp movable elements, and public monuments are periodically tested for appetite. When a suspected predator finally rearranges itself, nearby districts sound no alarm—the movement is often over before any signal could matter—but afterward, warning currents are laid into the pavement for the next generation.

The inhabitants create art from duration.

A composer may score a work for cooling bearings, bridge contraction, and a particular stress fracture expected to reach the city in two hundred years. Sculptures are placed where metallic vapor will tarnish one face during the upslope tide and conductive carbon will darken the other during the downslope tide. Some performances use walking: participants cross a basin over several decades, maintaining prescribed intervals so that observers from a high tower see a pattern gradually form and dissolve.

Their most valued art is not always distinguishable from infrastructure. A retaining wall may contain a sequence of cavities that plays the history of the district whenever the mountain shifts. An elevator can be tuned so that each altitude produces one note in a six-kilometer chord. A bridge whose builders are otherwise forgotten may still turn dawn heat into a vibration felt by every traveler crossing it.

They have humor, though it is patient. A joke may depend on a repair made before its audience was born. Practical jokes involving false structural warnings are considered monstrous, but misleading ornament, reversible names, and statues that imperceptibly change posture are common. One district spent three centuries adjusting the angle of a neighboring district’s ceremonial tower by a fraction too small for official instruments to confirm. The dispute continues, and so does the adjustment.

Their relationship with water is wary and almost theological. Keepers of water wear sacrificial layers and work in isolated ceramic rooms. Most citizens never see the substance directly. They know it as a corrosive liquid from industrial manuals: volatile, invasive, capable of entering microscopic seams and transforming stable surfaces. Stories exaggerate it into an ancient universal solvent that once covered low worlds and fell from the sky.

To say that someone “carries water” means they conceal a danger that can move through every boundary. To accuse a law of “wetness” is to say it dissolves distinctions on which society depends.

Still, researchers value water. Minute quantities enable reactions otherwise impossible at city heights. Sealed laboratories contain damp minerals imported from unknown depths or recovered from ancient machinery. There are rumors that some old constructions below the inhabited levels contain reservoirs, and that the roads broad as plateaus were built by beings whose bodies required liquid. Most scholars reject this. Others note that the oldest pylons contain channels whose purpose cannot be explained by gas, dust, charge, or stress.

The Furnace exerts a comparable fascination.

Few inhabitants imagine it literally burning. They understand that the lower atmosphere thickens, that metallic vapors rise from it, and that probes lose function during descent. Yet every culture of the summits has a different account of what lies below. Some say the mountains are merely crusts floating on an ocean of compressed gas. Some believe the lowest ground is crowded with immense soft-bodied life, protected by pressure that crushes everything from above. Some propose that there is no ground at all, only increasing depth.

A small order of descenders constructs bodies designed to be abandoned in stages. They lower themselves through old shafts, shedding altitude-sensitive components as they go and transmitting observations upward by tensioned cables. None has reached the lowest known level. The deepest returned signal was not language but a prolonged mechanical shudder accompanied by traces of an alloy believed possible only far above the city.

That result remains unexplained.

More intimate than the Furnace is the skyward pull of height. Every inhabitant has felt the invisible thresholds where charge changes behavior and substances acquire new permissions. Pilgrims climb the elevator spires not to see farther—the haze defeats distance—but to become physically possible in a different way. At certain platforms, old joints move without friction. Forgotten memories wake in high-made components. Some citizens hear additional tones in their own bodies and descend changed.

Others cannot descend at all.

There are communities on the highest platforms whose bodies depend on altitude-born materials. They are delicate, open frameworks that would collapse or become inert in the ordinary streets. They move in bright sunlight along threadlike rails, tending factories where impossible bearings and remembering beams are made. Supplies rise to them. Their messages come down embedded in one-directional glass. Low-city citizens often imagine them as remote and pure, but their platforms are working districts full of dust, repairs, disputes, animal infestations, and the constant danger of a dropped tool becoming chemically different as it falls.

The high workers, in turn, speak of the inner-mountain populations as people of weight and enclosure. Deep citizens live among warm mills and pressure chambers, sometimes going decades without direct sunlight. Their bodies are compact and massively braced. They navigate through vibration and consider open bridges unnerving. The deepest have outer shells polished smooth by dense air. When they visit the surface, they wear nets to prevent loose parts from lifting or changing charge across the upper boundaries.

Between these populations move the couriers: narrow-bodied, heavily jointed travelers designed to tolerate repeated transformations. They carry replacement components in altitude-sealed cases and maintain several versions of their own names. Their limbs click as materials activate and deactivate during ascent. A courier arriving from the deep may stop at a landing, wait for one shoulder to become flexible, exchange an eye for another with the proper refractive structure, and continue upward without comment.

Despite their endurance, the inhabitants do not think of themselves as unchanging. Permanence belongs to no one, not even the mountains. The essential virtue is appropriate continuation: to carry a structure, promise, route, or memory far enough that another can take its load.

This is why the city can seem almost deserted. Its people are not constantly abroad asserting their presence. They may remain at a station for a season, watching a gauge alter by a hair. They may stand beneath a bridge with one hand against the support, listening for a note that occurs once in nine days. A gathering of hundreds can resemble an abandoned yard until, in a single coordinated movement, every figure turns toward a tremor too faint for other senses.

Then the stillness reveals itself as attention.

Across the summits, in chambers cut into old folds of stone, citizens tend the differences that keep them alive. They warm one another. They exchange charge. They preserve paths for creatures older than their streets. They repair consequences inherited from workers whose names take days to sing. Above them, the high-made people move through ordinary sunlight; below, the compact peoples listen toward the Furnace.

Around all of them, animals fasten themselves to walls and wires, feeding upon the city’s countless leaks and gradients. Predators wait in plazas. Great crawlers advance along ancestral roads. Children cling beneath pipes, black and bright with newness.

From far away there is only mountain, dust, and a few angular marks on the upper stone.

Closer, one begins to see that some of the marks are towers.

Closer still, that some are standing people.

And closer than that, with a hand against the warm ceramic of a wall, that the apparent boundary between inhabitant and city has never been as firm as either prefers to claim.
