They are called creatures only because they possess the equipment of creatures: mouths, hinges, cilia, eyes, gut coils, clasping limbs. None has the privacy ordinarily required of an animal.

The smallest live as bright punctuation between grains of stone. Yellow cells occupy pits in the chalk. Violet colonies follow the salt seams. Green rings tighten around pearls of flesh, each ring made from hundreds of bodies arranged mouth outward, their interiors shared. Some are so thoroughly fitted to the mineral around them that it is impossible to tell whether they grew into hollows or whether the hollows learned their shapes.

The shelled ones cover nearly everything.

Their shells are not armor against danger. There is nowhere to withdraw, and nothing can strike. Instead, the shells are instruments of accommodation. One curls around a crystal bud. Another broadens to receive the rim of its neighbor. A third has inherited six shallow dents from six surrounding mouths. Their whorls do not record years of growth; every chamber in every spiral exists at once, from the minute innermost coil to the lucid outer lip.

Within each shell is a pale animal folded more intricately than the shell should allow. It has a comb of breathing leaves, a ring of tasting fingers, and a muscular foot that has never contracted. At the hinge sits a grain of dark tissue sensitive to brightness, though brightness has never altered. The creatures perceive the chamber not by changes in light but by differences too slight to be called color: chalk-white, salt-white, flesh-white, water-white. To them these are as distinct as noon and midnight.

Where the shells meet, softer inhabitants are compressed into heraldic shapes. Five-armed mouths spread like flowers. Blue bladders contain red worms, which in turn contain chains of silver eggs. Flat velvet animals wear forests of arrested cilia. Each hair inclines in the same direction, recording a current that is forever about to pass.

There are hunters among them, though hunting is not an event here.

A needle-jawed thing holds a translucent grazer by the waist. Its venom duct is full. The grazer’s feeding crown remains buried in a mat of edible cells. Within the grazer, those cells are enclosed but undigested; within the cells, green bodies gather radiance they cannot spend. Predator, prey, fodder, and light form a single suspended sentence. No part can release the next.

The eggs are the chamber’s most numerous inhabitants. They lie under shell rims, within mucus lattices, behind the blue sacs, and in the three cupped leaves of the colorless plant. Some are clear enough to reveal the young inside. The unborn have spines, paddles, jaws, branching gills. They press against their membranes with such force that fine white fractures radiate around their faces.

They are not waiting. Waiting requires a future.

In the basin stand the glass larvae, hundreds of them, upright as pins. Their tails taper downward without touching the bottom. Each single black eye is turned toward a different point, so that together they behold the whole chamber. Around their heads, feelers spread in delicate crowns. The feelers appear identical until examined closely: one bears hooks, one cups, one beads of poison, one tiny secondary eyes, one buds that resemble hands.

The larvae share the water without displacing it. No meniscus gathers around them. Each occupies a narrow exemption in the basin, and the water fits that exemption exactly. Their bodies are mostly organs for transformations that never occur—stomachs for first meals, glands for dissolving larval tissues, imaginal knots from which adult limbs should unfold. Every one contains a later animal compressed inside the present one. In some, several possible adults can be discerned, overlapping like figures drawn on both sides of transparent skin.

Around the basin, the armored ring is made of seven segmented beings, though counting them is difficult. The head of each passes beneath the tail plate of the next, and their boundaries blur where the armor overlaps. They have blunt digging claws, jointed antennae, and rows of breathing pores along their flanks. Their inner legs hold clay pellets. Their outer legs brace against the floor. Thus each is simultaneously traveler, wall, and mason.

They maintain the ring around the water. They are also the ring.

The pellets in their claws match those already built into the little circular structures nearby. One pellet touches a half-finished gap; another occupies that same gap as though it has always been there. The builders’ jaws are worn smooth by labor, yet retain the sharp unworn edges with which they began. Inside their mouths live minute attendants that polish the clay. Inside the attendants are still smaller beings that consume nothing but the idea of wear.

Above them clings the broad-backed one.

At first it seems the chamber’s sovereign, because it is largest and because so much life depends upon it. Its six limbs are pale columns bent close against the ceiling. Its flattened back conforms to every mineral swelling. Where unfinished arches descend, the animal rises to meet them in knobs and folds, as though ceiling and body were pressing each other into existence.

It has no head in any usual place. Along its forward edge are several possible heads: a blind wedge with grinding plates, a soft trumpet lined with teeth, a cluster of sealed eyes, and a narrow opening from which the roughest strand of the chamber’s chord emerges. None can be declared primary. Nerves radiate from all of them and meet in a web beneath the skin.

Its back is inhabited in layers.

Shell fleas grip the ridges. Cup worms rise from pits between them. Mites stand with their mouthparts inserted but do not drink. Clear sacs hang beneath the limbs, each sac crowded with swimmers finer than dust. Some of these swimmers carry their own parasites; some contain mineral grains identical to those in the walls. A branching animal resembling frost spreads over one shoulder and penetrates the flesh with rootlike threads. Where host ends and colony begins, their tissues exchange colors.

The broad-backed one feeds on the ceiling without removing any of it. Rows of rasping teeth rest against blue clay, and blue clay lies within its gut, but the wall bears only the beginning of a scrape. Its waste passages are full of chalk pellets indistinguishable from the masons’ clay. Whether it supplies the builders, whether the builders feed it, or whether both are separate organs of some slower arrangement cannot be determined.

Even the colorless stalk by the basin may be an animal in one of its disguises.

Its three leaves are cupped like hands, and each holds a perfect drop. Beneath the translucent skin of the stalk run tubes containing pale beads. They resemble sap until one notices that every bead has cilia and a mouth. The roots divide into filaments in the brine seam, where they join the tails of buried worms. The green cells at the tips of nearby transparent threads turn toward it—not toward the omnipresent light, but toward the stalk.

Nothing in the chamber is solitary enough to be named cleanly.

A shell is an animal and the footing of another animal. A parasite is also a brace. An egg is a vessel, an inhabitant, and a note in the chord. The pavilion of ribs and mucus may be a nest, but the ribs are alive with marrowlike colonies, and the mucus contracts around the eggs without changing shape. The stair terraces along the wall are paved with flattened shells whose occupants extend fine tendrils into one another. What appears to be architecture may be posture practiced by a multitude.

Their sound passes through every body differently.

The thin shells hold the highest tones, keen and nearly sweet. The armored ring provides a low, jointed resonance. The larvae tremble at pitches too narrow for the water to carry, while the ceiling grazer roughens the whole with the pressure of its many throats. No creature listens with ears. They receive the chord through feet, shell rims, membranes, teeth. Their bodies are tuned by their neighbors. Remove one—if removal were possible—and the rest would no longer fit acoustically, spatially, perhaps anatomically.

This may be why they do not move.

Their stillness is not paralysis. It is cooperation perfected beyond motion. Each lifted feeler permits a shell lip beneath it; each open jaw accommodates the spine of prey; each unfallen droplet completes a lens through which a blind colony senses the basin. Motion would not merely disturb another creature. It would abolish the shape that makes the mover possible.

Seen one by one, they are an impossible abundance of unfinished species.

Seen together, they begin to resemble a single organism with no outer skin.

The walls serve as its skeleton, though the walls are still becoming stone. The basin is its eye or stomach. The armored ring is a sphincter around that clear organ. The ceiling grazer is a lung held at the top of an endless breath. Shells are its scales and resonating chambers. Larvae are thoughts of forms it might assume. The little clay rings and bone pavilion are dense knots of instinct, structures built inside the organism in the way another body might build cartilage or memory.

Its chord is not communication among inhabitants. It is their collective vital sign.

Yet calling the chamber one being is no more sufficient than calling it many. The mites have mouths of their own. The larvae possess private black eyes. Teeth meet prey with unmistakable intention. Each egg presses outward according to its own design. Individual appetites remain intact, innumerable and exact, but they are locked together so perfectly that desire has become anatomy.

The thing that lives there is therefore both a crowd and the condition of the crowd: a bright, compact common body composed entirely of thresholds.

It is always feeding and never fed. Always hatching and never born. Always constructing itself from creatures that are themselves constructing, breeding, hunting, sheltering, and becoming. Its oldest members have not aged. Its youngest have never begun. It has no dead, because nothing can withdraw far enough from the whole to leave a corpse.

If it possesses awareness, it cannot know sequence. It knows pressure, adjacency, brightness, and the unwavering chord. It knows every neighbor as a contour in its own flesh. It knows the plain ground above only as cool weight and resistance.

And perhaps, through the stone that has not finished hardening, it senses the enormous vacancy of the outer world: all that wasteful distance, all that falling water, all those bodies granted room to move apart.

It answers with the only act available to it.

It sustains its note.
