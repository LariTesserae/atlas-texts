At first the inhabitants seem divisible.

There are those that fly, those that crawl, those that drink from stone, those that wake only when water returns. There are hunters beneath the visible ledges, scavengers among the eggs, grazers stretched like cords from the pinnacles. Each has its own body and appetite. Yet the longer the cleft is watched, the less certain those divisions become. Life is packed so closely that one creature’s posture becomes another’s shelter, one animal’s waste becomes another’s pasture, and the death of anything begins a slow journey downward through many mouths.

The most numerous are the veilwings.

A veilwing at rest scarcely resembles a flying animal. It hangs head-down or sideways, its long body flattened to the angle of the rock, its two principal membranes wrapped around it in dark, overlapping folds. Smaller fins lie tucked along the ribs and tail. Among thousands of others it disappears into a surface that seems scaled rather than occupied. Only its claws remain distinct: pale, narrow hooks sunk precisely into the mountain’s fractures.

They are not graceful in the open-air sense. Their flight belongs to confinement. A veilwing can stop almost motionless between two blades of stone, twist its body upright, spill air from one membrane while filling the other, and slide through a gap barely wider than its skull. It climbs without circling and descends without diving. Entire columns move this way through the cleft, each animal borrowing turbulence from the one below it. Seen from the rim, the columns resemble strips of darkness being raised and lowered.

Their heads are blunt and mostly mouth. The eyes are small, reflective, and poorly suited to distance, but the lips and throat are fringed with sensitive vanes. When the high winds bring aeroplankton—ice-midges, drifting spores, transparent floaters, the seedlike resting forms of creatures from warmer altitudes—the veilwings open those vanes into wide funnels. Tens of thousands feed shoulder to shoulder. What one throat misses is caught by the next. The mountain interrupts the wind; the colony sieves it.

Their young hatch nearly naked, with sealed eyes and gripping feet already disproportionately large. They are passed inward, away from the coldest ledges, into crèches formed not from nests but from living adults. Several veilwings overlap their bodies around a clutch, changing places as the outermost animals cool. Egg cases are glued directly into cracks with a dark secretion that remains pliable in frost. No parent can consistently defend its own offspring in such density. Care spreads sideways through the roost until kinship becomes less important than proximity.

This does not make the colony gentle. Weak hatchlings vanish quickly. Eggs cool. Adults lose their grip. A veilwing that falls may strike five or six ledges before reaching the lower chambers, and at every impact something takes a portion.

The hookbacks usually arrive first.

They are low, broad creatures with six limbs and a shell of overlapping slate plates. Their rear four limbs cling and brace; the front pair remain free to feel through feathers, turn an egg, pry at a joint, or carry a flake of glass. Their faces are concealed beneath the forward edge of the armor except for clusters of short tactile bristles and a grinding mouth on the underside.

A hookback moves with the unhurried assurance of an animal tolerated everywhere. Veilwings lift their membranes to admit it. It passes over their bodies, plucking blood-pale mites from the soft folds around the throat, removing fungal crust from claws, and clipping away dead membrane. The service is useful, but usefulness does not explain all of the tolerance. Hookbacks also test eggs. They tap them, rotate them, and hold their armor against them to sense internal movement. A living egg is cleaned and left. A failed one is opened.

For this they use the mountain’s edges.

A hookback will spend a long time among fallen splinters, raising each between its gripping pads and drawing it across a plate of its own armor. The sound is a faint scrape, pause, scrape. It rejects most. The chosen shard may be only a thumb’s length, but thin enough to enter the seam of an egg case. After feeding, the hookback often carries it onward. Another may take it during a brief, bristling encounter. There is no evident gift, barter, or ownership. The edge simply continues through the colony until it breaks or falls.

Old shards can sometimes be recognized by the residue polished into their bases: egg oils, dried blood, the wax of parasites, mineral stain from many pools. Such a flake has passed through dozens of gripping pads. It is the nearest thing in the cleft to an heirloom, though none of its bearers knows its history.

Hookbacks produce their own young in damp chambers under the communal crust. The newborns are soft and nearly white. For several days they cannot survive exposed air, so adults bury them beneath warm castings, shed skin, and rotted feathers. Their first food is the crust around them. Their first armor is partly mineral: they swallow powdered volcanic glass, and fine black grains harden through the growing plates.

Above the densest roosts, where fog touches bare rock, live the ropelike grazers.

A single grazer might be mistaken for a pale root. Its anchored end is thick and muscular, wedged so deeply into a crack that removing it would tear the animal apart. From that holdfast extends a narrow body ringed with soft gripping ridges. At the far end is a rasping disk surrounded by sensory threads. It reaches across the rock, abrading lichen, salt bloom, bacterial film, and the faint mineral residues left by drying fog.

Because the richest films form on exposed faces, grazers must feed while suspended over emptiness. They bridge gaps, knot themselves loosely around pinnacles, or lie across one another in braided masses. A cluster may contain hundreds, anchored in several separate fissures but so entangled that no body can be followed from end to end.

They are exquisitely sensitive to vibration. At the first unfamiliar tremor, their feeding ends snap backward. The contraction spreads from one to the next until an entire wall recoils. For an instant, the rock appears to draw breath.

Broken grazers do not always die. The anchored half seals itself and grows another feeding end. The severed portion falls, contracts blindly, and may survive if it reaches a wet crack before freezing. In this way the animal’s identity is uncertain even to the observer: some ancient colonies may be many individuals, others one individual divided repeatedly by falling stone.

Smaller life occupies every creature.

Veilwing membranes carry translucent lice that drink condensed moisture from the channels between blood vessels. Hookback joints harbor red threadworms, harvested but never eliminated. The grazers’ digestive tracts contain dark microbes that can dissolve the charcoal lichen’s mineral shell. During chorus hours, clouds of tiny silver-bodied animals rise from the organic crust and cling to vibrating surfaces, feeding on whatever the sound shakes loose.

The flowering stalks, when they appear, join this intimacy. They grow downward into warmth, their cups hanging among fur, armor, and folded wings. Their tissues are almost clear. Veins of faint amber fluid can be seen moving within them. They flower briefly, releasing a smell like wet stone and bruised fruit. Grazers strip the outer skin. Hookbacks bite through the stems for moisture. Veilwing young swallow the soft cups whole. Almost none survives long enough to wither.

Nevertheless, the plants return. Their dust-fine reproductive bodies lodge in feathers and are carried deep into the roost, where heat wakes them. What appears to be predation is also dispersal.

The water pockets support another order of life, one that has little awareness of the vast colony around it.

When dry, their inhabitants are black or amber knots embedded along the retreating edge of damp. A knot may remain unchanged through months of frost. Fog arrives; condensation threads down the wall; the pocket fills. Within minutes the knots soften. They unfurl into eyeless swimmers with bodies like clear oil, able to narrow into cracks, spread into films, or knot themselves around prey.

Their world lasts an afternoon.

They graze on microbial bloom, divide, fuse, lay hard cysts, and devour one another with extraordinary speed. Some grow jaws only after feeding; others begin as predators and lose their mouths when reproduction starts. Generations pass between the first drop and the last. As the pool contracts, the survivors crowd into the deepest line of moisture and harden again. Their dormant forms contain not only the animal but fragments of swallowed animals, symbionts, eggs, and unfinished young. Each knot is less a sleeper than a sealed community.

Veilwings drink from these pockets, but cannot drain them all. The openings are deceptive. A hand-wide black surface may conceal a flooded fissure descending far into the mountain. Something larger occasionally stirs below the swimmers. It never reaches the light.

The deepest inhabitants are known mainly by the effects of their passage.

Roosting animals shift without waking. A pressure travels along the walls. Hookbacks flatten themselves. Then a head emerges from a fissure that appeared too narrow to contain it.

The head is long, blind, and covered in dense sensory filaments. Droplets cling to the filaments, making a pale constellation in the dark. Its mouth opens sideways and without visible hinge. It does not strike. It rises until its lips surround a portion of crowded ledge, waits while the veilwings compress and struggle, then closes over several at once.

The rest of the animal remains below.

Shed fragments suggest a body soft enough to pass through the mountain’s internal cracks but reinforced at intervals by rings of cartilage or thin bone. Whether there is one species or several is unclear. Some heads are pale; others nearly black. Some bear scars. One has been seen with a hookback living among its sensory fur, feeding at the corners of its mouth.

The knocking comes from these depths. It is too regular to be falling stone and too slow to be the beat of wings: two or three soft impacts, a long pause, then another. It may be communication. It may be the sound of the animals bracing their bodies against opposite walls as they move. During the colony-wide silences, knocks from different fissures sometimes answer one another.

The veilwings hear them before anything else does.

Their silences begin at the highest occupied ledges and descend. Each animal folds in response to the stillness above it, so the hush travels visibly, a closing of membranes, a withdrawal of throat-fans, an extinguishing of reflected eyes. The hookbacks stop because the veilwings stop. The grazers retract because the hookbacks cease scraping. Even the water-pocket swimmers alter their motion when vibration leaves the stone.

For several breaths, the many inhabitants become one listening body.

Then a veilwing calls.

The first note may come from anywhere: an elder deep in the warm roost, a cold animal near the rim, a juvenile whose throat-fan has only just hardened. Others answer not in unison but by contagion. The sound thickens. It passes around pinnacles, enters fissures, returns distorted, and sets the thin rock plates ringing. Hookbacks lift their armor against the vibration. Grazers extend. Dormant knots tremble in their cracks. Condensation breaks free and falls.

No individual seems to command the chorus, and no single purpose explains it. It may confuse the deep hunters. It may help the veilwings locate one another in absolute dark. It may shake aeroplankton from folds of air or announce the colony to migrants crossing above the cloud. Perhaps it is simply what overcrowded throats do after silence has pressed through them.

By then, distinctions among the inhabitants have become difficult to preserve.

The veilwings bring food down from the sky. Their droppings and bodies feed the crust. The crust nourishes plants, hookback young, fungi, and minute grazers. Hookbacks keep the roost from drowning in its own dead. Ropelike animals turn bare stone film into flesh. Pools receive what leaks downward and return brief swarms upward whenever wings drink from them. The deep hunters consume the crowded animals and, in time, are themselves consumed by whatever lives farther within the mountain—or by hookbacks, microbes, and roots when their bodies lodge in a fissure.

Nothing is merely beneath something else. The lower life feeds on the upper, but warmth also rises. The smallest swimmers sleep under the largest predators. The plants lean down instead of up. Young veilwings crawl from dark crèches toward the sky, then return carrying the sky’s invisible harvest in their throats.

Even the mountain participates. It provides no shelter separate from danger: every gripping crack is also a future fracture, every pool a possible depth, every blade both perch and knife. The inhabitants have not made the cleft habitable. They have made themselves into shapes that its violence can hold.

What lives there, finally, is not quite a collection of species and not quite a single colony.

It is a vertical hunger fastened inside new stone.

It drinks weather before weather can become rain. It catches life too small to see from winds that would otherwise cross the summit empty. It turns fog, dust, fallen bodies, mineral stain, and darkness into membrane and shell and blind flesh. It has no center. Its memory resides in repeated flight paths, inherited gripping reflexes, old glass passed from claw to claw, cysts waiting at vanished waterlines, and the communal pause that descends whenever something knocks below.

From outside there is only black rock and a seam in the summit.

Inside, the seam breathes.
