What lives there is, at first, difficult to divide into creatures.

The moss seems to breathe when the water rises beneath it. Willow islands migrate by inches. Root walls drink, loosen, collapse, and grow again. A dam may appear to be a ridge of mud until one notices the fresh tooth-marks, the paths polished across its crown, and the submerged entrance breathing out a thread of bubbles. Even the stone participates slowly, giving off lime, accepting spores, opening seams to roots and pale underground animals.

The most consequential residents are the beavers, though they are seldom visible.

They are broad-bodied, dark-furred, and almost noiseless in the black water. Cold beads on their guard hairs and never reaches the dense undercoat. Their forepaws are small and capable, turning stems, pressing mud, feeling for leaks; their hind feet drive them through drowned branches with surprising delicacy. In the water they seem sleek and deliberate. On land they become low, burdened laborers, dragging willow through sedge or sitting upright to gnaw, orange teeth working beneath half-closed eyes.

They inherit structures older than themselves. A young pair may occupy a lodge whose buried core was assembled by generations they never knew. They repair one side, open another entrance, raise the roof, and pack fresh mud where winter air leaks through. Their kits learn the city as a succession of familiar passages: the warm chamber above water, the plunge through the hidden door, the safe canal under alder shade, the feeding shelf, the winter cache, the breach where the current is dangerous after rain.

No beaver understands the whole basin. Each knows a district by pressure, scent, depth, and use. One family maintains a chain of shallow ponds under a stone vault. Another controls a steep set of dams where descending water must be slowed repeatedly. Farther out, an old matriarch inhabits a lodge fused with living willow; the cut branches laid on its roof years ago have rooted, so her house now grows leaves and must be pruned like a tree.

Their work is not orderly in the way of plans. It is obsessive and responsive. Running water summons them. A new trickle through a dam is found in darkness and stopped with whatever lies near: mud carried against the chest, a stone gripped between the paws, fern root, peeled branch, sodden leaves. By dawn the pond has risen. A vole tunnel floods. A patch of sedge becomes an island. Fish enter a grove where yesterday wrens fed on the ground. The repair has redrawn an entire neighborhood.

Among the dam walls live the water voles.

They are compact, blunt-faced animals with bright black eyes and fur the color of wet peat. They cut green stems into manageable lengths and sit holding them in their forepaws, eating quickly, always near an opening. Their burrows lace the banks at several heights because no waterline can be trusted. Lower doors serve in dry weather; upper chambers become refuges when rain joins the ponds.

Whole banks are hollow with their domestic spaces: sleeping nests of shredded grass, food chambers, bolt holes, drowned passages, and old rooms packed with silt. Their roads are tunnels through vegetation rather than open tracks. From above, a vole’s passage is betrayed only by a moving seam in the sedge. Beneath the roots, however, its routes intersect those of shrews, frogs, beetles, and the probing noses of predators.

The voles are builders and saboteurs at once. Their tunnels drain small pools, weaken embankments, and sometimes begin the failure of a beaver dam. Yet their cuttings catch mud, their abandoned chambers shelter insects, and their paths become channels for overflow. The city uses their damage as readily as their labor.

Otters move through all districts without belonging wholly to any.

They arrive as long, fluid disturbances. A head appears in a pond, round-eared and whiskered, then sinks without a splash. Moments later an otter surfaces beyond the next dam with a fish twisting in its jaws. It eats on a favored shelf, leaving scales, bones, and a musky declaration that persists after the animal has gone.

Families travel in loose processions through the canals. The young wrestle on muddy slopes and slide down them until the bank shines. They investigate every pipe of water, every submerged hollow, every new lake opened by collapse. Their paths cross territorial boundaries that matter urgently to smaller residents. They enter under roots where foxes cannot follow and climb over dams the beavers must later repair.

An old otter route may survive longer than an individual dam. Where generations haul themselves from pond to pond, the soil becomes a trough, then a spillway. Rain deepens it. Water adopts it. What began as an animal’s convenience becomes a stream.

In the deepest pools, pike hold themselves almost motionless.

They are old-looking fish, green and barred, their long bodies obscured among drowned stems. Only the fins move, making corrections too slight to disturb the duckweed. Minnows pass above them. Frogs kick through the margins. A vole swims with its muzzle raised, unaware of the shape below.

The pike wait because the basin brings life to them. Floodwater delivers fry from one pond to another; failing dams briefly open hunting corridors; newly drowned ground fills with insects and small fish. Some pike become prisoners when water falls, ruling a single black pool until flood reconnects it to the city. In the pale northern lake, younger fish move among raw stones where no weed yet hides them. There they are restless and exposed, the first large hunters in unfinished water.

Smaller fish occupy the moving streets: minnows, sticklebacks, loaches pressed beneath stones. They know the basin through currents. A leaking dam is a mouth of oxygen. A cold spring is refuge in warm weather and danger in winter. An otter’s passage empties a channel; after it leaves, the fish return one by one from roots and shadows.

Frogs live wherever water holds long enough, though the cold makes them deliberate. Their skin carries the colors of mud, moss, and drowned leaves. They spend much of the year concealed under banks or among submerged roots, emerging into a brief urgency when the softer season arrives. Then males call from separated pools, each voice contained by wet vegetation. Spawn appears in clear shallows, black points suspended in jelly. Many pools fail; many tadpoles become food. Enough survive to fill the evening margins with small, sudden leaps.

Newts haunt colder hollows where the water is too still for fish. They pass beneath liverwort ledges with crested backs and delicate feet, living dragons no longer than a hand. Pale larvae shelter in flooded hoofprints. Salamanders and toads occupy the rubble above, entering cracks that breathe winter air.

In the moss itself resides a finer population.

Springtails gather like ash on the surfaces of pools and scatter when struck by drops. Mites travel through forests of moss stems. Snails rasp films from limestone, leaving silver script that ends beneath leaves or inside crevices. Caddisfly larvae armor themselves with grains of stone and fragments of reed; mayfly nymphs flatten against the current; beetles carry captured air below the surface in bright, trembling beads. Leeches extend from black mud. Worms work the drowned margins until fish or birds find them.

Mosquitoes breed in the smallest still waters, but the basin gives them enemies at every stage. Larvae are taken by beetles and minnows. Adults rise into the hunting circuits of swallows. Spiders suspend webs beneath the stone vaults, where mist pearls each strand and struggling midges make the whole construction tremble.

The birds occupy vertical layers.

Wrens live near the bottom, among roots, broken slabs, and leaning trunks. They are tiny, brown, and forceful, carrying voices larger than their bodies. Their nests are mossy domes wedged into almost any cavity: a split stump, a lodge wall, a dry pocket in a dam, the recess behind a curtain of roots. Some build several and abandon most. Others take over old nests, lining them again with feathers.

Swallows claim the sheltered stone. They arrive with the compressed green season and repair mud cups under the surviving roof. Each beakful is gathered from a pond edge, carried upward, and pressed into place. Colonies accumulate in the driest vaults, nests touching like clustered growths on the rock. Their flight stitches open water to ceiling. They drink on the wing and skim insects from channels too narrow for larger birds.

In willow thickets, warblers and finches bind grasses to flexible forks. Their nests hang over water, protected from foxes but vulnerable to flood and the casual cutting of beavers. A branch may be alive with young birds one morning and floating in a dam by night. The parent birds scold the dark water, then begin again elsewhere.

Ducks conceal nests on sedge hummocks and lead their young through lanes they have used for years. Moorhens step across floating leaves on long toes. Kingfishers, where the banks are firm enough, cut narrow tunnels into them and sit above the clearer channels like pieces of blue mineral.

Herons possess the high dead trees.

Their colonies stand above the basin on drowned trunks bleached silver. The nests are rough platforms, renewed until they become heavy masses of sticks. The birds rise from them with slow, ancient wingbeats. On the ground they hunt alone, motionless at pond edges, neck folded, eyes fixed on a world of minute movement. Their strike is sudden enough to seem like a break in time. Afterward the stillness closes again.

Woodpeckers inhabit the same drowned groves at a different depth. Beetles enter the dead timber first; woodpeckers follow, opening cavities that later pass to owls, ducks, bats, and swarms of insects. Nothing keeps its original tenant.

Owls hold the dim stone margins. They roost behind ivy and root curtains, their mottled feathers resembling damp bark. At dusk they travel along the boundary between marsh and rubble, hearing voles through vegetation and frogs beneath leaves. Their flight makes no announcement. A channel may continue trembling after one has passed, but the owl itself is already elsewhere.

Foxes prefer the slopes that stay above flood.

They are narrow, red, and often mud-dark to the belly. Their dens enter the seams under fallen slabs, where limestone roofs chambers no animal could dig from soil alone. Some entrances are newly scraped and smell sharply of earth; others are old, fringed by nettles nourished by generations of remains.

A fox travels the raised works as though the beavers built them for it. Dam crowns, fallen trunks, and rubble shelves form a dry patrol route through otherwise impassable marsh. It pauses at every junction to read scent. It hears voles chewing within banks and young birds shifting in low nests. It takes eggs, frogs, fish stranded by falling water, and occasionally a careless duck. Its cubs first encounter the city as a confusion of reeds taller than themselves, unstable logs, and forbidden black pools.

Badgers live deeper and more stubbornly. Their tunnels follow natural fractures between blocks, enlarging spaces begun by water and frost. They carry bedding underground and drag it out again when it sours. Their digging brings pale limestone chips to the surface, where snails gather and seedlings take root. A sett may have doors opening into separate climates: one beneath cool fern, another on a dry rubble face, a third hidden behind willow.

Deer come down from the upper forest, though they never seem entirely at ease below. They pick their way along firm shelves and enter the water to feed on soft growth. Their hooves punch deep prints that fill at once. These prints become drinking places for insects, nurseries for larvae, and points where the next rain begins to cut a path. Antlers scrape bark from leaning trees. Dropped hair catches on twigs, carrying scent through the thickets.

There are bats in the unfallen roof.

They sleep in narrow limestone joints above the wet groves, clustered where the stone keeps a stable cold. On summer evenings they spill into the open basin and turn rapidly over the ponds. Each animal follows a private architecture made of returning sound: the faces of cliffs, the gaps between willow crowns, the moving skin of water, the wings of insects. To a bat the city is not dark. It is crowded with edges.

And beneath all of these live the residents that cannot be seen as individuals at ordinary scale.

Fungi travel through the drowned wood, softening trunks from within. Their threads enter bark, mud, fallen leaves, lodge walls, and living roots. Pale caps rise after rain from beams no beaver remembers placing. Slime molds cross wet logs in branching fans and vanish into spores. Bacteria work in oxygenless silt, breathing what other organisms cannot, turning buried vegetation into black gases that wobble upward beneath pond ice or burst softly among duckweed.

The plants are not scenery around the settlement. They are its slowest inhabitants and much of its substance.

Willows survive being cut by multiplying. A branch dragged into mud becomes a row of trees. Alders feed the wet soil through their roots. Birch colonizes fresh rubble, thin and bright against the collapse scars. Mountain ash takes hold on higher blocks, where birds distribute its seeds in red-stained droppings. Sedges bind mud that would otherwise move. Moss controls the pace of droplets. Duckweed closes abandoned water with a living lid.

Individual plants are eaten, drowned, buried, or broken, but their manner of growth anticipates disturbance. They inhabit the beavers’ work even as they supply it. The city’s walls sprout. Its roofs flower. Its roads close behind travelers.

In the newest northern chamber, the inhabitants arrive without ceremony.

Spores are already in the rain. Midges find the pale temporary lake. A willow switch catches between two rocks, roots at the buried end, and opens leaves. Beetles fly in on warm afternoons. Small fish enter during a brief flood and remain when the connection narrows. A fox crosses the dust and leaves the first clear trail. Wrens investigate snapped root masses. Beavers come after dark, circling the outflow, testing its voice with their whiskers.

They do not see a ruin. They find gradients: water too fast, water deep enough, stone that shelters, mud that can be moved, branches that can root, openings that can be occupied.

Soon the first low dam will hold back silt. The silt will grow sedge. Voles will enter the sedge and pierce the bank. An otter will widen the breach. The outflow will shift against a slab. Moss will soften the old channel. A bird will nest in the willow that began as building material.

Thus the principal inhabitant of the basin is not the beaver, the willow, the water, or the moss, though at different moments each appears to rule it.

It is the entanglement among them: a many-bodied creature with ponds for organs, channels for veins, dams for temporary bones, and the entire crowded population for its restless flesh. It has no mind that can know itself. It remembers through paths, inherited lodges, rooted cuttings, old scent places, buried dams, and habits taught by survival. It feeds on rain, leaf-fall, limestone, migration, birth, and collapse.

When one chamber of it is destroyed, the destruction becomes an entrance. When one structure fails, the failure becomes a route. Its citizens eat their city, build with what they have eaten, live inside the result, and leave it to strangers.

Nothing there merely occupies a home.

Everything is home for something else.
