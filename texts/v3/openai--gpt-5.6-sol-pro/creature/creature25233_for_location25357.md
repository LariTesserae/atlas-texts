At first it seems that nothing lives there.

There are no bodies moving against the vegetation, no nests in the hanging vats, no pale swimmers beneath the films of fronds. Patient watching reveals no tracks, excrement, husks, eggs, or wounds. The pools contain no cells that are not part of their lining. Even the mist is sterile except for threads so fine they might be mistaken for condensation—and those threads remain attached at one end to the ceiling.

Then it becomes difficult to say where life is absent.

The plain itself is alive, though calling it a plant gives it too definite a shape. It has no central stem, no boundary that could be walked around, no individual organs that remain individual for long. Its tissues divide and rejoin. A root crossing a reservoir may become the reservoir wall; the wall may thin into a leaf; the leaf may continue upward as a tensile cable and disappear into the roof. Structures that seem separate at a distance prove, on contact, to share fluids, electrical gradients, and one uninterrupted skin.

Perhaps it is a colony.

In that case, its members are the pressure chambers, the black laminae, the mineral-fixing nodules, the sealed fruits that never open. Each possesses a narrow autonomy. A chamber regulates its own salinity. A root membrane rejects one isotope and admits another. A leaf plate holds itself precisely between absorption and emission. Severed from the whole, any such organ might continue functioning for centuries, preserving its internal balance until the cut edge quietly grew closed.

But there are no seams between members. No competition occurs where their tissues meet. They do not crowd, shade, poison, or consume one another. Information passes through them without regard for local borders. A chemical correction begun in one marsh may be completed on the far side of an inland sea. What appears to be a forest may therefore be more like a lobe; what appears to be a continent, one slow organ.

Perhaps there is only one inhabitant.

Its body may envelop the world. The dark roof would be its upper surface folded inward; the marsh its vascular interior; the apparent horizon merely the distance over which one portion of its flesh can be seen from another. Its roots do not occupy soil. They make soil where they require resistance, precipitating minerals around themselves and dissolving them again when the stress has passed. Its waters are not lakes but exposed circulatory spaces. The warm mist is something between breath and blood plasma.

Yet it does not breathe in cycles. It does not sleep. It has no heartbeat.

Its smallest actions oppose one another so perfectly that the whole remains still. Where one membrane contracts, another relaxes. Where sugars are assembled, equal structures are taken apart. Charges travel, but their departure is balanced by their return. The organism is not frozen; it is correcting itself everywhere, continuously, with infinite delicacy.

A disturbance would reveal it more clearly. A puncture would alter pressure across thousands of kilometers. Conductive veins would carry the fact outward as a faint change in potential. Reservoirs would constrict. Mineral threads would reroute the imbalance. Pale membranes would advance over the wound, not hurriedly, but all at once in the distributed manner of a body that has no meaningful distance within itself.

Nothing has ever punctured it.

Its sensory organs therefore resemble unused possibilities. Along the undersides of the roof are fields of translucent cells capable of distinguishing wavelengths that never vary. The sides of the hollow columns bear tension hairs delicate enough to register a footstep, though no foot has descended. Deep in the floating mats lie pressure cups that could locate a swimming body from the displacement of a single drop. Every cup reports the same answer forever: no intrusion.

It has no eyes, but much of it could see.

It has no ears, but every taut vein could hear.

It has no mouth. Consumption occurs across surfaces too broad to recognize as feeding. Dissolved metals enter through one district; heat is captured in another; water is split in buried chambers; gases pass through pores in the roof. Nothing is hunted or swallowed. The distinction between organism and environment has nearly vanished because the organism has incorporated every part of the environment it can use.

Whether it thinks is harder to determine.

There is no brain hidden beneath the plain. No privileged knot receives all signals. Instead, countless branching potentials lie poised along conductive strands. They form patterns of readiness: routes by which news could travel, responses that could recruit other responses, vast reflexes held just below completion. The green lines in the ceiling may be those reflexes made visible—not thoughts passing through the organism, but the permanent anatomy of thought.

If it has a mind, that mind has never encountered surprise.

It knows warmth because warmth is constant. It knows pressure as a perfect field of answering pressures. It knows water not as an object but as the continuity within itself. There is no moving creature for it to distinguish from background, no day from which to infer night, no injury from which to form a memory of wholeness. It may possess immense capacities for sensation without ever having had an experience.

Or its experience may be equilibrium itself.

The unfallen droplet, the held charge, the membrane balanced between two chemical states—these may not be signs of unconsciousness. They may be the form its awareness takes: not a succession of moments but one total condition, sensed everywhere at once. What a smaller life would call an instant may be its entire interior duration. It does not remember being young. It is youth maintained. It does not anticipate change. Every preparation for change has become a permanent organ.

Reproduction has likewise ceased to be a separate act. There are fruiting bodies, spores, buds, and embryonic folds throughout the plain, but none complete themselves. They are not sterile. Each is viable, nourished, held at the exact threshold of release. Perhaps the inhabitant once spread from seed. Perhaps the roofed world is the inside of that seed, and what seems to be mature vegetation is still an embryo preparing to break a shell that has never opened.

Perhaps the immense plain is only a meristem: one growth-tip paused before emergence.

In that case, the towers are not factories but unborn structures. The suspended vats are reserves for a future season. The fixed green lightning overhead is energy stored for a first dawn. Every needle-shoot stopped at the same height is waiting for a signal that has not come. The whole planet-sized body may be capable of sudden transformation—roof dividing, waters descending, pressure organs opening in sequence—yet no part can begin until some external condition changes.

No external condition does.

So the inhabitant remains difficult to count and impossible to meet. Up close it is a community of specialized tissues. Farther away it is an ecosystem with only one lineage. Farther still it is an organ without a visible body. At the greatest scale it may be less an organism than a living state into which matter has settled: water, carbon, metals, and heat arranged so perfectly that they cannot stop being alive and cannot proceed into anything else.

It has no face. The nearest thing to one might be the entire underside of the canopy, with its fixed veins and millions of receptive pores. It has no name for itself. Naming would require an alternative.

It fills every available place and leaves none from which it could be observed.

Alone beneath its lightless roof, it maintains the warm waters, renews its unscarred skin, and listens with organs that have never received a sound. It is waiting, perhaps. It is sleeping, perhaps. Perhaps those are merely animal words for a life whose only act is to remain exactly, immeasurably alive.
