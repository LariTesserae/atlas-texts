The first inhabitants one notices are usually not the living ones.

They are the **registrants**: objects that have persuaded the pavement they belong.

A registrant may be a shard of tower, a coil of wire, a sail-creature’s egg, or a low shelter built around the fused remains of something older. What distinguishes it from debris is invisible. The ground has entered it into some surviving inventory and no longer attempts to dismantle it. Repair tides divide around it. Sensor pins rise to test it, receive an acceptable answer, and fold away.

Some registrants have remained in place for millions of years. Others lose their status abruptly and are taken apart in a single black season.

Among them live the beings most nearly deserving to be called people.

They call themselves by sequences of pressure transmitted through the ground. The shortest translation is **the Interval**, though the name contains other meanings: a pause between instructions, a tolerated space, a distance measured in waiting.

An Interval is difficult to distinguish from the pavement when at rest. Its body is broad, low, and radially uneven, seldom more than a hand’s breadth above the surface. Six to twelve jointed limbs extend from beneath an armored mantle of pale plates. No two have the same number. Limbs are added, merged, or abandoned throughout life. The oldest individuals may have become almost circular, fringed with hundreds of fine contact needles; the young are narrow and quick, running on six black points.

Their armor is not grown in the usual sense. It is gathered.

An Interval begins with a soft internal knot sealed inside a pearl-colored capsule. Around that core it accumulates ceramic flakes, conductive dust, wire-thicket fibers, discarded sensor hairs, and fragments surrendered by older bodies. Catalytic secretions fit these materials together. Over centuries, the mantle becomes a record of where the individual has traveled. One may carry amber glass from a bright precinct, violet alloy from a charge province, a row of obsolete optical ports found beside a vanished ridge. Age makes them larger but also flatter, their histories spread over the ground like mosaics.

Inside the hard body, life is warmer and more vulnerable. Coiled vessels contain salts, oils, metallic gels, and a minute quantity of water.

That water is their most guarded possession.

They do not drink. There is nothing to drink. They inherit water in sealed droplets from their parents, reclaim it from every internal reaction, and lose almost none except in injury or reproduction. When an Interval’s mantle is cracked deeply enough for moisture to escape, the pavement responds at once. Catalytic pores open beneath the wound. The spilled water flashes into separated gases and disappears below.

For this reason they fear neither predators nor heights—there are few of either—as much as they fear being opened.

They feed on differences. A feeding Interval braces limbs across two plates of unequal temperature and lets the gradient pass through layered organs beneath its mantle. It grazes mineral dust, scrapes films from warm seams, and inserts hairlike electrodes into inactive conduits. Some follow thermal migrations for generations. Others settle where buried machinery leaks a steady trickle of charge and become nearly immobile.

Their settlements look like fields of slightly raised paving stones.

There are no walls unless an old wall is useful, and no roofs except plates laid almost flush against the ground. Height attracts dust, thermal stress, and inspection. A village is therefore built downward into tolerated cracks and abandoned access pits. Its visible parts are rings of registrants, cultivated wire fans, rows of polished fragments, and shallow basins where messages can be tapped into resonant ceramic.

Each settlement depends upon a **warrant**.

A warrant is part password, part genealogy, part ecological disguise. It may be encoded in the spacing of a village’s structures, in a repeating heat signature, or in an antique signal continuously passed from one resident to another. So long as the warrant remains accepted, local systems treat the settlement as a component awaiting service, or perhaps as service personnel themselves. Seams carry power into it. Repair tides spare it. Buried doors may open when approached.

No one knows what the warrants originally authorized.

Some appear to classify an entire people as ventilation equipment. Others identify them as a temporary diagnostic fault. One ancient settlement survives because the pavement believes its inhabitants are a set of replacement fasteners delayed in transit.

The Interval do not find this humiliating. To be misidentified successfully is one of the highest forms of wisdom.

Their speech passes through their feet.

A single tap can travel far through the plates, but the pavement is full of pulses, settling murmurs, diagnostics, and deep mechanical revisions. Interval language is woven around those noises. A sentence may begin before a thermal front arrives and conclude when the expanded plate answers hours later. Meaning depends on what the ground does between the speaker’s signals.

Young individuals speak rapidly and redundantly. Elders may communicate with one precise touch followed by days of silence.

Their histories are performed across landscapes. A procession stations itself along a seam, each person several kilometers from the next. The first strikes a pattern into the ground. Each listener receives it altered by distance, cracks, buried machinery, and intervening districts, then corrects it according to local memory before passing it onward. A history crossing the whole inhabited plain may take centuries to recite. It never arrives unchanged, but change is considered part of the account: the world has spoken inside it.

They possess no map of the Last Pavement. Instead, they maintain lists of permissions.

Here one may cross only during cooling.

Here one must carry no iron.

Here the ground draws moving things west.

Here a traveler should imitate a maintenance pulse every eighty-three steps.

Here lies a circular brightness in which sleepers wake with parts of their memory duplicated.

Here, for reasons unknown, the pavement recognizes any object with five equal projections as alive.

These instructions are learned bodily. Travelers reshape themselves for a route, adding insulating feet, mirrored dorsal plates, resonant cavities, or sacrificial strips that repair tides may remove. The greatest travelers are almost unrecognizable when they return. Their communities greet them by rhythm rather than appearance.

Reproduction is rare and communal. Several adults open protected chambers in their mantles and contribute microscopic portions of water, catalysts, memory polymers, and living tissue. The resulting pearl is placed in a warm seam beside a seedlike capacitor. For years it appears inert. Then it begins answering diagnostic pulses.

A child is considered born not when it first moves, but when the pavement first mistakes its movement for something permissible.

Many never receive that permission. Repair grains surround them, patiently testing the capsule. Families may spend decades tapping counter-instructions into the ground, adjusting the infant’s electrical signature, or moving it fractions of a meter between jurisdictions. If the repair tide accepts the child, it parts around the capsule. If not, the pearl is disassembled, its water drawn below and its useful elements returned without malice.

Grief among the Interval is consequently quiet and technical. The bereaved may spend centuries searching for the procedural error.

They are not alone.

Wire thickets are tended, although it is uncertain whether they were domesticated or whether the Interval adapted themselves to serve the thickets. The two exchange charge, carbon fibers, and warning signals. In some provinces, old thickets appear to recognize particular families, unfolding when their footsteps approach.

Six-legged gleaners live at the edges of settlements. They are not pets. They steal conductive dust and sometimes carry off newly molted armor, but their tapping calls reveal approaching repair tides. Interval children follow them to discover dormant sensor fields. A gleaner that remains near a village for more than a few centuries may be fitted with a tiny registrant tag, granting it limited protection from the ground.

The sail-creatures are regarded as creatures of terrible speed. They cross in a year distances that an Interval caravan might require generations to traverse. When one descends to lay eggs, settlements gather beneath it and question it through reflected light. Whether the sail-creature understands is unknown. Sometimes, years later, it changes course in answer.

Below the pavement live the **Hollow Ones**.

No complete Hollow One has been seen in open light. Evidence suggests immense, soft-bodied animals armored with scavenged insulation, their limbs branching to occupy ducts and evacuated chambers. They eat obsolete machinery, dead computation, and perhaps one another. Their passage alters gravity by minute amounts. An open hatch polished on the inner side is said to be a feeding mark.

The Interval disagree over whether the Hollow Ones are animals, damaged service systems, or ancestors who retreated beneath the plates and grew to fit the dark.

Very old individuals sometimes go to meet them.

An elder who has become too broad to migrate may settle over an access seam and begin tapping downward. The exchange can continue for years. Then the hatch opens by a finger’s width. Warm, mineral-scented air emerges. The elder dismantles its own limbs, distributes its water among the settlement, and lowers its dry mantle into the gap.

Nothing returns, though later tremors occasionally reproduce the elder’s name.

There are also those who cease to be individuals without dying. These are called **accords**.

When several elders remain connected to the same conductive seam for long enough, their nervous systems begin using the pavement as shared tissue. Memories leak between them. A thought started in one body finishes in another. At first they regard this as intimacy; later, as age advances, they can no longer say which member remembers which journey.

An accord may spread beneath an entire settlement. Its visible bodies sit motionless at crossings, mistaken for worn fixtures. It regulates heat, warns travelers, mediates warrant disputes, and sings through resonant plates at night. Some accords outlive every original member by continually adding younger participants. Others extend too far, enter incompatible districts, and break into contradictory selves.

The oldest known accord occupies a line of transit ridges stretching beyond any completed journey. It answers questions, but slowly. A petitioner taps at one end and may receive a reply several generations later from a body thousands of kilometers away.

Its answers are not always relevant to the question asked.

The Interval believe that many apparent features of the landscape may be inhabitants of this kind: intelligences dispersed until they resemble local laws. A cool slope might be a sleeping mind. A dustless line might be a creature maintaining its boundary. A repair tide’s century-long argument might be courtship, warfare, or thought.

They do not assume the Last Pavement itself is conscious. Consciousness, to them, is too small and definite a claim.

They say only that the ground notices, remembers unevenly, and can sometimes be persuaded.

So they live close against it, armored and careful, carrying their inheritance of water in darkness. They migrate with warmth, cultivate acceptable errors, and compose messages that require landscapes to speak. Their greatest buildings are permissions. Their oldest citizens have become weather. Their dead are dismantled, incorporated, or taken below.

From a distance their settlements vanish into the plain.

Up close, one sees that certain plates are breathing.
