At first, whoever lives there appears not to be present.

The galleries are empty. No silhouettes cross the blue seams. No faces turn from the high apertures. The flooded streets carry only the broad rasping grazers, the ribbons in the runoff, and the occasional soft collapse of a thousand fliers leaving a bridge. If there are citizens, they seem to have withdrawn behind the walls.

Then a wall withdraws.

A section of black ribbing loosens from beside a doorway and unfolds into several narrow limbs. What seemed to be condensation running down masonry changes direction, climbing over a shoulder. A hand—or the structure serving as one—remains pressed to the stone while the rest of the body angles outward, listening.

The inhabitants are tall only when they choose to stand. Most of the time they keep close to the surfaces, distributed across four or six points of contact, their weight spread through long articulated limbs. Their proportions are difficult to understand because no part is held consistently above another. In a corridor, the same individual may move along the floor, pass one limb to the wall, rotate without pausing, and continue across the ceiling. Up and down are practical conditions, not bodily certainties.

They are nearly black when dry, though almost none are ever dry. Under condensation their outer surfaces become reflective, each body outlined in crawling threads of water. The skin is not smooth. It is divided into overlapping mineral plates thin enough to flex and pale at their growing edges. Between the plates lies a dense, cold tissue that contracts slowly and with great strength. The plates contain metal salts, shell fragments, and grains harvested from the city. Older individuals carry strata from many districts. A limb may be dark basalt near the joint, translucent around an ancient repair, and silvered at the extremity where years of contact with conductive walls have altered it.

There is no obvious head.

Several regions of the body can attend, speak, feed, or manipulate, and none possesses permanent authority over the others. Along the inner limbs are fields of pressure-sensitive cilia. The torso contains paired resonant chambers, deep cavities crossed by stiffened membranes. Smaller listening organs open and close between the plates like gills. When an inhabitant presses against a wall, these chambers couple to the structure. Its whole body becomes an ear.

Sight survives, but as a sealed and specialized sense. Beneath protective folds are clusters of small reflective organs capable of distinguishing the faint blue structural seams and little else. They are uncovered rarely, often only during close work in isolated chambers. Exposure to brightness causes injury, disorientation, and a flood of involuntary metabolic heat. To look directly is intimate, hazardous, and somewhat archaic.

They recognize one another by resonance.

Each body has a chord: not a name in the spoken sense, but a shifting set of frequencies created by the dimensions of its chest cavities, the mineral content of its plates, healed fractures, current temperature, carried water, and the organisms living upon it. A young inhabitant sounds clear and narrow. Age adds undertones. Travel changes timbre. Grief, illness, pregnancy, recent immersion, and structural work can all be heard in the body before they are otherwise known.

When two meet, they do not face one another. They take hold of the same surface.

A greeting may be no more than fingertips placed on opposite sides of a column while a pulse passes through it. Longer exchanges involve the palm, wrist, chest, and feet, each contacting a different band of stone. Information travels in layered rhythms: immediate speech in quick taps, emotional emphasis in pressure, memory in slower harmonic sequences, and questions in deliberate gaps into which the other may sound an answer. Much of what passes between them is inaudible in air.

Conversation can therefore include the building.

A corridor contributes its water load. A foundation reports stress from a rising intrusion. Animals moving three chambers away enter as soft interference. The low machinery below everything supplies a continuous tonal reference, as fundamental to meaning as gravity or time. An outsider might believe three inhabitants are discussing a bridge. More accurately, the three, the bridge, its shell colonies, and a resonant shaft beneath them are briefly forming a single interpretive organ.

Their speech does not separate easily into statements about objects. To them, an object detached from its pressures and consequences is mostly an error. They speak of what bears, what is borne, what conducts, what returns, what must be allowed to shift. They have many precise distinctions for stillness. The stillness of balanced load is different from the stillness of an occupied channel, which is different again from the stillness preceding fracture. Silence itself has categories: safe silence, listening silence, damped silence, predatory silence, and the lethal silence of a foundational tone that has ceased.

Their dwellings cannot be identified from outside because they do not occupy rooms in the usual way. An individual’s habitation is a route.

It may consist of a warmed recess where its metabolism can safely rise, three feeding walls, a vertical well used for communal listening, a shelf where its symbiotic shell animals are cultivated, and a pressure-tight chamber several districts away in which it enters dormancy. Between these places lie corridors shared with hundreds of others and with species not regarded as intruders. Home is the recurring sequence of surfaces by which the body knows where it is.

They sleep attached to the architecture.

The limbs lock into shallow grooves, the chest presses to a low-frequency wall, and the body cools until its chord nearly disappears. Small translucent shell creatures gather over the plates, feeding on residual charge and sealing scratches. Reflective mites clean the listening folds. Hair-thin water animals enter temporary channels beneath the outer armor and leave behind mineral threads that strengthen it. A sleeping inhabitant may become so thoroughly colonized that it resembles a pale mineral blister grown across a corner.

No one wakes them by touch. The wall alters its tone.

They eat sparingly. Part of their energy comes from slow chemical exchanges across the wet skin, aided by colonies carried in specialized grooves. They also consume soft tissues scraped from beneath selected shells, mineral gels grown in warm conduits, and dense knots cut from ribbon swarms during their periodic migrations. Feeding is conducted in darkness and often communally, not from vessels but from prepared sections of wall through which nutrients seep. The eaters arrange themselves around the surface and lower their temperature until the flow recognizes them as a gradient.

Heat is their most difficult appetite.

Their bodies need it in brief, controlled intervals for growth, healing, and reproduction, yet heat released carelessly can summon animals or disturb the city’s balance. For this reason, warmth is handled almost as a dangerous animal. It is trapped, portioned, passed from body to body, and extinguished within insulated chambers. A fever is both illness and civic emergency. Those who begin to overheat are enclosed in water-bearing membranes and carried to deep cooling rooms by relatives who never touch exposed skin.

The very young are warm.

They begin as soft-bodied, many-limbed forms without plates, borne not in a single parent but in a heated cavity tended by a group. The cavity may be biological, architectural, or both: a sealed chamber lined with living tissue, mineral membranes, and thousands of charge-feeding shells. Several adults contribute cells, body salts, resonant patterns, and symbiotic colonies. Others contribute nothing biological but remain against the outer wall for months, sounding the stable rhythms by which the developing young acquire orientation.

Before birth, the young know the city as vibration.

They learn the buried chord, the near-water hiss, the warning beat of stressed stone, and the tonal signatures of those waiting outside. When the chamber finally opens, they are blind, bright with heat, and dangerously active. Their attendants surround them in successive cooling rings. The first act of care is to place one soft limb against the wall.

The city answers.

Childhood consists largely of learning what not to answer back.

Young inhabitants strike surfaces impulsively. They excite dormant mechanisms, attract fliers, confuse traffic signals, and occasionally send questions into structures too deep or too old to respond safely. Adults damp their movements by wrapping them in soft ribbon-derived cloth and taking them to acoustically dead rooms. Here they are taught controlled contact: one finger, one pulse, then waiting through the long return.

They also learn the animals, though “animal” is not a category they use with confidence.

The shell colonies are weatherproofing, food, circuitry, and historical record. Their growth rings preserve local chemistry and electrical events. Certain keepers can read a century of structural strain by running a sensory limb over a colony’s edge. Other shells are deliberately patterned so that, as they reproduce and spread, they carry instructions: close this fissure, avoid this warm seam, thicken along this load path. The city’s smallest workers are not commanded. They are given favorable gradients and allowed to interpret.

The broad street grazers are both dangerous and necessary. They strip exhausted shell layers from paving surfaces, expose conductive plates, and redistribute minerals through their waste. Their routes are negotiated with low barriers, water chemistry, and subsonic calls. Experienced tenders walk beneath the animals’ wide bodies, touching the rings of feet to diagnose injury. A grazer may carry an entire traveling workshop under its ventral plates, along with families who follow its route for decades.

Bridge fliers are messengers only accidentally. Their mass departures transmit disturbances across the basin faster than most fixed systems. The inhabitants read the sequence of bridges from which they release. A pattern peeling outward may indicate ground movement. A simultaneous drop into flight means heat. A flock that takes off and then returns without landing suggests something large is moving through the cloud.

The immense six-limbed wall listeners are treated with wary respect. They are competitors in perception. They can locate shell beds and hidden chambers through several meters of structure, and sometimes they mistake sleeping citizens for concentrations of prey. Yet they also detect deep fractures before the city’s instruments do. In outer districts, attendants follow them at a distance, mapping where they pause. If one presses its resonant chest to a tower for three days, engineers gather on the opposite side and listen through its body.

Engineering here is not construction so much as persuasion conducted over generations.

Those who practice it develop unusually broad chest chambers and reinforce their limbs with dense mineral bands. They work in choirs, arranging themselves through towers and foundations so that their combined bodies modify an existing resonance. A single engineer may sustain one interval for weeks, entering shallow sleep without losing contact. Food arrives through the wall. Shell colonies grow over the feet. Assistants cool the joints and replace cracked plates.

They cannot force young stone. They can only make one future easier than the others.

A buttress begins as a preference in granular rock. A drainage shaft is first a region where water is repeatedly invited to pass. A bridge extension may require forty years of slight tension, applied seasonally, while the material learns the direction in which it will be allowed to crystallize. The most honored engineers are rarely those who produce visible forms. They are those whose work prevents a form from becoming necessary.

Some inhabitants are born with chords that couple too strongly to the buried machines. They hear the foundation drone in overwhelming detail: every correction, every wandering phase, every massive suspended tuner on the mountains. They cannot tolerate ordinary districts, where walls speak over one another. These individuals move downward into old, compressed levels and live in near-isolation beside the primary resonant chambers.

The upper city calls them the Deep Attendants.

Whether they maintain the machines or are maintained by them is unclear. Their bodies alter over time. Limbs flatten against interfaces and cease to detach. Mineral plates fuse with black housings. Resonant cavities enlarge beyond what free movement would permit. Some have remained in contact for centuries, fed by conduits, their personal chords slowly absorbed into the foundation tone.

They still answer questions, but not promptly. A request sent down may receive its response years later as a slight change in the hum of an intersection. By then, the person who asked may be dead, migrated, or structurally incorporated elsewhere. The answer is nonetheless received and added to communal memory.

Death is not marked by the cessation of movement. Many living things there remain motionless for years.

Death is the loss of return.

When a body no longer alters the vibration passed through it, its companions detach its symbiotic colonies and carry it to a wall prepared for receiving. The plates are separated and placed where their mineral histories are useful. Soft tissue enters warm processing cisterns. The resonant chambers are preserved intact. Installed within buildings, they continue to modify sound according to their shape, giving old districts a population of faint ancestral overtones.

Thus the dead do not speak, but they remain part of how speech travels.

Some are placed in bridges, where every flock departure passes through them. Some line nurseries. Some are lowered into the deep foundations to correct tiny errors in the great sustaining chord. A person who spent life listening for water may, after death, become the cavity by which a neighborhood hears its wells.

This does not seem to them like immortality. It is closer to load transfer.

They have no ruler visible from the streets and no center from which the basin is governed. Decisions propagate as resonant agreements. A proposed change is sounded into selected walls and carried through occupied routes. Each district alters it according to local strain, water, animal movement, and remembered danger. If the proposal returns coherent, work begins. If it comes back fractured into incompatible rhythms, it is not considered rejected. It is considered insufficiently true.

Large decisions take decades.

Migration is one of them. When a district begins its imperceptible journey across the shelf, its inhabitants may spend several generations changing their routes to match. They move shell archives first, then nursery warmth, then the tuned chambers of the dead. Some remain behind in structures gradually becoming too remote to service. These are not necessarily abandoned. They become a separate listening population, their messages arriving later each year as stone thickens between them.

There are also those who do not live in the city’s agreements.

In unfinished rock beyond the outer pylons, solitary forms move through pores too narrow for plated adults. They shed their mineral covering, soften themselves chemically, and enter the mountain while it is still granular. They may be fugitives, specialists, a reproductive caste, or descendants of inhabitants from before the city learned its current shape. Their passage is known from warm traces and from tunnels that close behind them in branching scars.

Occasionally, after a new basalt column emerges through a plaza, a chamber is found inside it.

The chamber contains no entrance. Its walls bear pressure markings arranged in long sequences. Sometimes there is a body within, folded tightly and coated in clear mineral, still producing a pulse too slow to classify as living or dead. The Deep Attendants warn against opening such chambers. The shell colonies avoid them. Even the wall grazers rasp around their edges and leave them untouched.

The inhabitants wait.

Waiting is not passivity to them. It is a form of measurement. They may assign listeners for three or four generations, each taking the previous one’s place against the stone. If the enclosed pulse changes, a reply is sounded. If it begins to synchronize with the buried machinery, the surrounding district is quietly moved away.

They understand that something lived on the shelf before the present city, although “before” is complicated here. Some of it may live below them now. Some may be the machinery. Some may be the slow intention by which the mountain rises, using builders, animals, rain, and towers as temporary organs.

The inhabitants do not insist on knowing which.

They know themselves to be temporary arrangements of pressure and matter. Their bodies are partly cultivated fauna, partly mineral accretion, partly inherited design. Their memories reside in nerves, walls, shells, routes, and the cavities of the dead. Remove one of them from the city and the individual survives, for a time, but becomes less articulate. Remove enough of the city from an individual and there may be no clear boundary at which personhood ends.

In the hours when the basin’s many noises align, they emerge from the enclosed corridors.

Thousands attach themselves to towers, bridges, pylons, retaining walls, and the naked ridges of young stone. Others stand among the pale herds. Some descend beneath the clear water and fix their limbs to submerged streets. They do not assemble where they can see one another. They assemble where the same vibration can pass through them.

The foundational tone rises.

The animals answer without understanding. Shell colonies click in sheets. Fliers tighten beneath the bridges. The great wall listeners spread their chests against the towers. Water trembles over every inhabited surface. The citizens open their resonant chambers and add the minute, differing chords of their bodies to the city’s note.

For several hours there is no useful distinction between population and structure.

Then the mountain shifts.

When it is over, they detach one by one. Water fills the impressions left by their limbs. Their bodies fold back into the ribs, cavities, and wet black angles from which they seemed to emerge.

The city appears empty again.
