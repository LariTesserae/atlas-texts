What lives here is, first of all, waiting.

Not the inert waiting of seed in winter, but an attentive suspension. Beneath every apparently bare pavement, small bodies brace themselves against the stone. They are folded into capillary cracks, sealed under salts, tucked inside the dead ends of warm pores. Their fluids have thickened to gels. Their limbs are drawn close. Some have waited through hundreds of showers; others will wake only once, reproduce in the water left by a single storm, and die before the rock dries.

The smallest inhabitants are films finer than dust. They darken the basalt without changing its color and spread through it along microscopic fractures. Some feed on warmth and mineral gradients. Some use weak currents released when the bedrock is strained. Others live around crystal valves deep underground, harvesting the chemical differences between one pulse of fluid and the next. They are the first mouths of the plateau and, in time, its first soil—although no true soil forms. Everything larger eats them, shelters them, carries them, or depends upon their invisible labor.

Across the open stone move the shell-backs.

At rest, one is easily mistaken for a chip of cooled glass. It has six legs folded beneath a low oval carapace and a head almost completely enclosed by scraping plates. Its shell gives back no shine. Only the narrow bands along its sides distinguish it from rock, and those remain gray until the air pressure drops.

Then rain begins.

The bands blanch as if lit from within. A shell-back unfolds, tests the pavement with two fine forelimbs, and begins to graze. A second appears. Then dozens pour from a fissure no wider than a fingernail. They advance in loosely parallel streams, shaving living films from the wet basalt. Their mouthparts work continuously. Seen from above, their multitude would resemble iron filings arranging themselves around an unseen field.

They are hurried but not frantic. Each knows, through pressure and moisture, approximately how long the water will remain. Before the final sheen disappears, they return underground. Those caught too far from shelter lock their legs beneath themselves and secrete a mineral varnish. Months later, rain may soften it and release them. Many wake. Some do not. Their little black husks become part of the pavement’s dark glitter.

In the narrow pools live creatures built around disappearance.

They have no permanent adult shape. Dry, they are cords beneath crusts of salt: transparent tissue wound around a chain of dense organs. Water loosens the winding. The cords extend, flatten, and open fins along their lengths. Within minutes they become swimmers.

The gentlest strain particles from the water with ribbed fans. Their bodies remain clear except for dark knots of digestion and silver bladders that regulate depth. Hunters are more substantial. They carry rows of sensory pits capable of detecting the faint field produced by contracting muscle. They do not chase what they eat. They hang motionless until a swimmer passes, then bend around it with the speed of a snapped wire.

When the pools contract, these animals contract with them. They shed fins, absorb their own less necessary organs, and coil into the deepest wet sediment. Several generations may be layered one beneath another under the same mineral seal. The newest wait near the surface. The oldest lie below, waking only when an exceptional rain fills the basin deeply enough to dissolve them free.

The great fault lakes contain older and slower things.

They seldom approach shore. A watcher sees only the consequences of their passage: an area of water lifting without splash, a line of condensation trembling on the rock, a wake arriving long after the darkness has swallowed its cause. Their bodies are broad and soft-edged, supported as much by warm upwelling as by muscle. Some graze submerged mineral gardens. Some carry colonies of luminous organisms in furrows along their backs. The largest may live for centuries, feeding little, moving between the warm layers of a basin as subterranean pressure changes.

They do not surface to breathe. They surface to listen.

A broad head, if it is a head, rises until a thin membrane touches the air. Through it, the animal senses low vibrations traveling in the atmosphere and compares them with those arriving through the water and stone. In this way it locates rain beyond the basin, fractures opening many kilometers away, and the distant footfalls of migrants. After listening, it sinks. Days later it may vanish into a flooded fault passage that no surface animal could enter.

The black plants around the vents are less like trees than buried organs.

What shows aboveground—a cluster of low trunks, armored ribs, and narrow vanes—is only the exposed portion of a colony extending through kilometers of cracks. Separate clumps may be branches of the same individual. Their roots are conductive cables, water vessels, digestive surfaces, and pathways for chemical messages. They grow toward warmth, charge, and recurring pressure, mapping the geology through touch.

During calm periods their vanes remain closed, black blades pressed together. When charged weather gathers, they separate with a dry succession of clicks. Blue threads crawl over their edges. The colony drinks the current.

Belowground, swollen chambers convert that energy into oils and sugars. Some is stored. Some is sent outward to growing tips. Some feeds symbiotic films in the roots. When the chambers fill, valves open in sequence and the visible trunks exhale.

The mist smells of wet metal and sweetness. It condenses on stone in warm droplets.

Everything nearby responds.

Shell-backs alter course. Minute fliers rise from holes. Pale, many-jointed scavengers come out from beneath mineral ledges. Pool animals crowd the margins where the droplets fall. Even predators approach—not to feed on the colony, whose tissues are fibrous and electrically unpleasant, but to wait for those drawn to its breath.

The tall wanderers arrive later.

They seem, at first, impossibly delicate for the distances they cross. Four narrow legs hold a body high above the warm rock. Each foot ends in a flexible fan that spreads under weight, conforming to cracks and smoothing the force of each step. The legs appear jointed in too many places, though several apparent joints are elastic tendons used to store the energy of walking.

Their backs carry translucent plates. In cool air the plates lie flat, preserving heat. Near vents they rise and dark blood moves through them, shedding excess warmth. Static glimmers along their edges during storms.

A water pouch hangs below the throat, pleated and soft. At night—if “night” has meaning beneath an unchanging cloud mantle—the wanderer lowers its body over a cold depression and spreads fine filaments from the pouch. Vapor condenses on them and drains inward. A full pouch can sustain the animal across hundreds of kilometers of dry pavement.

They do not travel in visible herds. Nearness would exhaust local water and draw hunters. Instead, they distribute themselves across the country: one on a terrace, another beyond an escarpment, others moving through darkness too distant to see. Yet they are seldom alone.

Within the chest lies a resonating organ attached to the sternum. A wanderer pauses, places all four feet firmly against the ground, and contracts it. The resulting call is too low for most ears. The plateau carries it.

Answers return through the bones of the feet: the condition of a pool, the approach of rain, the presence of young, a blocked passage, alarm. No one animal hears the whole exchange. Information passes from traveler to traveler, repeated and altered over immense distances until a migration changes direction without any leader deciding it should.

Their young are born beside transient water. They are low, broad-footed, and almost plate-less, resembling dark amphibious things more than the adults. For several days they drink freely and feed on soft growth brought up by the rain. As the basin shrinks, their legs lengthen. The throat pouch deepens. The back plates split from the skin one by one. When the last mud has hardened, they walk away.

Things follow them.

The pool-waiters are almost indistinguishable from ledges. They flatten themselves beside water and adjust their circulation until their skin matches the surrounding stone in temperature. Eyes would reveal them, so their principal sensory organs are buried beneath opaque armor. They detect pressure through the ground and changes in the local electric field.

They can remain still for weeks.

When a wanderer lowers its throat pouch to drink, the pool-waiter opens—not lunging so much as unfolding. What appeared to be a seam in the rock becomes a mouth and two anchoring limbs. It grips a leg, relying on surprise and the uncertainty of wet footing. Most attacks fail. A successful one may feed the predator for a season.

The runners are smaller and more persistent. Their bodies stay close to the ground, supported on several pairs of short limbs. They emerge after rain and travel through temporary drainage corridors while the stone remains slick. They do not track scent, which weather and mineral vapors confuse. They follow pressure residues: minute changes left in damp films by the steps of prey.

Larger hunters use the plateau itself.

They have deep chests and dense pads that strike the ground in controlled patterns. Their pulses do not command prey, but they mimic danger signals and the opening tremors of fractures. A wanderer, feeling what seems to be unstable ground behind it, turns aside. Another pulse closes the safer route. Patiently, sometimes over hours, the hunter shapes the animal’s path toward rubble, a narrow terrace, or a crust concealing mud.

The final attack is brief. The hunt is mostly misinformation.

Overhead live the sail-bodies.

They are born in vent hollows as heavy, crawling juveniles. There they feed on spore paste and shed skin until internal bladders fill with warm gas generated by symbiotic microbes. Their limbs lengthen into spars. Membranes unfold between them. At last the juvenile climbs onto the lip of a fissure, catches an updraft, and ceases to belong to the ground.

An adult sail-body may not land for years.

It rides the broad columns of rising air, tightening one membrane and slackening another to circle beneath the cloud. Fine nets around its mouth gather spores, aerial cells, and lesser fliers. Moisture condenses over its cool upper surface and runs through channels into the body. Its muscles are slight; most movement comes from reading the air correctly.

Green lights live inside shallow chambers along its underside. They are symbionts, not organs, and brighten when fed. In ordinary conditions the sail-bodies keep them shuttered. But when pressure falls and the great vent systems begin to discharge, the shutters open.

One green point descends from the cloud. Then another appears far away. Soon thousands drift over the basins at differing heights, their lights revealing distances the darkness usually conceals. Some are no larger than spread hands. Others carry membranes wide enough to shadow a pool, if there were any light by which to cast a shadow.

They mate in these descents. Pairs circle the same updraft, exchange packets of living tissue, and separate. Later they release weighted young into warm fissures. Those that miss the vents die on the open stone, where plated scavengers dismantle them before the next rain.

The scavengers are everywhere.

They are pale because they rarely expose themselves. Their overlapping armor closes around them when the air dries, and their jaws can reduce skin, shell, bone, and conductive plant fiber. They consume the dead so quickly that death seldom remains visible. A fallen wanderer may attract no obvious gathering, yet by the next pressure cycle it has collapsed inward, filled with small articulated bodies. Soon only bones remain. Even those are gnawed for salts or drawn into root fissures.

In the driest basins, where scavengers cannot work for long, skeletons persist. Mineral seepage coats them layer by layer. Ribs thicken into arches of iron-black stone. Vertebrae fuse into nodules. After centuries it becomes difficult to say whether the ore took the shape of a body or a body anticipated the shape of ore.

There are parasites, too, though the distinction between parasite and passenger is uncertain here. Threadlike organisms inhabit the water pouches of the wanderers and keep their inner surfaces clean. In lean times they drink from the stored water. In rich times they consume infections. Mites live between the back plates and alter blood flow by biting, sometimes cooling their host, sometimes weakening it. Spores enter the lungs of predators and grow conductive fans there, increasing sensitivity to vibration until the growth becomes too dense and the hunter suffocates.

Nothing lives entirely by itself. Independence is too expensive.

Even the apparent individuals may not be individuals in the familiar sense. A shell-back carries ancestral films in chambers beneath its shell. A sail-body is an animal lifted by gases made by microbes and lit by another species. A black vent colony includes root, fungus, crystal-dwelling cells, and countless small feeders that prune its tissues. The tall wanderers distribute spores in their footpads and pool eggs in the wet folds of their throats. The basin giants carry gardens on their backs.

Far below them all, life enters the machinery of the rock.

It lines porous tubes, alters mineral valves, thickens some passages and dissolves others. Colonies flourish where hot fluid meets cooler strata. They feed on iron, sulfur, pressure-driven currents, and compounds carried upward from depths no surface creature could survive. Their accumulated membranes make vents pulse more regularly. Their secretions keep crystal hinges flexible. Their deaths form seals that redirect flow.

No organism understands this work. None intends to regulate the plateau. Yet living growth changes the underground circulation, and the changed circulation favors life that maintains it. Over ages, accident becomes rhythm. Rhythm becomes dependence.

When a deep chamber empties, the smallest cells sense the pressure loss first. Root colonies close their vulnerable tips. Pool coils begin to loosen beneath their crusts. Wanderers stop and listen through their feet. Predators rise from hollows. Sail-bodies turn in the high dark toward air not yet moving.

Then the plateau exhales.

For a little while, what lives there becomes visible: black grazers whitening in rain, tall forms crossing the blue-veined rock, transparent swimmers opening in newborn water, green sails descending through vapor. Mouths wake inside every pool. Roots pump sweetness into the fissures. Hidden young break their seals.

It may look like a sudden invasion of a previously empty country.

It is not.

The empty country was alive before it moved.
