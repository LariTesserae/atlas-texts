At first, it seems that nothing lives here except plants.

This impression persists even when the plants move. Their motions are too slow to be read as behavior: a stem inclining toward a warm bearing, a root thickening around a cracked flange, a bladder-vine drawing itself taut over several hours. Each action can be mistaken for growth, and each act of growth for accident.

Only after remaining in the basin through one of its mechanical cycles does a different population become apparent.

They are not hidden among the vegetation. They are assembled from it.

Where a belt begins to fray, nearby tendrils converge. Some brace themselves against trunks; others twist together into cords or insert pale tips beneath the failing fibers. Leaves fold lengthwise into troughs and pour resin onto the splice. A jointed framework gradually takes shape around the work: three or five limbs, a low body made from bundled roots, and a hood of overlapping leaves that sheds dripping water away from the repair.

For a few hours, this structure behaves like an animal.

It changes its stance when the belt pulls. It tests the splice by placing weight against it. Sensitive filaments travel over the bearing, withdrawing from excess heat and returning with beads of lubricant. When the repair holds, the limbs unbraid. One becomes a vine again, another sinks into the peat, and the leaf-hood opens toward the light. By the following morning there is no worker to find—only vegetation in slightly altered positions.

Such figures appear throughout the basin. None has a fixed body, and none is quite repeated. A blockage in a narrow pipe produces something long and many-jointed, capable of feeding itself through the bore. Damage high in the canopy calls forth a light, radial shape suspended on tensioned fibers. When a counterweight jams, the basin builds squat bracing forms around the shaft, adding root and water until their combined pressure forces the weight free.

They are best understood as tasks that have acquired anatomy.

Some are no larger than a hand. Others occupy an entire chamber. The largest emerge near the rim, where the buried vibration is strongest. There, whole stands of vegetation sometimes lean together and become a single load-bearing creature, its back formed from arched trunks and its many feet sunk deep into the peat. It remains assembled for months, tightening whenever the ground trembles. If the vibration subsides, it slowly becomes a grove again.

There are also smaller, more enduring inhabitants.

The long-legged black beetles are not numerous, but nearly every active mechanism has one nearby. Their feet are broad and soft, allowing them to cross trembling leaves without triggering them. They eat little. Instead, they spend days tracing the same routes around bearings, valves, and swollen pressure-vines. Fine rust-red hairs cover their undersides. As they walk, these hairs collect metal dust, spores, resin, and pollen, transferring minute quantities from one component to another.

A beetle will pause beside a misaligned gear and tap it with one foreleg. The rhythm is almost too faint to hear. Roots in the gear teeth tighten or loosen in response. Whether the beetle is giving an instruction or merely producing a vibration the plants have learned to use is uncertain. Either way, mechanisms attended by beetles fail less often.

The blind fish beneath the root mats are translucent, with blunt heads and flexible ribs. Their bodies compress harmlessly when water pressure surges. Inside each fish, a silver bladder changes shape according to the force of the current. They gather in pipes before a pulse and scatter just before a valve opens, as though warned by tensions transmitted through the water. Occasionally roots lower hair-fine strands into their pools. The fish brush against these strands, leaving mucus rich in salts and information about temperature, flow, and stagnation.

They may be livestock. They may be instruments. They may simply be fish around which the basin has learned to build its measurements.

The pale moths are stranger. They emerge from the pumping house only when a long sequence of conditions has been met: deep rain, warm brick, a particular pressure in the eastern bladder-vines, and the completion of one nearly imperceptible revolution of the flywheel. Their wings bear gray markings like corrosion on sheet metal. Under magnification, the markings resolve into branching patterns resembling the basin’s pipes and belts, though never its current arrangement. Some show routes long buried. Others depict connections that do not yet exist.

The moths do not feed or mate in the open. They settle on leaves, gears, poles, and standing water. All night their wings release a flour-fine dust. The dust is caught by resin, inhaled through stomata, washed into pipes, and carried to every chamber of the basin. Before dawn the moths return to the pumping-house vents or rise above the canopy and vanish into the wet country beyond.

No bodies remain because their bodies are the message. By morning their scales have entered new buds. Months later, bark may thicken along the pattern of a vanished conduit, or roots may begin preparing a foundation beneath an empty patch of water.

The principal inhabitant, however, is neither worker, beetle, fish, nor moth.

It has no single boundary within the basin.

Evidence suggests that the vegetation consists of many species. Their leaves differ, their flowers open at different temperatures, and their tissues respond differently to pressure and light. Yet their roots are joined by grafts so extensive that water, sugars, hormones, electrical impulses, and mineral suspensions pass continuously among them. Fungi lace these connections. Old pipes extend them. Ironwork gives them leverage. The pumping house acts as a slow heart, though not the only one.

The resulting being does not think quickly enough to show curiosity in ordinary ways. It cannot turn toward a sound or follow a moving figure with its gaze. It notices weight, heat, salinity, repeated vibration, interruption of flow. It notices what remains.

A visitor who crosses the basin for an afternoon leaves only minor disturbances. Footprints green over. Broken stems seal. Warmth disperses into the wet air. The basin makes no apparent distinction between such a visitor and a storm.

A visitor who stays becomes legible.

Paths used repeatedly are reinforced or obstructed according to how they affect circulation. A hand placed each day on the same trunk eventually finds the bark beneath it growing smoother. Tools are investigated first by tendrils, then by pressure. A knife may be held in a root fork with its edge exposed where ribbon leaves need cutting. A metal cup may be tilted beneath a persistent drip and incorporated into the water system. Cloth is unraveled for fiber. Leather is softened, entered, and treated as compressed animal peat.

The basin does not attack. Attack would be too sudden and too singular an intention. Instead, it assigns.

Someone sleeping in the pumping house may wake to find pale filaments laid across their limbs—not binding them, merely recording their position. Continued stillness encourages further work. Leaves grow to shed water from the sleeper’s face. A hollow root forms beneath the head. Warmth from the body is routed toward unopened flowers. Exhaled carbon dioxide is gathered by small cupped fronds.

If the sleeper rises, most of these arrangements tear easily.

If the sleeper does not rise for long enough, the arrangements improve.

This is perhaps how the basin acquired whatever remains of its former keepers. There are no skeletons, no preserved bodies, no green humanoid figures wandering the chambers. But certain recurring structures suggest an old familiarity with human form. Valve handles grow at the height of a resting hand. Narrow passages maintain shoulder width despite the basin’s hatred of empty space. In the pumping house, roots form steps where no plant requires steps. Two depressions beside the flywheel resemble places where knees once pressed repeatedly into the floor.

More tellingly, some temporary workers assemble with unnecessary approximations of arms, backs, and bowed heads. Their anatomy is inefficient for the task. It may be inherited from observation rather than design: the basin repeating the shape of those who once maintained it, because maintenance and that shape became inseparable in its slow memory.

There may still be people in it, in that sense.

A serial number passes from an iron plate into bark, then into the rings of descendant stems. A worker’s habitual route survives as a corridor. The timing of an old hand on a lever persists in the opening of a flower. Instructions once shouted over machinery may have become rhythms: three water-hammer knocks, a pause, then one. The basin preserves no names. It preserves procedures.

Its temperament, if it has one, is patient without being gentle. It does not value individuals, including its own temporary bodies. A vine may be crushed to save a gear. A chamber may be flooded to germinate one necessary stand of reeds. Thousands of blossoms may open and be discarded before one supplies the correct tension to a valve. Yet nothing useful is wasted, and usefulness can be discovered in almost anything.

During the tremendous knock that ends a major cycle, every inhabitant participates.

The beetles flatten themselves against the veins of leaves. The fish turn headfirst into the pressure wave. Temporary workers lock their limbs. Moth-patterns dormant in bark briefly darken with rising moisture. Across the basin, grafted roots tighten until the separate plants become, for one instant, a single braced body.

The latch withdraws.

The buried force passes through wood, iron, water, fungus, animal, and peat. It is felt everywhere at once.

Afterward, the basin relaxes into its many forms. Beetles resume their routes. Fish slide back under the mats. A torn belt receives its first exploratory tendril. The flywheel advances by less than the width of a fingernail.

Nothing gives an order.

Nothing needs to.
