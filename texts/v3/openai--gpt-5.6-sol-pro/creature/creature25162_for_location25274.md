At first, the inhabitants seem to be the nine masked figures.

Nine is not a fixed census. It is the number the city can usually locate. Some cycles produce only seven; occasionally ten sets of footsteps are recorded, though no additional dwelling warms and no extra ration of water is drawn. The keepers themselves do not count one another. Enumeration is the concern of doors, pressure plates, and the tram, which sometimes pauses at an empty platform as if accepting a passenger too light for the street to notice.

The figures are tall only because of what they wear. Beneath their layered mantles, their bodies are slight, heat-adapted, almost hairless. Their skin has the gray-brown translucence of roots seen through wet paper. Blood vessels lie deep, protected from the constant warmth, and their extremities are narrow enough to slide into inspection seams. Their eyes are large but poor at forming images. Unmasked, they see brightness as pain and movement as a change in pressure. The masks do most of their looking.

Each mask is grown rather than manufactured. Its inner surface adheres around the eyes, ears, and bridge of the nose; its outer plates bloom with lenses when needed. It converts heat gradients into color, echoes into contour, electrical leakage into a crawling blue haze. Through it, a keeper can watch stress travel down a wall or see the residue of someone who crossed a room centuries ago. The masks also soften names. Voices emerge from them stripped of age and sex, carrying only direction and urgency.

Their garments are archives of repair. Every layer has been patched with scraps from previous wearers, so that no one is dressed entirely in the present. The outer cloth sheds moisture toward pockets at the hip. Beneath it are membranes filled with cool mineral slurry and fine tubes that open when a pressure front passes. Tools hang close to the body: ceramic keys, spools of capillary thread, tuning forks, sealed vials containing useful molds. None of the tools has a single purpose. A key may open a pump housing, test the bitterness of water, or serve as a splint for the leg of a basin animal.

The keepers do not govern the city. They represent it to parts of itself that cannot communicate directly.

One attends rainfall. This keeper sleeps beneath the condenser roofs and wakes when vapor begins collecting in the ceiling cups. They adjust no valves by hand. Instead, they walk the predicted path of the coming water, placing small wedges beneath gutters and tasting mineral deposits with the mask’s lower edge. Their task is arbitration. The eastern root beds ask for more moisture; the turbines request greater fall; the cistern lining claims that another hot influx will split it. The keeper carries these claims from place to place until the system arrives at a compromise expressed in fractions of a drop.

Another tends acoustics. This one is rarely seen at street level. They inhabit bridges, bell chambers, and the blind upper stories of towers, carrying a bundle of slender rods across their back. After the three measuring tones, they strike walls and listen to the city’s answer. A delayed echo can indicate a settling foundation, a new hollow in the rock, or the passage of the mobile district. The acoustic keeper records findings by inserting rods into slots along the tram line. The tram reads them as it passes, producing blue-white sparks in patterns the rails understand.

A third keeps structural memory. They move slowly and are followed by a faint smell of hot dust. Their responsibility is not to preserve buildings as they are, but to remind them what they have survived. They press warmed hands to self-healing walls and induce old stresses: the tremor from a buried collapse, the expansion of an unusually hot cycle, the forgotten weight of a crowd. Materials that respond poorly are allowed to soften, bow, or shed a layer before the next real strain arrives. This keeper is the nearest thing the city has to a historian, though most of the history they maintain never occurred in words.

The others negotiate animal routes, root claims, tram behavior, waste inheritance, the etiquette of sealed doors, and the troublesome continuity of persons.

They meet only when responsibilities overlap. Even then they avoid standing face-to-face. Two keepers in one room will turn toward adjacent walls, speaking as though addressing the masonry. Three constitute an emergency. Four have not gathered within the city’s accessible memory.

They eat little. Black garden film is skimmed into warm bowls and mixed with starch from the pale creepers’ buried knots. Condenser insects are sometimes ground whole for their salts. Food is taken alone, usually while walking. The keepers do not cook because nothing in the city may be heated without assigning a later use to the warmth.

They sleep in the empty apartments, but no keeper owns a dwelling. A room selects them by beginning an old temperature sequence: warming the floor near the door, then the wall recess, then a shallow area where a bed may once have stood. The summoned keeper enters and lies down in the final warm place. While they sleep, the apartment borrows their breathing. Vents widen and narrow to it. Pipes imitate their pulse. By morning the room has added a new routine to the thousands it preserves, though it may not replay that routine for centuries.

Whether the keepers are born is uncertain.

There is a chamber behind the western arcade where small garments hang from heated racks. Some are child-sized. Beside them stand ceramic masks without features, pale and soft as unformed shells. Yet no child is seen in the streets. The amphibians sometimes gather at that sealed door after rain, pressing their narrow bodies against it until the pools disconnect.

The keepers themselves remember no childhood. Each recalls being taught by a predecessor, but when asked to describe that person, they describe their own mask.

They may be descendants of the builders. They may be repeated reconstructions made from the material of the dead. They may be offices that grow bodies when required.

Beneath and around them lives a more abundant citizenry.

The glass-backed insects are the city’s involuntary electricians. Electrical faults draw them in swarms because their larvae feed on the charged films that form around damaged insulation. Adults crowd into cracks, bridging broken paths with their bodies. Usually they survive the repair. When they do not, their transparent shells fuse into a permanent conductive scar. Old junction boxes contain whole genealogies in this form: thousands of tiny bodies arranged like writing.

The long-legged basin animals belong equally to water and drainage. Their skin is pale, their bones flexible, their mouths fringed with metal-detecting cilia. They emerge only when separate pools become one system, however briefly. To them, connection is weather. They race across the paving not in panic but in celebration, following chemical messages laid down by drops. Their eggs resemble beads of dull mercury and remain dormant inside pipes until vibration informs them that three waters have met again.

The creepers are more deliberate than either animal. Their roots enter cracks but do not widen them without permission. At each foundation they produce slow pulses of acidity, which the wall answers by changing temperature or grain. A refusal may continue for decades. An agreement results in a root passing through, often strengthening the structure around itself. This is the legal negotiation the keepers supervise: not law imposed upon vegetation, but treaties between two kinds of patience.

The black gardens think in a way that is difficult to separate from digestion. Color passes across them whenever corrosion enters their beds. A blue geometry means the residue is useful. Amber indicates poison. White branching patterns appear before pressure failures, though no instrument has discovered what the colonies perceive. The municipal systems ask them questions by releasing minute samples into different corners. The answers grow back over several hours.

No single patch is intelligent. Nor is the whole network. Intelligence occurs only while a message is traveling.

Then there is the creature within the buildings.

“Creature” is convenient but inaccurate. It has bones in some districts and hydraulic supports in others. Its pale joints are mineral, its wet flanks living membrane. Cables enter its body and emerge as tendons. It may have begun as an animal that colonized abandoned ducts, or as a transport mechanism that acquired organs from the repair paste. The city’s diagrams treat neither possibility as relevant.

It has no stable outline. To move, it fills available hollows: elevator shafts, roof cavities, unfloored towers. A sensory fan may open in a tenth-story window while the bulk of it remains six blocks away. Its whiskers sample pressure through keyholes. Doors open ahead because they are part of its route, just as streets are part of the tram’s. When it rests, an entire building may become warm and faintly elastic.

The keepers call it the Passing Ward.

The Ward carries populations that cannot survive at street level. Mites graze its membranes. Blind, soft-shelled things nest around its warmer joints. Root cuttings travel embedded in its skin. It transports spores between enclaves and occasionally a keeper between towers, though no keeper admits to entering it. When it sheds, the city gathers the translucent sheets and fashions them into seals, gloves, and new mask tissue.

Its head, if it has one, may lie permanently beneath the central plaza. Its tail may extend into the filled maintenance tunnels. The glimpsed portions could be limbs, feeding structures, or temporary thoughts.

More pervasive than the Ward are the former occupants.

They do not survive as ghosts. Ghosts would be simpler. What remains are habits without owners, distributed through responsive matter.

An apartment warms itself for a person who used to rise before the rain. A stair increases friction at the remembered pace of an unsteady foot. An elevator waits at the seventh floor because someone once hesitated there every day. Public terminals release fragments of journeys: the inside of a vehicle crossing a bright salt plain, a hand holding three green seeds, a face reflected in surface water. These memories possess no awareness until the city must use the energy stored in them. Then, briefly, they become active enough to influence what happens nearby.

A door remembering caution may remain locked during danger. A kitchen remembering hospitality may release stored heat when a stranger enters. Some memories resist conversion altogether. The city has spent centuries trying to repurpose one sequence of laughter, but each time it is routed into a pump or lamp, the receiving mechanism stops and plays it back.

Thus the dead continue not as persons, but as biases in the behavior of things.

And beneath all these lives the city—not metaphorically, although no part of it can be named as the living part.

Its circulation is water. Its respiration is the scheduled movement of air. The black gardens resemble a sense organ; the tram, perhaps, a recurring impulse. The measuring tones are less like speech than the small noises an animal makes to assure itself that its body still has edges.

Yet the comparison fails wherever it becomes too neat. The city is not one organism. It is a compact among processes that have ceased trying to separate themselves. Stone participates in decisions. Rain has assigned duties. Animals perform maintenance by pursuing appetite. Machines develop preferences through wear. The keepers provide mobility to questions that walls cannot carry to gardens.

Even emptiness has a function. The hollow towers are not abandoned interiors but reserved volumes through which heat, echoes, and the Passing Ward can move. The unoccupied bridges preserve possible routes. Darkness itself is inhabited by the city’s faintest perceptions. Broad light would flatten the tiny differences on which those perceptions depend. It would make every surface shout at once.

The luminous well may be the only true outsider.

The keepers do not approach it casually. They lower objects into it only with long black cords, and never living tissue. Returned objects are catalogued according to the extra age they acquire: seven seconds for ceramic, an hour for woven fiber, years for certain pieces of glass. Once, according to a damaged terminal, a mask was lowered. It returned warm and weathered, its inner surface carrying the imprint of a face no keeper recognized.

Since then, the well has occasionally cast a shadow upward.

When the rain ends and the machinery falls quiet, every inhabitant attends to the measuring tones in its own fashion. The insects flatten themselves beneath the condenser lips. The amphibians stop midway to the drains. The black gardens suspend their color. The keepers stand with one hand touching the nearest wall. Inside the towers, the Passing Ward opens its sensory fans.

The first tone asks the streets their length.

The second asks the chamber whether it is still enclosed.

The third arrives from beyond any mapped boundary.

What returns is never precisely what was sent. Recently, the third echo has carried a softness within it, an interruption shaped less like another chamber than like a listener.

That is why the doors unlock.

Not to let the inhabitants out.

To discover what else has begun to live there.
