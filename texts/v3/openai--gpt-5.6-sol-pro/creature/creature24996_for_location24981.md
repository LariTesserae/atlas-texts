The first inhabitants seem hardly separate from the plateau.

The pale grazers might be mistaken for pieces of limestone proceeding in single file. Their six legs are low and jointed close to the body, so that they do not stride so much as pass over the rock on a ripple of small, exact motions. Broad black plates cover their mouths. These plates are worn smooth by stone and renewed from beneath in translucent layers. As they feed, they remove only the uppermost bloom of lichen, never exposing bare rock. Behind them remains a faintly altered pallor, a track visible for a few hours before the hard light erases the distinction.

They have no obvious leaders. At certain intervals the foremost grazer stops, turns half a circle, and allows the line to divide around it. The others do not collide. One branch proceeds toward the damp fissures; another follows an older route across the slabs. Seen from above, these processions repeat the branching pattern of the buried watercourses.

Their young are carried beneath the central pair of legs, pressed so tightly against the parent’s underside that they appear to be additional folds of armor. When one is ready to descend, the line pauses at a place where the mountain’s chord produces a low, felt tremor. The young animal releases its hold and stands at once. There is no uncertain first step. It joins the route as though returning to a task interrupted before birth.

The round burrowers are warmer creatures, though warmth here is relative. Their dense coats are striped in limestone white, shale blue, rust, and the dull green of old moss. The bands do not run consistently from head to tail; they break and offset across the body, matching bedding planes in the rock. A colony at rest becomes not merely difficult to see but difficult to conceive as present. Eyes may remain open in a wall of stone without betraying themselves.

They live in deep cracks and drink at dawn. They do not lap. Instead they spread the soft inner surfaces of their forefeet against condensation-darkened rock. Fine vessels open there, drawing in the moisture before it can gather into drops. The entire colony moves from shadow to shadow in silence, each animal taking its place against the wet face, then withdrawing below as the sunlight reaches it.

Their burrows are not dug. They occupy only fissures already made by frost and pressure, widening nothing, carrying nothing out. Their bodies seem shaped by the available spaces: narrow where the cracks are narrow, thick where chambers permit thickness. In adjoining valleys, if any exist, they might have entirely different proportions while remaining the same kind of animal.

The long-necked creatures at the pools have a more watchful appearance. Their bodies are compact and four-legged, but from the shoulders rises a narrow, flexible neck ending in a hooked, dark beak. Along the throat lie overlapping mirrored scales. When the animals bend to feed, these scales reflect sky, stone, and water in succession, making the neck seem briefly hollow.

They stand at measured distances from one another. Each lowers its beak on a particular overtone of the mountain’s chord. It remains submerged for the duration of that tone, then rises with a cluster of nearly invisible pool organisms adhering to serrations along the beak’s edge. The organisms resemble glass seeds with trembling cilia inside them. Some are swallowed. Some are shaken back into the water. There is no apparent difference between the two, but the feeders never hesitate over which to take.

Their eyes are covered by several lids. One is clear, one black, and one reflective. In the brightest regions near the rim, all three may be drawn across at once. The creatures continue feeding blind, guided by the resonance passing through their feet.

The pool life is more abundant than the water can explain. Beneath the dark surfaces drift transparent disks, threads coiled around bubbles, minute lattices that open when certain notes sound and fold when others replace them. Nothing hunts continuously. Mouths appear, feed, and close. Bodies divide at fixed positions along the pool margins, while elsewhere an equal number become cloudy and dissolve into mineral sediment. The water neither fouls nor clears. It holds a precise density of lives.

Predators are present, but hunger does not make them roam.

They wait in cracks crossed by the grazers’ routes. Some are flat, soft-bodied things whose skin carries a pattern like fractured slate. Others have only two powerful limbs and a wedge-shaped head held flush with the stone. They may remain still long enough for orange mineral films to grow across their backs. When the mountain sounds a particular conjunction of tones, one animal strikes and one grazer is taken. The line closes around the absence.

A predator that misses does not try again that day. It withdraws. A successful one feeds until the next corresponding interval, whether that interval comes in hours or weeks. Nothing appears starved; nothing appears sated. Appetite here is less an urgency than an appointment.

Above them live the multitudes.

Close at hand, the dark-winged beings cease to resemble birds. Their bodies are slender, without feathers, and enclosed in a skin as matte as soot. Each has four wings: a long upper pair held nearly rigid and a smaller lower pair that continually alters curvature without visibly beating. The translucent margins are crossed by branching spars. Tiny joints along these structures open and close in response to pressure changes too slight to stir dust.

They do not ride the air. They ride the sound within it.

The great upwelling chord makes layers of compression above the cliffs, invisible shelves on which the winged creatures rest. A change in the angle of one wing shifts an animal from one acoustic surface to another. This accounts for their uncanny sideways movement and their patience over a single outcrop. They are not hovering by effort. They are perched on a note.

Their mouths are small and seldom open. Along the underside of the body are ciliated grooves that collect the minute airborne life lifted from the shafts: spores, cave gnats, pale membranes, droplets containing organisms from the hidden waters. At intervals a whole tier of fliers rotates its wings and slides through the tier below. The maneuver passes along the cliffs for miles, a dark rearrangement conducted without collision or cry.

They sleep aloft. They mate aloft. The young emerge enclosed in clear sacs and remain attached beneath an adult until their wings stiffen in the dry light. The empty sacs fall toward the clouds, but few descend far. Smaller fliers catch and consume them, and those are taken in turn by larger forms moving along the lower pressure bands.

Among the thousands are a few ancient individuals of immense span. Mineral dust has accumulated in the fine scars of their wings, mapping pale tributaries across the black surfaces. Lichens grow on the oldest spars. These great fliers occupy the deepest tones and may not shift position for years. Colonies arrange themselves around them, yet there is no sign that they command. They are landmarks made of animal life.

The tool users are not a separate species so much as a tendency appearing in several.

A burrower will choose a sounding flake and use it to lift moss. A long-necked pool feeder will brace a thin shard between its forefeet and split a mineral shell. Certain flat predators test flakes against one another before using the sharper edge to cut through a grazer’s armor. Even the smallest six-legged animals sometimes carry slivers for a few body lengths to scrape lichen from a narrow seam.

None keeps the tool. None seems to recognize it later.

The act is intelligent, precise, and without attachment. A suitable stone is discovered anew each time, as if the plateau itself briefly extends into a hand. When the need ends, the flake is released exactly where it is no longer useful. Over generations, frequently chosen pieces may migrate several paces, but no animal gathers them, improves them, or claims them.

The marks in the niches are made chiefly by small, dexterous creatures seen only near the central shafts. They have four walking limbs and two finer forelimbs folded beneath the throat. Their skin is pale and nearly hairless, darkening in bands when the chord swells through the rock. Large ear membranes lie flat against the sides of the head, not opening to the air but pressed to stone whenever they stop.

They enter a niche alone. There they choose a vibrating flake and draw parallel cuts into the softer wall. The spacing corresponds not to the size of the hand but to intervals within the mountain’s sound. A low tone produces widely separated grooves; a cluster of upper notes produces cuts so close they resemble a single shadow.

The maker does not inspect the finished marks. It places both forelimbs against them while the next vibration passes, then leaves the flake and returns to feeding.

Whether this is notation, echo, courtship, counting, or relief from some inward pressure cannot be determined. Another individual may enter centuries later and deepen the same cuts. No one reads them in any visible way. Yet the niches are visited in a sequence, and every set of marks belongs to the same immense chord.

No species governs the plateau. There are no masters concealed among the shafts, no builders in the caverns, no elders directing the routes. What resembles order is distributed through every body. Migration lies in the feet. Population lies in the appetite. The timing of death lies in the ear, the bone, the pressure-sensitive wing.

Even individuality is faint here. Animals recognize mates, offspring, rivals, and familiar neighbors, but recognition does not become possession. A parent tends a young creature until a particular tone returns; then care ends cleanly. A feeding place may be defended during one harmonic and yielded during the next. Grief, if it occurs, is expressed as a missed movement: one pause in a procession, one empty interval in a tier of wings. At the following cycle the pattern closes.

This does not make the inhabitants mechanical. Up close, each is full of minute decisions. A grazer selects among lichen patches. A burrower chooses which drop to drink. A flier adjusts a wing against a pressure no instrument could easily detect. They play, occasionally: young burrowers roll smooth nodules down shallow slopes and intercept them below; immature fliers slip deliberately between acoustic layers and are caught by adults before they fall. But even play is quiet and brief. Delight passes through the established forms without altering them.

The plateau’s true inhabitant may therefore be neither the grazers nor the burrowers, neither the pool feeders nor the suspended nations beyond the cliffs.

It may be the chord itself.

The creatures appear to be its organs dispersed into flesh: mouths where it requires grazing, wings where it crosses the abyss, dark eyelids where it meets unbearable light. They are not controlled by it in the manner of puppets. Rather, each species has grown into one narrow portion of its continuance. The sound gives them time without clocks, roads without paths, law without command, memory without accumulation.

Or perhaps the inhabitant is larger still: the mountain, not as a sleeping giant or buried god, but as a life too slow and extensive to distinguish respiration from geology. Its hidden waters circulate. Its shafts voice pressure from immeasurable depths. Mineral seepage closes wounds in its markings. Animals travel over it and through it as mobile processes, no more foreign to its being than blood cells are to a body.

Nothing on the plateau worships this larger life. Nothing names it. There is no need.

They live inside its sustained note, each perfectly unfinished, each passing without leaving the world either richer or poorer. And when one creature dies, its substance enters moss, pool, lichen, shell, or wing. The place does not replace it. Replacement would imply a loss had occurred.

Instead, somewhere in the crowded brightness, the same interval simply becomes flesh again.
