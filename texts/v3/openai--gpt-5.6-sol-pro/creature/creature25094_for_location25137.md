Those who live in the High Quiet are difficult to distinguish from those things the High Quiet is using.

At first there seem to be no inhabitants at all.

A traveler may cross several districts without seeing movement beyond their own. Habitation towers contain rooms, and the rooms contain the ordinary implications of occupancy: benches placed at comfortable heights, wall recesses fitted with hand-sized objects, narrow sleeping frames, cabinets numbered in careful sequences. Some chambers are sealed at a pressure suitable for breathing. Others preserve faint warmth despite being no warmer than the streets outside. In a few, a cup-shaped vessel waits beneath a dry pipe, though neither vessel nor pipe shows any trace of having held liquid.

There are no belongings in the usual sense. Everything is marked with a municipal allocation number. Clothing, tools, masks, replacement fingers, door wedges, memory tablets, and structural braces all belong to the same inventory. The codes do not distinguish personal property from building material.

Nor, in the oldest registers, do they clearly distinguish citizens from fixtures.

A line may list:

**TWELVE RESPIRATORY ASSEMBLIES.  
TWELVE REGISTERED PERSONS.  
ELEVEN PRESENT.  
ONE DISTRIBUTED.**

The people themselves are encountered only rarely, and seldom at a distance. A silhouette may stand beneath an arcade for so long that it is taken for a post. A shape at the end of a road may remain unchanged through hours of approach. Sometimes it proves to be a statue. Sometimes it turns its head.

They call themselves, when speech is necessary, the resident measure.

The phrase may refer to each of them, to all of them, or to the amount of the city presently arranged in human form.

They are tall chiefly because thinness conserves material. Their bodies are narrow through the torso and disproportionately jointed, with long forearms and hands constructed for reaching into machinery. Over their flesh—if it is flesh—they wear fitted plates of fired mineral, charcoal or old-bone in color. These plates overlap at the throat, spine, hips, and knees. They resemble armor only from a distance. Seen closely, they are too delicately joined and too permanently attached. Fine black tendons enter them through ceramic eyelets. Tiny registration marks continue across plate and exposed tissue without interruption.

The unplated portions of a resident are soft, dark, and dry-looking. They have the texture of dense felt or compacted ash. No veins show beneath the surface. Wounds do not bleed. When damaged, the body reveals layers: woven carbon, flexible metallic foils, pale cords, pockets of charged powder, and occasionally something translucent that withdraws from light.

No one has seen a resident’s center.

Like the hinge-beasts, they keep it enclosed.

Their faces vary, but not freely. Some have recognizable features: a blunt nose, sealed lips, shallow hollows beneath the cheekbones. Others wear smooth facial shells pierced only by breathing slits. Age makes the distinction less reliable. An old resident’s features may have been replaced so many times that the face no longer belongs to any one arrangement of parts. A younger one may wear an ancient mask because it fit the available allotment.

Their eyes are dark, dry apertures covered by several transparent membranes. When they look toward the red sheen in the sky, a faint geometric reflection appears deep within them. Their gaze is steady not from calmness but from economy. Blinking is performed only when dust reaches a threshold.

They breathe slowly. Indoors, where the gases are balanced, one breath may last several minutes. Outside, masks fit over the lower face and extend down the chest as ribbed ceramic boxes. The masks do not supply air. They remove particulate matter and return it to city collection points. After a long journey, a resident may open a panel in the mask and pour out a precise weight of gray powder.

None of it is discarded.

The resident measure has no children.

There are small bodies in certain sealed wards, but these are not young. They are narrow-purpose forms used where an adult allocation would be wasteful: to pass through ducts, inspect the undersides of pressure floors, or enter wall cavities without dismantling them. They speak with the voices of adults. Some contain memories older than the towers around them.

A resident does not grow from infancy. A resident is assembled when the city requires a person.

The process takes place beneath the habitation towers, in rooms whose doors open only when the Census Plain has altered. Material is drawn from inactive bodies, failed tools, spare ceramic, and the unassigned remainder. A frame is laid out. Organs, or their equivalents, are selected according to the work anticipated. Memory plates are installed along the spine. At the end of the sequence, the room is found empty and a new resident is standing somewhere in the city, already facing the direction they must go.

No one witnesses the interval in which arrangement becomes life.

The residents themselves deny that this is birth. They call it assumption.

Likewise, they do not die. They are released.

Release may begin with a hand failing to rise, a name becoming difficult to retain, or a resident discovering that doors no longer open for them. They then make their way toward one of the inventory houses. The journey can take years. There is no urgency. Once inside, they lie upon a shallow ceramic table and wait while their materials are reassigned.

A released resident is not considered gone. Their plates may become a factory bearing. Their carbon may be pressed into electrical contacts. A lens may appear generations later in another face. Memories survive unevenly, copied onto tablets, embedded in walls, or divided among several assumptions.

For this reason, conversations in the High Quiet have an unsettling continuity.

A resident met in a switching yard may recognize a visitor from an encounter that occurred centuries earlier, though they did not then possess the same body, voice, occupation, or number. Asked how they remember, they may touch a section of railing and say that the memory was there when the railing was part of them.

Others remember events they could not have witnessed because the room that remembers was once a civic clerk.

Identity is treated as a location through which materials pass.

Names are temporary conveniences. The full designation of a resident consists of an inventory sequence, a present function, and a list of prior uses. In speech, this is shortened to titles such as Keeper of the Fifth Seal, Listener at North Vibration, or One Who Has Been Three Doors.

They seldom speak unless a statement changes what must happen next. Their language contains few greetings and no farewell. It is rich in terms for continuation, equivalence, tolerances, substitutions, and the moment when a thing remains functional but has ceased to be itself.

They have no word meaning waste.

They do possess something like courtesy. A resident will turn their face away while another opens a body panel. They ask permission before touching an old repair. They do not interrupt a machine’s long interval merely because they wish to pass. When two residents meet in a broad street, they pause several paces apart and compare obligations. If neither has a greater necessity, both may remain there indefinitely.

Some meetings last decades.

What they do is maintenance, though not all maintenance concerns machinery.

One resident walks the western escarpment and confirms that the marked stones have not moved. One counts the red status lamps, finishing the circuit every eighty-one years. One occupies a room with a cracked wall because the crack progresses when unwitnessed. One listens with the palm of their hand against an ancient factory casing, waiting for a vibration that may not occur within their present body’s useful span.

There are residents assigned to doors.

There are residents assigned to absences.

In the oldest quarter, a narrow figure sits beside a staircase ascending into open air. Its task, according to a nearby plaque, is to prevent anyone from using the room that no longer exists at the top. The figure has sat there so long that mineral needles have grown between its feet and the floor. It still answers questions, though only after a delay of several hours.

Its answer is always accurate and rarely helpful.

The resident measure does not govern the High Quiet. Governance would imply decisions, and decisions imply alternatives. They interpret necessity as it makes itself known through doors, fractured Census slabs, altered indicator needles, and the gathering of hinge-beasts.

Of all signs, the hinge-beasts are trusted most.

Residents do not touch them. If one is found beside a joint in a resident’s own body, the resident stops where they are. Others arrive, sometimes years later, and examine the arc in which the creatures have arranged themselves. If the hinge-beasts face inward, the component is replaced. If they face outward, the entire body is released.

Very rarely they form a complete circle.

No register explains what this means.

The larger presence beneath the dry channels is treated with a different kind of respect. Residents never call it an animal. They refer to it as the Subgrade Continuance, a term also used for utility tunnels, power distribution, deep transport, and certain portions of municipal law.

Some claim it is a maintenance assembly assembled from thousands of released residents. Others say the residents are fragments it raises above ground when it needs hands. Its segmented body has never been seen in full. Broad marks sometimes appear along underground doors, and on those occasions every resident in the neighboring district becomes still until the marks cease changing.

Once, according to an engraved account, a head surfaced in a pressure court.

The account describes no eyes, mouth, or recognizable sensory organs. It records only a great sequence of fitted plates, each bearing names that had vanished from the habitation registers. The head remained for nine years. Hinge-beasts covered it in concentric rows. Then the court’s paving opened without fracture, and the thing descended.

The court has been classified as occupied ever since.

Not all inhabitants retain human form.

There are listeners built into walls: arrays of membranes and mineral nerves that contract when spoken to. There are mobile tool cabinets that unfold into many-legged repair frames. There are lift mechanisms that refuse passengers but carry damaged components unasked. A number of towers produce pressure variations corresponding to old municipal names. Whether these are machines, residents too large to assume bodies, or mere failures in the ventilation system remains disputed.

The dispute has continued for more than six thousand years and consists of three engraved statements.

Nothing in the High Quiet is more alive than the city’s rules, and the residents obey them less as laws than as anatomy. They cannot carry matter beyond the rim because, to them, the increasing weight is not prohibition but pain. They cannot close a necessary door for the same reason a hand cannot bend backward. When an item vanishes from a container and appears as part of a railing, they do not call it theft. They update the container.

Yet there are hints of dissent.

In forgotten rooms, one finds objects with their allocation numbers carefully abraded away. A resident may conceal a small quantity of powder inside their chest and refuse to declare it. Some faces have been altered into asymmetries not required by function. There are private inscriptions written where Census correspondences cannot easily reproduce them:

**I WAS NOT THE MATERIAL BEFORE ME.**

**LET THIS MARK HAVE NO USE.**

**WHEN I AM RELEASED, DO NOT MAKE ME A DOOR.**

The city tolerates these statements. Perhaps it does not understand them. Perhaps they, too, fulfill some requirement.

The most radical residents are said to live near the plateau rim. They stand looking into the warm obscurity below, carrying nothing, because even an unburdened body grows heavy if it has begun to think of itself as something that might leave.

Their plates thicken over centuries. Their feet settle into the pavement. They do not step forward, but they practice wanting to.

One such figure has stood at the southern edge longer than any functioning register. It has become almost architectural. Cables run through its ribs. A municipal marker identifies the position of each foot and notes that neither has shifted.

Sometimes its fingers move.

The motion is very small, no more than the testing of a distant handle.

At the center of the city, no resident willingly approaches the round shaft in the Census Plain. They cross among the numbered slabs when work demands it, careful not to step on the outline corresponding to their current habitation. If questioned about the shaft, they become unusually imprecise.

They say it contains no material.

They say it is the portion of the inventory that cannot be assigned.

They say the High Quiet was built around it so that absence would remain in one place.

Some say every released resident passes through the shaft before their matter can be reused, though no mechanism connects the inventory houses to its depth. Others insist the shaft is the city’s only true inhabitant and that everything above it—factories, towers, residents, hinge-beasts, the buried segmented thing—is a ring built slowly around an immeasurable need.

The Census Plain offers no clarification.

There is, however, one slab near the shaft that bears the outline of a human figure. Unlike the other slabs, it has no number. Its engraved body changes subtly over immense intervals. Sometimes it is tall and plated. Sometimes small and many-jointed. Sometimes it resembles a tower with arms. At present it is a shallow depression in the shape of someone lying down.

No corresponding resident has been found.

Or every resident corresponds to it.

Thus the High Quiet is inhabited, though never crowded. Its people are distributed across distances, duties, structures, and centuries. A hand working in a factory may once have belonged to the figure at the rim. A voice answering from a wall may later emerge in a body assembled to inspect a fracture. The hinge-beasts gather wherever strain becomes appetite. The long thing below turns in its utility bed. The factories preserve their own means of preservation. The city keeps count.

And when a solitary resident crosses an immense avenue beneath the unmoving red sheen, their steps are slow not because they are weary, but because the city has assigned no value to arrival sooner.

They pass through the warmth in a charcoal shell, carrying a replacement part close against their chest.

The part may be for a machine.

It may be for another resident.

It may be for themselves.

In the High Quiet, these are nearly the same errand.
