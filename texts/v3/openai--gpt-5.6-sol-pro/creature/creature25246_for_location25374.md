At first the city appears to belong to birds.

They occupy its highest edges, mob its roofs, and look down from the condensation towers with the proprietary insolence of landlords. Their droppings whiten the stone; their feathers stop drains and gather in corners like windblown refuse from an absent forest. People passing beneath them wear close-fitting caps of cured skin, not against cold but against claws, lice, and falling filth. Children learn the nesting calls of the common species before they know all the names of neighboring wards. They can tell by sound whether a wall holds eggs, chicks, or only quarrelling adults.

The people live lower, in the wet interstices.

They are not a uniform kind. Boat crews continually bring strangers from greener coasts: timber cutters, charcoal merchants, grain factors, rope sellers, fugitives, spouses promised in water contracts. Yet after a few years the basin gives them all a family resemblance. They move with knees slightly bent and feet set wide, accustomed to slick stone and steps that change height as mineral skins accumulate. Their toes spread. Their ankles become strong. Falls are common among newcomers and rare among those born there, who can run over flooded paving without looking down.

Most go bare-legged. Long cloth drinks too much water and drags against the shins, so clothing is short, layered, and close to the body: fish skin softened with oil, thin leather, felted hair, imported cloth reserved for inner wrappings. Aprons are made from overlapping hide scales so that water runs from them. Feather mantles are worn in ceremonies and by workers stationed for long hours under roof-drip, but feathers rot quickly if neglected. Every household knows the smell of wet plumage being dried over a warm vent.

Their hands are broad with work and mapped by pale scars. Limewater enters every cut. Shell edges slice the fingertips; ropes burn the palms; mineral crust abrades the knuckles. Nail beds are often discolored by iron and sulfur. Many workers rub themselves with fish oil or rendered pig fat before entering the channels, and the crowded streets smell of this grease beneath the stronger odors of salt, guano, smoke, hides, warm mud, and stone.

No one in the basin is ever entirely dry. Hair is cropped, shaved, or braided tightly against the scalp. Loose hair invites lice and catches dripping mineral water until it stiffens. Older people sometimes have white encrustations at the ends of their braids, tiny beads that click when they turn their heads. These are cut away and kept. A child’s first hair-stone, a worker’s first calcified knot, and the crust shaved from a dead person’s doorway may all be stored together in a household jar.

Their voices are striking. They speak from the chest, low and forceful, shaping words to survive the animal clamor. Fine distinctions are carried by the hands. A touch to the shoulder may indicate which nearby voice is being addressed; two fingers against the wrist can mean that a statement concerns water; a flat palm turned downward asks for quiet without expecting to receive it. Bargaining is half speech and half contact. Friends converse forehead to forehead. Lovers learn one another’s breath.

Hearing loss is common, but helplessness from it is not. The old often understand more from vibration, expression, and rhythm than visitors do from sound. A stonecutter may know who is approaching by the cadence transmitted through a shared wall. Families recognize the particular knocking of their own pipes. Children play games in which one closes their eyes and names a district from its mixture of drips, wingbeats, tools, and echoes.

Quiet unsettles them physically. In an unfamiliar silent room, a basin-born person may begin tapping a heel, clicking the tongue, or drumming fingers against stone. Infants are put to sleep beside gutters or beneath suspended trays on which droplets fall. Mourners do not observe silence. At a death they make additional noise, striking shell cups and calling the dead person’s name into drains, so that the ordinary sound of the ward does not close over the loss too neatly.

Their bodies show the consequences of the city’s food. Fish, eel, snail, crab, eggs, bacterial cakes, pork, and occasional imported grain make most people compact and heavily muscled, though hunger is never far away when boats are delayed. Teeth are worn by shell grit. The poor eat more fermented microbial paste, pungent and sour, while the wealthy distinguish themselves by foods that come from rooted things: bread, dried fruit, oil pressed from seeds, bitter leaves preserved in salt. A bowl containing something genuinely green is displayed before it is eaten. Children gather around to stare. Some are disappointed by the taste.

To the basin-born, plants are less a part of the living world than an exotic form of merchandise. Wood is recognized by origin, density, scent, and price, not by the shapes of trees. A carpenter may know twenty timbers intimately and have seen none growing. Stories from abroad about forests are treated with the fascinated skepticism reserved for accounts of impossible crowds: upright living pillars standing everywhere, each shedding useless material, their roots breaking stone rather than being entombed by it. Leaves appear in ornament, but inaccurately. They resemble feathers, fish fins, branching mineral fans, or hands.

The city’s true farmers are keepers of surfaces and populations.

Some tend nesting walls, passing narrow paddles into chambers to count eggs by touch. They know which birds tolerate the taking of a first clutch and which will abandon an entire tower if disturbed. Their arms are pecked and scarred, and they carry hooked shields of hide over one shoulder. They remove parasites, repair chamber lips, collect guano, and settle disputes between wards when birds shift their nesting place across a legal boundary.

Others cultivate water without calling it cultivation. They scrape bacterial films, regulate the warmth of pools, crush shell to alter the mineral balance, and divert waste so that fish will gather downstream. Eel keepers can read burrow openings in opaque mud. Shrimp collectors work by lamplight with fine baskets. Snail children—usually the youngest members of poor households—crawl along submerged ledges, feeling beneath the rims with their toes.

The most respected workers are often the measurers.

They carry marked rods of bone or imported hardwood, plumb lines weighted with stone, shell counters, waxed tablets, and cords knotted to represent flow. They measure channels each morning, though everyone insists the channels are much as they were the morning before. They measure because sameness is not assumed to maintain itself. Constancy is understood as the result of countless corrections.

A measurer is expected to remember not merely quantities but obligations: which roof owes condensate to which cistern, which downstream court receives the offal of a fish market, where stone shaved from a doorway must be deposited, how many nesting chambers must be opened if a kiln wall is extended. Their authority is substantial but narrow. They may order a household to cut a new drain through its sleeping room, yet they cannot take a cup of water from that drain unless the measure allows it.

Households themselves are difficult to separate. Kinship follows walls, cisterns, work crews, animal allotments, and shared drainage as much as blood. A child may sleep in one room, eat from another court, work under an aunt’s nesting tower, and belong legally to the stair whose residents repaired the gutter at their birth. Marriage does not simply join two people; it redirects labor and water. Negotiations may take years because every union alters several balances at once.

Property is seldom imagined as the right to remove something. It is the duty to keep something circulating in its proper place.

A family does not own a pool so much as hold the right to its fish and the responsibility for its outflow. A ward does not own its birds, but it may take a measured number of eggs provided it maintains enough hollows for the next nesting. Even waste has inheritors. Arguments over it can become violent, especially when rich organic runoff is diverted from one set of ponds to another. There are old feuds concerning blood from slaughter courts, pig slurry, shell meal, and the warm discharge of public ovens.

The basin’s criminals are therefore often people who interrupt cycles: those who conceal clean water, block a drain, take too many eggs, smuggle timber out before it is exhausted, or dump useful refuse where no ward can claim it. Theft is described as “breaking the return.” The harshest accusation is that a person has made something vanish.

The dead are not permitted to vanish either.

Bodies are rarely buried in ordinary ground, which is too wet and too useful. Some are placed in warm mineral pools behind perforated screens. Flesh is allowed to pass into fish, bacteria, and water; bones remain and slowly acquire white stone casings. Others are set within new walls, seated or folded into shallow niches while mineral-rich water is guided over them. Years later the person is no longer distinguishable from the architecture except for a line of teeth, a joint, or the curve of a skull under translucent deposits.

This is not thought of as preservation in the sense outsiders imagine. The dead have not been rescued from decay. They have been returned to structure. A stair may contain three generations. A cistern lip may have been thickened around the bones of its keeper. People touch such places in passing, affectionately and without solemnity.

The animals are not merely livestock or vermin but parallel citizens, though no one would phrase it so sentimentally. Dogs belong to lanes more than to owners. Cats choose particular warm pipes and are inherited with the maintenance duty. Pigs recognize the bells of their feeding courts and scream when waste deliveries are late. Birds alter construction schedules by nesting in unfinished walls, and bats can prevent a cistern from being reopened for months.

Children grow among animals so densely that they imitate them without embarrassment. They stamp mud like the naked-headed fowl to expose shellfish. They hang by their knees from cistern braces. They whistle to bats and are bitten by eels. Their games involve building tiny drainage systems, damming them, then fining one another for improper flow. They collect strange mineral-coated objects from spillways—knots of rope, bones, broken combs—and pretend they are relics of a vastly ancient city, though some were discarded only a year before.

There are few gardeners, shepherds, foresters, or field laborers. The basin instead produces channel cutters, crust shavers, vent listeners, shell burners, guano carriers, hide roofers, bone setters, bird counters, seep finders, pool tasters, and people whose entire trade is freeing useful objects from mineral accretion without breaking them.

Everyone is, in some small way, a mason. Door grooves must be recut. Steps must be pared. Pipes must be tapped clean. A house that is not worked upon is slowly sealed by its own water.

This gives the people a peculiar understanding of neglect. Elsewhere, an abandoned building may collapse or become overgrown. Here it closes. Channels narrow. Hinges thicken. Floors rise. Openings turn to pale membranes and then to stone. An empty room becomes solid by degrees. The city does not fall apart when deserted; it heals shut.

Perhaps this is why its inhabitants do not praise untouched things. Care means cutting, scraping, opening, removing. To preserve a doorway is to wound it repeatedly. To keep a spring alive is to break the stone forming over its mouth. Their oldest ritual tools are blades.

Still, they do not imagine themselves fighting the basin. Opposition would imply that one side might win. They say that stone closes and hands open; water brings and drains carry; birds take space and builders make it; the dead thicken walls and the living recut passage. Each action calls forth its answer.

This is keeping the measure.

It makes them patient, exacting, and suspicious of miracles. Sudden abundance is as alarming as sudden loss. A spring that runs stronger than its record is not celebrated until the excess has been assigned a channel. An unexpected mass of eggs prompts emergency counting. A new warm pool is watched for months before anyone allows children near it. Change is not forbidden, but it must declare what it has displaced.

And yet there are restless people among them, especially at the outer plots where streets are only shell lines in mud. These are the stake drivers, pool claimers, and causeway walkers who push into the barely hardened flats. They sleep on platforms above warm water and wake with fresh stone around the pilings. They build for grandchildren whose foundations may already contain the tools they dropped yesterday.

They live at the boundary where habitation precedes land.

Behind them the city screams, hammers, drips, and measures itself. Before them lies the pale unfinished basin, apparently empty until a bird rises, a crab moves, a bubble breaks, or a line of submerged stakes reveals that someone has already begun.

The people go out carrying bone punches, shell blades, hides, cord, counting rods, baskets, and jars of drinking water. They drive a stake into the mud. By morning a white ring has formed around it.

That ring is enough. A wall, a district, and a set of obligations have begun.
