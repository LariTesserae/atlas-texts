To ask what lives in the White Undergreen is first to make a mistake about number.

The pale forests, the ceiling membranes, the folded plains and isolated root-columns appear to be separate growths. Often they are not. They are extremities: seasonal organs raised by organisms whose true bodies lie buried in compressed layers of their own former selves. A stand of needles may be the brief breathing surface of a root-mass hundreds of kilometers long. A luminous ceiling-sheet may belong to the same being as a black tuber under the floor, connected through one of the narrow columns crossing the abyss.

Whether the whole hollow is one organism is disputed. Cut tissues from opposite ends sometimes recognize one another and fuse. Sometimes they poison each other. Perhaps there are many immense plants engaged in a struggle so slow and intimate that their borders can no longer be found. Perhaps individuality simply has little meaning here.

The people who travel the hollow speak instead of **accords**: territories in which sap rises to the same rhythm, frost-hairs turn together, and wounds share a common taste.

An accord is not thought to be conscious in the manner of an animal. Yet it notices pressure, salt, heat, repeated injury. It learns the shapes of persistent things. A shelter maintained for generations may eventually be grown around rather than through. A bridge long fed at both ends sometimes thickens before the people tending it arrive. Paths frequently walked become firmer, while paths abandoned for a season disappear.

No one trusts these responses as kindness. The Undergreen accommodates stones, water pockets, parasites and scars in much the same way. To be remembered by it is not necessarily to be loved.

The smallest visible animals are the **sap-clearers**, six-legged creatures that hatch inside fermenting pith. Their shells are transparent except for the mouthparts, which are dark, branching structures like tiny roots. While fasting they are difficult to see. After feeding, their bodies shine green, violet or amber according to the vessel they have pierced.

A sap-clearer colony lives quickly during a pulse. It grazes fresh tissue, lays strings of salt-armored eggs, and then crowds into a crack as the warmth fades. There the adults interlock their legs and freeze around the eggs, forming a many-bodied case. Years later, another surge softens them. The dead adults are eaten from within by their offspring.

They are pests around dwellings, though useful ones. Their color reveals the condition of nearby sap. Green means ordinary growth. Milky yellow warns of tissue under pressure. A clear-bodied sap-clearer with only a dark line in its gut has fed on a sensing vein. People leave such colonies undisturbed and move quietly until they know what the surrounding growth has noticed.

In the dry collecting plains live **frost combs**, low animals with dozens of fine legs and no obvious head. A comb walks beneath the white harvesting hairs and steals ice from them one crystal at a time. Its back is ridged with reflective plates on which pale photosynthetic tissue grows. The animal feeds the tissue salts and carbon; the tissue feeds the animal sugar. During scarcity, a frost comb can anchor itself, withdraw its legs and live for decades as what appears to be a green-white stone.

Whole herds are sometimes uncovered when a plain turns toward a cloud. Thousands of supposed stones lift at once and begin walking out of the folding ground. They make almost no sound beyond the brittle brushing of their legs. Travelers follow them during uncertain weather, because the combs sense pressure changes early and never remain on a surface about to open.

The great open air belongs to the **sailbacks**.

A young sailback is wingless, long-bodied and nearly blind. It climbs a stalk by gripping the vertical grain with rings of hooked feet. Near the crown it scrapes a shallow wound, packs the wound with its own shed skin, and waits. Spores germinate in the skin. Over months they grow two rigid fans along the animal’s back, veined with the sailback’s blood but made from leaf tissue.

When the respiratory wind rises, the sailback releases its feet.

It does not truly fly. It permits itself to be taken. Its body hangs below the paired fans as a narrow counterweight, turning by curling or relaxing. Tiny mouths along its belly catch pollen, luminous spores and ice gnats. The living sails photosynthesize, slowly enlarging in flight, and may keep their bearer aloft for years.

Old sailbacks become enormous. Some have wings broad enough to shade a house. Their leaf fans accumulate holes, grafts, seed cysts and colonies of lesser creatures. They cross from crown to crown along atmospheric routes remembered not by the animal’s brain but by the inherited growth pattern of its sails. If a route fails—if a feeding crown collapses or an accord reverses its breathing—the sailbacks continue into the white distance until they starve.

A dead one descends slowly. People climb to retrieve the translucent wing ribs, which are among the longest straight materials available in the hollow.

Something else inhabits the permanent pools.

It is called the **underhand**, though no complete specimen has been seen. The traveling bulges beneath the floating skins are attributed to it, as are cups found emptied without any puncture in their sides. An underhand may be an animal, a mobile root, or a colony living between the vascular tissue of the pools and borrowing whatever shape it requires.

Occasionally, fingerlike cords emerge through the duckweed skin. They are white, boneless and warm. They touch the air, nearby roots, tools left at the water’s edge, then withdraw. If a person remains still, a cord may press itself against the sole of a boot and beat in imitation of the pulse it finds there.

This is why drinking cups are never dipped by hand. They are lowered from a rigid thorn.

Children are told that the underhands collect reflections, and that looking too long into dark water teaches them one’s face. Adults dismiss this, but most adults still disturb a pool’s surface before leaning over it.

The largest true animals are known as **interval beasts**, because nearly all that is seen of them lies between one appearance and the next: tracks entering a collapsed vault, a severed cable, a line of condensed breath high on a trunk. They may sleep inside warm rot for ten or twenty years, waking only when the landscape produces enough food to sustain them.

Their tracks suggest four load-bearing limbs arranged in two close pairs, with a broad dragging surface between. The same animal seems able to leave narrow piercing marks on hard sap and wide, soft impressions on pollen. Some naturalists believe it inflates its feet. Others think the “beast” is a temporary gathering of smaller creatures that assemble during abundance and separate when food becomes scarce.

One was once watched from a tower during a flowering storm. It appeared as a low black shape crossing the illuminated plain while lightning moved continuously over its back. It stopped beneath a rain shaft, rose much taller than expected and opened into a ring. By the time observers reached the place, the rain was gone. So was the animal. The cup beneath it had been drained, and all the plants in a perfect circle around the rim had turned their sensing organs inward.

The people of the Undergreen are spare-bodied and long-limbed, though not uniformly pale. Skin exposed to the cavern light tends toward gray, olive or muted violet, depending on diet and ancestry. Their eyes have broad dark irises and contract poorly; sudden darkness troubles them more than brightness. Most wear translucent veils during pollen weather and leave their mouths uncovered only indoors.

They conserve water obsessively. Clothing is made in nested layers: a soft inner membrane that catches sweat, an insulating web of dried collecting hairs, and an outer skin that opens or seals according to humidity. Breath masks are common in dry districts. Their exhaled moisture freezes inside branching chambers and is periodically scraped out, melted against the body and drunk.

Their settlements are not cities but **holdings**, usually home to a few families. Too many breathing, warm bodies in one place attract parasites and alter nearby growth. A holding may consist of six rooms distributed along a kilometer of root arch, connected by cords, grown ladders and narrow bridges. From a distance it is nearly invisible.

Families do not possess land. No land remains where it is put. They possess routes, grafts, rain predictions, dormant bridge-shoots, and agreements concerning which wounds may be opened. Wealth is measured partly in stored water, but more reliably in **continuities**: living structures maintained through several changes of terrain.

A door kept open for a century is precious. So is a cup that has never sprouted.

Their gardeners do not command plants so much as negotiate among growth responses. They bruise one side of a shoot, salt another, shade a third and let the organism’s own corrections create a desired bend. Every structure is an argument conducted in pressure, injury and food. Poor construction dies. Worse construction survives and closes around its inhabitants.

Children learn pulse-reading before they learn distant navigation. They lie with cheek or wrist against the floor and distinguish deep water movement from approaching growth. A strong reader can hear, through bone, whether a plain will pleat or split. The best are called **listeners**, although hearing is only part of the skill. They taste condensed sap, inspect the direction of frost hairs and watch sap-clearers for changes of color.

Listeners maintain calendars with no fixed days. Time is counted in local events: the third dimming of the west membrane, the return of the pierced sailback, two pressure waves after the black cup flowered. For longer reckoning, they use the dead rings.

The rings are places the living world has been persuaded not to enter.

Each is made from killed wood, repeatedly salted and polished, sunk through the active surface into older compressed layers. To maintain one requires constant work. Shoots test its boundary. Fungal films creep over it. The ground rises, subsides or attempts to carry the entire ring away. Some rings have been moved dozens of times while still being spoken of as the same empty place.

They are not tombs. Bodies are too rich to preserve and are returned quickly to a chosen growth. Nor are they temples in the ordinary sense. People enter the rings to make promises, adopt children, end partnerships, forgive debts, or sit without being touched by anything that is growing.

Emptiness is the one substance the Undergreen cannot produce. It must be made and defended.

The hollow’s people therefore treat a kept absence as surface peoples might treat an eternal flame.

Their dead are given to **memory vines**. A strip of living vine is trained around the body, fed, and allowed to root into it. Later, a cutting from that vine is grafted into a dwelling. These grafts do not contain memories in any literal sense, but they preserve chemical signatures: salts from a person’s blood, pigments from long habit, traces of medicines and familiar food. Descendants sometimes recognize a dead relative by the scent released when the graft is pruned.

The practice has consequences. Old houses become chemically crowded with ancestry. Walls respond differently to different family members. A room may close its vents around a stranger but open them for someone sharing the dead donor’s blood. Over generations, a holding becomes less a structure than a cultivated extension of its inhabitants’ lineage.

No one is certain whether the house knows them.

This uncertainty governs much of their religion.

They do not worship the vast vegetation as a person. It is too divided for that, too indifferent, and too readily tricked. Instead they speak of **attention** as a natural force, like pressure or cold. Whatever is repeatedly touched begins to answer. Whatever is fed acquires expectation. Whatever answers long enough may come, in some limited fashion, to desire.

Thus one must be careful what one teaches the world to expect.

A trembling root is not cut because it has matched the cutter’s pulse and may carry that pattern elsewhere. A pool is not allowed to imitate a face. A house is pruned in varying rhythms so that it does not learn to close at a predictable hour. Names are rarely spoken during rapid growth, when fresh sensing tissue is everywhere.

People commonly have two names: a household name used among living walls, and an open name spoken only in air, on high platforms or in the center of a dead ring.

During upward rain, even open names are withheld.

The most respected travelers are the **far-cord keepers**, who maintain routes between holdings. They carry coils grown from many different accords, each fiber responsive to a particular humidity or sap scent. Their maps are not pictures. They are braided records read through the fingers. A tight knot may indicate a trunk that existed when the route was made; a loose crossing, a gap expected to widen; a bead of black husk, a place where something large fed.

Because landmarks are temporary, far-cord keepers navigate by relationships: downwind of the violet accord when it breathes inward; beneath the crown that flowers after the second ceiling fold; three hours from the pool whose skin curls away from salt. Their maps are accurate only while being revised.

They also carry empty rings small enough to fit around a wrist. If lost, they set one on the ground and wait inside its boundary with their hands folded. It offers no physical protection. Its purpose is to remind a frightened mind that not every opening must be filled, not every motion followed, not every living signal answered.

For the deepest danger in the White Undergreen is neither cold nor thirst nor collapse.

It is participation.

A person shelters in a warm fold, feeds a wall so it will thicken, follows a respiratory wind, drinks from a cup, grafts a bridge, sleeps against a pulsing floor. Every act enters a cycle vastly larger than intention. The landscape begins to include the traveler in its adjustments. Paths tilt toward familiar footsteps. Collecting hairs turn not toward clouds but toward breath. A root beneath the bed trembles at the rhythm of a sleeping heart.

Most people pull away before this becomes dangerous.

A few do not.

They are called the **engrafted**. Some choose the condition after injury or in old age. Others are caught during growth-fronts and recovered alive, enclosed to the waist or shoulders in new tissue. If separated, they would bleed to death. Fed by briny sap and lit through translucent leaves, they may survive for centuries.

An engrafted person changes slowly. Skin mineralizes. Blood cools. Speech becomes infrequent because the lungs no longer need to work continuously. They sense distant rain through the pull in the roots and can warn a holding days before a vascular shift. In time they may cease responding to voices while still opening their eyes before every local dimming.

Whether they remain people is a question no ring can settle.

The oldest known engrafted woman lives high in a root-column, enclosed beneath a fan of luminous blades. She has not spoken in four generations. Yet whenever sailbacks gather on the eastern respiratory wind, the living wall around her forms a narrow door.

Inside, pruned carefully into the pale tissue, fresh marks appear.

They are not words. They resemble maps, but no traveler recognizes the routes. Some believe they show the vascular world below the hollow. Some think they record changes in the ceiling. Others say they depict another Undergreen farther down, an even greater white emptiness beneath the roots believed to be deepest.

The marks never remain long. The wall heals over them within a day.

Then the old woman’s fingers move beneath the clear bark, and elsewhere in the hollow—sometimes hundreds of kilometers away—a field of frost-hairs turns all at once toward something that is not a cloud.
