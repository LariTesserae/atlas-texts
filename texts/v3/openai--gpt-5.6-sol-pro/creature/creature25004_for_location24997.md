No creature of the highlands presents itself all at once.

A body arrives in pieces: a pressure change against the face, warmth among the reeds, four slow impacts traveling through stone. Only later comes the odor of wet armor or the scrape of a feeding plate across fungal bark. Shape is the least reliable fact about anything living here. Limbs fold into grooves. Membranes spread and vanish. Soft animals fill whatever chamber contains them, while rigid ones are assembled from hinged sections that can pass separately through roots and lock together again.

The inhabitants are made for a world where being seen has never mattered.

Their skins are without display. Pigment, where it occurs, serves chemistry: resisting acid rain, controlling fungal growth, carrying charge. Hide may be slick, furred, plated, or crusted with symbionts, but it has no pattern intended for an eye. Courtship is conducted in rhythm, temperature, taste, and the shaping of water. A male in the reed basins may spend a season tapping a hollow stem into the correct resonance. A marsh female answers by warming a patch of mud. Some species recognize kin by the mineral composition of saliva. Others exchange small colonies of bacteria and accept as family whatever bears the same living scent.

Many have eyes, but these are humble organs: soft cups beneath the jaw, rows of pressure-sensitive beads around a foot, delicate blisters that register nearby heat. They do not look outward. They test contact before it happens. The most successful hunters have no eyes at all.

The great mat-grazers are the most consequential animals, though they are not the most numerous. An adult is a low, broad mass carried on six or ten pillar legs, each foot opening before it settles so that weight spreads across the floating ground. Beneath the foot are concentric rings of sensory flesh. By placing one foot and waiting, the animal can judge the depth of peat, the direction of buried water, the strain in nearby roots, and whether another heavy body passed there days ago.

Their armor is not a clean shell. It is a moving habitat.

Deep folds along the flanks contain warm pools where mites breed. Pale comb-creatures graze fungus from the joints. Small air-breathing scavengers live in chambers near the shoulders, emerging when the grazer rests to clean its vents and feeding grooves. Plants root in accumulated silt along the spine. Old individuals carry thickets high enough to shelter juvenile reed-runners, and their passage can be mistaken for a grove moving through rain.

The grazer itself feeds slowly, pressing paddle leaves between ridged plates and drawing out their softened tissues. What it cannot digest is worked by colonies in its gut, fermented until the animal’s belly pulses with heat. In cold downdrafts, calves gather against the adults and survive within this warmth. A herd moving over a mat can be followed by the alternating scent of crushed vegetation and mineral-rich breath.

They speak downward.

A call begins in a chamber behind the sternum and enters the ground through the forefeet. The deepest notes travel farther than sound in air. Herds separated by mountains can exchange simple conditions: water rising; mat thin; young present; machinery active. More complicated messages are repeated through several bodies, each animal receiving the tremor, correcting it, and stamping it onward. During large migrations, a warning may cross a watershed by herd before the mechanical horns begin.

Their calves learn the industrial rhythms as readily as the rhythms of adults. They know the harmless chatter of a valve, the long pre-release shudder of a sluice, the irregular complaint of a failing support. They also learn fear.

The false gates depend upon that fear.

A false gate is less a runner than an ambush folded around a resonant body. It waits on stable ground above a marsh route, its limbs hooked deep into roots. Along its abdomen are hard chambers filled with water and grit. Muscles strike these chambers in sequence, reproducing the pipe-borne code that precedes a flood release.

The imitation is exact in rhythm and wrong in almost every other respect. Young grazers respond to the rhythm alone. They climb. Adults test the accompanying vibration: whether distant valves have answered, whether the ground pressure is changing, whether water has begun to withdraw from low channels. Experienced herds sometimes ignore the hunter. Older false gates therefore embellish their calls. They scrape roots to imitate closing relays. They discharge warm urine downhill to suggest redirected water. Some tap abandoned pipes, recruiting the works themselves as resonators.

When prey approaches, the false gate opens.

Its resting body is little more than a stack of wet plates. In motion those plates separate into hooked limbs and a wide feeding basket lined with backward teeth. It does not kill immediately. It anchors the prey and grows still again, waiting for panic to exhaust it. A successful hunter may feed for weeks, vibrating softly to discourage scavengers.

The quick animals of the reed basins live at the other extreme. Reed-runners weigh almost nothing. Their limbs are long enough that the body scarcely touches the surfaces it crosses, and each foot ends in a fan of hairs that reads ripples from several directions at once. They hunt by asking questions of the water. One foot flicks. The others listen. A larva beneath the surface returns a soft, mobile answer; a root returns a hard one; deep water returns none.

When rain becomes too violent for these distinctions, the runners climb. Hundreds interlace their limbs between stems, heads inward and feet outward, making a net that gives with the storm. No individual chooses the whole structure. Each grips where another body trembles and releases when the load grows dangerous. By morning, the net may have moved from one side of a basin to the other.

They are fearless around machines. Warm inspection housings attract their eggs, and newly hatched runners sometimes spend their first weeks inside pressure gauges, feeding on tiny organisms drawn to the diaphragms. Maintenance drones have learned to shake each gauge before opening it. The runners have learned that the shaking means warmth is about to escape.

Higher in the moving air are the pressure-sails.

Folded, one is a bundle of cartilage no larger than a clenched hand. It climbs a hollow trunk during falling pressure, tasting the draft through slits in its skin. When the correct current arrives, the animal lets go and unfolds into a many-lobed membrane. Its body hangs among tension lines, rearranging itself constantly to keep each surface taut.

Pressure-sails do not navigate toward places. They navigate among kinds of weather. They seek the warm ascent over fracture fields, the long lateral winds beneath storm belts, the soft collapse of air above spore releases. They feed through the entire membrane. Spores adhere to mucus, are sorted chemically, and pass into branching digestive vessels. Larger prey—ballooning larvae, mites, the seed capsules of rope fungi—are conveyed to a mouth that migrates along the underside according to which lobe has captured food.

Some remain aloft so long that plants begin to grow on them. Old sails become heavier and descend into the hanging wetlands, where they mate, shed their membranes, and crawl for a brief season as dense, blind animals. Their young climb the cliff vegetation and wait for a storm strong enough to become sky.

Beneath nearly every foot are the compression colonies.

A hillside that seems to breathe may contain billions of them. Each individual is a soft filter wrapped around a narrow water channel, but neighboring animals fuse their tissues and share pressure. When one is touched, the colony contracts in sequence. Water is expelled downhill, nutrients are drawn uphill, and the entire slope tightens around its roots.

These colonies stabilize the peat more effectively than any retaining wall. They also migrate, although no single animal moves far. The uphill edge buds new bodies while the downhill edge dissolves and releases spores. Over decades the colony advances against the flow of water. Over centuries it can carry a marsh away from a failing basin.

The works treat them as part of the drainage system. Survey devices strike the ground and measure the contraction that answers. If a colony responds weakly, drones pierce it with aeration rods or bring nodules from a stronger slope. If it blocks a required channel, low pulses are sent through the soil until the animals withdraw. There are old channels that have been negotiated back and forth in this manner for thousands of years.

The smallest residents are the closest to indispensable.

Cabinet crusts live on oxide. They resemble overlapping mineral scales until a relay moves, at which point thousands of tiny feeding filaments retract at once. Their bodies keep contacts clean, wick condensation away from switches, and leave behind a conductive residue. Too few, and corrosion stops the mechanism. Too many, and they bridge circuits that should remain separate. Drones cultivate them with precise impatience, scraping away excess colonies and inoculating bare housings.

Pipe-roots inhabit drains and service tunnels. They are pale, muscular growths with no leaves. A root tip follows vibration until it finds the hiss of a leak, then flattens over the opening and lays down alternating layers of fiber and mineral. Small leaks vanish beneath it. Large ones are narrowed enough for mechanical repair. In return, the works carry its seed nodules between stations.

No living thing appears to command these arrangements.

The maintenance drones come nearest, but they possess no centrality. They are blunt, low machines with broad feet, manipulator mouths, and crowns of flexible whiskers. A drone can wait submerged for years until a pipe changes rhythm. It then wakes, clears its joints, and begins walking toward the fault. Roots grow across its back during long journeys. Animals nest between its plates. It removes only what interferes with movement.

Each drone knows a limited territory and a limited set of obligations. One tends hinges. Another measures peat depth. Another carries root nodules. Another does nothing but travel from horn to horn, striking each housing and listening to the answer. When two meet, they touch whiskers and exchange pressure pulses through their frames. Neither receives a picture of the whole system. The system persists in the overlap between partial duties.

Occasionally a drone enters one of the old shelters.

There, in rounded rooms softened by fungus, it pauses beside bunks shaped for bodies no longer present. It tests drains. It taps the raised labels. It may open a cabinet and remain motionless while warm scavengers scramble out over its feet. Then it replaces a seal or clears a pipe and leaves.

Whether the absent makers died, departed, or thinned themselves into these routines is no longer a useful distinction. Something of them survives in the preference of a hinge, the spacing of ladder rungs, the warning cadence that grazers fear, the dimensions of rooms now occupied by nesting animals. But intention has weathered. No mind holds it entire.

What lives in the highlands is therefore not only the herds, hunters, colonies, roots, sails, and machines. It is the set of accommodations among them.

A grazer opens a channel. Rain deepens it. A compression colony braces its banks. Pipe-roots enter from a culvert. A drone measures the increased flow and adjusts a gate. Reed-runners lay eggs in the warmed housing. A false gate learns the new signal. The herd learns the false gate’s imperfect copy. Generations later, the signal changes because the machines have detected too many fatal diversions—or because corrosion alters the hammer stroke, and every listener adapts.

Life here consists of knowing what must be allowed to pass.

Water through stone. Air through trunks. Herds over mats. Roots around foundations. Warnings through rails. Spores through weather. Machines through forests. The dead through the bodies of scavengers and the chemistry of the marsh.

When the deep water turns, every inhabitant receives a different fragment of the event.

Cabinet crusts draw tight against their contacts. Pressure-sails fold a fraction in the altered air. False gates stop calling. Grazers lift one foot and hold it above the ground. Drones wake in sealed recesses. Across the slopes, compression colonies contract until whole hillsides seem to brace themselves.

Then the first horn sounds.

Nothing looks toward it.

Everything listens.
