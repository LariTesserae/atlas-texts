At first, the city’s inhabitants appear to be the small things.

There are the lintel animals, if animals they are: patient lengths of gray substance laid above thresholds, each as particular to its doorway as an eyelid is to an eye. Their contraction when something passes beneath them is not fear. It is recognition. Doorways frequented by travelers acquire thicker creatures, sometimes two or three nested one behind another. Abandoned entrances lose theirs. The molding thins, turns translucent, and one dim hour is simply part of the wall.

They do not eat in any ordinary sense. They measure passage. Each crossing leaves a minute alteration in them: a shortening, a new pale line, a slight change in curvature. Over centuries these alterations accumulate until the animal no longer resembles the lintel it imitates. Then the doorway closes.

No one sees the creature depart. Elsewhere, a new stair appears.

The folded black things drifting above the high plazas are more active but less comprehensible. They travel in groups that are not quite flocks, because no individual maintains its identity through a turn. Seven dark sheets approach a tower; five pass around it; eleven emerge on the other side. Their pale seams open in clear air and close in dust. When one alights, it does not settle on a surface but inserts itself into the surface’s angle. For a while the wall has an additional corner. Later the corner unfolds and rises away.

They feed on obscurity. This is why corridors of perfect visibility remain behind them. Yet clarity does not seem to nourish them for long. After feeding, their black planes grow thin and reflective. They begin showing fragments of places that are not in front of them: the underside of a bridge, a sealed room, a stair seen from within the stone. Eventually they fold too tightly to remain visible. The others circle the place where one has disappeared, and the surrounding architecture changes orientation by a few degrees.

There are things among the crack-flowers as well. Their life is distributed too finely to be seen.

A white crystalline network may spread across three walls, retreat through a ceiling, and reappear two districts away in the floor of a basin. It responds slowly to warmth. Place a hand near one branch and, over the course of an hour, distant branches turn toward it—not toward the hand itself, but toward the absence the hand will leave after it is withdrawn. If disturbed repeatedly, the network learns the interval. It begins flowering before the hand arrives.

Some chambers are dense with these anticipations. Their walls whiten in patterns around events that have not yet occurred.

But these are no more the city’s principal inhabitants than mites, birds, and mold are the principal inhabitants of a human capital.

The first sign of the larger residents is usually mistaken for a flaw in distance.

A lone traveler notices someone standing at the far end of an avenue: a narrow upright figure, perhaps robed, perhaps merely a dark slit between pale buildings. It has no features that can be remembered. Looking directly at it reveals a column, an open door, or two overlapping towers. Looking away restores the impression of a person.

It does not approach. Instead, the avenue shortens.

With each glance aside, the figure remains at the far end while the architecture between grows less complete. Arcades lose bays. Side streets seal themselves. Stairs give up several steps. Eventually the traveler and the figure occupy opposite edges of the same hexagonal square.

There is never anything on the central plinth.

The colonnade, however, has acquired another pillar.

These figures are called residents only for convenience. They are not bodies moving through the city. They are arrangements of the city that have learned to move through themselves.

A resident may consist, at one moment, of a vertical shadow, three connected doorways, the hollow beneath a bridge, and a patch of deep-red stone on a tower several miles away. These elements form a single being not because they touch but because they obey one another. If the shadow narrows, the first doorway opens. If the doorway is crossed, the bridge-hollow deepens. If dust covers the red stone, the whole being turns its attention elsewhere.

Its thought is architectural correlation.

Its memory is wear.

Its body is wherever one change requires another.

This is why the city appears deserted at close range. A resident cannot be met in one room any more than a person can be met in one cell of the liver. Stand within it and there are only walls, stairs, mineral light, and localized rules. See enough districts at once, from a sufficiently high terrace, and postures emerge.

One region appears withdrawn, its bridges pulled close and its towers turned inward. Another extends long avenues into vacant stone, attentive or expectant. A third repeatedly assembles courtyards around an empty center, then dismantles them before the arrangement is complete. Their motions take hours, years, or fractions of a glance. Their expressions are changes in altitude and access.

They are not all of one scale.

The youngest occupy single thresholds. They are almost indistinguishable from lintel animals and may, in fact, use those animals as sense organs. They react to footsteps, warmth, spoken distortions, and the direction from which a doorway is crossed. A young resident has little continuity. It is a preference in the stone: stairs that tend to lead toward one another, shadows that gather in a certain room, dust that repeatedly assumes the same shallow outline.

Given time, the preference recruits more of the city.

A nearby courtyard begins closing whenever the threshold is entered. A tower develops windows facing the courtyard. One of the windows acquires a view of a bridge that belongs elsewhere. The bridge migrates until the view becomes accurate. By these slow agreements, an inhabitant grows.

Mature residents may contain entire districts. Their boundaries remain uncertain because they overlap. The same gate can be a joint in one, a memory in another, and a wound in a third. Conflict between them does not resemble violence. It resembles inconsistent geography.

Where two residents desire the same plaza, gravity may divide. One side of the square falls toward the mountain; the other falls toward the sky. Columns multiply according to incompatible sequences. The empty plinth stands between them, belonging to neither. Such disputes can continue for centuries, polishing the plaza smooth with repeated rearrangement.

There are also alliances. These produce the broad, traversable bridges and the long avenues whose rules remain stable for many days. Stability here is not passivity. It is cooperation so exact that movement cancels movement.

The residents have no tools because they cannot distinguish making from being. They alter stone in the way a sleeping creature alters its breathing. A facade forms where attention lingers. A room is exposed when a memory becomes accessible. Retaining walls appear around thoughts that must not spread downhill. Towers lengthen when two distant parts of a resident need to be adjacent without surrendering the distance between them.

Their apparent civic organization follows from this anatomy.

The hexagonal squares are organs of encounter. The dry basins collect not water but convergences: gravity, shadow, pressure, recollection, and the faint residues of speech. The central plinth remains empty because occupation would prevent its function. It is a place reserved for whatever is absent from the meeting.

The surrounding columns register participants. Seven, eleven, thirteen—numbers legible to an observer who still counts objects separately. The uncountable colonnades occur when one of the participants has no stable division into parts.

Meetings may last an afternoon or an age. During them, dust gathers in the basin and forms models of buildings that never stood nearby. These are proposals.

Most collapse.

Some appear the following dim hour on a remote peak.

The residents communicate through alignment. Two towers leaning at the same angle are in conversation, though one may have leaned for ten thousand years before the other replies. A gate opening onto the back of a wall is a refusal. A bridge extended into vacancy is a question. When a whole district exchanges places with another, something has been agreed upon.

Visible speech—the trembling remnants left by travelers—is prized because it offers a more rapid medium. Residents collect these bands in closed rooms and arrange corridors around them. They do not understand words as words. They understand the shape sound gives to space while failing.

A single spoken name may occupy them for centuries.

Because distance increases when named, a name supplies room in which to grow.

This explains why old conversations are often found in chambers too large for their exteriors. The resident has been feeding them space. Some repeat silently against the tongue of anyone who enters. Others have been reduced to pure intention: apology without speaker, warning without danger, affection without an object. These are transported from district to district in rooms that migrate at the darkest hours.

Whether the residents themselves experience anything like emotion is uncertain, but recurrent conditions suggest analogues.

There is a state resembling curiosity, in which streets converge on a traveler while never quite allowing arrival. Doors open a moment before they are noticed. Unsupported plazas drift nearer. Lintel animals contract repeatedly. The city does not trap the traveler; it surrounds the fact of the traveler with increasing precision.

There is a state resembling grief. A district under grief becomes excessively intact. Cracks close. Rounded stairs sharpen. Missing ceilings return. Every building assumes a pristine form that no living witness has seen and that perhaps never existed. Nothing can enter. Afterward, the district sheds entire facades as powder and is less complete than before.

There is a state resembling fear. This occurs when the eastern abyss is occupied.

The great transparent passage beyond the walls is not wholly external to the city. It may be an inhabitant that grew too large to remain supported by stone, or one that abandoned locality altogether. Its body is perceived only through serial concealment: first a remote bridge, then a tower, then a peak, each erased while the nearer air stays clear.

When it passes, all lesser arrangements simplify.

Lintel animals shorten. Folded fliers lock themselves into walls. Crack-flowers withdraw into hair-thin seams. Mature residents release their hold on peripheral streets. Doorways become ordinary holes. Gravity remembers one direction. For several moments the city nearly resembles a ruin.

Then the dry channels fill with darkness flowing upward.

That darkness is not the creature’s shadow. It is the city drawing its distributed awareness away from exposed surfaces and into the mountain. The entire urban order contracts, as an animal might pull blood from a wounded extremity.

The abyssal being never crosses the walls, though “never” may only mean that its crossing has not reached the present terrace.

The oldest inhabitants are scarcely distinct from the mountain range.

One announces itself through elevation tides. Another inhabits the agreement between arches. A third is spread across the pale saltstone and glows faintly in every wafer when a fragment is broken. These beings do not rearrange neighborhoods; neighborhoods are temporary expressions of their relations.

The city may be their colony, their argument, or their reproductive structure.

It may also be one creature in the process of acquiring individuality.

From the highest terrace, patterns can be seen that are invisible below. The isolated towers form a broken spine across separate summits. The bridges reaching into vacancies are not unfinished: they align with structures present during other elevation tides. The hexagonal squares mark joints. The districts that exchange places do so like plates sliding over one another inside an immense, slow articulation.

Even the bare tracts of tilted stone participate. They are pauses between impulses.

Seen in this way, the city’s ongoing rearrangement is neither construction nor erosion. It is metabolism conducted through geometry. The mineral dust is shed tissue. The mobile bridges are tendons seeking attachment. The empty basins are mouths that have outgrown the need to open. The rules—temporary, local, unbreakable—are reflexes.

Yet the image of a single city-creature also fails.

There are too many contrary intentions. Too many doors resist the streets that approach them. Too many towers lean away from the larger pattern. Some neighborhoods labor for ages to separate themselves from the mountain, thinning their walls, loosening their gravity, teaching their plazas to hang unsupported. Others sink inward, becoming denser, older, and less accessible until they are not places but pressures beneath places.

Perhaps individuality here is seasonal.

Perhaps during one arrangement the city is many citizens, during another a single organism, and during another no living thing at all—only stone performing habits left by vanished inhabitants. The categories alternate like weather. Life withdraws from creatures into architecture, from architecture into laws, and from laws into absence. Later it returns by the same route.

A traveler is noticed because a traveler insists on remaining one thing while passing through many places.

The city studies this.

Rooms reshape themselves around the traveler’s shadow. Stairs count the feet that climb them. Speech is stored. Warmth persists briefly in deep-red stones. Dust makes trial bodies in abandoned courtyards: a shoulder suggested by a collapsed arch, a hand assembled from five narrow alleys, a face whose features are entrances.

None endure.

But after a traveler has gone, there are sometimes reports of a new figure at the far end of an avenue. It stands too upright to be a tower and too still to be alive. Looking directly at it reveals only architecture. Looking away permits it one noiseless step closer.

The avenue shortens.

Somewhere, an empty plinth waits among an uncountable number of columns.
