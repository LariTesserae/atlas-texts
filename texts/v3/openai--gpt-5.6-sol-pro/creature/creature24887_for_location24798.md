Life here does not swim. It adheres.

At first it appears only as a failure of perfect cleanliness: a green thread overlooked in a high mortar seam, a translucent stain inside a stamped rosette, a slickness on one yellow tile where the shaft-light falls strongest. These growths have no thickness from a distance. They seem painted onto the construction. Only when the current slackens do their edges rise and disclose themselves—films finer than wet silk, trembling against the glaze.

They occupy what the builders’ tools left behind. A bubble in the mortar becomes a sheltered hollow. A comb mark becomes a row of tiny green chambers. Moss gathers where two bricks meet at an angle too narrow for a reed brush. It survives not by spreading freely but by retreating into the dimensions of workmanship: the depth of a maker’s stamp, the lip of a pipe collar, the shadow behind a ceramic fin.

Most of it is periodically erased. Brushes peel the films away in soft sheets. Lime pales the seams. For a short while the chamber returns to blue, white, and yellow alone. Then spores and fragments arrive with the filtered water, small enough to pass through cloth and charcoal, and the green begins again. It does not reclaim the reservoir. It merely resumes its few permissions.

The nearest thing to a true resident stands beneath the central light shaft.

It is a cluster of reeds, though underwater they have lost the posture by which reeds are usually known. They do not stand upright in wind or lift pointed leaves above a bank. Their roots grip baked clay pellets inside a shallow ceramic basin, and their shoots rise only a little before the current bends them westward. The leaves trail horizontally, pale green near their bases and nearly transparent at their tips. Fine veins show through them when they cross the shaft’s white brightness.

The whole cluster is small enough to encircle with two hands. In still water it would resemble a sheaf laid on its side. Under the usual flow it becomes a streaming shape, every leaf aligned with the invisible road toward the drains. The strongest morning draw presses it almost flat against the tiles. At noon it loosens. Individual blades lift, twist, and slowly exchange places in the broad revolving currents. When the inflow lessens in the evening, the cluster seems to breathe outward.

It was planted there to be watched.

The keepers read it as they read the settling troughs and pressure knocks. New roots should remain cream-colored among the clay pellets. Young shoots should have a clear green cast. Brown tips may mean too much copper has passed the collars. A general paling may indicate excessive lime. A soft darkening at the base warns that the current has weakened and sediment is collecting where it should not. The reeds are not ornament and not quite an instrument, but a living reaction made visible.

They bear the reservoir on their bodies. Mineral traces form faint rims along older leaves. Bubbles from the inflow lodge briefly in the angles between blades, turning the cluster silver-spotted, then shake loose and rush against the vault. Grains of grit abrade the west-facing edges. Sometimes a leaf splits lengthwise and continues trailing in two narrow ribbons. Sometimes a shoot works free of the pellets; if it is not caught in a grate, a weighted basket retrieves it before it can enter the baths.

Nothing tends the reeds continuously. There is no gardener below. Their care arrives as interruption: a diver’s hand testing the roots, bronze scissors removing a softened blade, fresh pellets poured into the basin during draining. On cleaning days, when the water lowers step by step and air enters the chamber, the cluster changes more dramatically than anything around it. Leaves accustomed to being carried become suddenly heavy. They collapse onto the wet tile in a pale heap. Workers splash them with reservoir water while they scrub nearby, because the sun descending through the shaft can dry their thin submerged growth quickly.

Then the gates reopen. Water climbs the steps, fills the grit pits, covers the basin, and lifts the leaves one after another. The reeds recover their shape only by surrendering it to the current.

Around them live the lesser growths: moss in protected cuts, green threads stitched along seams, microbial films too thin to possess a visible color until the light strikes sideways. Together they consume what little the filtered water offers. They take dissolved gases from the incoming turbulence and light from the nine shafts. Their world has no weather except the opening of gates, no seasons except cleaning days, no night except the passage of shadows over the courtyard glass.

Humans enter, but they do not belong to the chamber in the same way. Divers arrive tethered and purposeful, carrying scrapers and wax, their bodies enormous among the low ridges. They inspect, strike, bind, clear, and leave. The submerged plants endure between those visits, holding to the small flaws and designated pockets of the masonry.

So what lives in the reservoir is neither hidden beast nor flourishing garden. It is a sparse, repeatedly corrected greenness: one planted cluster asked to testify about the water, and many smaller lives surviving where inspection cannot quite reach. Everything else that seems animate—the throbbing pipes, the shuddering light, the crawling reflections, the chamber’s deep complaint—is water and workmanship in motion.
