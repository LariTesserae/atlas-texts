The first inhabitants one notices are the ones that mistake disturbance for weather.

A foot placed on the basin floor sends tremors through the stone, and the midges rise at once, arranging themselves into thin, upright congregations. They are nearly invisible individually: a clear abdomen, two smoky wings, legs finer than fern hairs. Together they become dark pillars that bend without breaking. Each pillar occupies a particular shaft of reflected warmth. When sunlight moves, they move. When one of the buried structures stirs, they gather over stone that still appears inert, sometimes hours before water emerges there.

The gliders come next. They roost upside down in the high fractures, packed together like overlapping seed husks. They are not quite lizards, not quite mammals. A membrane runs from each of four forelimbs to the flanks, while the smaller rear pair grip the rock and groom the flight skin. Their bodies are russet above and pearl-gray below, rendering them difficult to follow as they turn between red wall and open sky. Their shrieks are partly echolocation, partly alarm, and partly participation in the basin’s workings. A flock circling anticlockwise produces different echoes than one circling clockwise. The arches react to both.

No glider understands this. Yet the flock has rituals that amount to maintenance. At midday it divides into seven groups, each wheeling briefly above one of the arches. If an arch returns the wrong echo, the animals mob it for hours, clinging to its rim and filling it with cries until its temperature changes.

Under the ledges live the narrow frogs.

They look unfinished: long toes, translucent bellies, heads scarcely wider than their spines. Their colors belong to wet stone—tea-brown, mineral pink, algae-black—except when they call. Then loose skin at the throat inflates into vivid sacs of blue, yellow, or white. Different species produce knocks, trumpet notes, dry clicks, and a deep resonant burr impossible to reconcile with their size.

Their eggs are more peculiar than the adults. Some are laid in water that has not yet arrived. A female presses her clutch to the dry inner surface of an arch, where the eggs remain glossy and alive through days of heat. When water finally passes out of that opening, the clutch loosens all at once. The tadpoles hatch into a pool whose rain may have fallen weeks before, or whose condensation belongs to the following morning. Consequently, siblings do not always emerge together. A brood can be divided across several seasons without any egg truly remaining dormant.

The frogs call less to attract mates than to locate the present. A note returned immediately is ordinary. A note returning late, or from the wrong wall, tells them which pools are safe to use.

The pools themselves contain whole faunas.

Black fry move through them in coordinated bursts, vanishing under a mineral lip in one bowl and appearing beneath algae in another. They have blunt faces and sensory pits arranged in rings around their eyes. Their eggs adhere to water rather than stone: if a pool is drawn through one of the arches, the eggs go with its continuity even when its volume emerges elsewhere. Two fish swimming side by side may therefore have hatched in pools that never visibly touched.

Among them graze spiral-shelled creatures whose shells incorporate flakes of red gneiss. Flat larvae lie over the bottom like scraps of transparent leaf. Thread-worms knot themselves together when a shadow passes, becoming a single trembling mesh too large for most mouths. Fingernail crabs patrol the runnels. They are milk-pale except for a bright internal fleck that travels through the body as they digest. Their raised claws are largely theatrical, but the performance is tireless. They challenge droplets, mites, pollen grains, one another, and occasionally their own delayed reflections in the wet arches.

Every pool has predators. Needle-jawed nymphs hang beneath the surface. Diving beetles carry silver bubbles under their wing cases. Soft-bodied ambushers disguise themselves with algae and rasp at the stone, producing the basin’s slow sawing noise. Few creatures remain predators throughout their lives. The raspers eventually split open and release delicate winged adults that do not eat at all. The hunted fry become, for several nights during spawning, avid consumers of frog eggs. Roles pass around the basin more reliably than bodies do.

On the open rock moves the dewback.

It spends daylight folded into a wall fissure, its long body compressed almost flat. After sunset it emerges on six splayed feet. Each foot ends in a circular pad crossed by microscopic ridges, allowing it to cling to polished stone even under running water. Flexible ribs rise along its back like the slats of a half-closed fan. These cool rapidly in the night air. Moisture condenses on them and runs through channels toward its mouth, though much of it spills behind, feeding mites and minute mosses wherever the animal rests.

The dewback grazes by extending a broad, dark tongue over the rock. Its feeding paths are clean crescents through the algae, so regular that they can resemble writing. The marks do not last. By the next evening a green haze has begun to reclaim them.

Orange hunters attend it. They are quick, many-legged animals with narrow heads and tails held upright. They do not attack the grazer. Instead they race along the fresh arcs, taking exposed larvae and pale organisms uncovered by its tongue. The dewback sometimes pauses until they catch up. Whether this is tolerance, habit, or some deeper dependency is unclear. If the orange hunters are absent, it grazes less and returns early to its crack. If the grazer fails to emerge, they gather at the fissure and tap the wall with their tails.

The largest regular visitor belongs partly to another world.

It comes down only after heavy changes in pressure: a low plated animal with a wedge-shaped snout and feet made for both digging and adhesion. Dust from the plateau whitens its armor. It drinks from the pools in a fixed order, ignoring those that appear freshest, and tests each with a pair of tendrils beneath its jaw. Under the central shelf it lays a translucent chain of eggs, linking them end to end with mucus tough enough to resist the crabs.

Its arrival reorganizes the basin. Frogs shift upward. Beetles seal themselves in bark. The dewback avoids the central floor. Tiny parasites hatch from the plated animal’s footprints and persist for weeks after it has gone. Most of its eggs are eaten, but this may be their purpose. The few that survive produce soft, many-gilled young that enter the disk at the center of the basin. No juvenile has ever been seen climbing back to the plateau.

There are plants, though even these behave less like scenery than occupants.

The silver-barked trees pulse water down their roots at dawn and draw it back after midday. Beetle galleries in the dead branches whistle when wind crosses them. The sharply bounded ferns release spores only during particular combinations of frog calls. Orchids flower extravagantly into empty space because their pollinators are not present every year; some arrive from an arch as dry, wing-folded husks, revive in the humid air, visit one knot of flowers, and disappear again.

Even the crimson fungus is busy. Its visible body is a single hard fan, but hairlike filaments extend deep into a mineral seam and touch the base of the thin column. When the column darkens, the fungus releases a faint odor of bruised fruit. Beetles wake. Frogs reposition themselves. Within minutes, one of the wall bowls usually receives water.

Then there are the seven gray arches, the column, and the disk.

It is difficult to say whether they should be counted among the inhabitants. They do not grow, yet their dimensions alter by fractions. They do not heal, yet scratches disappear. They possess no visible organs, but each has a different disposition. One arch accepts water readily and returns it as cold mist. Another delays almost everything. The half-rooted arch produces small warmths that the tree has learned to follow. The arch in the pool vibrates when fry gather beneath it, as though purring.

Perhaps they are tools. Perhaps they are shells left by something absent. Perhaps they are the durable organs of a creature whose soft tissues have been replaced, generation by generation, by the life of the basin.

That last possibility is difficult to dismiss. The animals do not merely use the structures. Together they complete them. Wings supply vibration. Frog calls provide intervals. Roots measure moisture. Grazers erase algal films that would alter the stone’s response. Predators regulate the callers; parasites regulate the predators. The plated visitor carries something in and takes something out. Even panic has a function: before storms, the sudden silence exposes a missing frequency, and the arches hum to fill it.

Thus the basin’s largest inhabitant may be the basin-wide arrangement itself.

It has no single skin. Its boundary is the narrow opening above. It drinks through mineral seams, breathes through cracks, remembers in delayed water, and thinks—if thinking is the word—through recurrent relations among thousands of small lives. Individual creatures are replaceable, but not arbitrary. Each occupies a place in a pattern older than its species. Over generations, bodies have been shaped to fit timings and channels no ordinary landscape would provide.

Yet it is not a harmonious creature. It teems with hunger, failed broods, parasitism, error, and noise. Parts of it eat other parts. Signals are missed. Water returns too late. An arch sometimes waits for a call belonging to an extinct frog, humming unanswered until some new species blunders close enough to the old note.

Then, briefly, the entire basin changes its rhythm.

A pool warms. Midges lift. Black fry gather beneath stone. The dewback raises its ribbed spine to falling moisture. One small frog answers a sound that has waited in the rock longer than frogs of its kind have existed.

And something, distributed among water, wing, root, echo, and gray seamless matter, recognizes the answer.
