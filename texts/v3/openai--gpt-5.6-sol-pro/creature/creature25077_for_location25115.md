The first impression is that the warm cistern is inhabited by armor.

Everywhere the walls appear to have loosened into small, deliberate pieces. Black chips advance across black tile. Silver flecks gather under the conduit. Along the inspection filaments, pinpricks of amber and blue detach from the glow and move against it. Only when the rain begins does the multitude reveal itself: thousands of shellbacks lowering their bodies, spreading their fine legs, and fastening themselves to the chamber with such precision that the water divides around them.

A shellback has no permanent color. Its true body is soft, pale, and nearly without features, tucked beneath a roof assembled from the place where it lives. With rasping mouthparts it harvests the loosest grains of ceramic, mineral scale, oxidized metal, sealant, discarded shell, and repair compound. Internal organs sort these particles by size and thermal behavior. Along the animal’s back, glands soften and weld them into overlapping plates.

The result is less like a shell than a sample of local architecture taught to crawl.

Young shellbacks are almost transparent. The pulse of their organs can be seen through them, quickening and slowing with the eleven-minute cycle. As they age they become difficult to distinguish from the surface beneath them. Those living on the dark ribs grow matte, heat-resistant roofs. Those beside the atmospheric conduit carry reflective plates that shed excess warmth. Animals grazing near old warning marks often incorporate luminous dust, and travel at nightlike phases as dim moving characters whose original meanings have been lost.

They do not merely camouflage themselves. Their shells regulate heat, conduct vibration, collect condensation, and carry microorganisms in tiny protected pits. A shellback is both animal and terrain. Smaller creatures graze its back. Eggs are laid under its plates. Pale seam-threads sometimes root in cracks along its spine and remain there for the rest of its life.

They live by counting vibration.

The cistern’s roar contains distinctions no human ear could separate: the approach of rain, the engagement of the cooling membranes, the closure of pressure gates, the distant state of pumps. Shellbacks feel these through every gripping leg. A fraction of a moment before a resonance event, an entire wall population stills at once. Their limbs contract. Their armor settles against the surface. Moving patches of grazed ceramic become a single rough coating, and the animals endure the shaking as though they were merely another sacrificial layer.

When the vibration passes, the wall comes apart again.

Between the shellbacks are the thread-eels. They are so thin that several can travel side by side through the groove around a bolt head. Their bodies are clear except for a dark digestive line and a chain of bright sensory knots. In open water they swim with rapid lateral strokes, but most of the cistern’s water is too shallow or brief to be trusted. Instead they exude a sleeve of mucus around themselves and carry their own wet passage over the walls.

A thread-eel does not navigate by sight. It tastes gradients: heat, dissolved metals, microbial density, the chemical signatures of predators, and the faint electrical difference between old composite and newly repaired material. Its world is a map of favorable dissolutions.

Pressure is dangerous to them. Before the gates close, thread-eels wrap themselves around mesh crossings in tight living knots. Sometimes hundreds gather at the same intersection, silver cords braided through manufactured lattice. Their mucus catches grit and structural compounds. Over years, such knots thicken the mesh. The mountain’s repair system often reinforces them, mistaking the living accumulation for useful fiber. By the time the eels abandon one of these refuges, it may have become a permanent brace.

Some never leave. Their descendants inhabit the hollows inside the hardened mucus of their ancestors.

Above the gutters drift the fan-wings. They are nearly weightless animals with compact bodies and several pleated membranes arranged around them like open hands. They cannot support themselves in still air. If taken from the cistern they collapse, incapable of flight. Here, however, the standing pressure waves hold them aloft.

They rise when warm vapor meets the cooling membranes. Whole clouds appear from behind grilles, each animal making minute adjustments to the angle of its fans. They occupy invisible layers in the air: one species suspended close to the ceiling, another turning slowly above the pools, another traveling in thin horizontal streams between opposing ribs. They filter bacteria, organic aerosols, flakes of microbial film, and the fine debris of molting shellbacks.

During the wet phase they reproduce in flight. Pairs catch one another at the edge of a pressure node and rotate together, passing packets of eggs that are then pressed into droplets. The droplets fall. Most strike unsuitable surfaces or vanish into drains, but some lodge in pores, where the larvae feed until the next cycle lifts them free.

Their predators hang above them.

The ceiling animals have no hard parts and almost no symmetry. Each is a warm-colored mass folded into a cavity, with its body arranged around whatever space the architecture provides. One may be long because it occupies a seam; another becomes flat within the back of a grille; a third grows into several connected chambers and can no longer be determined to be one individual or a colony.

They lower adhesive feeding lines into the mist. These lines resemble strands of condensation, and the machinery’s constant trembling disguises their movement. A fan-wing that touches one is held without any obvious strike. The line contracts slowly, drawing the prey upward into darkness.

The ceiling animals are blind, but not insensible. Their entire skin listens. They distinguish fan-wings from droplets by the irregularity of their impacts, and can sever a line before a heavy fall of water tears it loose. Old individuals maintain dozens of threads at different lengths. Their cavities become crowded with transparent husks, discarded adhesive, dormant eggs, and the minute scavengers that live on whatever the hunter fails to digest.

On the floor and beneath drain lips wait the six-limbed hunters.

They are flat enough to enter gaps that seem closed. Four limbs grip; two remain raised, lightly touching the metal. Through them the hunter follows disturbances across an entire panel. A shellback rasping at the wall, a thread-eel crossing a seam, or a droplet striking a loose plate each produces a different pattern.

The hunter does not chase. It places itself where the pattern will arrive.

When prey comes within reach, its body unfolds with startling speed. The front limbs are not jaws but cages. They close around the victim and hold it against a ventral mouth lined with flexible grinding pads. Shellbacks are eaten from beneath. Their roofs remain intact and are left behind as empty pieces of architecture.

These empty shells are valuable places. Eggs are laid beneath them during the brief dry interval. Thread-eels shelter in their drainage notches. Seam-plants send roots through their thinner joints. Small shellbacks pry off plates to use in their own armor. If an abandoned shell remains in place long enough, repair compounds collect around its edge and the cistern adopts it. What began as one animal’s back becomes a permanent blister in the floor, then a refuge, then a channel junction.

The long-legged hammer crabs are less patient. They emerge during resonance, when other animals are clamped down and the whole chamber shudders. Their limbs are narrow and jointed high above the body, keeping their delicate organs clear of turbulent water. One forelimb ends in a dense striking knob made from scavenged composite.

As the walls flex, a hammer crab listens for the tiny difference between a shellback gripping livingly and a patch of inert wall. It strikes at the edge of the shell. The blow is timed to the chamber’s own movement. Large shellbacks are levered loose and swept into gutters, where the crab follows them.

They are among the few inhabitants that seem noisy even against the machinery. A feeding group produces an erratic tapping within the deeper roar. Their territories are marked not with scent but with dents: rhythmic signatures beaten into sacrificial panels. Other hammer crabs read the age, strength, and recent health of the maker by touching these marks.

Yet none of these creatures is the cistern’s most consequential inhabitant.

That distinction belongs to something barely recognizable as an animal.

In the oldest seams are pale, branching tissues called, by those few who have studied them, seam-laces. At first they resemble the sparse plants that open under inspection light. They are not plants. They have no leaves and derive almost nothing from illumination. Their threads penetrate porous metal, old adhesive, mineral film, and the hardened mucus of thread-eels. They feed on chemical differences between adjacent materials and on the microbial communities sustained there.

A seam-lace grows slowly across generations. No central body can be found. Its threads swell into nodules during the dry half-minute and release tiny soft carriers that crawl or are washed into neighboring cracks. Most die. A few establish new networks. When two networks meet, they may fuse, exchange chemical patterns, or poison the seam between them until water opens a new boundary.

Seam-laces alter flow. They narrow some pores and enlarge others. They collect dissolved compounds around damage, accelerating repairs in certain places while delaying them in others. Shellbacks graze their outer tissue but depend on the films that grow over it. Thread-eels follow their chemical gradients. Predators settle near the traffic they create.

The mountain’s maintenance systems respond to seam-laces as though they were irregularities in the material. The seam-laces respond to maintenance as weather. Over centuries, each has learned the other without knowing it.

Where a seam-lace persists, the wall changes around it. Repair meshes curve to preserve its most active branches. Drainage is redirected through its channels. Its dead inner tissues are mineralized and become indistinguishable from old sealant. Eventually the lace may cease to be alive in any ordinary sense, yet the pattern it made continues to govern water, and descendants occupy that inherited pattern.

Thus the largest organism in the cistern may be one that has been dead for a thousand years and is still shaping every rainfall.

Life here has no clean exterior. Every creature borrows part of the chamber to complete its body. Shellbacks wear it. Thread-eels carry dissolved versions of it in their sleeves. Fan-wings ride its breath. Ceiling hunters use its vibration as concealment. Hammer crabs make its resonance part of their strike. Seam-laces enter its pores and become routes the repair system preserves.

Even their sense of time belongs to the machinery.

They do not know a day beyond eleven minutes. Their metabolisms accelerate with the incoming warmth, brace during pressure closure, and reorganize in the short exposed interval after the pumps withdraw the pools. Courtship, molting, egg-laying, hatching, hunting, and retreat are not spread smoothly across life. Each has its proper seconds.

For a shellback, the dry interval is spring.

For a thread-eel, the rising gutter is migration.

For a fan-wing, the moment when vapor thickens is dawn.

There are disruptions: delayed gates, clogged pores, maintenance pulses that bring unfamiliar light. To the inhabitants these are droughts, winters, eclipses, and catastrophes. Afterward, surviving colonies expand into newly opened surfaces. Within a few hundred cycles the exposed material is filmed with microbes. Within thousands it is crossed by grazers and mucus paths. In time, repeated movement becomes drainage; drainage becomes reinforced; reinforcement becomes wall.

Death follows the same rule as life. Bodies are rarely removed whole. They are eaten, dissolved, abraded, mineralized, or built over. A shell plate may pass through dozens of animals before the mountain seals it into a rib. The adhesive thread of a ceiling hunter may become the first fiber in a new microbial mat. A hammer crab’s striking limb may lie in a gutter, divert water, gather sediment, shelter eggs, and ultimately establish the course of a channel.

Nothing remains itself, but almost nothing entirely disappears.

Seen during mist, the cistern’s inhabitants seem like a dense, nervous population clinging to a machine. Seen over centuries, they are slower and stranger: a living process by which the chamber explores new shapes.

The animals inhabit the mountain.

The mountain incorporates their habits.

And because anything persistent becomes structural, the oldest inhabitants no longer live merely inside the warm cistern.

They are part of what holds it together.
