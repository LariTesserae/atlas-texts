At first, it seems that the inhabitants are waiting.

The long-bodied ones wait with their heads lowered to the wet floors. The winged ones wait beneath cable bridges. The shelled ones wait in recesses, sealed so neatly into the metal that they resemble fasteners. Black fish wait in the canals, each held at an exact distance from the next. Great armored drinkers wait beside the sumps. Buried things wait under whole districts, warm enough to distort measurements but too deep and slow to be seen.

Yet waiting implies an event to come, and nothing here expects one.

They are not idle. They are keeping their places.

No species has a single form independent of its station. A shell taken from a drainage lip has the shape of that lip’s recess, down to the channels through which runoff passes. Its pale valves contain flecks of ceramic and alloy. Its soft interior is almost featureless except for a ring of cilia, a clasping muscle, and a minute branching organ that tastes the water for deviations. When the rain carries too much nickel, all the shells along one watershed close in sequence. Far below, a valve changes position. When the concentration returns to tolerance, they open again.

Whether the shells command the valve or merely report to it is impossible to determine. Their nerves enter the wall.

The herds on the flats are more recognizably animal, though only from a distance. They have hides, lungs, paired eyes, warm blood. Young stand beside adults. Their ears turn toward sound. They sleep.

Up close, their familiarity comes apart.

Their broad mouths are lined not with teeth but with nested sieves, each finer than the last. Condensation passes through chambers furred with translucent symbionts. Salts gather in one organ, metallic dust in another. The purified water leaves through pores along the flanks and returns to the floor as a delicate mist. Beneath the armor of the palate, stamped membranes carry identification marks matching those on nearby pumps.

They do not crop anything. They harvest the thinnest possible difference between one part of the system and another.

A herd may spend a lifetime moving no farther than the breadth of a plate. The adults stand over etched intake lines; the young fit between them, aligned with narrower grooves. Their legs differ subtly according to location. Those born near expansion joints have broad, divided feet. Those beside induction rails possess dense insulating pads. Herds beneath gantries grow pale reflective patches along their backs, completing patterns used by machinery overhead.

They appear to reproduce. Courtship occurs during warm rain, when the adults touch faces and exchange resonant pulses through the flooded floor. Gestation follows. A small, wet creature eventually emerges and is cleaned by the others.

But the timing is too exact. Birth begins only after a distant member of the herd has died or entered a removal bay. The newborn’s weight matches the missing animal’s final recorded mass within a few grams. Its markings correspond not to either parent but to the vacant position.

Sometimes there is no pregnancy. A hatch opens before dawn, and a young animal steps out already dry.

The canal fish are less individual still. A single one removed from its school becomes limp, colorless, and almost empty. Its organs are provisional folds. Its muscles twitch without coordination. Returned to the water, it is taken immediately into the moving geometry of the others, and anatomy seems to flow back into it. A gill opens. The black membrane brightens. The body resumes its portion of the school’s continuous tension.

The school is the animal.

Its thousands of bodies form a flexible surface over the canal. Messages pass through pressure, contact, and changes in reflectivity. At junctions, the school divides with the measured precision of fluid entering calibrated pipes. In narrow channels it lengthens. In reservoirs it spreads into tessellated sheets. Individuals die continually at the lower edge, where flat crustaceans dismantle them. New fish emerge continually at the upper edge from grilles too narrow to admit them.

Nothing swims upstream or downstream in any meaningful sense. The black animal changes which bodies compose it.

The crustaceans beneath are patient specialists in inscription. They occupy numbers, arrows, serial marks, and alignment crosses. Their carapaces mimic the symbols they inhabit, not merely in outline but in color and depth. An animal settled in the hooked lower half of a numeral remains there through molt after molt, trimming itself to the groove. If displaced into a different character, it attempts to bend into its old shape until retrieved by a pale many-jointed collector.

They keep the markings clean.

They may also be the markings.

Surveyors have found channels in which no engraved serial number lies beneath the animals. Lift them away, and the ceramic is blank. Return them, and the correct designation reappears.

Above, the hanging fliers distribute weight, heat, and electrical charge. Their wings are functional but secondary. Their true organs are the claws: layered structures of horn, coppery tissue, and living insulation, sensitive to fluctuations too small for instruments mounted nearby. Each animal grips a cable at a prescribed interval. Together they damp vibration, bleed unwanted charge, and warm the line with their blood.

They sleep in shifts so subtle that the span never changes its load. When one must feed, molt, give birth, or die, another crosses to its position in three strokes. The departing animal travels only after its replacement has released from elsewhere. Vacancy moves through the colony like a pulse, but no cable is ever briefly bare.

Their rare flights are not journeys. They are rearrangements.

Predators live among them, though predation here has lost its urgency. Long, narrow creatures cling to the shadowed sides of towers, their skins the texture of soundproofing. They descend only when a hanging animal’s temperature drops below a designated threshold. Sometimes they settle beside it hours before death. They rest their muzzles against its folded wing and wait.

When the final signal passes through the claws, the predator bites cleanly through the neck. It consumes the body without struggle, carrying nutrients to a digestion chamber connected by a flexible intestinal filament to the tower wall. Bones, membranes, trace metals, and excess heat are separated inside it. Nothing becomes waste. Nothing falls.

Such predators do not choose prey. Yet they watch, and their watching has acquired expressions.

Some appear mournful. Some seem tender. One may groom the doomed animal before killing it, drawing its tongue over the wing hinges in slow, careful strokes. Whether this tenderness is feeling or maintenance makes no difference to the one being touched.

The largest surface animals are habitats as much as creatures. The columnar ones planted in assembly basins have calcified dorsal masses incorporated into walkways. Railings terminate in their bone. Drainpipes enter porous openings along their sides. Their feet penetrate several decks and spread into foundations full of warm brine.

Within them, whole ecologies occupy the distances between breaths.

Transparent worms clean the blood channels. Blind shelled young cluster around mineral glands. Tiny fliers migrate through the lung cavities according to pressure cycles. A single opening of the great animal’s eye changes the local light enough to begin or end generations within it.

These giants are believed to move. Old survey diagrams place certain individuals a fraction of a meter from their current positions. No tracks have been found. Perhaps they walk only while the surrounding structures are dismantled and replaced, so that the world moves around each step.

Far beneath them are the inhabitants for whom there are no reliable bodies.

Workers call them the Underherd, though nothing proves plurality. Heat maps suggest overlapping shapes hundreds of kilometers long. Pressure sensors record slow contractions traveling between districts. Fluids classified as lubricants contain cells. Bundles labeled as communication trunk lines respond to anesthetic. In certain galleries, maintenance doors open onto surfaces that are warm, ridged, and faintly yielding. These surfaces have been called skin, insulation, lining, and interface substrate, depending on which department made the report.

All terms remain in use.

The buried life may consist of immense animals threaded through the installation. It may be one animal whose organs are mistaken for factories. It may be the continental works itself, maintaining a population above in the same way that the surface herds maintain films of symbionts in their mouths.

Its eggs are assembled rather than laid. Steel vessels receive layers of gel, braided nerves, mineral lattice, and folded tissue from separate production lines. No moment can be identified when the contents become alive. Before assembly they respond to stimuli. After assembly they bear serial numbers. The vessels descend on freight elevators and do not return.

At irregular intervals—irregular only by scales shorter than a century—the floors rise almost imperceptibly.

Then every surface creature pauses.

Mouths lift from condensation. Canal schools darken. Hanging colonies tighten their claws. Predators stop over their appointed dead. Even rain appears to hesitate, caught in tremors too slight to see except as a change in reflected light.

Something below turns in its sleep, or a pump changes phase, or an embryo passes through a buried gate.

Afterward, the inhabitants resume.

There may once have been a distinction between organisms made for the installation and organisms that entered it. If so, it has not survived. Every lineage now joins the works somewhere. Blood exchanges heat with cooling loops. Nerves share housings with wires. Skeletons bear load. Gut flora refine industrial fluids. Instincts correspond to maintenance schedules. Bodies inherit tolerances as other animals might inherit seasons.

And still they are animals.

They dream. The fliers twitch their folded wings. Herds sleeping on warm ceramic make low movements in their throats. Canal fish, in darkness, send unnecessary ripples through the school—patterns with no routing function and no corrective value. The great columnar beings sometimes alter their internal pressure in sequences that repeat for years but actuate nothing.

There are gestures the installation does not seem to require.

A grazer may raise its head during rain and hold its mouth open, letting clean water spill unused over the sieves. Two crustaceans may exchange engraved places and remain incorrectly arranged until collectors separate them. A predator may refuse a scheduled body, forcing another to descend. A winged animal may fly past the vacancy intended for it and circle once in the ordinary daylight.

Such departures are small. The system absorbs them. Another mouth lowers. Another numeral is occupied. Another hunter feeds. The circling flier returns to a cable, and the distribution of weight becomes exact again.

Nothing accumulates into rebellion, evolution, history, or loss.

But perhaps these unnecessary acts are what live there most fully: not the shells, herds, schools, colonies, giants, or buried organs, but the minute surplus within them—the impulse that rises without function and is corrected without ever being entirely designed away.

For one circuit of three silent wingbeats, an animal crosses the bright manufactured sky with no work in its body.

Then it grips the waiting cable.

The pattern closes around it.
