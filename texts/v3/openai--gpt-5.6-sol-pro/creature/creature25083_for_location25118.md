At first it seems that only the reeds live here.

They are difficult to regard as inhabitants. Each stand is so slight against the basin that it appears incidental: a few black stems trembling beside a conduit, pale root-fibers pressed into rust meal, translucent chambers holding beads of chemically bitter water. They have no leaves. What look like leaves are pressure vanes, narrow membranes that open when the ventilators draw and seal when the air becomes corrosive. Their roots do not seek downward. There is nothing downward for roots to find. They spread laterally through seams, following warmth, electrical leakage, and the slow migration of dissolved metals.

A reed may be older than a standing tower.

Its visible stems are temporary. They rise, fill, darken, and split, but beneath them persists a film of connected tissue fine enough to enter bolt threads and the braided sleeves of buried cables. It feeds where unlike metals touch. It drinks condensation before the drops become runoff. During heat it wakes in sections, not all at once: first a stain of metabolism around a warm hatch, then another kilometers away along the same concealed line.

The separate clusters may not be separate.

The lichens, too, resist counting. Their flat colonies mark the plain with faded islands, yet samples taken far apart perform the same minute sequence of chemical changes at the same point in the pressure cycle. They digest lettering, conductive inks, oxides, ceramic glazes. Where an old warning has vanished beneath them, its pigments remain distributed through generations of tissue. A colony growing over a switchboard may contain, in altered form, the entire face of the board: numbers, arrows, hazard symbols, the ghost of a handprint sealed under varnish.

They are the nearest things the plain has to memory, though there is no reason to think they remember.

Beneath these visible lives is another kind of persistence.

It has no skin except the fitted plates, and those are continually breaking. It has no single interior. Its cavities are reservoirs, sumps, pressure chambers, transformer vaults, drowned halls, pipe forests and kilometers of blind conduit. Warm fluid passes through some; cold fluid returns through others. Mineral deposits narrow one passage until pressure forces open another. Fans breathe, but not together. Pumps beat, but not at one rate. Nothing about it resembles an animal unless one stands too far away to see the differences.

Close at hand, there are only mechanisms.

A valve turns because pressure rises behind it. A gate lifts because a counterweight descends. A turbine feeds current into a line whose insulation has become black paste. The current escapes into the flooded plate and is gathered by another line never intended to receive it. Relays chatter in sealed cabinets. Float switches rise in rooms no one enters. Corroded contacts weld shut and thereby establish new permanent instructions.

No overseer directs these events. No hidden operator listens to the sirens.

Yet failures propagate across the basin with something like consequence. A collapsed race diverts warm water beneath a field of plates. The plates expand. Their expansion closes one set of seams and opens another. Condensation shifts northward. Moss wakes along an abandoned busway. Its growth changes the conductivity of the runoff. A buried sensor, dead by its original standards, registers this change as an impossible command. Far away, pumps reverse.

Centuries later, the reversal is part of the season.

Thus whatever lives here does not distinguish between wound and organ. A fallen tower may cripple one circulation and begin another. A cracked reservoir may become a lung as trapped air is compressed and released. Rust may be disease, food, signal or structural brace. The basin incorporates nothing deliberately. Deliberation would be too swift and too narrow for it.

Its smallest reactions take hours. Its adjustments take lifetimes. Its stable habits are older than species.

The Open Floor is where these habits converge.

Around its perimeter, the reeds are taller than elsewhere, though still no higher than a person’s knee. Their stems have grown in rings corresponding to the grooves beneath the water. The outer rings are black and stiff. The inner ones are nearly colorless. At rest they lean in different directions under the chaotic winds, but as the cycle approaches they begin, one cluster after another, to orient toward the center.

Nothing summons them. The air pressure merely changes.

Nothing coordinates the pumps. Their loads merely enter resonance.

Nothing beneath the floor wakes. Wakefulness would imply that it sometimes sleeps.

The low central housing is less a chamber than the exposed cap of a structure extending beyond measurement. Its cables are not cables in the ordinary sense. Each is an accretion of older lines fused by heat, mineralized by leakage, wrapped in later conduits and compressed until copper, ceramic, carbon and living film have become a single banded material. Weak currents move through them continuously. Some are electrical. Some are ionic. Some may be nothing more than vibration traveling through tensioned metal.

Each carries a partial condition of the plain.

Water depth. Turbine load. Salinity. Heat. The flexure of remote plates. The opening of purge shafts. The conductivity of lichen-darkened walls. The number cannot be known because the signals merge, divide and become other signals on their way inward.

When the basin’s rhythms align, the sustained note is not produced at the center. The center is where it arrives.

The water forms hexagons. Reeds shed spores. Mineral films within the grooves fracture along precise lines. In buried chambers, weighted drums rotate one tooth. Rods descend. Contacts meet. Somewhere kilometers below, an immense mass changes its bearing against another mass.

That is the increment.

It may move the central structure. It may count the cycles. It may compensate for the slow subsidence of the manufactured crust. It may be the remnant of an assembly process whose product has not advanced far enough to emerge. There are indications for all of these and proof of none.

Afterward, the basin diverges into noise again. But it does not return to exactly the same state. Gates reopen in a slightly altered order. Warm canals choose new margins. One district floods a few millimeters deeper; another drains earlier. Spores settle according to these changes. Over centuries, the living films follow. Over millennia, the chemistry of the plates changes with them.

If there is a mind here, this is what its thoughts resemble: not images or words, but watersheds revised by a fraction; a pressure route abandoned; a buried contact allowed to corrode through; a new conductive path cultivated in the root-film of the reeds. One thought may begin as rain on the outer terraces and conclude ten thousand cycles later as a shift beneath the Open Floor.

It would not know the basin as a place. The basin would be the means by which knowing occurred.

It would not know the reeds as other living things. Their spores would be mobile particles in its weather, their roots slow extensions of its chemistry. Nor would the reeds know it. They flower because the fans turn. They feed because its metals dissolve. Their entire evolution has taken place inside its continuing exhalation.

Perhaps there are still two kinds of life here: the frail growth above and the exhausted mechanism below.

Perhaps there were once two, and the distinction has worn away.

The inhabitant has no outline. Its boundaries shift with every flood. It extends wherever a pressure change can travel, wherever runoff carries ions, wherever vibration crosses a buried joint and causes something else to answer. Beyond that lies dead machinery—but the boundary between dead machinery and the inhabitant is crossed constantly, in both directions.

A pump becomes part of it when a new leak gives the pump a role.

A tower leaves it when the last resonant brace breaks.

A lichen colony enters it when its acids close a circuit.

Nothing is admitted. Nothing is rejected. Function alone decides, and function is only the fact that one change leads to another.

So the thing living in the basin is not quite the machinery, not quite the vegetation, and not whatever shifts below. It is the long agreement among them: water obeying ruined channels, ruins guiding roots, roots altering currents, currents turning buried engines, engines making the rain.

It lives as the landscape wears out.

It grows by becoming less distinct from its own wreckage.

Once in each cycle, when the entire plain finds the single note, this dispersed life draws together as closely as it ever can. For that sustained interval, every tremor belongs to one trembling. Every vent, reed, flooded hall and buried rotor participates in the same event.

Then something underneath advances by one increment.

The note breaks.

And the inhabitant becomes a place again.
