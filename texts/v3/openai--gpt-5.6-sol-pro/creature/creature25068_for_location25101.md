At first the basin seems inhabited by many small, unrelated lives.

There are the gutter crabs, each loyal to a few branching lengths of water. They know every ceramic pebble in their reach and stack them into low dams that last only until the next overflow. There are the drain mice, whose territories are not measured across the paving but through it: one animal may vanish at the western pool and appear moments later beneath the eastern ledge, yet refuse to cross the trickle between two adjacent tiles. The frogs belong to particular wall mouths. The birds belong to the ribbed house. Even the gnats hold themselves within invisible shafts of air.

But these are tenants. The creatures that truly live there are harder to separate from the place.

The jointed hunters are their most visible form. They are called hunters only because hunting is what can be watched. Their soft central bodies are almost helpless when stripped of their apparatus: blind gray lengths with delicate jaws, six rings of muscular attachment, and a fringe of feelers fine enough to test the grain of fired clay. They do not grow legs. They acquire them.

A young one begins with reeds, splinters, mouse bones, the shafts of large feathers, or lengths of rush root. It bores holes with its mouthparts and threads them with gut. As it ages, it exchanges these first limbs for heavier pieces. Tool handles are especially prized. A fragment with old finger wear is better than freshly carved wood; a peg that has served in three previous bodies is better still. They recognize such pieces by touch and will carry an unsuitable leg for many cycles before finding the individual whose gait it fits.

Their contests are therefore seldom fatal. Two meeting at a channel edge may raise themselves high and click their limbs together, comparing age, material, and workmanship. One sometimes yields a shin peg or a bone hook to the other. In return it receives sinew, shell plates, or information carried as knots in a short cord. Their memory is partly bodily. A hunter fitted with an old limb inherits the paths worn into it. Its first steps may turn involuntarily toward a drain it has never visited.

They eat frogs and mice, but meat is only the quick portion of their diet. They rasp mineral bloom from the walls. They swallow clay, charcoal, tannin-softened wood, and the black fibers of buried mats. Inside them these materials are warmed, sorted, and altered. Their droppings are neat plugs of workable clay.

That is why a hunter is often found beside a crack before a repair. It presses its abdomen into the break and remains there through the dim hours, feeding clay beneath itself while its borrowed limbs tamp and smooth. Several may work together, though they never cross a running channel to assemble. They pass supplies downstream instead. A pellet placed in one branch is caught by a crab, rolled into another, stranded by falling water, nudged by a mouse, and finally recovered on the proper side.

No single animal understands the repair. The work is distributed through appetite, habit, current, and the shapes of old things.

With age, the hunters become less mobile. Their living bodies broaden while their assembled limbs grow shorter and stronger. They seek a loosened slab, a hollow wall, or the underside of a gutter bridge. There they wedge themselves out of sight. Some shed their external legs entirely. They put out pale, muscular buds along the joints of the masonry, each bud no larger than a fingertip.

These are the hands whose traces remain.

A single hidden body may possess dozens of them, emerging only into enclosed spaces: beneath slabs, inside conduits, between the layers of reed mat and tile. They turn pebbles, pull cord, pinch clay, and brace objects while the water sets them. They cannot lift much. They do not need to. One loosens a tile, another clears grit, a third feeds in clay; water supplies pressure, settling, and polish. What would be a moment’s task for a person may occupy them for forty floods.

They use the tools on the stools collectively. At night—or during the stillness after overflow—cords rise from narrow joints and loop around a mallet’s handle. A hundred hidden contractions drag it to the edge. It disappears by degrees, used below until the work is finished, and is then returned by another opening. This is why the tools come back damp and why they are seldom placed exactly where they were found. The workers know the stools only as three reliable regions of dryness. They have no view of the upper surface as a whole.

Their mature bodies spread along one course of masonry but do not cross active water. The prohibition is not reverence. Running water is where one body ends and another begins.

Each dry enclosure is, in a sense, a household: a colony occupying the layers beneath it, descended from hunters that settled there across many generations. Its channels are skin, border, sewer, road, and speech. Vibrations carried through the slabs tell it of pressure and breakage. Tannin in the water conveys slower messages. A bitter pulse released at one grate may cause distant mouths to close; a warm pulse may open a drain or summon young hunters to a damaged ledge.

To step over a stream is to join two regions that must remain distinct. The pressure of the crossing foot passes from one colony’s pavement into another’s without traveling through the water between them. Both feel the impossible continuity. They respond by closing valves and raising the stream until the contradiction is removed. The frogs turn because the wall-body around them turns. The shadows of birds bend because the thin surface films over the channels are tensioned by living tissue below.

Flood is the dangerous exception. Boundaries vanish, chemical speech becomes confused, and neighboring colonies briefly taste one another everywhere. Some recoil. Some exchange fragments. Some fuse along submerged cracks and must spend many cycles afterward cutting themselves apart. Their mobile young drift across territories, which is how old craft practices spread. A hunter built with eastern knots may settle under a western shelf. A new kind of clay plug may appear years later on the far side of the basin.

The central ribbed structure houses the oldest and largest of them.

Its visible ceramic cage is only the upper portion of a body that reaches through many buried pavements. The nesting birds warm it, and it in turn regulates the drafts that keep their chambers dry enough for eggs. It does not command the smaller colonies. It is less a ruler than an archive. Within its lower cavities lie leg pieces too important to use: ancient awls, polished hinges, carved knuckles, broken handles bearing the pressure of vanished palms. Young hunters enter through cracks below and leave carrying copies of old joints.

The flock’s periodic eruptions are part of this exchange. Their metallic cries vibrate the ribs in distinct sequences. The old body answers too slowly to be heard as sound, altering the draw of air through one slot or another. Birds select their nesting chambers according to those currents and carry fibers, bones, clay, and parasites between them. Thus the archive is continually misread and renewed.

The leaning frame at the eastern wall serves a younger purpose. During the cold cycle, when the colorless vine flowers, mature colonies extrude threads through the nearby pipes. These threads climb the frame without appearing on its exposed surface, hidden inside lashings and hollow poles. Warm drops from the flowers pass into them. For a short time, the colonies produce small translucent sacs containing soft-bodied young.

The sacs are released into the gutters. Most are eaten. A few lodge in cracks and wake after the next flood with the urge to gather six legs.

There may once have been people in the ordinary sense. The proportions of the stools suggest knees and backs unlike those of any present animal. The old mallets fit a single large hand better than a hundred small ones. Beneath the deepest accessible pavements are prints that resemble heels, palms, and the ends of five fingers.

Yet the basin’s inhabitants do not remember makers as persons. They remember pressures, sequences, materials, and gestures. A cord goes around twice and under once because it has always done so. A gutter curves left because every gutter beneath it curved left. Clay is pinched to the width of a vanished thumb. The form of the absent builders persists not as an image but as millions of inherited motions.

So what lives there is neither one creature nor a population in the usual sense.

It is a society whose adults become architecture, whose young wear the discarded workmanship of their elders, and whose customs are enforced by water. Its speech is hammering in pipes. Its genealogies are layers of pavement. Its laws are gradients, knots, and channels. It has no word for ruin, because broken things are merely materials that have become available again.

When the basin falls silent after the great overflow, the inhabitants are not sleeping. They are listening across their temporarily clarified borders. Beneath the open floor, soft bodies tighten around stone. Small hands test joints in the darkness. Somewhere a tile has shifted by the thickness of a gnat’s wing.

Before the condensation falls, they begin to set it right.
