The first inhabitant is the country’s green surface itself.

What appears to be heath is not a single species, nor quite a community in the ordinary sense. It is a long-lived congress of plants that have become inseparable. Cushion leaves shield moisture-holding vines; vines bind old rootwood; rootwood houses fungi that pass water and minerals between districts of the mat. Some portions flower and die in a few seasons, while the deeper framework may have persisted since before particular ravines opened beneath it. Detached sections continue growing independently, yet when they meet again their runners graft together. The people therefore speak of the mat as one being—not because it possesses one mind, but because no reliable boundary can be found between one part and the next.

Its slowest motions shape all other lives. It tightens around stone during dry periods, loosens after rain, and extends pale exploratory cords over gaps. These cords are followed by smaller vines, mosses, and rooting bulbs until a bridge has formed. Old bridges become meadows. Old meadows become heavy, water-filled shelves, and eventually fall. Thus the mat migrates even when its green surface appears motionless.

Within it lives a second country.

The breathing chambers are inhabited by translucent mites, blind burrowers, soft-bodied grazers, and whole colonies of resonant insects. Many never see daylight. They follow gradients of warmth and vibration through the roots, scraping fungus from chamber walls or drinking from the skins of water bladders without puncturing them. Some resemble pale crickets with broad shovel heads. Their chambers amplify their calls, so an animal no longer than a finger joint may make a patch of ground tremble across the width of a valley.

Among them are the pulsekeepers: colonial insects that bite into old runners and drum in synchrony. Their rhythm changes with the strain in the mat. A deep, evenly spaced pulse indicates tight, healthy rootwork. A hurried irregular patter often comes before a tear. Browsers notice this and turn aside. Human travelers listen for it as well, kneeling with an ear or palm against the leaves. The sounding posts near shelters imitate the same principle.

Above the mat, the air belongs to insects.

Rain midges hatch from every temporary cup and stone basin. For a few hours after a shower they rise in veils dense enough to alter the color of the light. Their bodies contain reflective scales that flash silver when a flock turns, giving the impression of rain moving upward. Most will be eaten before sunset.

The great pollinators are slower and more conspicuous. They are furred, heavy-bodied creatures with four short wings working in paired strokes. Their droning can be heard long before they arrive. Each carries a hooked feeding tongue suited to the deep flowers of the solitary trees, and each has a memory for routes across enormous open distances. During cool mornings they land on warm root ridges and lie there vibrating until their flight muscles are ready. Travelers sometimes sit beside them without disturbing them, both waiting on the same stored heat.

Other insects are less benign. Blade-winged hunters patrol condensation hollows, seizing midges in baskets of hooked legs. Broad-backed sap drinkers can weaken a runner until it bursts. After such an injury, sweet fluid wells up through the mat and draws feeding crowds from kilometers away. Beetles arrive first, then birds, then small egg thieves and, finally, predators attracted by the commotion. A wound in the vegetation can become the center of a temporary city.

The permanent pools support their own enclosed worlds. Their warm black water is filled with clicking shell-creatures, threadlike fish, air-breathing larvae, and amphibians whose calls pulse through inflated throat sacs. Many pool animals survive drought by sealing themselves inside waxy cases attached beneath leaves. Others retreat through narrow channels into the mat’s cisterns. A healthy pool is never quiet because its inhabitants constantly advertise territory, court mates, hunt, flee, and surface for air. When those sounds stop together, something large is present.

That something may be a veil-jaw: a long, low aquatic ambusher whose mouth opens sideways beneath a fringe resembling submerged roots. It waits without rippling the surface and can live for months in the wet passages under a pool. Its arrival is often betrayed only by silence and by a faint oily trembling at the water’s edge.

Birds are the plateau’s most visible inhabitants, though most sleep below its surface.

The cliff colonies contain innumerable small species packed into separate vertical bands. Needle-billed swarmers occupy the upper shelves and feed on midges. Lower down are mud-cup nesters that climb the rock with gripping wrist claws. Fruit birds prefer cavities behind temporary waterfalls, where their bright plumage is hidden by spray. The deepest shafts belong to pale echoing birds that navigate in darkness and emerge only at dusk.

At dawn these distinctions vanish into the common ascent. The birds rise together because the first flock draws predators and the thousandth does not. Their columns turn within the chasms until they gain enough height to spill over the rim. The smallest ride in the rising wakes of larger birds. Youngsters sometimes misjudge the transition and are blown sideways across the plateau, landing exhausted among the leaves.

Not all birds are strong fliers. Heavy hammer-beaks live on the great trees and use reinforced bills to split fruit stones against hollow limbs. Their blows carry for kilometers. They store kernels inside root crevices, many of which they forget, helping the trees establish their rare offspring. Long-footed leaf runners hunt across the mat without breaking its upper skin. When frightened, they spread feathered toes nearly as wide as their wings and race over weak growth that would swallow a heavier pursuer.

The great trees are territories because each supports a distinct population. A single crown may hold fruit flocks, nectar feeders, gliding lizards, nest-building rodents, comb-furred pollinators, and miniature frogs that complete their entire life cycle inside pitcher leaves. Some creatures never descend from the tree where they hatch. Neighboring trees, though visible across the open carpet, may contain different dialects of birdsong and different colors of the same animal.

The largest grazing animals travel between these islands.

They are high-shouldered, narrow-bodied browsers whose four spreading toes divide again at the tips into flexible pads. They test each step before committing their weight. The oldest individuals recognize root ridges by texture and lead their herds along paths invisible to human eyes. Their deep chests house large lungs, but they do not run far. When alarmed, they spread apart rather than bunching together, forcing a predator to choose one body while the others escape across safer ground.

Their mouths are made for delicacy. Split upper lips comb young leaves from the mat without pulling up structural runners. They also prune the great trees, standing braced against low limbs to strip vines and fruit. Seeds pass through them unharmed and are deposited with enough dung to nourish a seedling before the mat absorbs it. Because beetles dismantle the dung almost immediately, flocks of small followers shadow the herds, catching larvae and scavengers as they arrive.

Among these followers are hand-faced gleaners, quick animals with narrow muzzles and mobile forepaws. They pry into leaf rosettes, turn loose scales of rootwood, and raid insect chambers exposed by hooves. They are intensely curious about tools, cords, buckles, and knots. Around shelters they become thieves. Some imitate the sound of a striking post, producing a hollow knock that can make an incautious traveler stop and test the ground.

The ground predators are built less for pursuit than for judgment. They are lean, long-backed creatures with sensitive whiskers along their legs and jaws. By touching the mat they can detect where weight is poorly supported. A hunting group does not simply chase a browser; it pressures the herd toward young bridges or water-swollen shelves. The hunters themselves know where to turn aside. Their young learn the landscape through play, rushing one another toward safe mock edges and leaping away at the last instant.

Gliding predators hunt differently. They climb the buttresses of the great trees and launch into the wind, steering with membranes from wrist to hip. Their coloration is pale above and dark below, concealing them against green ground from the air and against cliff shadow from beneath. They rarely kill large prey. Instead they strike birds, tree animals, and newborn browsers, often carrying the catch into a chasm where ground scavengers cannot follow.

Above even these are the pinnacle nesters, the largest airborne animals of the plateau. They spend mornings motionless on isolated columns, waiting for the cliffs to release stored heat. Their wings are too broad for easy flapping in still air. Once the updrafts begin, however, they can remain aloft until evening. They feed on cliff birds and carrion, sometimes snatching animals already falling through a broken mat. Their approach is marked first by a moving darkness on the ground, then by the sudden hush of smaller wings.

Their nests accumulate the few durable remains the ecosystem fails to reclaim: polished bones, glassy stones, copper scraps, and tools dropped by people. Reaching such a nest is perilous, but the reward can be considerable. Old copper is sometimes more easily gathered from a pinnacle than mined from a cliff seam.

The human inhabitants are few, and they have learned not to behave as though open land were empty land.

They tend to be compact and deep-chested, with strong hands and feet made broad by a lifetime of walking on surfaces that move. Even so, bodily adaptation matters less than attention. Children are taught to distinguish dozens of tremors before they are allowed to travel between shelters: rain striking leaves, pulsekeepers drumming, a herd approaching, roots tightening after sunset, a cavity shedding fragments, and the loose shiver that precedes collapse.

They seldom walk in single file. A line concentrates weight on one route, so groups spread sideways and maintain distance, linked by bright cords in mist or heavy rain. Each person carries a probing fork of rootwood, a coil of fiber, a leaf vessel of water, and several wide pads that can be thrown down to distribute weight. Knives are kept short. A long blade invites the dangerous habit of hacking through whatever obstructs the way.

Their shelters are not villages so much as stations in a seasonal circuit. Several related households may meet at one during a fruiting or bird migration, then separate when water pockets shrink or nesting noise becomes unbearable. Possessions are light, but relationships extend over great distances. People may regard families several days apart as immediate neighbors because they share the same route of trees, pools, and tested root ridges.

No one owns an area of mat. Use belongs to those who maintain safe crossings, clear dead weight, mark weak ground, and leave water bladders unspoiled. A great tree may be associated with a lineage, but the claim is custodial rather than absolute. The family tends its vines, harvests in sequence, protects its nesting pollinators, and leaves part of every fruiting untouched. If the tree sickens under their care, their standing diminishes.

Their most serious prohibitions concern severance. Cutting one runner is ordinary; cutting every connection through a section is treated as both recklessness and cruelty. A person who isolates a piece of mat without need has made a “green orphan,” a patch that may dry, rot, and fall. Even deliberate removals—when diseased growth threatens a shelter—are done gradually. Bridges are left until new runners have taken their load.

Sound governs much of their social life. Speech across the plateau is difficult at midday, so people use arm signs, bright cloths, mirrored insect wing cases, and patterned strikes on sounding posts. Personal names often have a spoken form and a drummed form. At night, when the layers of animal noise become distinct, messages can be passed between shelters by low rootwood horns whose notes travel through the mat as well as through the air.

Their songs are not attempts to overpower the plateau. They are built from gaps in its noise. A singer waits for the cicada colonies to pause, enters between hammer-beak blows, and lets the returning cliff echo complete the phrase. Group songs may last an hour while containing only a few dozen human notes.

The dead are not buried in the mat, which would require cutting too deeply into the living structure. Some are placed on cliff shelves for scavengers; others are wrapped in root fiber and lowered into sunless shafts. A few lineages bring cleaned bones to the base of a great tree, where mineral-hungry roots slowly enclose them. The disappearance of the body is not considered erasure. To remain visibly intact would mean one had failed to reenter the life of the plateau.

For all their caution, these people are not solemn. Gatherings are loud, competitive, and full of mimicry. Children imitate bird columns with streaming leaf ribbons. Adults wager on the arrival of rain, the direction of a herd, or which distant tree will flower first. Pollinator fur is woven into warm bands. Beetle cases become rattles. Fruit shells are carved into masks with exaggerated spreading toes and glider membranes.

Their oldest stories do not say that the mountains were destroyed. They say the mountains lay down.

Stone settled into broad sleep; the mat covered it for warmth; the animals entered its breathing; and people came last, light-footed and temporary. This is why no one speaks of mastering the heights. The country beneath them is ancient, but the surface is always being made and unmade. Every road is alive. Every meadow may be a roof. Every shelter stands only by permission of roots, weather, wings, and stone.

To live there is to know that abundance need not be crowded, that emptiness may be full of sound, and that the firmest-looking ground can be nothing more than life holding itself together over air.
