Nothing here lives by hurry.

The smallest inhabitants are scarcely bodies at all. They are clear films spread over metal-bearing stone, one or two cells in thickness, invisible until disturbed. Each cell braces itself between a radioactive grain and the surrounding water and makes a livelihood from the difference: a minute electrical imbalance, a catalytic exchange, an ion arriving where it should not remain. They divide slowly enough that a mineral grazer may revisit the same ledge centuries later and find the film only just restored.

These microbes have no need of color. They do not compete for light, and so the rocks remain naked beneath them. Their most conspicuous structures are internal threads that align with the planetary vibration. Under magnification, a colony resembles transparent hair laid in parallel across glass. When a pressure mill changes phase somewhere beyond the horizon, all the threads flex together.

They are not merely feeding. They are listening.

The six-legged animals of the plateaus listen through their feet.

A grazer’s body is low and broad, plated in overlapping shells the color of unglazed porcelain. Its six limbs end not in toes but in resonating cups, each held a fraction above the ground by a cushion of water. Through these cups it reads the stone: the distant operation of turbines, the minute static made by bacterial films, the interruption caused by another animal crossing a ridge. Sight is secondary. Most have only a line of cloudy pits along the front edge of the carapace, sufficient to distinguish a cliff from open water and a working amber lamp from the general brightness.

Their mouths are elaborate mineral mills. Paired ceramic plates descend onto the rock, vibrate until a microscopic layer fractures, and draw the powder inward. Soft tissue never touches the cutting surface. Worn mouthparts are pushed out and left behind like white tiles. In old feeding grounds these discarded pieces form neat crescents around the scored stone.

A grazer may spend thirty years traversing one ore shelf. Its path looks aimless only from close by. Seen across generations, the herd is tracing the fixed harmonics of the plateau, moving from one resonant seam to another as predictably as current through a circuit.

They seldom touch. Courtship consists of silence.

Two animals approach opposite sides of a humming slab and place their bodies against it, dampening its tone. Each adjusts its pressure until the stone between them falls momentarily quiet. If they can sustain the absence together, they mate. Their eggs are inserted deep into cracks with a needle-like organ and sealed behind a plug of ground mineral. The young do not hatch because they have matured. They hatch when the pressure overhead reaches the exact value encoded in the shell—sometimes weeks later, sometimes after the parent has been dead for centuries.

Above them move the bell colonies.

A bell colony is not one creature, though from a distance it behaves like one. It begins as several transparent chambers linked mouth to mouth. New bells bud at the ends, then between existing members, until the chain extends for hundreds of meters through the clear water. Each chamber contracts independently. Together, under the guidance of electrical pulses moving down the conductive lattice, they produce a slow, rippling stroke.

The colony has no head. Its memory is distributed along its length in magnetite knots. A route through the sea is not learned as a sequence of landmarks but stored as a pattern of bodily tensions: contract here, slacken there, turn when the iron hammering beneath the western plateau enters the third harmonic. If part of the chain is torn away, the surviving fragments retain different portions of the journey. They may wander for decades before meeting other fragments and exchanging bells until a complete route exists again.

Their white flashes are partly metabolic, partly speech. A short pulse announces a current boundary. A sequence running forward and backward indicates danger. Long, dim illumination is associated with reproduction, when several colonies meet and braid themselves into lattices kilometers across. For a few hours—or several months—the water becomes a vast geometric nursery. New chains condense at the intersections and depart in all directions, carrying mixed magnetic memories of their parents.

They do not understand distance as a traveler does. To them, the fixed currents are organs of the world, and migration is circulation through a body that never changes.

The armored shoals around the summit funnels are more active, though only by local standards. Each animal is the size of a hand, enclosed in jointed metallic plates. They paddle with dozens of transparent fins hidden beneath the shell. Their orbit around a collector station is so precise that they are often mistaken for loose machine components.

They feed on charged particles escaping the funnels. At regular intervals an entire shoal turns, exposing different layers of shell: gray, pearl, cobalt, gray. The flash is not a display but a means of discharging excess energy through neighboring bodies. Remove one animal and the pattern closes around its absence. Add an object of the correct conductivity and the shoal will incorporate it. Nuts, tool heads, flakes of nickel alloy and, in one documented case, a small warning lamp have circulated among them for centuries.

The great rays possess less movement than mountains.

They hang along escarpments with their pale wings opened into the upwelling, so thin at the margins that light passes through them. Their flesh is reinforced by pressure rather than bone. If brought into shallower water, they would not swim or thrash; they would simply come apart.

The red organs beneath them are not eyes. Each is a chamber filled with conductive fluid, sensitive to changes across immense distances. Through these organs the ray feels bell colonies entering the current network, machinery altering its load, grazers gathering on ore beds, even the repeated opening of a remote pressure door. Its awareness may encompass an entire mountain range, but only as tensions and currents. It knows almost nothing of shape.

When hungry, the ray changes the electrical character of its skin. The alteration is minute and may take years to spread from wingtip to wingtip. Far away, migrating colonies begin to make small errors. Their remembered current no longer matches the water around them. They drift sideways, correcting and correcting, until their route bends toward the escarpment.

Feeding is gentle. The bells settle against the ray and disappear one chamber at a time into translucent furrows. A large colony can sustain it for a century. Afterward, the ray closes its feeding grooves and resumes waiting. Some individuals have remained over the same valleys longer than the oldest accumulations of shells beneath them.

There are scavengers, although decay offers them little. Narrow creatures like articulated rods inhabit the white shell drifts. They pry apart abandoned carapaces and extract residual salts, polishing the pieces in the process. When threatened, each rod separates into a dozen rigid segments. The current scatters them along established loops, and years later the segments converge and reassemble in the same hollow. Whether they are animals that can become pieces or pieces that can temporarily become an animal is difficult to decide.

There are also beings that live inside the industrial works.

Some are plainly native intruders. Threadlike swimmers nest in cable insulation, nourished by leakage current. Flat, many-legged things seal themselves over pressure joints and feed on microscopic differences between the metal on either side. Factories tolerate them. In several installations, machinery has even altered its cycle to avoid crushing established colonies.

Other inhabitants are harder to classify.

They are encountered only as corrections.

A jammed conveyor is found clear, the obstructing stone stacked beside it. A tool missing from a maintenance rack returns to a different hook. Fresh scoring appears around a turbine bearing, too regular for teeth and too shallow for any known cutting arm. Doors that should open every thirty hours begin opening every thirty-one, then resume their former schedule after a distant current stabilizes.

No creature is seen performing these acts.

The shelters and handrails suggest bodies approximately human in scale, but the maintenance passages are also full of holes too narrow for a human arm and polished by repeated transit. Some observers have reported pale jointed forms folding themselves into these apertures. Others have watched an empty suit of protective armor standing motionless beside a transformer yard, its limbs slightly rearranged between lightning flashes. When approached, it proves to be clamped to the wall and full only of water.

It may be that a population survives within the machines: descendants of the builders, altered beyond easy recognition, moving through conduits instead of corridors and measuring their lives in service cycles. It may be that the factories have developed mobile organs from spare tools, local fauna and abandoned clothing. It may be that there are no secret custodians at all, and the apparent repairs are simply another equilibrium—breakage provoking response as pressure provokes flow.

Certainly the machinery behaves less like a collection of objects than like an ecology.

Towers draw charge from the upper water. Turbines translate current into vibration. Foundries remove dissolved metal and return altered salts. Pumps maintain density layers that guide the bell colonies. Rays feed according to the electrical fields generated by factories; grazers follow harmonics carried through factory foundations; microbes align with the standing wave produced by every moving part.

The system contains predators, migrations, nurseries, digestion and waste. It also contains warehouses, warning lamps, numbered doors and neatly hung tools.

Perhaps those categories have ceased to be different here.

If there is a dominant intelligence, it does not speak from a central chamber. It exists in timing. One mill delays a piston stroke; a tower on another continent adjusts its vanes; a shoal turns cobalt at a summit; a bell colony flashes in the deep; a ray alters the conductivity of its left wing. The exchange may take months to complete. To a visitor it appears that nothing has happened. To the world, a thought may have passed.

Such a mind would have no clear boundary. Parts of it would be nickel, parts protein, parts fixed current and vibrating stone. Its memories would be the routes that never shift. Its senses would include the pressure beneath every plateau and the tone of every glass slope. It might experience an individual grazer as a brief moving interruption, and a ten-thousand-year factory cycle as something like a breath.

It may remember the operators. It may be the operators, distributed so completely through their work that no separate bodies remain.

Or it may be what came afterward: a planetary inhabitant assembled accidentally from the machinery of catastrophe and the animals that learned its noise.

In either case, it is not asleep. The endless thunder is its active state. The suspended ocean is not silent water polluted by engines, but a medium crowded with signals too slow, too large and too exact for an outsider to separate.

Everything there lives by keeping faith with a pattern.

The microbes hold their filaments in line. The grazers return to the resonant seams. The bells remember the currents in their bodies. The rays wait where food will eventually come. The hidden mechanisms cast ingots for recipients who may no longer exist. Even death leaves clean shells arranged beneath unchanging flows.

And through them all passes the same sustained vibration: not quite a voice, not quite a command, but the condition under which every creature knows where it is.

The sea rings.

Its inhabitants answer by remaining in tune.
