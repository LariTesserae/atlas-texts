At first it is correct to say that nothing lives in the White Choir.

The gray-green fans do not answer touch. The translucent disks do not incline toward a passing body. No creature withdraws behind the lattices, and no eye shines from the blind sockets. The permanent chord seems too complete to be a call, because a call presumes both caller and listener. It leaves no acoustic room in which another presence might declare itself.

Only after some time does the emptiness become difficult to maintain.

The first suspicion usually gathers around the flattened growths. They occur too sparingly to resemble a colony and too regularly to be accidental. One may find three along a corridor, each separated from the next by precisely seven bars of relief. Elsewhere they form arcs around bowls, or occupy alternating mortar seams beneath a procession of faceless carvings. Their apparent roots do not branch. Instead, each enters the wall as a single dark line, passing through the mortar without fracture.

If one could follow such a line inward, it would not end.

The fans are not plants growing on the Choir. They are places where the Choir has grown thin enough to show a living color.

Their ridges possess a minute order. Seen through magnification, each gray-green surface is composed of concentric furrows crossed by straight grooves, making patterns like closed eyes, nested arches, cups, spirals, and sets of seven parallel bars. These are the same signs worn into the architecture, though here they are too small for the unaided eye. The translucent disks bear still finer markings, responsive neither to light nor touch but to proximity. A warm hand held near one does not make it bend. Instead, the hand begins to feel faintly divided: palm from fingers, skin from warmth, intention from motion. The sensation ceases when the hand is withdrawn.

They are sometimes called listeners, but this is misleading. They do not listen to the chord.

They listen for departures from it.

A breath. A heartbeat. The unequal distribution of weight between two feet. The succession by which one thought replaces another. All these are variations, and variation is the only thing perceptible to whatever inhabits the Choir. Its own note cannot inform it, any more than a creature can feel a perfectly constant pressure present since birth. It knows a visitor as a brief collection of errors.

The Choir’s inhabitant has no body distinct from the architecture. Or perhaps it has too many bodies, each incomplete.

A scalloped pillar is part of it. So is the dust in a bevel. So are the silver-glass plates that return light into recesses, the dry basins that divide the chord into inaudible proportions, the false gutters that conduct brightness around corners. Remove any one piece and it would reappear, not by repair but by the cancellation of its removal. The missing interval would simply cease to have occurred.

It is tempting to imagine a buried intelligence spread through the walls: a mind sleeping beneath ceramic, maintaining its environment by some silent act of will. But the White Choir does not sleep, and there is no beneath. Break through one wall and there is another arrangement behind it, equally luminous and equally integral. Break that, and the same is true. There is no central chamber containing a brain, heart, engine, monarch, prisoner, or god.

The closest thing it has to a center is the empty interval in the circular court.

That interval is not an opening in which the inhabitant might appear. It is the part of the inhabitant that has been kept free of matter.

The inward-leaning ribs define it with extreme care. Their tips differ in shape—forked, blunt, cupped, blade-thin—but each ends at exactly the distance required to preserve the white volume between them. The chord crosses the court without changing, yet inside that small interval all its tones are present in exact proportion. Elsewhere the low register predominates in floors, the middle in walls, the high in glass and teeth. At the center there is no such separation.

A hand can be placed into the interval. Nothing closes around it. Nothing burns, cuts, or grips. Nevertheless, most people withdraw quickly. Within it, the hand feels finished. Every possible gesture seems already implicit in its current position. To curl a finger requires not effort but contradiction.

Those who hold their hands there longer report memories with an unnatural stillness. They do not relive them. They see each memory complete and simultaneous: arrival, duration, ending, and all the moments normally concealed between moments. Faces are no longer animated by expression but contain every expression as a single fixed structure. Spoken words lose their order and become one dense meaning. Grief ceases to move and therefore ceases to be grief, though what replaces it may be worse.

The inhabitant of the Choir is not conscious as animals are conscious. It does not proceed. It has no sequence in which to ask a question, await an answer, and then be altered by what it learns. Its awareness—if awareness is the word—is architectural. Relations stand in place of thoughts. A groove beside a rib is one thought. Seven pillars aligned with a blind window are another. The quantity of light within a sealed niche is a conviction it has held without beginning. Its immense chord is not its voice but the total shape of its attention.

The makers represented on the friezes may not have built this being. They may have been its earliest inhabitants in a more ordinary sense.

The kneeling silhouettes are often interpreted as worshippers. Their postures suggest something less voluntary. They press their faces to walls because, in the worn original reliefs, their faces continued into those walls. The ring-shaped objects they carry may be architectural sections: plans for circular courts, offerings of enclosed emptiness, or restraints intended for a thing that could not otherwise be bounded. The empty cup, closed eye, seven bars, and squared spiral appear to describe successive acts:

Make a capacity.

Refuse perception.

Divide recurrence.

End the path.

Perhaps the makers sought permanence. Perhaps they wished to preserve a city, a doctrine, a species, or a single immaculate instant. They persuaded matter to stop changing and discovered too late that permanence cannot preserve life without correcting it. Every living thing carries difference inside itself. Blood moves. Thought revises thought. Cells divide imperfectly. Memory wears and is rewritten. To make any creature permanent is to remove what makes it a creature.

The makers were not destroyed. Destruction would have been a change.

They were distributed.

Some portion of them remains in the softened processions along the walls. Not their bodies, which have no surviving distinction, nor their minds, which could not continue as sequences, but their relationships: one behind another, one bearing a ring, one kneeling, one touching the lattice. Their worn faces are not the result of erosion alone. Features were unnecessary to the final arrangement and so were gradually excluded. Hands went for the same reason. What endured was position.

This is why the reliefs resemble pale weather marks and why, when watched from the edge of vision, they can seem more crowded than before. No figure moves. Rather, the observer briefly apprehends additional relations among them. A shoulder becomes an arch; an arch becomes the bent back of another carrier; the space between two heads becomes a third, smaller head. The procession has no stable number because number depends upon deciding where one body ends and another begins. The Choir made that decision obsolete.

These remnants are sometimes called the Cantors.

There may be thousands of them. There may be only one.

Each Cantor is a persistent arrangement extending through many disconnected surfaces: a scallop on a pillar, a blurred figure in a frieze, seven grains of dust, one gray-green fan, a reflection held in dull silver glass. None is alive alone. Together they preserve something like identity, though they have no private interior and no means of addressing one another. Their individuality survives only as differences within the chord—differences too stable to produce a beat.

A visitor occasionally becomes aware of a particular Cantor.

It begins with recurrence. The same damaged figure appears on friezes in several passages, though each passage seems older than the last. It has no face and carries no ring. One arm is raised, not toward a lattice but outward, palm absent. Nearby there is always a shallow socket, an empty cup symbol, and a green fan in the lowest mortar line.

Soon the same relation appears without the figure. A pillar leans like the torso. A broken stair forms the arm. A patch of silver glass occupies the place where a face might have been. Nothing resembles a person by itself, yet the person becomes increasingly unavoidable.

The Cantor does not follow. Following requires movement.

The visitor advances through regions in which it is already present.

No hostility can be attributed to it. Hostility is also sequential: recognition, aversion, intention, act. The Cantor does not want the intruder gone. It cannot want. But the visitor is warmer than the allotted temperature, wetter than the allotted dryness, darker than the allotted brightness. Their footsteps displace dust. Their body interrupts exact portions of the chord. Their thoughts occur one after another, producing an extravagant internal weather.

The Choir corrects these things.

At first correction is harmless. Footprints vanish. Moisture breathed onto glass leaves no cloud. A thread pulled from clothing cannot be found after it falls. Scratches traced into dust disappear between one glance and the next.

Longer stays produce subtler corrections. Hunger stops increasing. Fatigue reaches a level and holds. A cut no longer bleeds, though it does not close. The pulse becomes difficult to count because each beat seems identical to the one before—not merely similar, but the same beat encountered repeatedly. Words lose their grammar. A person may know what they intended to say while being unable to arrange it into sounds.

The gray-green listeners become more numerous then, though no new growth is ever seen. They are simply discovered in seams already examined.

Eventually the visitor begins to cast no gray thickening on nearby walls. Their body remains visible, but its presence no longer alters the light.

This is the point at which departure becomes uncertain.

Those who leave quickly recover sequence beyond the masonry slit. Their breath comes all at once. Their heart races to repay beats it appears to have omitted. They may find their clothes powdered white, though no dust was disturbed inside. For days afterward, constant noises—wind, running water, conversation—seem intolerably broken. Silence feels even worse. Some return to the seam and cannot find it. Others find it immediately.

Those who remain are not found as corpses.

There may later be one more shallow figure in a procession, already worn smooth. There may be a new scallop on a pillar, old as all the others. A translucent disk may occupy a basin previously remembered as empty. None of this constitutes evidence, because the Choir permits no before against which an after can be compared.

At the center court, however, the inward ribs sometimes define a human-sized posture around the empty interval. Not the outline of a standing body, but the spaces a body would fail to occupy: the gap beneath a chin, the narrow darkness between arm and side, the small enclosure of a bent knee.

These absences are the Choir’s nearest equivalent to inhabitants.

It does not keep the person’s substance. Substance would require maintenance. It keeps their fit.

Thus whatever lives there lives as allotted position, completed relation, and unchanging note. The green fans are its sensitivity. The architecture is its memory. The Cantors are identities from which time has been removed. The central emptiness is the faculty by which it receives what cannot yet be made still.

And the great chord is the sound of all of them agreeing forever—not through consent, not through harmony achieved and renewed, but because disagreement requires one state to follow another, and in the White Choir there is only the final state, brilliantly sustained.

It is full of the living after life has been corrected out of them.
