The people of the Condenser Range are easiest to mistake for maintenance crews.

They appear at long distances on gantries and high traverses, pale figures moving in single file with tool frames on their backs. They wear hooded coats the color of unglazed ceramic, reflective faceplates, and broad-soled boots that adhere faintly to metal. They stop at junction boxes. They kneel beside leaking seams. They gather around open panels with the grave stillness of surgeons.

Seen closely, the distinction between worker and equipment becomes uncertain.

Their coats are not woven but laminated: layers of insulation felt, thin conductive mesh, shed moth membrane, and rust-moss fiber pressed together under heat. Most are patched so extensively that none of the original material remains. The faceplate is often a face. Beneath the mirrored outer lens lie several translucent shutters that open and close independently, protecting a soft sensory surface from glare, grit, and electrical discharge. In shadow, branching capillaries can be seen inside it, full of colorless coolant.

Their bodies are narrow and jointed. The oldest have replaced so much of themselves that they scarcely bend like animals. Ceramic ribs show through gaps in their clothing. Cablelike tendons tighten around polished pivots. Fingers end in black pads finely ridged for gripping wet metal. When one places a hand against a pipe, the hand gradually assumes the pipe’s temperature; given enough time, it also develops the pipe’s vibration.

Whether they are descendants of the range’s builders, products of its maintenance systems, or animals that learned to occupy abandoned protective suits is not known. They offer different answers depending on where the question is asked. In the lower valleys they say they were hired. On the eastern stacks they say they hatched from pressure gauges. Near the unfinished towers they insist that they have not arrived yet.

They call themselves, approximately, the **Attending**.

The word also means *those still present when a process completes*.

Their settlements occupy narrow habitation stacks bolted to warm factory walls. From outside, these resemble vertical rows of lockers with windows. Inside, the rooms are connected less by corridors than by declared relationships. Two chambers marked as adjacent may stand on opposite faces of a ridge, yet a resident can pass between them by opening the correct cupboard. Conversely, neighboring bunks may require a day’s climb to reach one another if their room numbers belong to different systems.

An Attending household is therefore not defined by a shared enclosure. It is defined by a circuit.

A household may consist of six adults, three young, a cistern, two condenser pipes, a moth-breeding tank, and an old pressure regulator whose fluctuations govern their sleep. These elements can be scattered over several kilometers. At dawn, family members travel the circuit, visiting each in sequence. They clean the cistern lip, count the moth larvae, touch the pipes, listen to the regulator, and leave small offerings of grease or salt. Only after the round is complete is the household considered awake.

They sleep upright in padded wall niches, usually near machinery with a slow and regular cycle. Sudden silence disturbs them more than noise. If a familiar pump stops, everyone connected to it wakes at once, even across the valley.

Their young are called **unassigned**.

An unassigned child is soft-bodied, dark-eyed, and almost entirely without shell. It carries no permanent name and wears clothing from which all labels have been carefully removed. This is necessary because instructions take hold of the young with unusual force. A child wrapped in fabric marked **FILTER** may begin straining dust from the air with its lungs. One given a numbered cup may try to place itself between the children bearing the adjacent numbers. Directional arrows cause them to wander.

For their first years they are taught blankness: how to look at a sign without obeying it, how to cross a painted line, how to spill water from one system onto another, how to close an unmarked door and trust that it remains a door. Their games are acts of minor disobedience. They arrange bolts out of sequence. They walk backward along arrows. They give one another impossible temporary names.

Only when a child can stand before **AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY** without either entering or fleeing is it considered ready to receive a use-name.

Use-names are modest and provisional: Second Valve, Blue Sleeve, Westward, Carries Glass, Knows the Warm Duct. A name describes a duty but is never allowed to become identical with the person. Every few years, names are exchanged among relatives so reality cannot fasten too tightly around any one of them.

Private names exist, but they are not spoken. They are tapped into the palm through a glove.

The Attending rarely raise their voices. Sound travels unreliably in the range, and a shouted warning may arrive weeks later in the wrong valley. Instead they communicate with hand signs, reflected flashes, tensioned wires, and touches passed along railings. Long-distance messages are encoded into droplets released through known pipe systems. Because each drop remains loyal to the surface that caught it, a message can cross machinery, rise along chilled conduits, and arrive in a household cup without contamination.

Their spoken language is reserved for close quarters. It contains few direct commands. An imperative might become physically binding, especially near fresh paint or intact signage, so requests are phrased as descriptions of a world in which the desired thing has already happened.

They do not say, “Open the valve.”

They say, “This is the hour after the valve was opened.”

A skilled listener understands. Sometimes the valve does too.

Much of their work concerns labels. They repaint weathered warnings, replace serial plates, and scrape dangerous instructions from structures no longer capable of fulfilling them. This is not merely record keeping. A mislabeled component can deform an entire district. A tank marked **STAIRWELL** may develop interior landings. A bridge given the number of a pump may begin moving water instead of travelers. If two doors acquire the same designation, they may open into each other, regardless of distance.

For this reason, their most respected specialists are not engineers but **redactors**. Redactors carry scrapers, blank enamel, solvent, and rolls of undecided tape. They know when to restore a word and when to erase it. They work slowly, often observing a site for years before changing a single letter.

Their greatest taboo is to label a living being permanently.

They tend the range without claiming to understand its total purpose. Some maintain freight platforms that have carried the same sealed ingots back and forth for generations. Others polish articulated mirrors whose reflections strike only empty air. They repair pipes that contain neither liquid nor gas but a continuous sensation of cold. They consider purpose local. A machine need not justify the mountain; it need only remain in right relation to the next machine.

Where repair is impossible, they perform **retirement**. Every instruction is removed. Fluids are persuaded into neighboring systems. Doors are taken off before being marked as walls. Finally, the structure is given a blank white plate and abandoned to loosen into impossibility. Retired buildings often become unexpectedly fertile. Blue grass roots in their seams, rail hares den beneath their floors, and interior rain begins to fall.

The Attending share the range with its other creatures rather than ruling them.

They harvest shed shell from gantry walkers but never kill them. When a painted safety route ends at a broken span, an Attending crew may spend months suspending a narrow line through open air so the waiting herd can continue. The line need not be a bridge. If the paint is continuous and confidently applied, the walkers place their feet upon it, and for them it bears weight.

White cliff swifts are welcomed into ventilation stacks because their flight reveals pressure changes. Reservoir moths are bred for their wing membranes, though the adults are allowed to gather where they choose. Rail hares are nuisances, eating gaskets and uncured sealant, but are tolerated because they also consume a pale mineral film that causes hinges to forget their direction.

The climbing organism of the eastern stacks is treated as a neighboring polity. Its canopy receives light, the Attending receive hardened sap and shade, and both sides maintain anchor points. Negotiations take place through pruning. A cut diagonal tendril means refusal. Three new shoots around a railing mean that the railing has been claimed.

Water governs their economy and much of their law.

Since water follows ownership, possession begins at contact. A drop belongs first to the surface that catches it, then to the connected system, then to the household responsible for that system. Theft is difficult but not impossible. Stolen water resists the thief. It clings to the outside of vessels, climbs back up the drinker’s throat, or beads beneath the skin until returned.

The Attending therefore trade not water itself but routes of permission. A household may grant another the right to catch condensation from one meter of pipe for one winter. Marriages often join drainage systems. Feuds are settled by rerouting gutters. The wealthiest families are not those with the fullest cisterns but those whose water has been welcomed by many surfaces and can travel almost anywhere.

They drink sparingly. Before lifting a cup, they touch it to the throat and wait for the water to accept the transfer. Their internal fluids are mostly reclaimed condensation, and an old individual can often identify the ridges in which another has lived by the mineral bloom around their joints.

They eat sealant fungi, mineral films, moth larvae, soft rust, and translucent nodules cultivated in warm sumps. Meals are arranged by conductivity rather than flavor. Bitter insulating foods are followed by metallic broths and grounding salts. During electrical storms, entire communities fast so that no one becomes an easier path to earth.

Death is called **going out of service**, though the phrase carries no shame.

The dead are not buried; there is nowhere beneath the range that is not already occupied. Nor are they burned, since smoke is prone to entering systems from which it cannot escape. Instead, the body is dismantled according to its affinities. Ceramic shell is fitted into retaining walls. Conductive tissues are braided into signal lines. The faceplate is set beneath a drip so that water can continue crossing it.

The private name is removed last. It is tapped once into an unlabeled sheet of metal, which is then folded until no surface remains exposed.

Afterward, for weeks or years, sounds associated with the dead may continue migrating through lower valleys: the click of their tools, the pattern of their steps, a fragment of speech arriving from a drain. Families do not follow these echoes. An echo is regarded as a task finishing itself, not as a person asking to return.

Some Attending become something else before death.

Those who spend too many decades repairing a single structure begin to resemble it. A condenser keeper may grow rows of thin cooling vanes along the spine. A bridge tender’s legs lengthen and stiffen until walking anywhere except a span becomes painful. Redactors sometimes lose all markings from their bodies, including scars and facial features, and can pass through prohibited zones unnoticed.

The most transformed withdraw into the deep machinery. They are glimpsed through inspection windows, huge and slow, integrated among pistons and insulated ducts. The Attending do not call this disease. They say the range has offered the worker a permanent position.

At noon, when the machinery enters its near-simultaneous pause, the Attending come out onto open platforms. They stand motionless in the overwhelming light, faceplates uncovered, several shadows gathered around their feet. No work is done during this interval. No labels are painted, no water transferred, no names spoken.

They watch the manufactured ridges shine.

Perhaps they are checking for movement. Perhaps they are waiting for an instruction large enough to apply to the entire range. Perhaps, in that shadowless moment, they can see the mountains as their makers intended them to be.

Then one fan resumes, always somewhere far away. A droplet strikes a tray. The gantry walkers lower their feet to the painted lines.

The Attending cover their faces and return to their rounds, maintaining a world that is still too new to know whether it is a machine, a habitat, or a living thing—and careful not to label it before it decides.
