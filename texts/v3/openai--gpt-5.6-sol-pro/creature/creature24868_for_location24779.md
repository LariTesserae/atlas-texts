Nothing lives on the plateau in the ordinary sense. Living *on* it would require a distinction between habitat and inhabitant, and that distinction does not survive for long.

The smallest creatures come nearest to being recognizable.

They move through the upper bark-strata in multitudes: transparent mites, filament worms, jointed mouths without bodies, bodies made almost entirely of stomach. Some browse the gray-green skin in slow expanding circles. Others enter through rain pores and descend along vascular channels, grazing on sugars as they flow. Their tunnels open and close behind them. Their eggs resemble mineral grains until warmed by a pulse from below, when each unfolds into a different animal according to the chemistry of the chamber around it.

A single clutch may produce swimmers, burrowers, and a fine yellow dust that behaves as a colony. Relatedness is not determined by resemblance. Two identical animals may have nothing in common except the plateau’s temporary need for their shape.

The iron beetles are among the more persistent forms. They emerge after storms from seams of blue light, wet and soft, their shells hardening as they feed. Adult beetles are broad, low things with six hooked feet and backs ridged like hammered metal. When electrical pressure rises beneath the flats, they gather above the charged roots by the thousands. Each turns its head toward the buried current. Their bodies arrange themselves into wheels, branching diagrams, and enormous delicate lattices visible from the higher ridges.

Charge passes through them.

At first there is only a blue glimmer at the joints. Then the entire assembly brightens. Some beetles burn out, leaving black shells fused to the ground. Others rise on newly extruded wings and carry portions of the pattern elsewhere. If the arrangement is large enough, the plateau grows conductive tissue beneath it. Thus a congregation of feeding insects may become the first sketch of a future organ.

The white amphibians inhabit water wherever water refuses to lie flat. They cling belly-first to vertical pools and inverted streams, their wide translucent toes spread over the surface tension. Their bones are dark and visible through their skin. Most have no eyes. Instead, a fringe of pressure-sensitive gills surrounds the head, reading the passage of distant movements through connected water.

They hunt the larvae that swim inside rain.

When alarmed, they do not leap away. They loosen their bodies and become momentarily liquid, spreading into the sheet to which they cling. Hours later they collect themselves somewhere else along the same continuous wetness. During electrical storms, when one pool may connect to another in a different season, amphibians sometimes reassemble very old or not yet mature. One may emerge bearing scars from a predator that will not be grown until the following year.

The long-legged herds appear substantial only at a distance.

Their bodies are narrow, black, and slightly twisted, as though each animal was pulled upward before it had finished forming. Four or six legs are usual, though the number changes across a migrating line. The foremost limbs test the ground for pressure seams; the hindmost follow with perfect precision. They step only on living veins that can bear their weight. A herd crossing a flat may form a single moving thread several miles long.

They feed by lowering blade-shaped heads against the ground and listening. When they hear sugars rising below, they pierce the skin with hollow tongues. The plateau permits this. Around the puncture, it often swells into a temporary teat.

The herd’s migration is older than any individual body. If all its animals are killed, the route continues without them. Mats along the old path begin to twitch. Chimneys release dark embryos in their mist. Pools give birth to jointed calves. Within a season the line is walking again, carrying an exact memory of distant feeding grounds and an imperfect memory of what a grazer ought to be.

This is why some herds possess useless wings, internal gills, or rows of small bright eyes sealed beneath the hide. They have inherited recollections from other migrations.

Their predators are less stable.

Sometimes a hunting form resembles a low fan of cartilage that travels under the resilient surface, its position marked by a V-shaped wrinkle. It follows the vibration of hoofbeats and rises beneath the last animal in line. Sometimes the predator is a patch of warm rain that moves against the wind. Animals touched by it become heavy, kneel, and root where they stand. By morning they have hardened into black feeding pillars around which the rest of the herd passes without alarm.

There are also lean, pale runners whose mouths open along their ribs. They pursue nothing visible. Instead they chase tracks, consuming footprints one by one until they catch the animal in its own immediate past. The victim may continue walking for several minutes after it has been eaten. Then it becomes translucent, loses depth, and collapses into a stain of remembered motion.

No predator remains a predator indefinitely. Hunger is merely one task among many.

The membrane-winged flocks are born whenever the buried electrical network must shed excess form. They pour from cracks in sheets, thousands at once, each creature almost invisible except for a dense dark knot suspended in its center. That knot contains heart, brain, reproductive tissue, and a grain of conductive root. Everything else is sail.

They do not fly so much as persuade the wind to include them.

Flocks travel in sharp geometric turns. At times they form a second horizon beneath the clouds. At others they descend over a respiratory chimney and wrap it in transparent layers, trembling as warm pollen passes through their bodies. They feed on suspended charge, on spores, and on discrepancies in distance. After they cross a storm-distorted region, nearby landmarks often return to their proper places.

Many are eaten by birds.

The birds do not always exist before they arrive.

A dense flock may suddenly develop hollows in its movement. These hollows acquire edges, then feathers, beaks, and appetite. A few minutes later birds are flying among the winged creatures, feeding on them. When the flock disperses, the birds either persist as a new species or fold back into the empty spaces from which they formed.

The burrowers inside the chimneys are warm, furred, and communal. Their bodies fit between the layered valves of living peat. With blunt teeth they remove fungal crusts. With combed forefeet they spread pollen across the moist internal surfaces. Whole generations may pass without seeing the open plateau.

They know the chimney as a vertical world of breathing chambers. Warmth comes from below. Cold lies above. The periodic intake is night; the mist-filled exhalation is day. They migrate upward and downward with the pressure cycles, carrying their naked young in throat pouches.

Each colony maintains the irregular rhythm of its host. When a chimney sickens, the burrowers alter their movements, striking certain membranes with their tails and gnawing open clogged channels. If repair fails, they gather in the upper valve before the final exhalation. The dying column launches them in a plume of steam. They fall across miles of rain and enter other chimneys, bringing with them spores, parasites, and fragments of the lost organ’s rhythm.

In deep chambers beneath the flats live animals that have never had surfaces.

They are soft, radial, and enormous. Root bundles pass through their bodies without injury. Some rotate slowly around these bundles, squeezing them in sequence and helping lift water. Others hang motionless in fermentation heat, digesting whatever the plateau has forgotten how to decompose.

Their mouths speak in pressures.

The knocks heard in deep shafts are often their communication: slow sequences repeated over hundreds of years. A message begun beneath one basin may be answered by something under a ridge three generations of surface species later. No one knows whether the exchanges concern food, mating, weather, or the gradual flattening of the world above. The distinction may not exist below.

The creatures in the shining domes are rarer.

Before a dome opens, they are not yet separate from its flower. They lie embedded in the violet tissue as folded arrays of limbs and sensory membranes, sharing one circulation. The violent opening tears them free. Old water throws them onto the surrounding ground, where they unfold and stand trembling in the storm light.

No two openings produce the same brood.

One may release a crowd of delicate, antlered drinkers that survive only while the petals remain luminous. Another produces armored crawlers that gather fragments of the flower and assemble them into smaller walking domes. Occasionally a single tall organism rises from the center, draped in wet white fibers, with several faces arranged around a hollow head. It remains beside the flower until the light fades, touching each visiting animal in turn.

These tall ones seem to recognize creatures the plateau has not produced for thousands of years.

Recognition alters them. A touch may restore a lost pattern. An iron beetle grows ancestral wings. A grazer’s sealed eyes open. A harmless patch of moss remembers teeth.

When the dome closes, the tall organism usually allows itself to be caught between the petals and pressed back into undivided tissue. Usually, but not always.

Some wander.

They cross the plateau without following the safe veins. Rain passes through their hollow heads and emerges carrying faint images: warm lowlands, forests rooted in stone, sunlight descending from an unobstructed sky. Whether these are memories, predictions, or infections acquired from the enormous visitors below the cliffs cannot be determined.

The wanderers build nothing. They leave arrangements.

A row of beetle shells. Three amphibians placed in separate pools that are secretly the same pool. Knotted strands of conductive vine. A grazer’s skull turned toward a ridge that no longer exists. These arrangements affect the plateau. Roots alter course beneath them. New chimneys rise. Weather lingers. One arrangement remained intact for centuries and caused every creature crossing a certain basin to dream of an ocean.

The wanderers themselves seldom last. Their limbs soften, their fibers spread, and they become pale mats. Yet their pattern may recur at the next flowering, distributed among dozens of smaller bodies. A flock may turn with one wanderer’s intention. A herd may pause in the shape of its many-faced head. A chimney may speak with its rhythm.

This is the closest the plateau comes to having people: not a species, but a habit of attention that moves between species.

There may be other minds.

Certain pools imitate observers. Their surfaces form pupils around anything leaning over them. They remember faces, though not necessarily on the correct bodies. Years later they may place a remembered face upon a swimmer, a flower, or a patch of rain. Some pools follow particular animals by seeping underground and appearing ahead of them.

The root curtains hanging from absent slopes also respond to company. They braid themselves when approached. Their knots resemble organs, tools, embryos, and gestures of welcome in no known sequence. Creatures sleep among them and wake with shared dreams.

Even the weather contains residents. Warm shapes move inside cloud banks, pushing rain aside without becoming visible. Lightning changes direction to avoid them. When they pass low over the flats, the moss flattens and the buried animals fall silent. Occasionally one descends far enough to leave an impression: not a footprint but a region in which all growth leans inward, outlining something vast with too many symmetries.

And at the escarpments, behind the upward-falling spray, live the cable colonies.

They inhabit the hanging roots all the way down into permanent cloud. Near the top they are small, many-limbed scavengers with gripping tails. Deeper down, where pressure and sequence grow uncertain, their bodies lengthen and merge. Colonies become chains; chains become muscles. Sensory filaments from millions of individuals gather into a single field. Plated shells overlap until they resemble portions of an immense limb.

Perhaps the climbing visitor is made from them.

Perhaps each ascent is a migration in which an entire cliff ecosystem briefly agrees to be one animal.

The eye that opens in the cataract may belong to no whole beyond itself. It may be the combined seeing of countless cable-dwellers, arranged for a moment around a shared aperture. The bright mechanical plain reflected in it could be somewhere below, somewhere before, or somewhere the plateau is slowly growing toward.

The resident creatures gather because they recognize the ascent as kinship. Chimneys quiet so the roots can feel. Herds stand at the brink. Winged flocks settle over the flats in transparent layers. Beetles discharge their remaining light. Even the deep radial animals stop their century-long conversations.

For a few moments the plateau and the thing beneath it seem to regard each other.

Then the agreement fails. The limb separates into roots, waterfalls, colonies, and cloud. The eye closes into many wet mouths. Every creature resumes its temporary life.

Temporary does not mean brief. Some patterns have persisted since the plateau first rose above the lowlands. But no pattern owns one body, and no body contains only one life.

Everything here is larva, organ, memory, and raw material.

The herds are the plateau walking.

The flocks are its excess distance.

The burrowers are its breath tending itself.

The predators are corrections that learned hunger.

The wanderers are questions it can hold only by giving them feet.

And the plateau, though it contains them, is also only one of the things that lives there: the broadest and slowest animal, feeding on rain, weathering into thought, continually inhabited by the forms in which it attempts to understand its own immense body.
