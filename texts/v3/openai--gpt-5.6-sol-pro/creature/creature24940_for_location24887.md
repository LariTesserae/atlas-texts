At first the basin’s inhabitants seem to be the birds, because they are the only lives large enough to alter the emptiness.

They arrive out of the cloud without warning. A few dark flecks become a loose front, then a flock, then many flocks settling wherever water has spread a skin across the gravel. They do not inhabit the basin evenly. They condense around its temporary edges: the lip of a thawing pond, a runnel newly diverted by fallen stones, a patch of mud warmed faintly from below. Their legs are narrow and dark; their bodies are compact against the cold. They run with their heads lowered, stopping to probe the silt, then rush onward as if the whole flock were being drawn across the ground by an invisible tide.

The waders know no fixed shoreline. Each season, perhaps each afternoon, the water has placed it somewhere else. They feed by reading minute disturbances—the burrowing of a larva, the ripple of a crustacean, the soft place beneath an algal film. Their bills enter ground that only recently ceased to be glacier bed. Behind them remain pinpricks and three-toed tracks, soon filled by seepage or erased by sleet.

Geese come more heavily. They descend with a clamor that the basin seems too large to hold, circle once or twice, and land among the sedges. Their grazing is methodical. A line of bowed necks moves over the rust-colored growth, clipping without uprooting, fertilizing the mineral soil as it goes. At night they gather on bare gravel bars where approaching feet would crunch loudly. The adults sleep in turns. The young huddle against wind-polished bodies while ice forms around the edges of nearby pools.

These birds belong to the place only briefly, but their briefness is consequential. They carry seeds in mud dried between their toes. Spores cling to their feathers. Their droppings enrich water too new to contain much nourishment of its own. They connect the raw basin to valleys and coasts beyond sight. Some arrive exhausted and leave fattened. Some do not leave. A pale feather catches against a lichen crust; a small body disappears beneath snow or is opened by a raptor, and what came from elsewhere enters the first thin making of soil.

Above them live the hunters of distance. Pale falcons and broad-winged hawks hold themselves against the wind with scarcely a movement. Their territories are enormous because prey is abundant only in bursts. For hours one may appear fixed over a ridge, merely a notch in the sky. Then the flock below folds sharply, flashing dark and light, and the raptor drops through it with a speed that makes the entire basin seem momentarily smaller.

On the ground, the larger hunters are harder to distinguish from stone. They travel along moraine crests where the footing is dry and the wind carries scent. Their coats are grizzled in the colors of scree, thick around the shoulders and flanks, wet at the tips from mist. They do not often chase. In such open country, a chase can be seen too early and continued too long. They wait instead for an animal to misjudge a stream, for a calf to fall behind, for sickness or unstable gravel to create the weakness that vegetation cannot afford to hide.

The shaggy grazing herds are not true residents of the basin either, though their paths make them seem ancient. They enter from passes cut into the surrounding ridges, climbing in scattered files rather than compact groups. Horns rise above heavy coats. Their muzzles work close to the ground, cropping sedges, cushion plants, and the freshest grass around warm springs. Their hooves are broad enough to grip rock but not always the flour-fine sediment between it. When one crosses a saturated patch, the ground trembles and fills its print with gray water.

The oldest animals proceed confidently until they reach a place the glacier has changed. There they stop. The inherited route may lead toward a ridge that is now split by a meltwater channel, or descend onto moraine too loose to bear weight. The herd bunches behind its leaders. Nostrils steam. Calves call in short, uncertain notes. Eventually one animal tests another way, and a new trail begins—not yet a path, only a sequence of dark hoof marks over pale ground.

Closer inspection makes these conspicuous animals seem almost incidental. Most of the basin’s inhabitants are smaller than the signs they leave.

In the pools, midge larvae lie curved in tubes of silt, feeding on algae and the organic dust brought by birds. They endure water at the edge of freezing by slowing nearly to stillness. When a calm interval coincides with sufficient warmth, they rise and hatch almost together. For an hour the air above a pond becomes crowded with fine wings. Males gather into wavering columns sheltered behind boulders. Pairing, egg-laying, and predation proceed urgently beneath clouds that may close again at any moment.

Beetles row under thin ice, carrying bubbles of air against their bodies. Tiny crustaceans multiply in ponds that may exist for only a few weeks. Some stain the water faintly red with protective pigments; others graze it clear. Their eggs settle into the mud and remain after the pond drains, enduring frost, dryness, and burial until water returns. A pool can vanish into the stones while retaining, in this dormant form, nearly all of its inhabitants.

In cracks among the rocks are springtails, mites, minute worms, and translucent larvae. They live in films rather than bodies of water. A single wet seam beneath a slab is landscape enough: ceiling, shelter, feeding ground, and route of migration. When the seam freezes they withdraw deeper, following unfrozen moisture toward the heat held in the ground. When it thaws they rise again, consuming fungal threads, pollen, algae, and one another.

The plants live by refusing height. Cushion plants make tight hemispheres that turn aside wind and preserve a little warmth at their centers. Their outer leaves may be blackened by frost while, a finger’s width within, green tissue continues to grow. Sedges bind themselves into small tussocks whose dead leaves shelter the next season’s shoots. Grasses send runners across protected mud, but every advance is tentative. A mat that spreads in summer may be cut in half by needle ice, buried by a slump, or drowned when a stream abruptly changes course.

Mosses are among the more effective colonists because they need so little ground to begin with. They fill pits in polished rock, line the rims of springs, and hold meltwater after the surrounding stone has dried. Windblown grit lodges among their stems. Dead insects accumulate there. Over seasons, the moss builds the pocket in which it lives, turning a fracture into something approaching soil.

Lichens proceed more slowly and on harsher surfaces. The black ones disappear against wet basalt; the orange ones announce themselves with startling brilliance whenever the cloud lifts. Each colony is an alliance housed in a crust: fungus giving structure and protection, algae or cyanobacteria converting the weak light into food. They dissolve the rock grain by grain. Their growth can scarcely be watched, yet their chemical work is among the basin’s first acts of weathering by life.

At the geothermal field, life changes character. The springs are too hot for sedges near their mouths and too mineral-laden for most pool animals. Yet their margins are densely inhabited. Films of bacteria and archaea spread in bands determined by temperature and chemistry: pale cream in the hottest flows, yellow and green where the water cools, rust and black where iron or sulfur becomes available. The colors are not decoration but metabolism made visible.

Some of these organisms live without needing sunlight. They draw energy from sulfur, hydrogen, iron, and other substances carried upward from fractured rock. They occupy pores in mineral crusts, submerged channels, and warm darkness below the surface. A spring may seal itself with silica and kill one colony, but the changed pressure opens another vent nearby. Warm water finds a fresh path, and microscopic settlers establish new layers along it.

Here the most permanent inhabitants are not individuals but processes passed from cell to cell. A microbial mat grows, traps sediment, precipitates minerals, and is entombed by the crust it helped create. Another film forms on top. The terraces become a record of successive communities, each buried beneath the ground it manufactured.

Fungi travel between nearly all these worlds. Their threads enter moss beds, sedge roots, bird remains, and damp silt. They release nutrients from sparse organic matter and trade them with the first plants. Spores cross the basin invisibly in every wind. Many find nothing suitable. A few fall into protected cracks and become networks more extensive than anything visible above them.

Thus the basin is inhabited at several speeds. The herds pass through over days. Birds arrive and depart with weather. Midges compress a generation into a narrow interval of calm. Cushion plants persist for decades while scarcely increasing in diameter. Lichens work more slowly still. Beneath the springs, microbial communities reproduce continuously in darkness, indifferent to migration and snow.

None possesses the whole place. Each occupies a margin: between ice and water, heat and cold, stone and silt, arrival and disappearance. Together they make the apparently unfinished land more habitable. Birds bring fertility. Grazers press seeds into mud. Moss catches dust. Roots restrain sediment. Microbes build mineral shelves on which later moss may grow. Predators return bodies to the ground. Even abandoned tracks become tiny channels where water pauses and algae darken the print.

The basin’s inhabitants are therefore not simply surviving in a new landscape. In small, dispersed, mostly unnoticeable ways, they are helping it become older.
