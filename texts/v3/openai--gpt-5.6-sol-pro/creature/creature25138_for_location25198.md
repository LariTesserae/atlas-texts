At first it seems that nothing larger than an eel lives there permanently.

There are the fish, certainly, and the seals when the tide permits them, but these are visitors. Even the crabs migrate from ledge to ledge as the flow changes. The thing that belongs to the basin is less easily separated from it. Divers have passed within arm’s reach of portions of it without recognizing an animal. In inspection reports, those portions have been called cable insulation, calcareous fouling, degraded gasket material, bacterial rope, and once, inaccurately, the roots of a plant.

It has no roots. It is not one rope.

The most visible parts occupy the widened mortar joints and the sheltered undersides of the iron conduits. They appear as gray cords, seldom thicker than a finger, branching wherever the masonry branches. Their surfaces are tough and faintly ridged, incorporating rust grains, shell fragments, grease, and mineral scale until they resemble the seams they inhabit. Older cords are nearly black. Younger growth is translucent and has a bluish cast in lamplight. When cut, it contracts slowly and releases clear mucus with a smell like opened mussels.

Each cord contains many small bodies.

They are colonial animals, remotely related to the soft, sedentary filter feeders found beneath piers. An individual is little more than a muscular cup with a ciliated throat, a pressure-sensitive knot of nerves, and two valves. Thousands are joined by living channels through which nutrients, chemical warnings, and slow waves of contraction pass. A cup alone can feed and respond. A length of colony can remember a repeated vibration. The whole aggregation does things that no cup initiates.

Most of it lies out of sight.

It has entered bolt sockets, dead instrument lines, cracked ceramic ducts, drainage holes, and the annular spaces between nested pipes. Where corrosion opened the casings of obsolete machinery, the colony followed the damp interior inward. It now threads through several generations of harbor engineering. No one knows whether all its branches remain connected. Samples taken from opposite walls are genetically identical, but that does not settle the matter. It may be one animal broken into cooperating provinces, or many animals that have ceased to distinguish their boundaries.

The basin calls it into shape.

During steady flow, the feeding cups remain closed and flush with their sheaths. Only their sensory filaments project: colorless hairs too fine to see unless a diver’s lamp catches them side-on. They read the chamber through vibration. The western impeller has one signature, the northern rake another. A sluice beginning to open produces a tremor that travels through bronze differently from the tremor of a chain striking a rail. The colony reacts before the water visibly moves.

A contraction passes along the walls.

It is not quick. One must know where to look. Seams tighten in succession. Pale pores vanish. Cords beneath the pipe humps flatten and grip the iron. In exposed places, whole lengths draw back into grooves a few seconds before the surge arrives. Amphipods shelter among them and are sometimes enclosed unharmed in the folds.

When an impeller coasts down, the response reverses. Thousands of feeding crowns emerge into the slackening water. They are small, glassy, and many-lobed, more like wet frost than mouths. For less than a minute the old steps appear furred with them. They strain out bacteria, pulverized tissue, oil-fed microbes, mussel larvae, and the finest organic dust lifted from the trench. Then the currents reorganize and the crowns withdraw.

This is why the walls seem bare. The animal does not build reefs or broad mats. Exposure is dangerous here. It lives in lines, recesses, and intervals of time.

Its largest structure surrounds the central purge trench. Beneath the grates, beyond ordinary inspection, the cords gather into folded masses as thick as a person’s torso. These are storage bodies: pale, muscular reservoirs filled with nutrient-rich fluid. They swell during summer and become lean after winter storms. When the purge opens, the masses compress before the mud begins to fall, forcing fluid outward through the colony. For several minutes the entire network stiffens. The living seams become braces. Feeding mouths close. Damaged regions seal themselves behind plugs of mucus and shed cells.

The purge feeds it despite this violence. Once the sediment cloud settles, branches beneath the grating open mouths much larger than those on the walls. They consume injured worms and amphipods, but also scraps that would appear too large for a colonial filter feeder. A scrap is not swallowed whole. It is held among soft lobes while digestive tissue grows over it. Over hours or days, the food becomes enclosed and dissolved. Crab legs, fish skin, even the leather from an old glove have been found inside the storage folds, stripped clean within translucent chambers.

It does not hunt fish. It does, however, exploit them.

Small fish shelter against its thicker cords during pump cycles. The colony tolerates their touch and secretes a thin film that contains salts attractive to amphipods. Fish return to pick off the gathering crustaceans. Their waste feeds the colony. Their movements warn it of predators and abrupt flow changes. During summer, some lay eggs in cavities lined by its living sheath. Many eggs are eaten by crabs, but the colony encloses a portion in mucus, and those survive pressure reversals better than exposed clutches.

Eels are less welcome. They push too deeply into the pipe spaces and tear new growth from the walls. Around frequently used eel holes, the cords produce hard, bitter nodules. An eel that mouths one recoils and shakes its head. Older eels learn the marked passages and use other ducts.

The seals know there is something there.

They do not eat it, except accidentally. Yet a hunting seal will sometimes pause at the central pillar and press its whiskered muzzle to a seam. The colony closes ahead of the touch, a tightening line running through the masonry. The seal follows that line for a short distance, nosing the wall as though tracking movement inside it. Perhaps the contraction resembles prey retreating through sediment. Perhaps the pressure wave interests the seal in its own right. The encounters rarely last more than a few breaths.

Divers have had stranger contact.

A hand placed on an occupied rail feels a faint sequence of taps. These are not deliberate messages. They are contractions reflected from joints and branch points, distorted by the geometry of the metal. Nevertheless, the sequence changes when the hand is moved and returns when it is replaced. On one recorded inspection, a diver struck a valve housing three times with a wrench. The seams nearby closed. Several seconds later, cords on the opposite side of the housing contracted three times—not in imitation, but because the impacts had triggered three waves that traveled around the bronze collar by different routes.

The diver struck again.

The same answer returned, more closely spaced.

After that, crews began calling it the Knocker, though it never knocks on its own.

Its reproduction occurs during the first warm influx of spring. For most of the year its bodies are sexless feeding units, but rising temperature and falling salinity cause certain buried chambers to fill with orange grains. On a quiet high tide, usually when no pump has run for several minutes, the colony releases clouds of tailed larvae. They shine briefly in the maintenance lamps. Nearly all are carried away into the harbor.

They settle readily on bronze, less readily on iron, and almost never on modern polymer coatings. Small colonies exist in neighboring lock chambers, but they remain isolated patches. Only here do the descendants interconnect across walls, floor, pipes, and trench. The old basin provides the exact sequence they require: tidal pressure, periods of slack water, mineral surfaces, warm electrical leaks, violent purges, and machinery predictable enough to be learned by a nervous system distributed through stone.

How old the colony is cannot be determined. Its deepest tissue lies behind collars that have not been removed for more than a century. Some central cords contain alternating layers of growth and mineral deposit like the rings of a tree, except that inner layers remain partly alive. Fragments reveal traces of copper compounds no longer used in harbor works. One sample had grown around a square-headed bolt from an earlier pumping system and then continued into the replacement pipe installed over it.

It is younger than the basin, but perhaps not by much.

It has no face, no central organ, and no place from which it experiences the whole chamber. A disturbance at the eastern lock may take minutes to alter feeding behavior at the western screens. Parts of it can be crushed while distant parts continue opening and closing normally. Yet over longer spans it behaves with coherence. It abandons pipes before they are permanently sealed. It thickens around fittings that begin to leak. It directs growth toward new vibration and away from surfaces frequently scraped by maintenance tools. These responses may be no more than local rules passing through connected flesh.

Or local rules may be what it is.

The machinery divides water. The colony occupies those divisions. It has made a body out of junctions, pauses, sheltered sides, and repeated shocks. Bronze collars serve it as bones do other animals. Mortar grooves determine the course of its circulation. The purge trench is both stomach and season. Pumps give it danger, food, and a clock.

Nothing in the basin sees the entire creature. Not the seals, not the divers, perhaps not even the creature itself. It lives as the harbor lives: by pressure transmitted through old structures, by one chamber answering another, and by countless small openings that know when to close.
