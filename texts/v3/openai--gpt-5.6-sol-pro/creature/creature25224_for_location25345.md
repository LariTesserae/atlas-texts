At first the inhabitants seem to be a people of bent backs.

Seen on the ramps, nearly everyone leans: into a carrying strap, against a sledge rope, beneath a jar, toward a wall whose sound they are judging. Even those without burdens adopt the city’s habitual caution. They place their feet flat. They touch corners before turning through dark passages. They pause under new vaults and glance upward, not from fear exactly, but from lifelong attention.

Yet they are not stooped in repose. In courtyards and on roofs they straighten into a broad-shouldered population, strengthened less by sudden exertion than by endless lifting. Their hands are thick at the palm and finely sensitive at the fingertips. Quarry workers can distinguish damp clay from sound marl by rubbing dust between finger and thumb. Cistern keepers test plaster with their nails. Builders lay an ear against stone. Children learn early that the city must be touched to be understood.

Dust marks everyone, though differently. Cutters are pale with limestone from hairline to heel. Brick makers carry rust-colored clay in the cracks of their skin. Those who work the black silt seams emerge gray around the eyes, as if smoke has settled there. Water tenders are cleaner but never dry; their hems are dark, their knuckles chapped, and they often smell faintly of wet mineral and the bitter resin used to seal jars.

The city has no single ancestral face. It is too new for that. Its people descend from herders of the surrounding plain, from laborers drawn by the first quarries, from families whose farms failed in thin soil, from captives incorporated generations ago, from traders who came to exchange grain or copper and remained when they discovered that a carved room could be claimed by work. The oldest households speak as though they were present when the first basket of spoil was lifted, but even their grandparents arrived from somewhere else.

What unites them is not blood but adaptation.

Their clothing is narrow, wrapped, and repeatedly mended. Loose cloth catches on projections and drags in wet passages. Adults wear close tunics of wool or plant fiber, belted high when climbing, with short mantles for the wind above the rim. Workers bind their hair under cloth or leather caps. Sandals have thick, replaceable soles, and many people go barefoot indoors to preserve them. A person’s garments may contain patches from several generations of household cloth, each piece reduced over time from cloak to tunic, from tunic to child’s wrap, from wrap to sling, seal, or cleaning rag.

Adornment is compact. Long pendants and wide bracelets are impractical. People wear pierced beads sewn close to collars, copper wire braided into hair, tiny shells, polished black pebbles, teeth, and bits of colored stone found during excavation. A blue-gray bead from an unusually deep seam may be more admired than imported ornament, because everyone knows what labor was required to reach it. Quarry crews sometimes wear matching cords around one wrist, stained with the color of the district where they work.

Fine dust has entered their idea of beauty. Faces are washed carefully before festivals, but pale powder is also used deliberately along the brow and cheekbones. Red clay stains the lips and palms. Soot defines the eyes and, on workdays, serves as something more practical: a thumb dipped in soot can mark a crack, a dangerous block, a disputed boundary, or the number of loads owed.

They are economical in motion and prodigal in attention. A stranger may mistake their quietness for severity. In truth, they talk constantly, but the city discourages careless volume. Loud speech carries too far and reveals too much. Conversations take place at arm’s length, amid work, with long pauses while someone ties a knot or listens to a wall. Their humor is similarly restrained. A remark may travel three terraces before its target realizes it was intended for them. Mimicry flourishes: the complaining rasp of a bad pulley, the grand voice of an upper-rim official, the hollow note of a mason who claims a wall is sound.

Personal names are often common and brief, so people are distinguished by place, craft, or relation: Sava of the Blue Steps, Meret Below the Fig, Oren Who Hears Gravel, Little Taman of the West Tanks. Because houses alter and streets move, these additions can become inaccurate without being abandoned. An old woman may still be called “of the High Door” long after the door has become the entrance to a cellar.

No one simply lives in a house. A household occupies a sequence of claims: sleeping rooms carved by a grandfather’s crew, a roof shared with a sister’s family, a storage niche inherited through marriage, half the yield of a seep behind another person’s wall, the right to cross three roofs before sunrise, and responsibility for a drain that begins two districts higher. Kinship is mapped through use. To belong to a family is to know which jars may be opened, which stairs must be repaired, and whose children can be sent to fetch water without asking.

Their households are large but porous. Grandparents, siblings, spouses, apprentices, widowed relations, debt workers, and children of uncertain parentage sleep under connected roofs. Adoption is common and often practical. A skilled child without surviving parents is not left unattached for long; neither is an old person who remembers the route of a buried channel. Memory itself can earn a place at the hearth.

Marriage agreements speak as much of drainage and access as of affection or goods. One household may offer labor to deepen a room; another grants roof passage, storage rights, or a measured share of cistern water. Because rooms can be created, inherited space matters less than the strength to finish and maintain it. A poor couple may begin in an extraction hollow with no front wall and, over twenty years, acquire a court, a sealed tank, and rooms above them. A wealthy household can lose standing quickly if its retaining face fails and damages those below.

Status is therefore visible, but not simply in size. The most respected homes are dry where they should be dry, damp where water is kept, and sound underfoot. Their plaster is uncracked. Their roof channels are clean. Their doors close squarely. They possess old timber, good rope, reserve jars, and enough stored grain to feed a repair crew without bargaining first.

The openly rich display copper, imported salt, dyed cloth, or oil lamps. The deeply secure display well-maintained walls.

Children belong first to the roofs. Before they are trusted on ramps, they crawl among low parapets, grain mats, pigeon holes, and drying herbs. They learn the city from above, where boundaries between dwellings are marked by custom rather than walls. Older children carry messages because they can take routes impossible for loaded adults: through a vent chamber, down a fixed rope, across the narrow crown of a retaining wall. They are expected to know which roofs are public in daylight and forbidden after dark.

Their games imitate excavation. They cut miniature rooms into spoil banks, roof them with twigs, and flood them with cups of water to see which survive. They trade colored layers of earth as treasure. One child hides an object in a network of shallow holes while the others find it by tapping the ground. Adults discourage play near real work faces, but they watch these games with interest. Talent often reveals itself early. A child who hears a concealed hollow, ties a reliable sling, or remembers the path of runoff may soon be taken along by a crew.

Formal teaching occurs in shaded courts and workshops rather than in a single school. Marks used for counting loads, recording water shares, and identifying households are scratched on plaster tablets, broken pottery, or smoothed pieces of limestone. Few people master every form of notation, but many can read the signs relevant to their work. A reservoir keeper reads volume marks. A quarry foreman reads tallies and fracture symbols. A grain steward knows seals. Children memorize public rules as chants timed to handclaps or hammer strokes.

The city’s most trusted people are often listeners.

Some are master cutters who can judge concealed flaws from a sequence of blows. Some are elderly women who oversee tanks and know the behavior of water through every season. Some are plasterers who recognize a failing wall by the pattern of dampness. They do not form a single priesthood or guild, though outsiders may imagine they do. Authority gathers around proven perception.

When danger comes, titles matter less than proximity. The first person to hear shifting stone calls out. The cry is sharp and standardized, unlike ordinary speech. Work stops. Fires are covered. Children freeze against walls or move into designated courts. Neighbors arrive with poles, baskets, rope, wedges, and lamps. No household is permitted to conceal a dangerous crack, because collapse ignores ownership.

Someone who gives a false warning is teased afterward, but not punished. Someone who notices a true danger is fed by the affected households and may be given a bead cut from the stone that was removed. Such beads are worn without ornament. Their meaning is understood.

The dead remain entangled with construction. Bodies are not placed beneath ordinary floors, since no floor is certain to remain closed. Some families carry their dead beyond the rim to shallow grave fields marked with low stone rings. Others use sealed chambers in stable faces, reopening them for generations. The bones of the long dead may be gathered into jars when space is needed, but their chambers are not quarried again. To cut into a burial room knowingly is considered both impious and structurally foolish: the dead were placed where the rock was believed sound.

Funerals are quiet processions upward. This reversal—the burden borne toward the plain rather than down into the city—is among the few times broad ramps are cleared entirely. Mourners carry no white stone dust on their faces. At the rim, where wind takes voices away instead of returning them, names are spoken loudly.

Their religious life does not treat the earth as dead matter, though neither do they worship it as a single being. They speak of layers as having dispositions. Gravel wanders. Clay remembers pressure. Limestone answers honestly if struck correctly. Water is said to have no loyalty and must be continually persuaded. These sayings are practical knowledge, metaphor, and belief at once; the inhabitants make little effort to separate them.

Household offerings are modest: the first clean cup from a new seep poured back onto the stone; a pinch of grain pressed into wet plaster when a roof is finished; a strand of hair sealed behind a lintel; milk left overnight in a newly cut room to discover whether insects, mice, spirits, or hidden cracks will reach it. Before a chamber is occupied, someone sleeps there alone with an unshielded lamp. If the flame burns poorly, the room is not yet considered ready, whatever the builders say.

Major observances follow rain and construction rather than sun or harvest alone. The filling of the western ponds draws crowds. So does the opening of a new public ramp. At such times work crews parade their tools after washing them, and neighborhoods exchange bread stamped with patterns resembling stone bands. Songs are built in overlapping lines, begun on different terraces so that echoes enter at planned moments. A visitor hears disorder. The inhabitants hear the basin answering itself.

Food is plain, dense, and carefully portioned: coarse grain breads, lentil mash, onions, curds, dried goat meat, bitter greens, and occasional pigeon. Stews are favored because little moisture is lost. Ovens are shared to conserve fuel. Households send covered pots marked with clay seals, and children return later to identify them among dozens by nick, handle, or smell.

Nothing edible is discarded quickly. Sour grain becomes mash. Whey enters plaster or soup. Bones are boiled, shaped into tools, then burned. Feast days are distinguished less by abundance than by wastefulness: bread eaten while still hot, oil allowed to drip, herbs used only for scent, a whole piece of wood burned for brightness.

There are quarrels, naturally, and the city gives them particular forms. People fight over seep rights, blocked light, roof access, dust falling into courtyards, unauthorized excavation, and the ancient offense of emptying dirty water downhill without warning. Theft from a cistern is judged more harshly than theft of food. Damaging a public stair in anger is close to sacrilege.

Disputes are heard at the relevant site whenever possible. Judges, elders, craftspeople, and affected neighbors gather around the wall, drain, roof, or tank in question. Arguments involve objects as much as testimony: stained plaster, lengths of cord, old groove lines, sealed jars, the remembered position of a doorway. A person may lie persuasively, but water leaves evidence.

Punishment tends toward repair. Those who block a channel must open it. Those who weaken a wall must carry stone. Those who steal water repay it in labor at the tanks, where their work can be watched. Exile is rare and terrible, not because the plain is unsurvivable, but because identity consists of accumulated access. Outside the city, a person loses not merely a room but a thousand small permissions.

They are wary of outsiders, especially those who look down from the rim and see only a quarry crowded with roofs. Traders are welcomed at upper markets, where grain, salt, wool, timber, copper, hides, and resin are exchanged for dressed stone, pottery, plaster, pigments, and skilled labor. Few visitors are invited deep without a guide. This is partly caution and partly mercy. Outsiders become lost, step through weak roofs, foul collection channels, and complain that streets have moved since their last visit.

The inhabitants themselves navigate by signs too minor for visitors: a band of rust-colored marl at shoulder height, three pigeon holes, a lintel scorched on one end, the smell of a particular kiln, the draft from a covered stair. Directions are given as actions rather than distances. Go down until the wall sweats. Cross where you hear the potters. Pass beneath the room with the fig roots. If you reach direct sun, you have climbed too far.

Those who regularly travel beyond the city become subtly different. Rim herders speak louder and scan farther. Traders develop a taste for open routes and complain of the basin’s smoke. Quarry people distrust the horizon’s emptiness; without walls nearby, they feel exposed and unable to judge scale. Some sleep poorly on the plain because no footsteps pass overhead.

Conversely, a few residents seek the upper edge whenever they can. Young lovers meet there, beyond the carrying walls. Old workers sit in the grass to rest their hearing. Dissatisfied apprentices stare across the level country and imagine towns where streets remain in place. The city is not universally beloved. Its closeness exhausts some people. Its rules enter every roof and cup. Privacy is difficult, sunlight is rationed by architecture, and another family’s mistake can descend literally through one’s ceiling.

There are always departures.

There are more arrivals.

At night the inhabitants become silhouettes moving between points of lamp flame. Water keepers make late rounds. Midwives cross roofs with bundles tucked close. Cutters returning from the eastern face wash their hands in used water, then sit while relatives pick grit from their hair. In the deepest workshops, craftspeople continue where reflected light has been trapped by pale plaster. Somewhere a baby cries, and the sound emerges from three doorways at once.

Before sleep, households secure not only doors but loose objects. Jars are wedged. Tools are counted. Lamps are pinched out. A small vessel of water is left where it can be reached in darkness. Those nearest new cuts sometimes place a shallow bowl of pebbles beside the bed; if the floor shifts, the faint rolling sound may wake them.

They sleep lightly, but not unhappily.

Above them lie roofs, paths, neighbors, stored grain, dripping cloth, perhaps another family’s hearth. Beside them is the cool wall from which their room was taken. Below them are unknown layers. The city has taught them that shelter is not the opposite of uncertainty. Shelter is uncertainty watched carefully, reinforced together, and inhabited anyway.
