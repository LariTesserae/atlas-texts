At first, what lives in the drowned interval appears to be a community of separate things.

There are the black fronds, rooted wherever young basalt has cracked. Each begins as a stain no larger than a thumbprint: a dark velvet of mineral-eating cells spread across the stone. From this stain rises a single filament, then another, until a plume has formed. The visible plant is only the exposed portion of a much finer body. Its roots enter microscopic faults in the basalt and divide there, following iron through one seam and sulfur through the next. Some threads descend into warmth. Others climb toward colder water. Between them passes a minute electrical current.

The fronds live on this difference.

They are neither plants nor colonies in any clean sense. Their tissues contain chambers crowded with smaller organisms, and those organisms contain metallic grains arranged like compass needles. When the chemistry of the seep changes, the grains turn together. Hours or years later, every frond in the interval inclines the same way.

A young plume is soft enough to pass through the eye of a needle. An old one acquires a mineral skeleton. Its lower branches stiffen, blacken, and fuse to the rock, while its living crown continues to divide above them. Thus each frond slowly becomes its own fossil, carrying a delicate ruin beneath its greenery. The oldest forests are supported by generations of themselves.

They have no need of light, but they retain organs that resemble eyes.

These occur at the tips of certain branches: clear beads containing no pigment, lens, or nerve. They react not to illumination but to available distance. Where the water has folded deeply, a bead turns blue-black and swells. Where space is thin and ordinary, it contracts. In this way the forests sense the chamber’s changing dimensions. They grow preferentially into the longer gaps, although those gaps may be visibly narrower than the spaces they avoid.

The faint haze around their tips is not a lure. It is the waste product of measurement.

Among the fronds are the hollow columns. These are slower beings, if beings they are. Each contains a vertical succession of membranes, thousands of them, floating one above another without touching. Warm water enters near the base. Cold water descends through the center. The membranes harvest salts from both, building green-gray ribs around themselves.

Nothing circulates quickly inside a column. A chemical change at its root may take six years to reach its crown. Injury travels even more slowly. If a column is cut, the upper part may remain unaware for decades, continuing to bend, feed, and thicken. Then, on no apparent occasion, every membrane will contract at once. The severed portion collapses into a translucent rope, and the nearest fronds cover it.

The pearly films, red veins, white nodules, and threadlike mats are harder to separate from one another. A film may become a nodule after a century. A nodule may release threads. Threads may braid themselves into the vascular core of a frond or enter an empty shell and disappear.

There are no parasites here in the usual sense. There are only tissues that have not yet declared what body they belong to.

The ribbon swimmers are the interval’s most recognizable animals. They keep near the entrance, where temperature boundaries still behave almost like surfaces. Their bodies are pale and extremely thin, but not fragile. A swimmer can be drawn out until it is several times its resting length, and it will contract unharmed once released. Its fins are broad membranes folded so finely that they seem to vanish when viewed edge-on.

They possess neither mouth nor gut. Along the underside runs a groove lined with trembling hairs. Dust, bacteria, and dissolved metals settle there and are passed from cell to cell until nothing remains but a chain of dark crystals. These crystals migrate into the reflective line along the animal’s back.

That line does not mirror its surroundings.

In the youngest swimmers it shows only brightness: a pale trembling expanse like sunlight seen through waves. As they age, forms appear within it. Clouds. Branching silhouettes. Long vertical masses swaying in moving air. Occasionally there is the unmistakable shape of a horizon.

The swimmer does not appear to see these things. It has no eyes and makes no response when the images change. Yet individuals bearing similar scenes gather together. Those carrying bright water drift near the entrance. Those carrying forests venture farther among the fronds. Very old swimmers, whose lines reflect black towers or distant moving lights, disappear into the chamber’s folded depths.

No one has seen them die.

It may be that the images are not memories but destinations, and that a ribbon swimmer spends its life slowly becoming able to reach the place it has always reflected.

The fixed shells are not truly empty.

No flesh occupies their spirals, but the inner surfaces are coated with a transparent layer one cell thick. This lining extends into the stone through pores too small for water to enter. Beneath the basalt, the extensions join. Every shell in the chamber is therefore the exposed ear—or mouth—of a single buried membrane.

Its extent is unknown. It may be confined to the drowned interval. It may spread through the entire layer of young rock.

Touching one shell sends a pressure through the membrane, and all the mouths close. Their closure is not defensive. It forces a measured pulse into the surrounding water. The black forests turn toward that pulse and remain inclined until it has passed through the chamber’s accumulated distances. This can take days.

The shells listen to the return.

From the delays, distortions, and echoes inside bone and stone, the buried membrane appears to maintain an understanding of the interval’s shape. It knows when a fracture opens before the fracture reaches the chamber. It knows where a bubble will vanish. It may know how far away the opposite wall has become, even when that wall remains close enough to touch through the basalt.

Near the supposed center live the builders.

They have never been observed directly.

Their rooms are found complete or nearly complete, fitted from flakes of stone too heavy for the ribbon swimmers and too regular to be the work of settling. Yet construction is impossibly slow. Two slabs may spend thirty years leaning toward one another before their edges meet. A threshold emerges because the mineral film beneath it dissolves grain by grain. Stairs descend into rock as narrow sheets of basalt separate and are carried away by currents too weak to stir dust.

If builders exist, they do not lift stones. They persuade stone to arrive.

One possibility is that they are the pale tufts enclosed by some of the walls. The tufts resemble small hands with many fingers, each finger ending in a hard pad that grips the basalt. They are found only inside the structures, never in the open forest. When removed, they curl into compact knots and remain so. Returned to any room, even a different one, they slowly unfold.

Another possibility is that the builders inhabit the dark depressions.

These yield beneath contact but cannot be pierced. Their coolness spreads upward through anything that touches them, leaving the object unchanged except for a fine coating of sediment. Within their surfaces, the interval appears old and depleted. Sometimes, if watched for several hours, something moves among the collapsed walls of that future chamber.

It is small.

It advances on several jointed limbs, pausing at each ruin. Where a tuft once stood, it bends as if gathering something. Where a shell is fixed in the present, it touches bare stone. It never looks upward. Perhaps there is no upward direction on its side of the surface.

After it has passed, one of the structures in the living chamber will alter. A wall falls. A new flake appears beside a threshold. Three steps become four.

Thus the builders may work backward, arranging their ancient ruins in order to make the young rooms that will eventually collapse into them.

During the transparent season, all these lives seem to vanish. Frond, column, film, nodule, shell-lining, swimmer and buried root lose their opacity together. The chamber appears empty.

Then the larger inhabitants can be discerned.

They do not stand among the forests. The forests stand within them.

One outline resembles a many-limbed animal folded tightly around the chamber, though no limb can be followed to its end. Another suggests a vast head pressed sideways between the basalt layers, its depth extending through distances unavailable to the stone. There are curves like ribs, branching masses like antlers or vessels, and circular hollows larger than the interval’s measurable width. Some overlap without obscuring one another.

They never move while visible.

It is tempting to call them creatures concealed by the ordinary density of life. It is equally possible that they are the complete forms of that life: bodies whose apparent organs are separated not by membranes but by centuries. A black frond may be one sensory hair. A hollow column, one slow nerve. The connected shells may form an inner ear. The little rooms may be repeating units in an organ still assembling itself. The ribbon swimmers may carry images the way blood carries oxygen—collecting traces of an exterior world the greater body cannot enter.

If so, the drowned interval holds not an ecosystem but an embryo.

Its development is geological. It feeds on mineral gradients and builds basalt around itself as a larva might build a case. Its thoughts, if it has them, occur as synchronized tiltings, delayed echoes, chemical seasons, and the patient placement of walls. A single impulse may require a century to cross from one side of its body to the other. What appears to an observer as stillness may be the interval considering whether it has been touched.

Its future image shows the chamber empty because maturity does not happen there. The crowded tissues recede. The rooms collapse. The stone rounds and silts over.

Something has left.

Perhaps, when the basalt layers finally split, the inhabitant will not emerge into the ocean. It will emerge into the additional depth it has been accumulating all along. The fold that receives the sideways bubbles may be the beginning of its passage. The old ribbon swimmers may already travel through it, bearing rehearsals of skies, waves, forests, and structures for which the unborn thing has no names.

Until then, it lies distributed through the water and rock, indistinguishable from everything that lives upon it.

It tends its mineral forests. It closes its empty shells. It remembers a future in which it is gone.

And at the narrow entrance, its pale filaments rise from the stone and feel, with exquisite uncertainty, for a light they know only from the bodies of blind animals.
