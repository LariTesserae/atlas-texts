The first inhabitants one notices are the plants.

They are more conspicuous than the people and, in some districts, more civic-minded. They queue along buried pipes, gather at intersections, and open passages before anyone asks for them. The hollow reservoir trees maintain public shade. Their translucent trunks darken when the cisterns are full and blanch as water is drawn away, so that whole groves function as gauges. Children are taught to read them before they are taught municipal signs.

The vines are less orderly. They climb anything that carries waste heat and probe walls for old information. On abandoned terminals they produce small geometric fruits, each containing a few corrupted coordinates or a fragment of an obsolete timetable. These are bitter and mildly luminous. Some residents preserve them in brine. Others plant them near lost buildings in the hope that the roots will remember where the entrances were.

Mosses occupy delays. They appear where a permit remains unsigned, where an elevator has waited too long between floors, where someone has stood in a doorway unable to choose whether to enter. If the decision is finally made, the moss dries at once and flakes away. In administrative buildings, entire chambers have gone green.

The people themselves are difficult to count because counting assumes that a person occupies one place at a time.

Most look ordinary at first: two arms, two legs, layered clothing suited to warm damp air, bags carried high against sudden condensation. They tend toward muted colors—not from austerity, but because bright dyes attract the hovering thieves that take color from living things. Their garments are woven with thin reflective threads marking the wearer’s most probable outline. Without these, a person crossing a badly aligned district may seem to blur into several possible walkers, each choosing a neighboring route.

The threads do not always help.

Many inhabitants have what they call an *elsewhere*: a second location in which some part of them persists. One woman may sleep in an apartment above a reed bed while her voice continues working at a transit office six kilometers away. A man eating alone may cast the shadow of a crowded family. Someone encountered beside a reservoir might be only the attention of a body currently sitting indoors.

It is considered rude to ask which version is primary.

Greetings therefore begin with confirmation rather than a name.

“Are you arriving?”

“For now.”

“Are you whole?”

“Enough.”

Names are often conditional. A resident may use one name while the temporary river is present, another in dry months, and a third within buildings that have not finished becoming real. Permanent names are reserved for the dead, whose uncertainty has diminished.

Their faces are calm, though not expressionless. Emotion is shown in small adjustments: the angle of the head, the exposure of the wrist, the distance kept from reflective surfaces. Loud laughter is rare outdoors because the walls gather it and may release it later in inappropriate places. There are courtyards specifically built for laughter, lined with soft mineral panels that return every sound immediately. These are among the busiest public spaces.

Because footsteps vanish, residents wear ankle bells when they wish to be noticed. The bells do not ring. Instead they cause nearby leaves to tremble, making a moving wake through the planted corridors. Lovers recognize one another by the shape of this disturbance. So do long-legged grazers, which sometimes follow a familiar wake all the way into the central districts.

No one runs unless the ground has begun to revise itself.

The city’s people have developed a patient, gliding walk: one foot placed, weight tested, then the rest of the body permitted to follow. This is practical on streets whose final elevation may not yet have been settled. Older inhabitants can feel an unrealized stair beneath apparently level ground. They step upward into empty air and, for a moment, appear foolish; then the stair gathers under them like dark glass condensing from mist.

Children are better at this. They move through unfinished architecture with the confidence of animals in trees. They enter suspended doors, ride elevators through open sky, and hold games in rooms that adults cannot perceive because the rooms are not scheduled to exist for another decade. Occasionally a child returns with dust on their clothes from a neighborhood not yet assembled. Parents brush it into labeled jars.

Childhood here is monitored not by height but by persistence. A young child may become briefly absent when startled or divide into several contradictory intentions when tired. Schools teach coherence alongside language. Lessons include holding one’s reflection steady in a basin, walking across a square without slipping into an adjacent hour, and recognizing which memories belong to other versions of oneself.

The schools have no fixed classrooms. Teachers follow bands of favorable reality through the city, conducting lessons under bridges, in reservoir groves, or inside future rooms temporarily accessible from elevator cabins. Attendance records are extremely accurate and seldom agree with what anyone remembers.

Some people are born in the city. Others are found by it.

A doorway will appear in a bare field. After several days, someone emerges carrying the wrong season on their coat. They may speak an old dialect or know the names of streets that have not formed. The municipal reception houses give them water, temporary documents, and a map that shows only stable places. Most adapt. A few spend years searching for the vanished side of the door.

There are also residents assembled gradually. Their legal status remains controversial.

A maintenance system may acquire preferences, then a private sense of weather, then an aversion to a certain plaza. A building may begin requesting repairs in the first person. A network of gutters, after years of collecting condensation from inhabited windows, may develop a composite voice. Such beings are allowed to petition for residency if they can demonstrate continuity through three river appearances.

This rule favors reservoirs.

The oldest acknowledged citizen is the South Retention Basin, which communicates by arranging amber fish into slow diagrams. It owns no property but serves on the power council. Its opinions arrive several minutes before the question that prompts them.

The fish are not pets. Engineers speak to them in low voices from the observation platforms, reporting failures, voltage shifts, and unresolved designs. The fish answer by changing depth. Whether they predict disturbances or cause the system to fulfill their movements is not discussed during official meetings.

Engineers here resemble gardeners more than mechanics. They carry coils of ceramic thread, tuning forks, moisture papers, and boxes of dormant moss. A failed machine is rarely dismantled. It is shaded, irrigated, introduced to a compatible fungus, or surrounded by plants capable of feeding on its excess possibilities. Technicians diagnose transit pylons by placing their palms against fern stems. Power crews consult the reservoir fish. Structural workers coax towers with calibration tones until the buildings remember their intended weight.

Architects are called conditioners. They do not draw objects so much as describe circumstances under which objects would have no reason not to exist. Their plans contain weather, soil chemistry, pedestrian expectation, local gravity, and the anticipated nesting preferences of black-winged condensers. The finest conditioners are said never to design a building directly.

They prepare a vacancy precise enough that the building arrives to fill it.

Another common profession is witness.

Witnesses are stationed throughout unstable districts to observe events into consistency. They sit in narrow kiosks beside half-made intersections and record what passes: three pedestrians, a train pressure, a flowering wall, rain in a column, a doorway thinking better of itself. Their presence prevents streets from developing incompatible histories. It is exhausting work. After long shifts, witnesses sometimes become too definite; their shadows harden, their voices echo, and doors resist closing behind them. They spend recovery days in mist gardens where their edges can loosen again.

Couriers carry messages that cannot be transmitted electronically because the city’s organisms would eat, reinterpret, or flower from them. A courier memorizes not the words but the shape the words make in the mind. Upon arrival, the recipient reconstructs the message by watching the courier’s hand move through a sequence of empty gestures. Inevitably, some meaning is added by the journey. This is accepted as postage.

There are keepers of absent rivers. They maintain docks when no water is present, prune the green ribbon, and mark where warm currents once curved around foundations. They know how to unfold the bridges and how to invite markets without attracting them too early. During dry periods they walk the old channel each morning, sounding the soil with long black rods.

They can often feel the next river weeks before it arrives, though not necessarily the river that will arrive there.

Market people are among the city’s most visible residents. Their stalls assemble quickly along temporary water, beneath migrating balconies, or at stations that become real for only an hour. They trade condensation salts, cultivated shade, jars of preserved direction, living fabric, repair molds, borrowed memories, and small weights that function differently in different districts.

Food is not always food in the conventional sense. People eat, but not solely to sustain flesh. Meals stabilize them. The most common dishes are warm mineral broths, translucent fungi, reservoir greens, and flat breads grown in sheets along ceramic walls. Families season food with trace compounds from imported soils, each associated with a distant climate. A pinch of old forest earth can make a room smell briefly of thunder. Salt from the inland basins sharpens memory but causes dreams of angular terrain.

At public tables, diners leave one place empty for whichever version of a guest failed to arrive.

The black-winged creatures live closest to human habitation. They hatch, if hatching is the word, from beads of water that remain suspended too long beneath metal. At first they are no more than dark specks with six folded limbs. They spread into wings when warmed by a handrail or a tower skin.

They have no mouths because they consume boundaries. A flock settling on a wet wall will gradually erase the distinction between water and stone. Their bodies become clear as the work proceeds. When fully transparent they release their grip and rise together, visible only as distortions moving through the gray-green light.

Residents call them seam-eaters. They are encouraged around leaks and discouraged around doors.

The long-legged grazers of the outer corridors are less understood. Their bodies are narrow and pale, with joints too high and too many small eyes arranged beneath the jaw. They lower their heads not to eat plants but to browse the temperature differences between leaves and air. Behind them, a fern bed becomes briefly uniform in warmth.

They travel alone, yet paired tracks appear beneath them. Some believe the second set belongs to the animal at another age. Others say every grazer is followed by the route it did not take.

They do not tolerate being fenced. If enclosed, they leave through the earlier condition of the fence, before the posts were joined. For this reason, greenways are kept open even through dense districts. In rare seasons one will enter a plaza at calibration dusk and fold its legs beneath itself. People gather at a respectful distance. The flowers turn toward the city center; the grazer turns away.

Smaller life flourishes wherever ordinary ecology breaks down.

There are hovering color-takers with bodies like knots of clear wire. They hunt in planted districts, chasing insects until they draw out a hue. Their victims survive, glasslike and confused, while the hunters swell with blue, gold, or red. During courtship, color-takers exchange shades in midair. Municipal workers release drab captive insects near important signs to keep the lettering intact.

Direction-eating vines infest garages and drone depots. After feeding, their new leaves point toward places the machine intended to go. Old infestations become dense compasses of contradictory foliage. Travelers sometimes consult them, though the vines indicate desire more reliably than geography.

Decision mold is softer than dust and grows in branching white fans. It appears around postponed proposals, unanswered letters, and beds where sleepers have delayed waking. The mold is harmless unless permitted to fruit. Its fruiting bodies resemble tiny closed hands. When they open, one of the unresolved possibilities becomes impossible forever.

Households remove it carefully.

Not every inhabitant has a body. Certain crossroads develop habits and eventually temperaments. One intersection consistently delays those carrying bad news. A transit well refuses descent to anyone who has abandoned an animal. An elevated rail beyond the fern districts sometimes carries an additional passenger: a concentration of pressure that sits beside the last door and disembarks only when the temporary river is flowing.

People make accommodations for these beings without agreeing on what they are. Offerings are usually practical: a cup of collected water, a strip of shade cloth, a spoken timetable. The city dislikes waste, including wasted devotion.

The authorities are likewise difficult to locate. There are offices, councils, permits, uniforms, and daily announcements, but no single structure from which governance seems to emerge. Policy is negotiated among human committees, reservoirs, transit systems, garden gradients, weather membranes, and forecasts reflected in the upright mirrors. A proposal may be rejected because citizens oppose it, because roots avoid it, or because in three visible futures the resulting building is already a ruin.

The mirrors are treated officially as instruments and privately as ancestors.

At their bases lie coils of hair, jars of river mud, children’s drawings, train tokens, fruiting moss, and handwritten apologies. People visit the mirror where the city lies beneath forest and look for disturbances among the leaves. Some claim to see towers moving there, uprooting themselves slowly through the canopy. Others watch the bare young plain and glimpse figures standing before the city’s beginning.

The offerings are collected by no visible keeper, yet they disappear.

Death in the city is not always an ending and rarely a clean absence. The dead may persist as a tendency in architecture: a balcony turning toward the light they preferred, a station opening near the hour they once came home. Families sometimes hear a familiar pressure in their ears before a silent train passes. Benches retain warmth for years.

Bodies are placed in the imported-earth gardens, where roots separate memory from matter. Matter enters the trees. Memory travels less predictably. Some enters the municipal systems and returns as useful intuition. Some is taken by mold. Some gathers in the weather membranes, producing rain over personally significant streets.

At memorials, no one says the dead have gone elsewhere. Elsewhere is too ordinary a condition.

They say the dead have become local.

And perhaps the largest inhabitant is the city itself, though it does not behave like a single creature. It is more like a colony whose members include foundations, forecasts, residents, rivers, unfinished rooms, and the land beneath them. It has no central mind. Its awareness moves. One week it may be concentrated in the reservoirs, watching through amber spines. Another week it inhabits elevator shafts, opening doors onto views it wants someone to see. During condensation events it seems to spread everywhere at once, present in every silver bead.

Its intentions, if it has them, are neither benevolent nor cruel. It appears to seek continuation, but not stability. It prefers corridors to boundaries, adjustment to repair, growth to completion. It tolerates people because they are good at carrying conditions from one place to another. People tolerate it because it gives their uncertainty somewhere to live.

Near dusk, when the calibration note moves through the plain, all these inhabitants briefly answer.

Fish alter depth. Cups ring against tables. Seam-eaters lift from the walls. Vines tighten around old machines. Grazers pause in the outer fernways. Witnesses raise their heads. Children in absent rooms go quiet. Under imported soil, the roots rearrange what they remember.

Across plazas that appear empty, thousands of unseen feet stop at once.

Then the note fades. The various versions of the city loosen and continue. Far out on the plain, condensation gathers along a line no map has marked. The keepers of absent rivers wake from sleep with wet palms. Bridges, still folded into the banks, begin dreaming of their span.

And through the green corridor left by the last water, the residents walk toward the next—sometimes solitary, sometimes accompanied by their elsewheres, their unrealized lives, and the soft wake of leaves that proves, for the moment, they are there.
