The people are small beside what they have inherited.

An adult can stand beneath most lintels without lowering the head, though the doors were made tall enough for three such people one above another. The old stairs have steps reaching nearly to the knee. In ceremonial avenues, the handrails pass above their shoulders. Whether the builders were larger, or merely expected grandeur to require inconvenience, no one knows. The present inhabitants have learned to insert narrow flights between the ancient steps, so that every public stair resembles two staircases interlaced.

They are compact, heavy-boned people, broad through the ribs and hips. Their feet are long and bare, with widely spread toes and soles callused almost to horn. Yet those soles remain remarkably sensitive. A citizen can identify a family member’s tread through two floors, distinguish a public drum from a collapsing wall, and follow foundation-messages while asleep. Children are taught to stand with their knees loose. A locked knee invites falls, cracked teeth, and fainting when the afternoon note passes through the stone.

Their skin ranges from limestone-pale to the dark red-brown of the city’s mineral seams. Whatever its natural color, it is usually filmed with dust and plant oil. The oil prevents splitting in the hot, dry air and gives exposed shoulders, cheeks, and hands a dull polish. Old people develop fine crystalline lines around their eyes and mouths where salt has worked into the skin. These are considered beautiful. Lovers sometimes trace them with black grazer pigment.

Their ears are small and close-folded. Many adults hear poorly in air, but this is not regarded as deafness. Air-hearing is imprecise, easily overwhelmed by ducts and windows. Bone-hearing matters more. Their jaw hinges are thick; their front teeth are often worn flat from pressing the mouth and cheek against speaking bowls. Builders develop pale patches on the forehead from years spent listening to walls.

The face is expected to speak as fluently as the hands. Eyebrows are left unshaved and darkened for clarity. Hair is braided away from the temples so that a tilt of the head can be read at a distance. Their sign language uses the whole upper body: fingers for nouns, wrists for tense, shoulders for certainty, chin and eyelids for evidence. A statement made with still shoulders means *I was told*. The same statement with the left shoulder lowered means *I felt it through stone*. The distinction can decide a trial.

In the roar, they appear grave and deliberate, conducting silent exchanges across bright streets. After dark, when the city quiets, their nature changes. They are talkative, quick to laugh, and often startled by the sound of their own voices. Spoken language is intimate. A person’s hand-sign is public, used in markets and records; the spoken name belongs to the household and is rarely given to strangers. To whisper someone’s spoken name into a wall is either a declaration of love or a curse, depending on which wall receives it.

They wear layered wraps of leaf fiber, woven vine, and mineral felt. Nothing hangs loose. Sleeves tie at the wrist, hems at the calf, hair at the nape. A stray strip of cloth can become a whip in the rising currents or sing distractingly against the body. Belts are weighted with ceramic beads, tools, fruit hooks, and small bags of smothering sand. Masons wear aprons sewn with pockets of graded grit. Gardeners carry crescent scrapers of sharpened shell-ceramic. Almost everyone owns a wax case for the ears, though using wax outside a wind court is seen as timid: it blocks certain warnings that enter through the skull.

Their food is pale, dry, and aromatic. Bladder-fruit pulp is pressed into cakes. Fan-tree scales are roasted on sun stones and ground with salt crystals harvested from living leaves. Copper vine yields a resin that can be chewed for hours and leaves the tongue numb. The flat grazers are eaten only during the dim cycle, toasted whole until their shells crack; the black pellets they leave are more valuable than their meat. Wealthy households cultivate cupboards of edible lichen, each strain fed on a particular blend of ash and powdered tile.

Meals are accompanied by vapor rather than drink. Heated bladder-fruit skins release cool, mineral-scented gas into hooded bowls. Diners place their faces beneath the hoods and inhale slowly. The practice eases thirst without satisfying it. Actual liquid exists only in tiny quantities: condensed oils, medicinal distillates, the contents of certain immature fruits. A spoonful may be part of a marriage exchange. Tears are not wasted sentimentally. Mourners collect them on absorbent threads and burn the threads at the funeral.

They live in households larger than families and smaller than streets. A household may contain siblings, adopted children, two or three married groups, unrelated specialists, and several rooms of dead. Kinship follows maintenance. Whoever repairs the same roof, tends the same trees, and answers the same foundation alarm is considered close relation. A child can acquire new grandparents by carrying mortar for them through one full construction cycle.

Births are uncommon. Infants are wrapped tightly so the city’s vibration does not startle their limbs apart. Before they learn hand signs, they communicate by tapping their heels against the floor. Adults answer in kind. The first recognized word is often not *mother* or *food*, but *again*—two pauses and a blow, meaning that a sensation should be repeated.

No one grows up alone, but many grow up surrounded by absence. Children claim entire abandoned houses as playrooms. They know which empty halls amplify footsteps, which shutters are named for the dead, which courtyards harbor grazers, and which sealed passages must not be touched. They make wind maps with ribbons and invent imaginary populations for vacant markets. Adults discourage the latter. To speak too precisely of nonexistent crowds is said to give the Outside measurements.

The inhabitants’ central labor is building, but “builder” is too broad a word for the distinctions they make. There are cutters, setters, listeners, weight-keepers, seam-readers, stair arbiters, mirror turners, rope singers, and those who specialize in structures intended never to be entered. A listener approves each block by pressing brow and teeth against it while an assistant strikes the pavement. Stones that answer at the wrong pitch are used for garden walls or tomb rooms. Stones that answer with no pitch are carried out of the district and abandoned under red moss.

They do not build from ambition. They build with the dutiful anxiety of people tightening knots during a storm.

To them, an empty building is not useless. It is reserve shape. It gives the city somewhere to strain, somewhere to direct a current, somewhere for an approaching silence to lose itself. A new balcony may be intended to hold no person, only a mirror and forty years of pressure. A staircase may end against a blank wall because the old plan commands it. Questioning such plans is permitted; leaving them incomplete is not.

Their oldest accusation is not murder or theft, but *making an edge*. An edge is any place where the city ends too abruptly: an unfinished wall, an unroofed stair, a family line with no adoptees, a name spoken but not answered. Edges attract the Outside. This belief explains much of their generosity and nearly all of their cruelty. Orphans are taken in immediately. Strangers are fed. Builders who abandon work may be sealed into their own unfinished rooms until they agree to continue.

Government belongs nominally to the wind priests, though the priests claim they only report what the dust has decided. They are recognizable by swollen jaws, missing teeth, and a slight permanent sway. Years in the wind courts loosen the balance organs. Between readings, they move through the city holding walls.

A priest’s interpretation is never delivered alone. Three enter the court: one watches the dust, one feels the paving, and one observes the other two for involuntary movements. Their combined signs become law. This produces judgments of impressive complexity and dubious clarity. A disputed inheritance might be resolved by ordering both households to construct a bridge together. A theft might be punished by silence: no one responds to the thief’s signs for nine days, forcing them to speak aloud if they wish to be understood.

The city’s history is preserved less in writing than in alterations. A narrowed doorway records a year of dangerous currents. Black mortar marks a fire. Steps cut sideways across older steps commemorate a population loss. Whole districts bear evidence of sudden departure: tools aligned on benches, fruit hooks still hanging, names cut halfway through shutters. The inhabitants say those people were “released,” the same word used when a dead person’s shutter tears free. They do not say where the released go.

Some believe the pale kite-creatures are released citizens made thin by distance. Children wave to them. Gardeners do not; kites passing over orchards cause bladder-fruit to rise too early. A kite that descends low enough to cast a visible shadow brings every household onto the roofs. No one has seen one land. Once in several generations, a translucent strip is found snagged on a tower vane. It is warm, weightless, and patterned with lines resembling streets.

The thing in the walls is regarded differently.

No image of it is permitted. Even vague gestures for its shape are taboo. It is called the Neighbor, the Weight Behind, or—among those too young to fear correctly—the Quiet-Maker. Priests insist there may be several. Masons insist there is only one, because all traveling silences have the same cadence: cessation, settling, three or five slow pressures, then grinding.

After it passes, listeners sometimes find marks on the inner faces of sealed stones. The marks resemble neither scratches nor tool cuts. They are broad, soft-edged impressions, as though something had pressed a folded body against the rock while it was still pliable. Red fiber is used to close the district, but not because red repels the thing. Red means the sealing is for the inhabitants’ benefit. The thing itself appears able to move through fitted masonry without displacing it.

There are those who think it feeds on noise. Others say noise is what keeps it asleep. A forbidden school of listeners believes the moving silence is not the passage of a creature at all, but its attention.

They point to the central grille.

The people who tend the grille are the city’s heaviest and calmest inhabitants. They shave their heads, wear thick felt around the chest, and seldom leave the basin. Their task is to add weight without obstructing the breath. They inspect the green stone, count the intervals between tremors, and sleep in shifts with their palms on the tiles. Over years, their hands curl into the posture of listening and cannot be straightened.

They do not call the sound beneath the grille a voice. That would imply language. Nor do they call it wind, because wind travels and this seems to remain where it is, forcing the city to move around it.

During the rare stoppages, everyone becomes briefly equal. Priest, child, mason, gardener, thief—all stand with bent knees and open hands. Their carefully expressive faces go blank. In that silence, the city’s people look less like masters of the hollow than like minute creatures inhabiting the chambers of some colossal organ.

Perhaps this is what they are.

The fan trees open and close like valves. The ropeways tighten like tendons. Mineral scales accumulate and are scraped away. The ducts breathe; the walls transmit; the central basin emits its foundational tone. Even the constant building begins to resemble not culture but healing: layer after layer laid over an ancient wound that will not close.

The inhabitants reject this comparison when it is spoken aloud. A city is made, they say. A creature grows.

Then they return to their scaffolds and add another course of stone, though none can say who first drew the plan.
