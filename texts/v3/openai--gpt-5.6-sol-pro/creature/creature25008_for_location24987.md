The inhabitants are, at first glance, ordinary working people: brickmakers, fitters, pump attendants, reed cutters, bakers, clerks, quarry surveyors. Nothing in their faces advertises the peculiarities of the town. They wear dark wool, waxed canvas, knitted caps, boots with replaceable soles. Their clothes are patched most heavily at the shoulders, where condensation drips from overhead pipes, and at the knees, from crouching beside drains and inspection hatches. Nearly everyone carries a rag, a stub of chalk, and some small tool whose purpose is not immediately apparent.

They tend to be deliberate in their movements. This is partly caution. A yard that was level last season may now pitch toward a sump; a stair may have pulled away from its landing by the width of a finger. It is also habit learned from machinery. People wait for the third beat of a pump before crossing a service track. They pause beneath sluices without seeming to listen, knowing from vibration whether the gate above is about to fall. In conversation they leave generous silences, because a fan may turn toward them or sheet metal begin to chatter. What looks like patience is often precise attention.

They stand close when they speak. Two people discussing bread, municipal levies, or a troublesome bearing may do so almost forehead to forehead, with one hand lightly touching the other’s sleeve. Visitors sometimes mistake this for intimacy. It is only the local distance at which words remain words.

Their voices are low and economical. Those who work near the towers develop a soft, carrying resonance, more felt in the chest than heard distinctly. Names are short. Instructions are made with gestures: a flat palm for pressure, two hooked fingers for a clogged line, a circling thumb for delay. Children learn the warning-board colors before they learn letters. An orange square means hot metal, blue means a line has been emptied, white means water fit for kitchens, and black means that no one is certain what is moving through the pipe.

No one wastes water, but neither do they treat it reverently. Reverence would be cumbersome in a place where every roof, sleeve, leaf, and wall is engaged in collection. Water is accounted for, routed, strained, warmed, borrowed, fouled, recovered, and accounted for again. A bathhouse attendant speaks of it as a miller might speak of grain. A gardener knows the week’s yield by the firmness of cabbage leaves. Children are scolded not for splashing but for splashing where the runoff cannot be caught.

The tower crews are the most visible workers. They climb among the ceramic fins in gray harnesses, carrying brushes made from bundled reeds and long wooden scrapers that will not chip the glaze. Their hands are broad across the knuckles and pale at the fingertips. Most have tiny cuts from slag or wire, sealed with black resin. They inspect gutters for leaf pulp, mineral crust, feathers, and coherent drops. Each crew keeps a shallow copper pan into which the round, unjoining water is coaxed. It cannot be poured in the usual sense. The separate beads must be rolled down a spiral and released one at a time onto dry charcoal, which breaks their stubborn surfaces.

The crews do not kill the snails. Some workers even move them aside before cleaning a fin, placing them in the damp fold of an elbow until the work is done. This is not affection exactly. The snails are considered fellow operatives—unlicensed, inconvenient, but clearly attached to the works.

Older fitters claim they can identify every major pump by touch through a wall. They sleep through the ordinary cycles and wake when a familiar vibration is absent. When machinery stops unexpectedly, the town’s first response is not alarm but a sudden population of doorways: faces emerging, heads tilted, floury hands and soapy arms held still. Silence is the one sound no one has learned to ignore.

The people who maintain the underground tanks are quieter than the tower crews. They work in rubber aprons and carry caged lamps low against the floor. Their trade runs in families, though no law requires it. They know which cistern echoes cleanly, which has a crack that opens only in cold weather, which root must be trimmed and which root is holding a wall together. They descend by iron ladders into greenish chambers where every noise becomes enormous.

Tank keepers are said to dream in measurements. They wake murmuring depths and diameters. Their children are often found tapping bowls, cupboards, and empty jars to hear what volume remains inside.

Above ground, households organize themselves less by blood than by catchment. A roof, courtyard, cistern, and drain make a practical unit, and everyone served by that system has obligations to it. A widow may share her tank with the family next door; three unrelated workers may own one glazed porch between them; a child might sleep in one building, eat in another, and be listed on the water account of a third. Local kinship terms reflect this. People speak of roof-sisters, pipe-uncles, and basin cousins. A roof-sister is someone entitled to your stored water in illness. A pipe-uncle is expected to come at night when the walls begin to drip.

Children spend little time indoors unless rain is actually falling. They follow freight carriages, hunt frogs in settling beds, and lay bits of colored glass over signal lamps. Their games are shaped by gradients. They race coherent droplets down panes, polished slates, leaves, and lengths of discarded gutter. They build elaborate channels from brick fragments and argue over whether nudging the ground counts as cheating.

They also play at settlement. One child stands upright as a survey peg while the others slowly pull at their boots until they lean. Another marks a straight street in chalk; the group then shifts the paving pieces beneath it by tiny increments, trying to distort the road without breaking the line. Adults pretend not to see the resemblance.

Every child knows that coherent water must not be sealed in glass. This makes sealing it in glass irresistible. Small forbidden bottles sometimes circulate through schoolrooms, padded with wool and kept upright with great care. Most contain ordinary rain. Occasionally one does not. Such a bottle seems full of loose crystal seeds, each drop preserving its own edge no matter how it is shaken. Teachers confiscate these without accusation and carry them directly to the nearest copper threshold.

The clinic treats predictable injuries: crushed fingers, infected cuts, strained backs, damp coughs, falls from ladders. It also treats complaints not well understood elsewhere. Some residents develop “echo ear” after years near the quarry and begin to hear repetitions after a sound has ended. Others suffer from stillness sickness during maintenance shutdowns—nausea, agitation, the sensation that the floor has ceased to support them. The usual remedy is to sleep beside a small reciprocating pump kept in the clinic cellar.

Births are registered twice: once by date, once by tank level. People remember that they were born in a low year, a green year, a year when the south reservoir overflowed, or during the six weeks when Tower Three produced water with a faint taste of pears. Funerals are modest. The dead are buried above the bowl where the ground is more stable, but a cup of cistern water is poured into the grave, returning a household’s share to open soil. Brass identification tags from old workers are often nailed inside the repair hall rather than placed with the body. There they tremble when the heavy pumps engage.

The bakery near valve square is run at night, when waste heat from the day can be drawn through its ovens. Its bakers have become skilled at judging dough in damp weather. Their loaves are dark, close-grained, and scored with the mark of the household catchment that ordered them. On collection mornings the bakery is crowded with workers warming their hands near the brick flues. Gossip travels there more reliably than official notices.

The people gossip mostly about maintenance: whose porch gutter has been badly aligned, which surveyor has concealed a leaning wall, whether the reeds in the western beds indicate a hidden leak. Moral judgment and hydraulic judgment are difficult to separate. Someone who lets a cistern foul is considered untrustworthy in other matters. Someone who repairs a neighbor’s drain before being asked may be forgiven almost anything.

There is a municipal administration, though much of its upper floor belongs to birds. The clerks work below in rooms hung with felt. They keep ledgers of roof area, household draw, pipe access, subsidence, and snail concentration. Because the town shifts continuously, property lines are recorded as relationships rather than fixed coordinates: six paces from the pear wall, downhill of the blue valve, touching the shadow of the east gantry at noon in late autumn. Disputes arise when a wall moves but its shadow does not move in the expected way.

The surveyors are disliked until needed. They cross the town carrying folding rods and small mirrored instruments, driving fresh pegs beside old ones rather than removing anything. As a result, some lots have accumulated little forests of stakes. Surveyors speak of movement without blame. A house has not failed; it has “expressed the ground.” A road has not buckled; it has “accepted curvature.” Everyone mocks this language and gradually adopts it.

There are gardeners in the concentrated green places, though few call themselves that. They are sump tenders, bed clearers, leak watchers, willow cutters. Their work is to negotiate with vegetation rather than defeat it. Moss is removed from valves but left between bricks. Ferns may remain in an inspection pit if their roots do not obscure the gauges. Willows are cut back in stages, because killing one too quickly can expose a leak that its roots had temporarily sealed.

These workers know water by plants. Mint means a shallow seep. Rushes mean a steady one. A single vigorous alder on raw spoil is treated almost as an accusation. The best leak watchers are sometimes hired by the works, sometimes fined by it, because they also guide households toward unregistered moisture.

The market contains people from beyond the rim: dryland farmers seeking frogs and reeds, metal buyers, kiln traders, itinerant knife sharpeners. Outsiders speak loudly and stand too far away. They complain that no one answers them, then discover half the market has heard every word through the paving. Local traders let negotiations lapse without embarrassment. A price offered in the morning may hang between buyer and seller until the next day, neither accepted nor withdrawn.

No one hurries to fill silence. There is rarely silence enough.

The town’s animals live beside the people without being fully domesticated. Swallows are tolerated in workshops unless their nests interfere with belts. Starlings are cursed, admired, and occasionally consulted. Certain old signal housings produce lineages of birds that reproduce specific alarms. Workers learn the differences: the real bell has a flaw on its second note; the bird’s version is perfect. New employees are fooled repeatedly.

Frogs are collected under license and traded alive to outer farms, but breeding pairs are left in every sump. Water voles are trapped only where they undermine channels. Foxes receive scraps near the abandoned sidings, though everyone denies feeding them. The pale reservoir eels are never eaten. Tank keepers maintain that creatures appearing in sealed water belong to the water and are therefore municipal property. The municipality has never confirmed this, perhaps because ownership would require an inventory.

The snails belong to no one. After rain they appear in such numbers that walls seem stippled with moving soot. Their shells are nearly clear, showing a dark folded body within, and under amber lamps each shell carries a pinprick of light. Children keep them briefly in damp matchboxes. Tower workers scrape them from pressure seals. Old residents place one on a windowsill when testing whether weather is about to change.

There are stories that the snails arrived in the clay used for the first ceramic fins. Others say they condensed out of the air during the works’ inaugural winter. A few believe the snails are ordinary and that it is the town’s water that has become strange. No one argues long. Origin matters less than maintenance.

The people have adjusted to coherent water in practical, intimate ways. Thresholds are channeled. Drawers are built with slight forward slopes. Cellars have copper combs across their lowest drains. Tailors sew tiny downward-facing gaps into pocket corners, so escaped drops cannot collect against the lining. After a funeral or a birth, someone always checks the house for jars.

Yet coherent water has also entered local metaphor. A person who refuses company is said to have “taken a shell.” A family that will not mingle its affairs with its neighbors is “running round.” A stubborn quarrel is expected to travel downhill until it finds a cellar. Lovers promise not that they will remain one, but that they will continue to join.

The eldest residents are not truly old. Few remember the bowl before the first tower, and those who do had arrived as laborers from elsewhere. They describe a raw camp of tents, portable boilers, mud, and stacked brick. Their authority comes not from ancestry but from having witnessed the first accumulation: the first tank filled, the first fern in a pit, the first child born to the pump rhythm.

They are amused when younger people call the town ancient. To those born there, rusted bolts, established willow roots, and a wall that has moved three inches are evidence of immemorial time.

At dusk the inhabitants emerge from workshops and bathhouses in scattered groups. Some tend shrubs against warm walls. Some sit on exterior stairs with bowls balanced on their knees. Tower crews descend, leaving the upper walkways to snails and birds. Children are called home by lamps placed in windows, each household using a different colored shade because voices do not carry reliably.

Nobody seems to watch the towers, but everyone registers their condition. A fan’s pitch changes and conversations pause. A sluice closes, pigeons rise, then settle. Beneath the square, water enters a tank in threads too thin to hear.

The town’s inhabitants are not stoic, exactly, nor unusually industrious. They complain, neglect duties, fall in love with unsuitable people, falsify meter readings, and postpone repairs until a root enters the kitchen. What distinguishes them is a faith in increments. They do not expect rescue in abundance. They trust the patched roof, the quarter-turn of a valve, the cup saved from rinsing, the wall corrected before it leans too far.

Even their idea of permanence is cumulative. Nothing stays where it was placed. The ground settles, pipes strain, streets bend, machines wear themselves smooth. To live there is not to preserve things unchanged but to notice them soon enough, and adjust them by hand.
