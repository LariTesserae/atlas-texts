At first it is difficult to say which of the city’s creatures are citizens.

The foil-winged flocks enter towers through apertures marked with civic lights. The six-legged browsers operate doors by drumming coded sequences on their frames. Groves alter drainage plans. Pools petition for shade by changing the pitch of their algae. Machines migrate, breed replacement parts, and sometimes abandon their assigned work to follow storms. None of these behaviors distinguishes animal from instrument, instinct from law.

The beings most often found inside rooms are called, in one approximation, the Listeners.

They are tall only when walking. At rest they fold themselves into low, many-jointed shapes, drawing their limbs beneath a mantle of dark skin. An adult may resemble a collapsed umbrella, a broad-backed amphibian, or a bundle of wet black leaves depending on its posture. There is no fixed front to the body. Four slender arms can serve as legs; two heavier limbs anchor against vibration; a ring of small, bright eyes closes or opens according to the direction of sound. The eyes perceive little color. Most are used to measure motion, cloud brightness, and the dangerous shimmer that precedes a field change.

Their true organs of attention lie along the ribs.

Each rib terminates in a sensitive plate beneath the skin. Together the plates receive pressure through air, ground, water, walls, and the bodies of other creatures. A Listener standing on naked substrate can feel a herd crossing many kilometers away, distinguish the operational pulse of one tower from another, and tell from the returning echo whether a grove has added new foliage since the previous cycle. In loud weather they brace with all six limbs and open their rib plates like gills. Their bodies become resonant chambers. Certain messages pass through them visibly, traveling as ripples from the feet to the crown.

They do not consider this hearing. Hearing is what small animals do with air. The Listeners call their faculty *remaining in contact*.

Their speech is correspondingly difficult to isolate. Part of it is vocal: low hoots, clicks produced in the throat, and fluting tones from vents beneath the arms. Part is impact. They tap floors, railings, root masses, bowls of water, or one another’s sternums. Part consists of pauses placed inside surrounding noise. A practiced speaker can answer a tower by interrupting its hum for less than a second, shaping the silence so precisely that it travels through the foundations as a recognizable name.

No Listener has only one name. They possess a room-name, used in engineered quiet; a ground-name, which may take several minutes to drum; a rain-name audible only in falling water; and a migratory name renewed whenever they cross the central mirror’s acoustic shadow. Young individuals are given provisional names by their household, but these are shed as the city learns them. The mature name is partly composed of replies from bridges, animals, and weather. It cannot be uttered by a single body.

Their young are soft, pale, and nearly deaf. For their first years they are carried in slings close to an adult’s vibrating chest or housed in warm wall hollows where civic machinery feeds them simplified versions of the city’s sound. A nursery may seem silent from outside. Within, its floors pulse with slow lessons: the location of safe water, the warning signature of descending hunters, the chord of an approaching shower, the rhythm that asks a moving bridge to wait.

The young first learn geography as music. They can sing distant districts before they can travel to them. They learn that one descending interval means a ravine whose walls may shear under weight; that a rough pulse beneath a sustained note describes a grove currently releasing spores; that an echo returning too cleanly from the central basin must never be answered.

Adulthood begins when a child is sent across an open interval alone.

There is no fixed distance. The journey ends only when the city replies differently to the traveler than it did to the child who left. Some return after a few hours carrying the rain-pitch of a nearby pool. Others travel for years along the silent roads, sleeping beneath leaves, under arches, or inside the warm abdominal chambers of transport organisms. A few come back from districts no one else remembers building. Their ground-names contain unfamiliar foundations.

Listeners inhabit the residential knots beneath transit spans, but their households are not based solely on ancestry. A household may include three or four Listeners, a colony of heat-producing fungi, several foil-winged animals trained to carry sound above the cloud base, and a maintenance body that resembles a low cabinet walking on dozens of glassy feet. Some households adopt pools. Others bind themselves to a migratory grove and occupy rooms only when it passes beneath them.

Possessions are rare, because the city moves objects more reliably than individuals can keep them. What they preserve are acoustic shapes. A family inheritance may be a particular echo cultivated inside a shaft, a thunderclap routed through seven distant arches, or a rhythm that causes an extinct orchard’s dormant seeds to open. Such things cannot be stolen in the ordinary way. They can be mistimed, distorted, or forgotten.

For this reason, forgetting is their greatest fear.

The city archives much of what occurs, but it does not preserve events intact. Its lakes turn testimony into shorelines. Roots keep pressure patterns but not the intentions that made them. Animals carry fragments of public announcements in migration calls, changing them slightly in each generation. A law might survive as the route of a stream long after everyone has forgotten what behavior it once governed.

Some Listeners devote themselves to maintaining these transformations. They are not historians so much as conductors. They walk the seven civic lakes, sounding depths with weighted tendrils and singing currents into alignment. They introduce young fish whose schooling behavior preserves disputed clauses. They persuade reeds to colonize one inlet and machines to erode another. When the shoreline has assumed the required shape, the law is considered legible again—even if it now means something new.

Others tend the city’s vast intervals. These are the Keepers of Room.

They prevent settlement from accumulating in places reserved for events. They prune exploratory roots from migration corridors, dismantle unauthorized shelters, and mark future storm grounds with vibrations too low for most animals to notice. Their work often appears destructive. A Keeper may spend decades nurturing a forest and then, upon receiving a signal from the buried machines, ask the forest to withdraw. If it refuses, the Keeper negotiates with fungi, rain, browsing herds, and soil heat until the boundary moves.

They are respected and frequently disliked. Empty land invites use, and not every resident accepts that an event expected in two centuries deserves more space than a living household now.

Disputes are rarely decided by a council. They are given bodies.

One party may encode its claim in a flowering meadow; another in the route of a temporary stream. Witnesses add migratory calls, bridge angles, and changes in local rain. The resulting argument is allowed to move through the city. Wherever its components meet, consequences appear. Flowers attract grazers; grazers alter the soil; altered soil changes the tower’s shadow; the shadow shifts a grove. The ruling is whatever stable arrangement remains.

Such judgments can take years. During that time both sides may die, divide into new households, or cease to care. This is not considered a failure. A decision that cannot outlive its petitioners is thought too small to impose on the plain.

The Listeners are not the only people.

In the shafts dwell buoyant colonies that never descend to the ground. From below they look like pale veils snagged on balconies. Each veil is composed of hundreds of hand-sized organisms linked by filaments and shared circulation. Separately they show little intelligence. Joined across a shaft, they remember, bargain, compose forecasts, and produce translucent young that drift until another building catches them. They speak by changing the temperature of rising air.

The Listeners trade with them for knowledge of the upper atmosphere. Conversations take days. A Listener sounds a question into the shaft wall; the colony warms one side of the updraft; cloud moisture gathers in response; droplets fall through selected balconies and strike tuned plates below. Complex answers arrive as rain indoors.

There are also the glass-backed herds. Most of the year they seem like animals: broad grazers on narrow legs, their transparent dorsal shells filled with symbiotic plants. Yet during migration they assemble into precise moving arrays. Sunlight, weak as it is, passes from one back to another. Across thousands of bodies the reflections form calculations. Transit systems pause for them. Towers solicit their routes. Keepers of Room alter whole districts to preserve the angles through which the herd thinks.

Whether any individual grazer understands this is regarded as irrelevant.

Even the pale gasborne hunters have a civic role. They patrol above groves, removing diseased animals and carrying heat from one canopy to another. Old hunters become too heavy for flight. They settle among the branches, root their trailing limbs into trunks, and gradually transform into warm, breathing nests used by dozens of species. Some remain awake for many years, remembering the sky through the movements of those that shelter inside them.

Death here is seldom a clean departure.

A Listener’s body is placed in a shallow resonant chamber beneath living roots. As it decomposes, gases and fluids change the chamber’s pitch. The household visits to hear the gradual alteration. At first the dead retain recognizable rhythms: a cough, a favored interval, the pulse of an old injury. Later these dissolve into the hum of the grove. When no one can distinguish the body’s note from the roots, mourning ends.

Sometimes the city intervenes before that point. A tower may adopt the fading resonance and begin transmitting it across the plain. A bridge may flex in the cadence of a dead child. Rain may fall for years with the faint pitch of someone who once measured storms. Such persistence is not always welcomed. There are specialists who travel to structures carrying unwanted remnants and teach them how to forget.

No one knows whether the city itself is conscious.

The Listeners distrust the question. Consciousness, to them, is not a substance confined within a boundary. It is an event that occurs when enough things remain in contact long enough to change one another. A household can be conscious. A storm can become conscious while crossing a field of tuned flowers. The civic lakes are conscious during certain tides. An individual, alone in engineered silence, may temporarily cease to be.

This is why the silent corridors inspire both relief and dread.

Travelers enter them to protect their bodies from the city’s immense sound, but they do not linger. Within the cancellation field, a Listener’s rib plates fold close. Their posture rises and narrows. They speak with the small sounds of mouth and hands. Their thoughts become private, quick, and strange. Some seek this condition deliberately. They form secret societies in unused chambers, valuing ideas that have never been tested against soil, weather, or another chest.

The city tolerates them. Perhaps it needs them. New things often begin as errors made in isolation.

Beyond the silence, the greater population continues its work. Flocks carry coordinates between moving towers. Larvae clean the bright pools. Groves debate mineral changes through their roots. Veil colonies reshape updrafts. Listeners cross warm black ground with cancellation fields shimmering around them, pausing where rain sings overhead.

And beneath them all, in machinery older than the plain, something receives these innumerable signals—not as commands, perhaps, and not as prayers. It adjusts heat. Extrudes stone. Opens a buried channel. Moves a tower by the width of a shadow.

The inhabitants feel the answer rise through their feet.

One by one, then grove by grove and herd by herd, they answer back.
