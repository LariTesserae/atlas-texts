At first they seem fewer than they are.

The water conceals their footsteps, and the breadth of the town separates one task from another so completely that a visitor may pass a morning seeing only signs: a ladder moved to a different wall; fresh knife marks on a gutter; a line of wet footprints crossing a vacant court and ending at a measuring stone. Smoke rises from six or seven roofs. A red sleeve appears high in a timber tower, then is gone. Somewhere nearby, behind the roar of the upper springs, two people are speaking with their foreheads almost touching.

There are usually between forty and sixty inhabitants, though no one gives a precise number. Children below their naming age are omitted from formal tallies, as are visitors who have not yet stayed through a change of season. The very old sometimes ask to be removed from the count before they die, saying they have become part of the town’s memory rather than its labor. Thus the number kept on the tablets is always smaller than the number of bodies at supper.

They are not a lost people, nor are they exiles. Paths descend from the basin on three sides, and in clear weather the lower valleys can be seen through breaks in the ridge. Pack trains come several times a year with grain, salt, lamp oil, charcoal, fruitwood, bronze, and news. Most inhabitants were born elsewhere. They come young, often after apprenticeships in ordinary towns where walls are expected to remain where they are put. Some stay for a few years. Some descend as soon as they understand what a lifetime of minute adjustment demands. A few discover that all other places feel dead beneath their feet.

Those who remain acquire a characteristic stillness. They pause before setting down a cup. They listen after closing a door. When crossing a threshold, they touch the lintel cradle with the backs of two fingers, not as a blessing but to learn whether the timber has begun to bind. Even in the lowlands they are said to look at stairways as though expecting them to breathe.

Their bodies are sturdy rather than large. Nearly everyone has strong hands, thickened fingertips, and a slight asymmetry of the shoulders from carrying poles, plumb frames, coils of wet rope, and baskets of cut moss. Their knees are often scarred from working close to the paving. The constant damp reddens their cheeks and roughens the skin around their nails. Older inhabitants suffer from swollen joints, yet many preserve unusually keen hearing—not for softness, since little here is soft, but for distinctions inside noise. They can separate a blocked culvert from a full one by its boom beneath the street. They know which wooden bracket is clattering loose three courtyards away. A change in the upper fall wakes some of them from sleep.

They speak quietly. Shouting is useless and, more importantly, imprecise. Indoors they converse normally; outside they stand near, often gripping one another lightly at the elbow so that attention cannot be mistaken. This gives strangers the impression of great intimacy. It is not always so. Two surveyors who have disliked each other for twenty years may argue nose to nose, hands resting with perfect courtesy on one another’s sleeves.

Their clothing is layered wool, felt, and tightly woven flax, darkened by oil at the shoulders. Long outer garments are split high enough to climb ladders and are weighted at the hems with small copper pieces so they do not drag into channels. Hoods fit close around the face. Bright dyes are reserved for cuffs, cords, and glove backs, places visible through mist. Each working household favors a simple combination—white knots on green, three red bars, yellow stitching in a broken square—so people can be recognized at a distance when cloud reduces them to moving blurs.

They wear many things from their belts: wedges, wax tablets, folding rules, chalk in horn tubes, a dry twist of tinder sealed in bladder, and little wooden cups for covering the ends of measuring pegs. Almost everyone carries a narrow mallet. A newcomer thinks these are weapons until an inhabitant stops in mid-conversation to tap a wall and listen.

No one rules the town in the manner of a mayor or chief. Authority changes with the question. The Keeper of Courses decides which walls may be dismantled. The Listener of Waters can order any building emptied if the sounds below it alter. The senior felt worker has final say over roof loading. Storekeepers govern winter rations. Surveyors hold the greatest prestige, but not unchecked power: every important measurement must be witnessed by one person who cannot read the level marks, on the principle that learning can teach a person to see what a record predicts.

The town’s oldest office is the Rememberer of Errors. This person recites failed expectations at the beginning of each building season: the court where no shelf rose, the bridge beneath which no spring appeared, the eastern tower shifted twice because the mountain emerged elsewhere, the buried chamber opened in the wrong century. The recital is not comic, though some failures provoke fond laughter. Its purpose is to prevent hope from hardening into certainty.

The inhabitants do not worship the mountain exactly. They are wary of saying it wants anything. Wanting belongs to creatures that can be thwarted, and they do not believe the mountain can be thwarted. Nor do they call it alive in ordinary speech. They say it is arriving, unfolding, becoming present. Their reverence lies in accommodation.

They have no temple. The nearest thing is a narrow, roofless house beside the First Court containing shelves of malformed objects: a cup pinched oval by a rising floor, a door hinge bent around a new nub of stone, a perfectly straight survey rod that no longer agrees with any street, a child’s footprint preserved in a tile that hardened too early. Nothing is offered there. Nothing is prayed to. People enter when they need to be reminded that accuracy is temporary.

Their children are noisy by local standards and silent by anyone else’s. They play at surveying, stretching threads between stools and announcing grave corrections when a sleeping dog—on the rare occasions a trader brings one up—disturbs the line. They sail bark boats down the street canals, build dams of moss, and are reprimanded sharply because even games must not teach water a bad route. They know the empty districts better than the adults do. To them, an uninhabited house is not eerie. It is a place to climb, hide, gather white flowers, or claim as the palace of some invented future family.

A child receives a public name only after noticing a change that an adult confirms. It may be something small: a new seep beneath a bench, a stone slab’s altered pitch, a crack closing in a cistern rim. Until then the child is called by household endearments. The custom ensures that each named person has, at least once, paid attention to the town and been answered by it.

This produces names that sound peculiar in translation: Finds-the-Cold-Step, Heard-the-Third-Drain, Moss-at-the-North-Joint. In daily use they shorten to Step, Third, North. Outsiders sometimes assume these are occupational titles. The inhabitants rarely correct them.

Households are fluid. A dwelling may contain siblings, former spouses, apprentices, widowers, two unrelated old women, and a child whose parents are spending three seasons in the lower valleys. Marriage exists but does not determine residence. Buildings do. People sleep where the floor is most stable, move when damp gets into the bedding, and eat wherever a stove is already hot. Affection organizes itself around maintenance: who remembers to dry whose boots, who checks a ladder before another climbs it, who quietly takes over a midnight cistern watch.

They have little privacy in inhabited rooms and enormous solitude everywhere else. Quarrels can be carried into vacant avenues where the water swallows every word. Grief often moves outward too. After a death, those closest to the dead sweep one of the unused plazas from wall to wall. The work may take several days. No marker is erected; a fixed grave within the basin would be an assertion no one trusts. Bodies are taken below the ring wall to a narrow terrace of imported soil, where rowans are allowed to grow untrained.

The dead remain in the measurements. Survey tablets are signed, and old hands are recognizable in the shape of their cuts. A careful reader can follow one person across sixty years: first as witness, then apprentice, then master, then an increasingly shaky annotator in the margins. The inhabitants speak of consulting these people, not remembering them.

Food is plain and wet-weather practical: barley porridge, goat curds, flatbread baked on hot stone, onions, dried pears, bitter herbs, and stews kept simmering for days. Fresh greens are gathered from roofs and walls, with strict distinctions between what may be eaten and what must remain to hold a joint together. Children are taught early never to pull moss casually. Every cushion may be performing work.

Meals are held in the southern quarter at several long tables. The tables have adjustable legs, and before anyone sits, the youngest named child checks them with a small weight on a string. Visitors laugh at this only once. Spilled bowls are common enough without carelessness.

They celebrate slowly. At the festival of First Mist, lamps are set throughout the empty town, one in every sound window and sheltered doorway. The few inhabitants walk the broad streets among hundreds of lights, and for a night the city appears populated beyond reason. There is no pretense that the absent citizens are spirits. The lamps are for future occupants, though no one claims to know whether those people will arrive in ten years, ten generations, or at all.

During the festival, each person chooses an empty house and leaves something useful inside: a dry spoon, a coil of cord, a roof peg, a scrap of felt, a child’s carved figure. Years later the giver may find the object shifted, decayed, borrowed, or enclosed by new stone. Such discoveries are regarded as correspondence.

Music must contend with the water. Flutes disappear into it, and drums add only confusion. Their instruments are suspended slabs, hollow wooden boxes, taut cords stretched across frames, and copper bowls partly filled with water. They do not try to overpower the basin. Players wait for recurring gaps and accents in the roar, placing notes where the town leaves room. The best musicians can make it seem, briefly, that the drains and falls have learned the piece.

Their humor is similarly patient. Many jokes depend on an answer delayed for months. Someone paints a tiny door on a blank rock face; the following year another person adds a handle where a seam has appeared. A surveyor who predicts a shelf incorrectly may find a miniature bridge built over the unchanged spot. No name is attached. Everyone knows.

The pale newts in the cisterns are the only creatures granted something like civic standing. Each large cistern has a watcher who records their appearance, color, appetite, and depth. A newt found in a drinking reservoir is greeted with relief. One found climbing a wall prompts immediate inspection. They are never handled bare-skinned. When channels must be drained, children carry the newts in cool copper-lined bowls to temporary pools, walking with the solemnity of a procession.

The goats are less honored and more loved. They have names before many children do. Their roof grazing is carefully assigned because a careless goat can strip a week’s sealing from a parapet. They sleep in raised sheds and complain whenever the ground shifts beneath the posts. The inhabitants trust this complaint more than several kinds of instrument.

As for the slate-colored birds, no one claims them. Their arrivals are entered in the weather records. Some believe they follow newborn rock; others say they merely follow fog-borne insects. A few roofs bear shallow stone hollows made in hopes of nests, but the birds ignore them. This failure has not discouraged the builders. Empty provision is, after all, the town’s most characteristic form of generosity.

Newcomers often struggle with the apparent futility of life there. They ask when the city will be finished, when the expected population will come, whether the painted streets will ever be cut, whether generations of work have produced anything except maintenance. The inhabitants offer no satisfying doctrine. They show the records. They point to a doorway that was once half its height, a cistern now too shallow for use, a wall lifted intact on wooden cradles, a tower whose invisible core has at last begun to press pale stone through its floor.

For some, this is enough.

Those who stay come to understand the emptiness not as absence but as allowance. The broad courts leave room for what cannot yet be seen. The unused drains acknowledge water before it arrives. The timber towers give outlines to stone still under the world. Even the inhabitants think of themselves this way: not founders, not owners, perhaps not even citizens, but the temporary human framework around something unfinished.

Their deepest fear is not that the mountain will rise too violently. It is that it will stop.

A still year makes them uneasy. If pitches remain unchanged and no paving lifts, measurements are repeated until tempers fray. People descend the slopes searching for fresh cracks. Cistern watchers sleep badly. The First Court fills each morning with silent observers staring at the low dome for a bead of water moving the wrong way.

Then, perhaps, a stair tilts by the width of a fingernail. A dry hollow begins to knock beneath the rain. One of the suspended slabs rings a fraction sharper at dawn.

The news passes from house to house by touch and close speech.

Work crews gather. Children run ahead with chalk. Someone brings hot broth to the surveyors. In the blank districts, lamps are lit though no festival has been declared.

And all day the few inhabitants move through the enormous town with the grave, relieved haste of people whose expected guest has finally been heard at the door.
