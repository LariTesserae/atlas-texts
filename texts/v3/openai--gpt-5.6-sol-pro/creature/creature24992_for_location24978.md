No single creature can be said to inhabit the cleft. The cleft is inhabited in layers, each life fitted to a different expression of the mountain: its breath, its heat, its charge, its smallest tremor.

Closest to the stone is the black film.

It has no shape of its own beyond the shapes imposed by cracks and currents. In darkness it resembles soot worked deep into the lava, but when the buried electrical field strengthens, branching maroon lines travel through it. Those lines grow, fork, fail, and reconnect. The film consumes what the mountain exhales—hydrogen, sulfurous vapor, carbon compounds—and fixes them into brittle microscopic frameworks. It is less like a plant than a patient mineral metabolism spread over acres in fragments. Nearly everything else in the cleft is built from what it makes.

The film is never permitted to grow undisturbed for long.

Obsidian-shelled disks move over it in herds. Their upper surfaces are glossy and nearly featureless, while beneath each shell is a dense field of cutting mouthparts and heat-sensitive cilia. They rasp the black growth from the stone and incorporate its carbon lattices into their bodies. When food is abundant, a luminous seam appears across a disk’s middle. The seam widens until the animal becomes two smaller disks, which remain side by side for several hours before their movements diverge.

They have no leaders, but a herd turns as one body. Each disk feels the pressure of its neighbors through the ground. A change at the front passes backward in a widening curve, so that thousands may pour around an obstacle without touching it. They avoid the old circular walls even when the film within is thick enough to shine. Hunger will bring them to the boundary, but not across it. There they collect in black crescents, scraping bare every reachable fraction of growth and leaving the interior untouched.

On the walls live the ivory colonies.

An individual is a thumb-sized, six-limbed animal enclosed in a hinged shell. Alone it appears unfinished: blind, pale, almost helpless. In a colony, its shell edges lock against those of its neighbors, making a continuous armor over the cliff. Gas seeping from the basalt passes through chambers beneath them. They filter metallic dust and chemical traces with the copper plates in their mouths, closing in coordinated waves whenever the flow becomes poisonous or the stone carries an unfamiliar vibration.

Their coordination does not depend on a central mind. Pressure travels through the interlocked shells. One animal tightens, then six around it tighten, then dozens, until a warning can cross an entire wall faster than any member could move alone. The colony’s apparent gestures—the ripples, openings, and sudden white stillness—belong to the arrangement rather than to any individual.

At certain deep tones, the creatures open not to feed but to reproduce. They release a dry cloud of coppery particles that settles into hot cracks. Most particles die. A few adhere in sheltered places and assemble themselves gradually, first as films, then as tiny plates, then as a new pale animal fused among its siblings. No juvenile survives alone.

Around the sulfide spires coil the thread-hunters.

They are long enough to encircle a person’s arm several times, though scarcely thicker than wire. Bands of red, black, and metallic yellow run along their bodies, matching the mineral layers on which they rest. Their paired heads form a blunt fork pressed constantly against the stone. One branch sends minute stresses into the crystal; the other receives the returning pattern. In this way they perceive hollows, fractures, moving grazers, and one another.

Their strike is not a lunge through open air. The whole body tightens around a spire, and the tightening travels in a loop too quickly for the eye to separate into motion. Anything between the coils is crushed or cut. Afterward the hunter remains still while digestive fluids pass through hair-thin channels into the broken prey.

They are solitary except during the mountain’s low songs. Then neighboring hunters touch their forked heads to opposite sides of the same spire. They exchange patterns of compression through it, testing one another through the mineral. Some separate. Others wind together and leave behind chains of ceramic eggs in the hottest fissures.

Above them all are the soot-kites.

Their membranes are mosaics of interlocking scales, each scale adjustable by a minute muscular root. By lifting and lowering thousands of scales independently, a kite can hold itself in a rising column of air without flapping. Its hooked body hangs beneath the membranes like a plumb weight. Along its underside is a folding comb used to gather disks from the floor.

A kite may remain aloft for most of its life. It sleeps by surrendering fine control to simple reflexes that keep it aligned with the updraft. It feeds in sudden descents and drinks nothing. The small amount of chemically bound moisture in its prey is dismantled and used repeatedly within sealed tissues. Its waste leaves as dry crystals dropped among the spires.

The canopy has its own weather. Lower kites ride the hottest, strongest currents but accumulate damaging mineral dust. Upper kites cool and clean themselves in thinner air but cannot remain there indefinitely. They exchange positions throughout the day, producing the immense slow rotation visible from below. No individual directs this wheel. Each responds only to heat, pressure, exhaustion, and the angles of nearby wings.

Their courtship occurs during the buried tremors. Two kites align one above the other, close enough that the scales of their membranes exchange static charge without touching. If the charge patterns correspond, they rise together into the upper canopy. Later each sheds a cluster of fertilized scales. These descend into particular hot vents, where the scales fuse around a larval hook. The young crawl among the grazer herds until their membranes harden and the mountain lifts them.

Yet even these multitudes are not the cleft’s strangest inhabitants.

The tall ones live inside the cliffs.

At rest, they are folded into lengths and angles that scarcely resemble bodies. Their ribs overlap. Their six legs double back beside the spine. The skull divides along flexible seams, allowing the broad lower jaw to turn edgewise. In this condition one may occupy a crack no wider than a hand, sealed behind a skin of ceramic powder. It can remain there through thousands of heat cycles, almost without metabolism, waking only when a particular family of vibrations rises from the faults.

Unfolded, it stands taller than most of the spires. It is ash-gray, narrow as a blade when seen from the front, and broad from the side. Each foot ends in several fine points that test the lava before bearing weight. It never dislodges a pebble if another placement is possible.

The tall ones do not appear to feed on animals. They scrape neither film nor mineral crust. While concealed, they may draw energy directly from thermal differences across the cliff, or from chemical colonies embedded in their tissues. They emerge with one purpose: to visit the upright slabs.

Each settles its jaw against a groove worn into the stone. Cavities in its skull alter the fault’s tremor, just as the chamber of an instrument alters a vibrating string. Different individuals have differently shaped cavities. As they arrive, one by one, the vibration changes. Frequencies combine beneath hearing. Beats pass through the floor, the spires, the circular walls, and the tiled colonies.

Whether the tall ones create the signal or complete it is impossible to distinguish.

They may be attendants responding to the mountain. They may be animals using an ancient natural formation for courtship. They may be descendants of the beings that raised the slabs. They may themselves have raised them, not through planning as another intelligence would understand it, but through a hereditary compulsion enacted over generations: shift one stone during one tremor, wedge it during another, return centuries later to alter its angle.

Their patience is geological because much of their life is spent outside ordinary time.

Nothing indicates that they command the other species. Nevertheless, when their jaws meet the slabs, the entire cleft reproduces. The wall colonies release their metallic dust. Grazers split. Kites pair and shed living scales. Thread-hunters migrate and lay eggs. Even the black film rearranges its branching glow.

Thus the signal may be a summons, a calendar, or merely a vibration to which every surviving lineage has adapted. The distinction may no longer matter. Whatever first made the circles, the present ecosystem has grown around them so completely that stone structure, animal behavior, and subterranean fault now form parts of one event.

This is the cleft’s largest inhabitant: not the tall animal, nor the canopy, nor the pale colonies, but the accord among them.

It has no continuous flesh. Its organs are species. Its pulse is the heat tide. Its circulation is the movement of grazers through the aisles and kites through the convection layers. The black film is both pasture and memory, altering its electrical branches according to what has crossed it. The spires are sensory structures, carrying pressure from predator to prey and from fault to slab. The tall ones are perhaps its slow reproductive caste, or perhaps only listeners within a far older arrangement.

This greater creature does not think quickly. It notices pressure before shape, rhythm before presence. A single foreign step is not understood as a foot. It is understood as a new pattern entering the stone.

At once the disk herds stop. The thread-hunters flatten themselves against their spires. Ivory shells close until the walls become blank. The kites lock their membranes and hang overhead like a dark, rigid vault. Deep in the cliffs, the tall ones remain folded, receiving the disturbance through every point of contact.

The cleft appears to empty because all its inhabitants share the same first defense: silence.

But beneath that silence, the unfamiliar rhythm is being passed from shell to shell, spire to coil, wall to slab. The entire place attends to it. If the rhythm repeats, some lives will flee it, some will hunt it, and some may eventually incorporate it into the mountain’s long song.

For the moment, they do nothing.

They listen.
