Nothing lives in the basin as it would live in a settled country. There are no creatures of the whole place. There are creatures of ledges, culverts, warm skins of water, gravel islands, steel chambers, and the brief dry intervals between floods. Even the largest inhabitants possess it only by routes.

The first life one notices is the life that cannot hide.

Frogs crowd the shallow terraces. They are small, mud-colored things until they open their throats, when the margins of the pools become studded with pale, trembling bladders. Their numbers rise and collapse with the water. A newly flooded bench may be empty at dusk and loud by morning, as though the frogs had formed from the rain itself. They lay eggs in wheel ruts, sump fringes, flooded cable trenches—anywhere warm water can hold still for several days. Most of these nurseries vanish. Tadpoles blacken the shrinking puddles, feeding furiously while the edges draw inward. Gulls learn which pools are failing and arrive before the mud closes over them.

The frogs know the machinery by vibration. The steady labor of pumps does not trouble them. They call through it, fitting their pulses into the larger pulse until motor and animal seem coupled. But a new vibration—a bank shifting, a truck approaching, pressure entering a pipe—passes through the water differently. Then a whole pond may fall silent at once. Workers heed this. The frogs feel certain changes before instruments register them, though no one mistakes the frogs for infallible. Sometimes they fall silent because a heron has landed.

The herons are great gray fixtures among the pipes. They stand ankle-deep beside rusted flanges and drowned stair rails, patient enough to resemble another form of abandoned equipment. Mineral water stains their legs. Their feathers become soiled beneath the wings where they brush iron scum, and the oldest birds carry a reddish cast that no molt entirely removes. They feed on fish, frogs, and the pale crustaceans that multiply in sheltered sumps. When a pump starts and the water draws down, they walk behind the retreating edge, spearing whatever has been stranded.

Other birds are less grave. Lapwings occupy the gravel caps, nesting where no sensible animal would trust the ground. They defend bare circles among tire fragments and shards of shale, attacking surveyors, foxes, and excavators with equal conviction. Terns follow the moving water. Gulls break mussels and snails on concrete roofs. Wagtails hunt insects along handrails, their feet leaving momentary marks in the condensation.

The sand martins are the basin’s most numerous miners.

They arrive when the clay faces have dried enough to hold a tunnel but not enough to become hard. A colony can transform a blank wall in days. Thousands of beaks and feet work the ocher bands, making a soft continuous scratching beneath the greater noise. Their burrows are shallow where the clay is wet, deep where a dry seam permits it, and abandoned without hesitation when seepage begins. After heavy rain, sections of wall collapse with nests inside them. The survivors wheel before the fallen clay, then begin again a few meters away.

Their cliffs do not last from one generation to the next. The birds inherit no ancient colony, only the habit of recognizing fresh earth.

Insects inherit even less. Midges live by the hour and in such numbers that they give the air texture. Their larvae occupy stagnant films where oil, algae, and mineral precipitate meet. Dragonflies breed in cleaner cuts and patrol along channels as straight as survey lines. Water beetles arrive at night, mistaking every silver surface for a natural pond: sumps, puddles, open tanks, rainwater trapped in the scoop of a bucket. Many choose badly. In the morning their bodies collect against grilles, along with leaves from plants that grow nowhere nearby.

Below the reflective surface lives a less visible population. Fish enter through culverts during periods of high water and remain when the levels separate. Each pool becomes an experiment. In one, minnows proliferate until the water seems granular. In another, perch eat everything smaller than themselves and then thin into a few large, scarred individuals. Eels inhabit flooded pipes and can pass between compartments thought to be isolated. Carp nose through drowned soil, releasing methane in beads behind them. Some fish are pale from turbidity; others are dark-backed, nearly black, matching the sumps in which birds hunt them.

No species occupies every pool. The water differs too much. A drainage ditch safe for tadpoles may be lethal ten meters downstream, where acidic seepage enters from a spoil heap. Some pools support reeds, insects, fish, and birds in crowded abundance. Others hold only films of bacteria: orange threads at an iron-rich outflow, white veils in sulfurous hollows, green skins over warm, still water. These are inhabitants too, perhaps the most faithful ones. They accept conditions no animal can endure and alter them slowly, fixing minerals, consuming what leaks from buried soil, making the first frail sediment in which other life may later take hold.

On land, every animal travels as though crossing a set of traps.

Foxes use the conveyor galleries because the steel and concrete remain dry above floods. They den in cable vaults, drainage pipes, and the cavities beneath broken slabs. Their coats are often thin from heat, their paws gray with clay. They learn shift changes. A fox may wait beside a lit station for food scraps, then disappear before the workers leave the building. They hunt lapwing chicks on gravel pads and frogs where receding water concentrates them. Their droppings appear on survey points and junction boxes—the highest, driest markers available.

The feral dogs are less discreet. They descend from village animals, abandoned guard dogs, and litters born among the sheds. There is no single pack. Groups combine and divide according to season, flooding, and food. Long-legged yellow dogs favor the open roads; heavier, dark-coated animals keep near workshops and occupied stations. Most avoid people at close range but shadow vehicles from ridge to ridge. They know which sirens precede blasting, which trucks carry lunches, and which pump houses have night crews.

Their paths are inherited, but only briefly. An older dog may lead younger ones toward a crossing that has gone underwater since the previous wet season. They stand at the edge, bewildered by the failure of memory, then search the bank for another way.

Bats possess the upper darkness. They roost behind corrugated cladding, inside idle housings, and in cracks where different layers of the cut have pulled apart. At dusk they stream low over the pools, taking midges in numbers too large to matter. Certain pump sheds contain maternity colonies, and maintenance crews leave marked panels undisturbed until the young can fly. Other colonies are discovered only when a dead motor is opened and warm air, ammonia, and hundreds of small bodies spill from the casing.

There are also animals that arrive without becoming inhabitants: deer stranded on gravel islands, cattle that descend a service road and cannot climb the slick return, migrating birds brought down by the false breadth of the water. After storms, ducks rest on the long ponds. Some depart the next morning. Some remain, molt, breed, and become part of the basin’s calculations.

The human beings do not quite live there, though many have spent most of their lives below the rim.

They come down at shift change in mud-colored trucks: pump attendants, electricians, mechanics, survey crews, slope inspectors, water chemists, machine operators. Their clothing is bright because everything else is not. Lime yellow and orange figures move between gray banks, disappearing completely when mist closes over a road. They wear hearing protection even in places that seem quiet, knowing that the quiet is only a temporary arrangement of noise.

They speak with their hands. A flat palm means stop. A finger circled overhead means start the motor. Two arms raised toward the wall mean look above you, not at me. In the pump houses they converse by touch, tapping shoulders before pointing at gauges. Their spoken voices become oddly soft away from work; at the rim cafés they sometimes lean close out of habit, though there is no machinery between them.

Every crew develops an intimate territory. One knows the western culverts and can tell by smell when peat-dark water has entered them. Another knows the electrical islands and which cabinets collect snakes for warmth. Surveyors know the moving terraces not as landscapes but as sequences of numbered posts, each with a history of minute displacement. Pump attendants recognize machines by tone. They hear a failing bearing inside the basin’s general thunder as readily as a parent hears a child calling in a crowd.

The dragline crew inhabits a world above ground. The operator sits in the lit housing with the boom extending into mist, judging unseen distances from cable tension, bucket weight, and the delayed arrival of sound. Meals are taken in the machine. Spare gloves dry beside warm cabinets. Moths gather against the windows at night, and martins sometimes try to nest under the eaves while the housing is still in operation. To those inside, the dragline is not immense. It is a set of stairs, rooms, smells, vibrations, and familiar faults. Its great cycle is experienced as a sequence of small decisions.

No one trusts the basin merely because they know it. Experience produces caution rather than confidence.

The oldest workers remember structures now underwater. They refer to submerged roads by their former destinations and to flooded workshops as if they might reopen. “Behind the stores” may mean a place two meters beneath a green pool. Younger workers learn this drowned geography because pipes and cables still follow it. The vanished layout remains active below the water, directing currents, carrying fish, releasing pockets of gas, and occasionally collapsing.

People do not build homes on the floor. They make temporary interiors. A switching hut acquires a kettle, a rack of damp coats, a calendar, three mismatched chairs. A pump house gains a shelf of bird nests removed from unsafe machinery. Someone keeps a jar containing unusual mineral crusts. Someone else feeds a fox despite the notices. Names are written inside electrical cabinets where weather cannot erase them.

Then the water rises, the access road fails, or a slope classification changes, and the interior is stripped out. The building remains, suddenly available to bats, dogs, swallows, and damp.

There are unofficial inhabitants too. Scrap thieves descend at night when mist hides them from the rim, though the most valuable metal is often the most dangerous: live cable mistaken for dead, pipe under pressure, unsupported steel above softened ground. Fishers come by old tracks with short rods and plastic buckets. They favor ponds whose fish no one has tested. Teenagers from the villages enter on hot evenings to swim from concrete platforms, daring one another toward the blacker water. The basin tolerates such trespass until, abruptly, it does not.

Most lives there depend on reading thresholds: the difference between wet and saturated, idle and disconnected, shallow and merely opaque, stable and not yet moving.

Even plants live by thresholds. Rushes hold where silt has accumulated to the depth of a hand. Willows root in cracks that remain moist but not submerged. Their seeds arrive everywhere, silvering the margins for a few days, yet only a handful survive. Those that do become small centers of habitation. Aphids gather on them. Caterpillars feed on the leaves. Spiders bridge their branches. Birds use them as lookout posts. Foxes sleep beneath them. The tree’s roots bind a little soil, the soil catches more silt, and for a time a bank begins to form around the tree.

Then the bank slides, carrying the willow upright into deeper water. It may live another season with drowned roots and leaves turned toward the new angle of light.

What inhabits the basin most completely is not any single species but a manner of living: provisional, alert, opportunistic, attached to structures that may fail. Nests are dug into walls still eroding. Dens are made in conduits that may flood. Eggs are laid in water that may drain. Machines are tended on terraces known to be moving. Every creature uses the unfinished work of another. Birds turn excavations into cliffs. Fish turn drainage systems into rivers. Foxes turn conveyors into ridgelines. Bacteria turn industrial seepage into colored marsh. Workers rely on frogs, smells, echoes, stains, and the behavior of birds alongside their instruments.

Nothing waits for the basin to become stable.

Life has entered it at once, before the slopes are settled, before the streams have learned their courses, before the machinery has stopped. It does not heal the excavation or disguise it. It occupies the rawness directly. It fits itself into bolts, bubbles, cracks, warm mud, warning lights, and moments between the movements of water.

From the rim, the inhabitants disappear into scale. A heron becomes a pale stroke. A worker becomes a fleck of color. Whole colonies of martins resolve into nothing but a darkened patch on clay. Yet below, each narrow refuge is contested and crowded. The basin seems empty only because its lives are distributed vertically—above the water, beneath it, inside the walls, under the roads, within the machines.

And where there appears to be nothing at all—only a wet shelf newly exposed by pumping—tracks begin before the mud has finished shining.
