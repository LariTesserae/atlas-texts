Nothing lives there in the usual posture of life.

There are no bodies crossing from shelter to water, no small hungers working at the edges of larger ones. Nothing hunts, startles, mates, nests, or sleeps. The city has never learned the quick grammar of animals: pursuit and escape, call and answer, wound and healing. If inhabitants are sought in doorways or behind the blind mineral windows, the search will produce only chambers—wet, warm, and often larger than the structures containing them.

The smallest living things are the black-green mats in the cracks. They seem simple until rain touches them. Then they contract, not randomly but in sequence, each portion tightening after the next as though a message were passing through the tissue. Their glossy knots resist the hottest drops. When the paving dries, they loosen into delicate radial films, pressing themselves against the stone as if reading what the water has left behind.

They feed on residues too slight to see: iron washed from walls, salts carried through impossible channels, pale mineral light. They grow with extraordinary slowness. A mat may take fifty years to cross a threshold. Yet it sometimes appears on both sides of a sealed wall at once, the two patches sharing the same pattern of folds. Whether they penetrate the stone, inhabit its water, or are one organism interrupted by architecture is unclear.

At the marsh edge stand the translucent reeds. They are less like plants than instruments placed upright in the mud. Their stems are hollow and divided by fine internal chambers. Condensation enters at the tips and descends without dripping. In the roots it becomes warm, faintly metallic fluid, which later rises again under darkness.

Each reed bends alone. The gesture is slow and deliberate enough to seem like indication, though what is indicated may lie many districts away or beneath the basin. During the deep resonance, all of them incline inward. Afterward they resume their separate attentions. One points toward a drowned street. Another toward an empty reach of salt. A third may lean toward a reflection in which no reed is visible.

They do not reproduce by seed. New patches appear where old canals have changed direction, particularly at places where water has crossed itself. A single transparent tube first pushes from the mud. Decades later there may be seven. No patch has ever been found with more than thirteen stems.

The pale columns in the courtyards are more difficult to classify. Their outer skins are laminated mineral, cool at the base and almost feverish near the crown. Inside, dark water pulses upward through branching vessels. The pulse is far slower than a heartbeat: one ascent may occupy an entire night. When it reaches the top, the courtyard walls become damp. Seams open in nearby stone. Water travels sideways beneath arches and into neighboring buildings. Only then does the dark fluid withdraw.

They grow by making architecture. Thin deposits gather around their bodies, first as collars and ridges, then as screens, partitions, vaults, and blind walls. Given enough time, a column encloses its courtyard completely. Once enclosed, it may divide. New pale supports appear at measured intervals, bearing a ceiling that did not exist before. What resembles a deserted hall may therefore be a mature colony. Its pillars are not holding the building up; they have secreted it around themselves.

Some colonies have the dimensions of neighborhoods.

Their separate trunks share pressure through the stone. If one is cut—or if erosion opens it, which amounts to the same thing—every column in the district stills. The canals stop. Condensation hangs motionless beneath the ledges. Then the exposed trunk closes itself with a translucent crust, and the others resume their circulation one by one. No scar remains except a new doorway somewhere nearby.

It is tempting to call these growths the inhabitants and the city their habitat. That distinction does not survive close attention.

The black mats appear wherever the paving is about to fold. The reeds indicate routes before those routes shorten. The courtyard columns thicken walls in districts where material has vanished elsewhere. Each may be an organ of one dispersed life, separated not by anatomy but by distance. Yet distance is the least trustworthy thing in the basin. Roots hundreds of paces apart may touch in a space beneath the streets that is only a hand’s breadth wide.

The water, too, behaves less like a medium than a resident.

It enters rooms without crossing their thresholds. It preserves remembered facades after stone has worn them smooth. It carries dust from streets that exist only in reflection. When confined, it does not always seek release; sometimes it enlarges the enclosure. It can wait for years in a wall, then emerge from the underside of an arch and travel upward into cloud.

This water is not composed into a single body. A puddle may hold a different depth from the rain feeding it. Two ends of the same canal may belong to different currents. The black central pool is warmer than all other water and appears to receive every drainage system, though none visibly empties there. Perhaps the basin contains many waters, each with its own memory and direction. Perhaps they exchange those memories when the low resonance passes through them.

If so, their thoughts are shapes.

A stair remembered by water becomes traversable, whether or not stone still supports it. A courtyard repeatedly reflected may acquire entrances. An avenue kept in rain’s memory lengthens beyond its district. What water forgets does not necessarily vanish, but loses attachment to neighboring things. This may be why isolated towers appear across the flats, and why certain rooms can be entered from buildings miles apart. They have not moved. The connective tissue of location has ceased to hold them.

The city itself may be the oldest inhabitant, though “city” may only be the form its life takes when observed from within.

It has no central brain. The depression at its heart is more like a deep organ of orientation, a place where levels, reflections, and pressures are compared. The submerged inverted city may be its memory—or its growing surface, turned inward because there is nowhere else to expand. The horizontal bands of pallor passing through the lower windows resemble impulses moving along buried nerves. When one reaches the pool’s unseen depth, a wall elsewhere in the basin may soften. A canal reverses. A service passage opens onto the marsh. The city has made a small decision.

Its metabolism is erosion. It dissolves itself in one quarter and deposits itself in another. Its nourishment is difference: wet and dry, warm and less warm, pressure above and pressure below, a gradient between one impossible depth and the next. Its circulation is performed by rain. Its growth is not an increase in matter but a rearrangement of relation.

This life is extraordinarily slow except when unobserved. A person might watch a facade for years and see only water draw deeper grooves in it. Turn away during a warm shower, and the facade may gain another chamber. The city does not seem hostile to witnesses. It simply cannot include sustained observation in the same way that it includes weather. Attention fixes distances. The living basin prefers them fluid.

There may also be inhabitants that never enter the upper city at all.

They are suggested by narrow disturbances beneath flooded streets: not movement through water, but temporary changes in where the water believes its surface to be. Sometimes the facades visible far below the central pool darken in sequence, as though something immense were passing between their windows and an unseen light. No outline appears. There is only occlusion, traveling downward.

These presences may be creatures of depth rather than flesh. They inhabit pressure differences, moving wherever one level bears upon another. The low resonance could be their passage. The darkening of doorways could be their shadow, though it falls from within the stone. If they possess bodies, those bodies are distributed among basements, wells, drowned staircases, and the underside of the rain.

Then there are the absences.

The city maintains them too carefully for them to be accidental. Plazas leave room for gatherings that do not occur. Doorways open at heights and widths unsuited to any visible organism. Windows face walls at distances from which nothing could see. In some halls, dry spaces persist in the shape of upright figures while warm water fills everything around them. The shapes vary: slender in one district, broad and many-lobed in another, sometimes extending through floors and ceilings. Water refuses to occupy them. Mineral films grow around their edges.

They may be molds left by vanished residents. They may be residents whose substance is exclusion. Perhaps what lives in those rooms is precisely the volume the city cannot fill.

Such beings would leave no tracks because a track is an occupation of ground. They would cast no reflection because reflections are already one of their habitats. Their movements might be registered only as omissions: a cascade falling silent, a route losing one of its turns, a chamber becoming smaller while retaining all its corners.

The personless street seen in the pools may belong to them. Its upward rain, pale plants, and low arch recur too consistently to be mere error. Each appearance brings reddish dust into the upper drainage channels. Something passes between that reflected district and this one, even if nothing can be seen making the crossing. On certain nights, one of the three pale plants is absent. On later nights it returns, and a new column is found in an enclosed courtyard above.

So the inhabitants cannot be counted.

There are the crack-mats, if each patch is separate. There are reeds, if a reed is an individual rather than a sensory filament. There are mineral columns, buildings grown by them, and buildings growing into them. There are autonomous waters and remembered streets. There are pressure-moving presences under the basin. There are preserved absences shaped for lives that may occur elsewhere.

Or there is only one inhabitant.

It lies across the entire shallow basin, without skin and without a stable boundary. The marsh reeds are its distant sense organs. The black-green mats are its slow receptors. The pale courtyard trunks are its glands and bones. Streets are channels temporarily hardened around its circulation. Reflections are not images but deeper layers of its body. The inverted city is not beneath it; it is the portion folded away from the sky.

What appears empty is simply a life whose organs resemble places.

When the rain stops at the hottest point of night, all its separated processes briefly align. The mats loosen. The reeds turn inward. Dark fluid pauses at the crowns of the pale columns. Canals become still without ceasing to flow. Rooms hold their surplus corners. The lower city’s pallor goes out window by window.

Then the basin takes depth into itself.

It is not a breath of air, and not a sound made for hearing. It is the inhabitant gathering its scattered distances close—marsh to plaza, roof to buried stair, puddle to starless courtyard—until, for one slow moment, every part of the city is adjacent to every other.

Then it releases them, and the streets are far apart again.
