What lives there is, at first, almost indistinguishable from wetness.

A clear membrane tightens beneath a stone lip. A filament retracts into clay. What looks like a bead of condensation detaches and walks away on six hair-fine legs. The inhabitants possess little color or silhouette of their own. They borrow the black of the mud, the milkiness of vapor, the brief blue of mineral light. Most are small enough to be thrown by a droplet.

The midges are the first multitude. They hang head-down beneath overhangs in translucent ranks, each with a dark knot of organs and a pair of wings folded like flaws in glass. They do not flee after a vent opens; they flee before it. A pressure change too slight to wrinkle the pool passes through their hooked feet, and the whole colony lifts at once. For a moment they turn in a tight, pale column. Then the fissure strikes open and the column collapses into shelter elsewhere.

They live by forecasting catastrophe fractions of a second in advance.

The beetles are less delicate. Their backs are black, highly polished, and divided by fine grooves that scatter condensation. They skate the pool’s surface until the tremor of a rising bubble reaches them. Then they lock their legs flat and become little armored rafts. Some are pulled into the mineral throats. Those that return may emerge hours later from a different opening, stained orange or violet, carrying films of buried bacteria on their shells. Others do not return at all.

Among the fresh plates crawl the many-legged grazers. They are nearly white except for rust-colored mouthparts. Their bodies are low and ribbed, built to let a pressure wave pass over them without peeling them from the rock. Each has a crown of soft sensory vanes along its rear segments. The vanes fold before a discharge and open afterward, testing the water for dissolved metals.

They feed quickly. A new mineral surface may remain edible for only minutes before it hardens, poisons itself, or is buried beneath another layer. The grazers arrive while the stone is still being made. Their jaws leave parallel scratches in films no thicker than breath. When the vents begin to clack, they wedge themselves into cracks and pull their many legs close, becoming pale corrugations in the rock.

The frogs appear solitary, but they are not lonely.

Each occupies a separate seep, usually no larger than its own body. Their backs are charcoal and rough as cooling slag; their bellies show impossible colors—sulfur yellow, furnace orange, bacterial blue. They hold themselves high on their toes to keep their skin from prolonged contact with the mineral water. Their calls are single blunt notes delivered into the pauses between underground blows.

No frog answers immediately. The response may come three or four pulses later from across the pool. In this way a conversation can last all night without ever becoming a chorus. Each animal inserts its voice into the machinery as if the basin itself were speaking unevenly through them.

Their eggs are laid in separate droplets beneath moss blades. A whole clutch may fit inside one trembling globe. The tadpoles hatch before the droplet falls, and when it finally joins the pool they enter it already able to swim. They graze the blue conduits beneath the mud and vanish into holes too narrow for the adult frogs to follow.

The condenser plants seem more substantial than the animals, though they too are mostly process. Aboveground, each is only a handful of dark leaves. Below, its swollen root is chambered and muscular, lined with valves of living tissue. It drinks when the pressure falls and braces itself when pressure rises. Around the root live blue bacteria that feed on iron, sulfur, and the meeting of waters that have never touched before.

The plant gives them sugars when dim light permits. In darkness it gives them access instead: one stream rich in buried salts, another carrying rain and air. The bacteria bloom where the two mix. Their chemical work warms the root by a degree or less, and that slight warmth keeps the plant alive.

The bare circle around each plant is not merely poisoned ground. It is territory maintained by leakage. Salts, acids, and bitter compounds seep outward from the root, preventing moss from sealing the nearby vents. Thus every plant preserves a portion of the plumbing it depends upon. During a strong pulse its leaves shiver, its chambers contract, and a dark thread of fluid is driven deeper into the earth.

The moss survives by accepting destruction. It grows in copper-green pads along stable edges but sends temporary strands across the dangerous stone. These strands trap spores, carcasses, and mineral dust. They may flourish for a day. Then a vent tears them away. Only the recessed portions remain, thick with stored fragments. A moss pad is therefore older than it looks: its exposed surface is young, but its hidden core contains generations of ruined growth packed into a wet felt.

Deeper in the pool, the needle-fish hold themselves against currents no eye can see. They are colorless except for a silver line along the spine. Their fins have become narrow sensory fans. Rather than watch for prey, they feel the minute distortions made by bodies in flowing water. A valve’s opening rearranges their world completely. They wheel around it in bright, coordinated arcs, catching stunned midges and uprooted larvae.

They have no fixed nest. When the channels change, they change with them. Eggs are released into descending flows and drawn beneath the clay, where they develop in cool brine pockets. Young fish sometimes emerge from vents far beyond the basin. Most are eyeless. A few retain pinprick eyes that react only to the blue flashes running under the mud.

The flat worms belong almost wholly to the ground. When feeding, they spread from cracks in soft, petal-shaped bodies, red or gray or translucent. Each “flower” may be only the exposed portion of something branching through several handfuls of clay. They absorb bacterial films and fallen animals, then withdraw before sediment seals them in. Severed pieces retreat independently. Whether these pieces remain one creature or become several is difficult to say. At times an entire section of mud seems to contract with a common decision.

And beneath them moves the silver hunter.

It is long enough to cross the pool without fully emerging. Its skin is unpigmented, marked by pressure scars and rings of mineral deposit. Small external gills lie folded along its neck like wet feathers. Its forelimbs are narrow but strong, each hand ending in hooked fingers that grip the interiors of flooded tubes.

It has little use for sight. Along its jaw are rows of pits sensitive to vibration, and before surfacing it rests with its chin against stone, listening through the basin’s skeleton. It knows which frog has shifted, which beetle has struck the water, which pressure chamber is near discharge. Often the first sign of it is not movement but absence: calls stop, grazers flatten, fish scatter, and the open pool becomes blank.

Then a pale head rises where there was no visible opening.

It feeds only once or twice before withdrawing. The machinery conceals its passage. A thud, a cloud of iron sediment, and it is gone into the lower fissures. No one knows how large it becomes in the chambers below. Sometimes two sets of ripples cross beneath the pool. Sometimes, after rain, a small silver juvenile is found curled inside a condenser root, breathing water through bright red gills.

Yet the largest inhabitant may not be the hunter.

The mineral tubes are lined with membranes. At first these seem to be deposits: translucent skins laid down where brine and groundwater meet. But the skins heal after ruptures. They thicken around useful channels and decay in blocked ones. They contain bacterial colonies, fungal threads, root hairs, worm branches, and the cast-off cells of animals drawn through the vents. No single component controls them. Together they alter the timing of valves and the direction of flow.

A tube heavily grazed by larvae closes sooner. A root chamber under strain diverts water to a neighboring fissure. Bacterial films precipitate new baffles exactly where turbulence brings them food. The geology shapes the organisms, and the organisms, by increments too small to witness, teach the geology new habits.

This distributed thing has no central body. It has pulses instead of a heartbeat, vents instead of a mouth, and the entire basin as a damp, replaceable skin. It does not maintain peace. It maintains interruption. Anything spreading too evenly is broken apart. Anything blocking the exchanges is poisoned, drowned, or blasted clean. What survives does so in intervals: between stones, between floods, between one blow and the next.

Perhaps it is not an organism. Perhaps it is only many lives caught in the same mechanism.

But after heavy rain, when the chambers labor rapidly and blue light travels in branching sequences beneath the mud, the inhabitants all move with such coordination that the distinction becomes difficult to preserve. Midges rise. Beetles brace. Frogs call into the pauses. Roots close. Worms withdraw. The silver hunter turns below. Mineral valves strike in order, and the whole small hollow draws water downward and releases vapor upward like a creature breathing through stone.

Then the pressure passes.

Everything separates again into tuft, shell, leaf, egg, and hidden limb. The pool lies shallow and dull. Nothing occupies its center. To anyone looking in from the seam above, the place appears almost empty.

That appearance is one of the ways it lives.
