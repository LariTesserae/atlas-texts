At first they are difficult to separate from the town.

A figure rests against the veined wall of a house, and seems only another dark root tied there. Someone crosses an avenue below, body drawn long and narrow in the green water, and for a moment might be a needlefish passing through shadow. Hands emerge first from doorways. Faces appear behind translucent membranes. Then a hatch opens and three people spill out in a scatter of bubbles, barefoot and close-cropped, each turning instinctively toward the nearest pocket of air.

They are human, or near enough that no visitor would doubt their kinship. The lake has not given them gills, webbing, or any single marvelous alteration. It has changed them by accumulation instead: stronger lungs, clear sinuses, heavy shoulders, flexible ribs, eyes accustomed to the sting of cold water and the green uncertainty of distance. Their toes are long and dexterous. Their fingers are blunt-nailed and crosshatched with old cuts from root fiber and stone glass. Most have pale seam-scars around wrists, ankles, and waists where working cords have rubbed for years.

They tend toward compact bodies, lean without being slight. Fat is valued rather than despised, especially in children and deep workers, because warmth is life. A prosperous adult has a soft layer beneath the skin and eats enough oil-rich fish to keep it. The very thin are watched anxiously. Elders, whose joints no longer forgive the cold, wrap themselves in quilted barkcloth inside the air rooms and descend only during the warmest hours.

Skin color varies as it might anywhere, though life underwater alters how it is seen. In the lake’s light, everyone takes on some shade of jade, pearl, bronze, or blue. Those who spend long hours near the surface are darkened across the crown of the head, shoulders, and upper back, while their undersides remain comparatively pale. Gardeners are often stained with resin: amber fingers, green-black palms, brown streaks along the forearms. People who work in silver moss carry its shining dust in their hair.

Hair is worn short, shaved close, or gathered into cords tight enough not to obscure the eyes. Loose hair is considered beautiful but dangerous. It belongs to dry chambers, courtship, mourning, and certain ceremonies. A person who ordinarily seems severe and smooth-headed may arrive at a feast with a hidden braid unbound to the waist, revealing years of growth kept coiled beneath a work cap. Children’s hair is cropped by stages as they learn the town: first behind the ears, then at the neck, and finally at the forehead when they are trusted to travel alone between breaths.

Clothing is spare in open water. The usual garment is a close belt or short wrap of water-flax, with loops for tools and a weighted cord that prevents the cloth from drifting. Divers wear soft caps over the ears and resin-slicked bands around the chest to reduce heat loss. Rootworkers add guards at the shins and forearms. Anything loose is treated as a hazard. Ornament is fastened tightly: snail-shell disks stitched flat to a collar, beads buried in braids, black-glass studs, copper wire pressed close around the upper arm.

Their most intimate possessions are breathing cords.

A breathing cord is thin enough to coil at the waist but stiff enough not to collapse when drawn through a narrow passage. Some are hollow reed. Others are braided around tiny linked vertebrae or formed from the veins of a particular bladder-vine. A swimmer can push one end into a roof-pocket and take air from below without surfacing fully. Children use short ones. Gardeners and tunnel-clearers carry several lengths with joining collars. The finest cords belong to the throat-watchers, who work near the cold descent and may need to breathe while keeping both hands fixed to a guide-rope.

No cord is casually shared. It carries the warmth of the mouth, the shape of the teeth, and the smell of its owner’s breath. To give one away can mean trust, adoption, apology, or love.

They speak in two languages.

In air they use voices lightly. The chambers magnify speech, and loud conversation disturbs the frogs nesting in ventilation tubes. Their spoken language is full of terms for growth, pressure, buoyancy, degrees of decay, and kinds of silence. It distinguishes air that is fresh from air that has been breathed, plant-made air from trapped surface air, safe bubbles from foul ones, and the cold clear water that rises from the throat from the softer water descending at the rim.

Underwater, words become movement and percussion. A flat hand turned outward means stop; two fingers drawn down the breastbone mean low breath; a closed fist against the cheek means cold confusion, the dangerous muddling that precedes loss of consciousness. Direction is indicated not by pointing but by laying the whole hand along the intended current. Names can be tapped on rootwood. Families have short identifying rhythms, while occupations have longer ones. A gardener announces herself with knuckles. A fisher uses a shell clapper. Children slap messages against their own chests and often lie about who sent them.

Facial expression remains important, but the lake exaggerates and obscures it. Hair does not frame the face. Tears vanish at once. A smile releases bubbles from the nose. For this reason they learn to read shoulders, hands, and the set of the feet. Grief is seen in a swimmer who no longer streamlines the body. Anger appears as unnecessary resistance against the water. Affection is a palm placed over another person’s exhaled bubbles, holding them briefly before letting them rise.

Their children learn water before they learn distance. Infants are born in the larger dry bladders and kept warm there for their first months, wrapped in moss fleece and fishskin. Yet they are washed daily in the lake, first a hand, then a foot, then the whole body held against a parent’s chest. They learn to close the throat almost as early as they learn to sit. By the time they can walk along an air-room floor, they can also kick from one adult’s hands to another’s.

A child is not considered a swimmer merely because they can cross open water. They must know where to breathe without looking. Their first education is a map of invisible refuges: the dependable bubble under the west market leaf; the narrow pocket beside the old resin store; the seasonal breath caught in the silver moss; the roof hollow that fish sometimes foul. Lessons are recited as routes.

Later come the laws of light and flow. Children release threads of dye and watch them twist through the streets. They count drumfish notes. They are taught never to seize a startled companion from the front, never to exhale completely in a deep ascent, and never to trust a beautiful clear chamber until it has been smelled by a frog. The black frogs are tolerated partly because they are excellent judges of air. When frogs abandon a room, people follow.

Play imitates danger. Children race through root hoops, steal breaths from beneath one another’s leaves, and dare each other to touch the guide-rope above the throat. They build miniature bladder houses and argue over whose design draws the strongest current. They train snails to follow trails of mashed algae. Root otters steal their toys, which is considered both a nuisance and a form of instruction.

Adulthood is not marked by age but by usefulness without supervision. One person becomes adult when trusted to prune a stem. Another when able to bring an exhausted swimmer home. Another when they can listen to bell-roots through a full night and distinguish the accidental strike of a crab from the first shifting of the rim.

The most honored people are the gardeners, though “gardener” is too small a word for what they are. They are architects, physicians, hydrologists, historians, and negotiators with slow life. They know which wounds cause a vine to branch and which make it sicken; where to bind a young root so that in fifteen years it will pull against a weakening wall; how much light a leaf may take before the moss below begins to fail. They carry black-glass knives in padded sheaths and cut less often than apprentices expect.

A master gardener may spend most of a lifetime completing only a few gestures. She bends a stem as a young woman. She opens it when her children are grown. Her grandchildren move into the chamber it becomes. Reputation therefore rests not on visible abundance but on the accuracy of old intentions. The greatest praise is: *It grew as they believed.*

There are also airkeepers, who inspect the larger bladders, cultivate breathing moss, and decide when a room has become unsafe. They are known by small caged frogs worn at the shoulder. Fish-herders guide grazing schools along walls in need of cleaning. Moss-combers harvest fibers without stripping the rhizome mat. Resin workers enter the dry chambers smelling sharp and medicinal, their hands wrapped against caustic sap. Bell-listeners sleep in shifts with cheek or breastbone pressed to resonant roots.

The throat-watchers are the strangest among them.

They spend long periods in the descending blue, below the comfortable town and sometimes below the lowest rooted walls. They are weighted with smooth stones and tethered in pairs. Their work is to keep the central rise unobstructed, remove drifting mats, measure cold flow, and observe whatever ascends from the vent. They return slow-moving and quiet, with bloodshot eyes and numb mouths. Some say the deep drumfish speak in patterns there. Some say warm water occasionally touches the soles of their feet. Throat-watchers rarely repeat such things in air, where claims become too definite.

They do not worship the throat exactly. Nor do they consider it merely a geological feature. It is the place through which the lake inhales. It is forbidden because it is necessary, and sacred because no one knows how far necessity descends.

Their society is organized less by households than by breath circles. A breath circle consists of those expected to keep air for one another: kin, companions, apprentices, former spouses, rescued strangers, and sometimes an entire work crew. A person may sleep in one bladder, eat in another, raise children in a third, and still belong most deeply to the six or seven people whose routes they know by heart.

To ask who someone’s people are, one asks, “Who waits when you dive?”

Authority follows the same principle. Decisions are made by those who will still be present when their consequences mature. Young adults speak on urgent matters—storms, hunts, broken channels. Gardeners speak on changes measured in decades. Elders speak for structures begun by the dead. Children are consulted whenever a plan concerns a room they may one day inhabit. This does not make the town gentle or free from rivalry. It only makes argument slow. Feuds can last across generations because a disputed branch may take twenty years to prove who was wrong.

Punishment seldom involves confinement; every house is already a sealed chamber, and denying water would be grotesque. Instead, serious offenders lose the right to carry a blade, to enter certain channels, or to draw first breath from a public pocket. The worst sentence is exclusion from one’s breath circle. Few survive the shame of it unchanged.

Death returns a person to the exchange that sustained them, but not immediately. The dead are washed, warmed, and kept for one night in air. Their hair, if long enough, is unbound. Friends recount every breath the dead gave them: rescues, shared pockets, warnings, rooms yielded to children, air surrendered after difficult work. At dawn the body is wrapped in weighted flax and lowered to a secluded decomposition garden well inside the town, never near the outer wall. Bone is recovered later. Some becomes needles, clasps, mouthpieces, or tools; some is placed in the rhizome mat beneath a dwelling begun by the dead.

They do not say that ancestors live in the houses. They say houses remember pressure.

This is not entirely metaphor. A chamber trained by a cautious person curves differently from one trained by an ambitious one. A doorway reveals the height of the hands that repeatedly shaped it. A stair remembers an old injury because its lower knots are close together. The character of the dead persists in practical inconveniences: a shelf too narrow, a passage beautifully placed, a ventilation tube that always sings during storms.

They eat fish, crab, frog eggs in season, snails, moss starch, tender root tips, and the pale hearts of carefully culled water plants. Food is often eaten raw, fermented, dried in warm air pockets, or softened with resin enzymes. Fire-cooked meals belong to rare gatherings. The lighting of a flame brings everyone close—not only for warmth or spectacle, but because flame reveals air as something consumable. They watch it diminish what they breathe.

Smoke is guided into a sacrificial bladder afterward, never released carelessly into the town. Children are told that fire is a hungry land animal that drowns easily but fouls the room where it dies.

They know the world below the mountain exists. Copper proves it; bird feathers prove it; now and then a climber reaches the summit rim, or an object dropped by travelers works its way into a spillway garden. A few lake people have descended the outer waterfalls with trading parties. Those who return speak of open air with discomfort. They describe it as depthless, dry, and impossible to hold. On land, there is nowhere overhead to take shelter, no visible measure of how much breath remains, and people fall with terrifying speed.

Visitors find the lake folk fearless underwater and uneasy in rain.

Rain is air invaded by water with no wall between them. It confuses every category.

Still, they are curious. They value surface timber, metal, salt, woven cloth, stories, and anything that can endure immersion. In exchange they offer medicines grown in the bladder walls, summit glass, fish oil, resin seals, and living cuttings that seldom prosper below the clouds. They do not trade maps of their breathing pockets.

Their festivals are acts of maintenance made beautiful. During the cutting of the upper leaves, whole families swim in procession carrying hooked glass knives. The severed leaves are guided into compost trenches while drumfish are coaxed into the resonant roots. At the yearly turning of cold water, lamps made from captive luminous shrimp are hung along the central avenue. The town appears doubled: stars above in the clear summit sky, small moving stars below among the roots.

Their music is felt as much as heard. Singers brace their feet against walls and send tones through the chamber membranes. Percussionists strike water-filled bulbs with padded palms. Skilled performers can make a house vibrate around its listeners without alarming the fish outside. Courtship songs are often played through neighboring walls. The intended person may feel the tune in their ribs before learning who sent it.

They are not serene merely because their work is slow. They are impatient, vain, lustful, comic, possessive, brave, and frequently foolish. They race otters. They steal each other’s tools. They quarrel about pruning. Adolescents pierce forbidden leaves to make private bubbles. Old gardeners conceal mistakes beneath new grafts and blame currents. Lovers choose isolated watch-bulbs and neglect their shifts. Children put snails in ventilation pipes to hear adults shout.

But panic is shameful—not because fear is weak, but because panic spends breath belonging to others. From infancy they are trained to become still when frightened. In crisis they draw close, touch wrists, count bubbles, and act in order. Their courage looks less like boldness than economy.

When a bell-root gives the true pattern of a breach, the whole town changes.

Fish are driven inward. Children and elders enter the secure bladders. Gardeners take cutting tools to the rim while resin workers prepare plugs and woven mats. Swimmers form chains through channels that are normally left empty. No one speaks more than necessary. Air is inventoried. Every person knows which wall to close, which rope to follow, which chamber to surrender if the pressure turns.

Afterward, if the seal holds, celebration waits. First they restore circulation. Then they count the living. Only then do they sound the lower bells.

Seen together in the market light, they do not resemble a hidden race or a people transformed into something other than human. They look like people who have made different parts of humanity visible. Dependence is visible. Breath is visible. Time is visible in the rooms around them. Every wall displays the labor of bodies long dead; every open channel shows an agreement not to fill it; every child crossing alone carries the remembered locations of a hundred small mercies.

They live inside a structure that is alive, and so they cannot pretend that shelter is passive.

They feed their walls. They prune their streets. They listen to their foundations hum. They inherit not buildings but intentions, not territory but circulation, not air itself but a place in the order of breathing.

And when they pause beneath a leaf, mouths lifted into a silver pocket, they take only what they need.
