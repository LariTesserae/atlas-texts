At first the inhabitants are difficult to distinguish from their work.

They wear layered coats of felted moss-fiber, bark cloth, and resin-stiffened canvas in the same gray, black-green, and tarnished silver as the streets. Their boots have broad, flexible soles so they do not bruise young road tissue. Their hats are low and close-fitting against the wind. Tools hang from them in wooden cases: pruning blades of glassy seed-shell, spools of grafting thread, stoppered gourds of antiseptic sap, tuning forks for testing walls, folded membranes for catching condensation. Seen crossing one of the enormous plazas, they resemble loose fragments of the city being blown from one district to another.

Only in the factory wards do they become vividly human. Heat opens their coats. Faces shine with oil and exertion. Workers shout, laugh, curse, and beat time against railings while the piston houses hammer around them. They are generally lean, though not delicate, with the deep chests of people born into thin air. Many have enlarged knuckles and heavy palms from a lifetime of gripping vibrating wood. Their hearing often deteriorates early, but this is not considered wholly disabling. A mature worker may understand the city better through the soles of the feet than through the ears.

Children are taught the ward rhythms before they are taught street names. A pumping district is three slow blows and a shudder. The loomworks chatter in descending sequences. The solvent refineries have a double beat followed by a long, uneasy pause. Lost children lie flat with one cheek to a road and wait until they recognize where they are.

The inhabitants speak softly outside the mills. This is partly because sound travels so far, partly because there is little animal noise to speak over, and partly because careless language can have structural consequences. Their everyday speech is precise, full of qualifications. They rarely say that a house wants, remembers, refuses, sulks, or welcomes, even when it plainly does those things. Metaphors are handled like volatile chemicals.

There are licensed places for figurative speech: tiled recital rooms lined with dead, kiln-hardened seed cases and isolated from the root mass by beds of dry ash. Inside them poets may call towers lonely and roads homesick without fear of municipal contamination. These chambers are inspected after every performance. If the outer walls develop listening buds, the building is closed and burned from within.

This has not made the people unimaginative. It has made them exacting. Their jokes turn on technical distinctions, on the difference between resemblance and identity, on the legal interval between being shaped like a mouth and constituting one. Engineers are famous for telling long, dry stories in which nothing is personified until the final sentence. Children, who delight in danger, whisper compliments to bridges and then flee before the handrails can tighten around them.

Their most important professions concern negotiation between categories.

Arboritects train towers, walls, reservoirs, and conduits. They do not regard themselves as gardeners. Gardeners encourage a plant to fulfill its nature; arboritects give it a civic nature to fulfill. They work from living diagrams: thin sheets of dormant tissue on which proposed structures are induced to grow in miniature. Their plans must account not only for load, wind, and water, but for temperament, inherited tropism, neighboring memories, and the likelihood that a public building will misunderstand its use.

Pruners carry more authority than police. They patrol in pairs, wearing pale sleeves so their hand signals can be seen across windy distances. Most pruning is modest: removal of an unauthorized bud, correction of a door beginning to resemble a throat, separation of two walls whose dreams have started to overlap. But an experienced pruner can condemn an entire district. A band of blue pigment painted around a trunk means that its upper stories will be cut away before dawn. Residents vacate without appeal. Appeals require repeated descriptions of the structure, and repetition can strengthen the very idea being contested.

Surveyors measure growth after rain. They are severe, stooped people with belts of numbered thorns and long sighting frames grown from perfectly straight stems. They track the migration of walls, the swelling of plazas, the appearance of unofficial rooms. Their maps are issued seasonally and are never called maps within reach of the ground. Officially, they are “temporary agreements concerning distance.”

Convergence clerks maintain records of impossible adjacency. Their ledgers are bound in resin and written in mineral inks that roots cannot digest. They document doors opening into prior decades, staircases descending into communal pressure, and rooms that have acquired functions from buildings across the city. Many wear several clocks, none of which show hours. One marks factory shifts, one indicates daylight, one measures the metabolic pace of the archive itself. If the clocks diverge too far, the clerk leaves by the youngest available exit.

The oldest among them are recognizable by their ribbons. After years of archival work or repeated passage through unstable quarters, a person may accumulate competing biographies. A woman may remember apprenticing under a master who has not yet been born. A man may carry grief for a spouse who is alive and has never met him. These memories are not dismissed as delusions, though neither are they accepted without documentation. Such people braid colored cords into their hair or sew them down the fronts of their coats: green for the publicly recognized life, white for a probable life, red for a life known to belong to another version of the district.

They are treated with courtesy and caution. They are never asked, “Do you remember me?” The approved question is, “From when do you know my face?”

The rooted structures are also inhabitants, though the census does not state this plainly.

Every building has a keeper. In a small dwelling this may simply be the family living inside it. In a factory, a whole maintenance household occupies rooms near the lower vascular junctions. Keepers monitor moisture, scrape fungus from warm cavities, redirect new growth, and learn the local gestures. One residential valve opens to three strokes of the thumb. Another prefers a forearm pressed flat against its grain. A neglected door becomes suspicious. A frequently slammed door becomes vindictive only if someone has called it so.

People form attachments to particular structures while insisting that attachment is irrelevant. A family may inhabit the same hedge-house for generations, grafting in sleeping chambers as children are born and sealing them when elders die. The wall gradually incorporates the shape of vanished furniture, old arguments, habitual routes from bed to stove. It will thicken around the shoulders of familiar residents during winter. It may refuse a new tenant for years.

Marriage often involves a period of architectural courtship. The couple sleeps alternately in each other’s dwellings while both houses are observed for adverse response. If walls begin producing partition buds, the match is discouraged. If doorways widen and roof leaves overlap in the direction of the other house, relatives celebrate but municipal officers intervene before the structures can root together. A successful household is one whose members love one another without allowing their homes to develop a theory about it.

Birth takes place in detached infirmary pods grown on short-lived rootstock. Hospitals are kept sparse, bright, and deliberately forgetful. Their walls are replaced in sections every few years so they cannot accumulate too coherent an idea of suffering. Midwives wear aprons waxed against pollen and carry heated blades for cutting both human and vegetal cords. Newborns are introduced to the city slowly. For the first weeks they sleep in baskets suspended from ceiling hooks, insulated from floor vibrations by coils of dry fiber.

Infants nevertheless respond to machinery. They settle during steady mill shifts and wake when engines stop unexpectedly. Many parents say the first rhythm a child recognizes is not the mother’s heart but the transfer house below the street.

Food is almost entirely vegetal, but not simple. Pale barrel nuts are sliced, steamed, pressed, fermented, and ground into dense cakes. Root starch becomes translucent noodles. Sap curds are cultured in warm wooden vats. Black clover is made into bitter paste, silver leaf-skin into mineral-rich broth, and pollen into yellow flour used for festival breads. Flavor comes from smoke, acids, aromatic resins, and controlled decay.

Eating fresh fruit is a private luxury. Fruit is water made visible, and therefore morally complicated. At public meals it is cut into exact portions. Children are scolded for biting directly into it, less from manners than from the obscenity of letting juice run down the hand.

There are no herders, hunters, or fishers. Many inhabitants have never touched fur, heard an insect swarm, or seen an animal larger than themselves except at a great distance in the sky. Their images of beasts come from old pressed diagrams and from analogy with machines. A bird is understood as a self-guiding leaf with bones. A horse, in surviving technical texts, is described as a mobile reciprocating engine fed on grass.

The rare long-winged birds are watched with a mixture of longing and alarm. Entire work crews sometimes stop to follow one black shape crossing the sky. No one calls out to it. A creature that can land anywhere but chooses not to has a status approaching prophecy.

Worms receive a more formal welcome. When one appears in a reservoir bed or newly opened trench, work ceases. A moisture tent is erected over it. A soil officer records its direction, color, and apparent health, and children are brought from nearby schools to observe. Worms are among the only beings capable of moving freely through the underlayer without being absorbed into its dreaming. Engineers study their tunnels for principles of insulation. Priests—though the city officially has no priests—touch two fingers to the road and thank the visitor for keeping one path separate from another.

Religion survives mostly in procedures.

The inhabitants do not worship the plateau as a god. To name it one would be dangerously reductive, and to address it would imply that it was listening as a single thing. Instead they practice rites of non-unification. At funerals, the dead are not buried. Burial risks giving the root network too complete a human pattern, and cremation is difficult, expensive, and feared near living structures. Bodies are dried in high wind chambers, reduced in fermentation retorts, and separated into water, salts, heat, and carbon.

Each fraction is sent to a different district. Water enters an industrial cistern. Minerals are scattered beyond the outer roads. Heat performs useful work. Carbon is sealed into lacquer or ink. Nothing of one person is permitted to return to the plateau in one place.

Names are divided for similar reasons. Everyone has a household name, a work name, a legal name, and a name used only beyond the city’s living surface. To speak all of them together is considered a way of inviting convergence. False names are not lies here. They are structural spacing.

The people themselves dream cautiously.

They sleep in hammocks or on raised bedsteads whose legs rest in cups of dry ceramic. Direct contact with floors is avoided during the deepest part of the night, when factory noise diminishes and the impressions moving through the roots become easier to distinguish. Even with insulation, sleepers report shared fragments: the taste of distant ironless minerals, pressure from rain that has not yet arrived, sunlight experienced as weight, the slow ache of a wind-bent tower three wards away.

Sometimes a whole neighborhood wakes with the same inexplicable intention. Residents may all begin moving their chairs eastward. They may become convinced that an unopened room exists beneath the square. They may crave a fruit not grown for centuries. Such events are reported to the Office of Aggregate Impulse, which checks pollen levels, root pressure, and proximity violations before permitting anyone to act.

This vigilance produces people who are disciplined but not cold. Their tenderness is practical. They oil one another’s cracked hands. They carry hot sap broth to night crews. They memorize the opening strokes of elderly neighbors’ doors. During drought, households give part of their water allotment to stairwells and nursery walls. During rain, when buildings swell and rooms shift, families stay awake together, singing songs made entirely of names and measurements—language believed too literal for the walls to misuse.

Festivals are held after lightning.

As the stored charge enters the deep bulbs and veins ignite across the plateau, people pour into the open spaces in insulated boots. Factories accelerate. Rail runners hum. Windows glow green-white from within. The inhabitants fasten strips of translucent leaf-skin to their sleeves so every movement leaves a brief phosphorescent trace.

There is dancing, but little music is added. The city provides enough: piston thunder, seed-hopper rattle, steam bursts, root-cables groaning belowground. Dancers organize themselves by ward rhythm. Groups meet in the great plazas, overlap for a few measures, then separate before their movements become too synchronized. Complete unison is considered beautiful and unsafe.

At the edge of the plateau live the withdrawn.

They occupy caretaker cottages among the empty towers, collapsed wind organs, and silent pumping stations. Some are retired engineers. Some are fugitives from conflicting histories. Some have become too sensitive to architectural dreams to tolerate the central districts. They dress in dry fabrics and walk on removable plank soles, minimizing contact with the ground.

These people learn the ruins individually. They know which tower still believes itself full of workers, which shadow repeats footsteps, which pumping station exhales once each winter though its membranes have been gone for a hundred years. They leave offerings of water but do not enter often. Their task is not restoration. It is to keep the abandoned structures from returning to urban time all at once.

A few caretakers gradually become difficult to separate from their stations. Their skin takes on a resinous smell. They stand for hours facing the sun with their hands slightly open. They answer questions only after a delay corresponding to the nearest factory beat. Whether this is illness, adaptation, or a form of civic appointment is disputed.

And beneath all these moving inhabitants are other lives: the translucent mites feeding on warmth in bearings; dormant seeds waiting centuries for an instruction; root tips passing chemical rumors across districts; pale underground leaves exposing only their lichenlike skins; reservoirs that tighten during footsteps; roads that know traffic as pressure and abandonment as permission to curl.

No single one of them contains the city.

The people maintain that distinction fiercely. The city is not an organism, they say, because an organism has boundaries. It is not a colony, because its parts do not agree on what they are. It is not a machine, because its errors can remember. It is not a mind, because minds—at least in principle—wake up separately.

Yet when the wind bends the towers and the engines begin drawing power from their sway, everyone adjusts without being told. Workers take their stations. Clerks steady the shelves. Children lift their feet from the roads to feel the first deep pull of the cables. Doors open along whole avenues. Conductive vines rise from distant crowns. The rooted and the rootless enter the same rhythm.

For a few moments the inhabitants appear not to live in the city at all.

They appear to be the brief, warm, walking thoughts by which the plateau notices what it has become.
