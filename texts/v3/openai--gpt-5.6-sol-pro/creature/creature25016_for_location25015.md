The first living thing one notices is usually not an animal, nor even a whole plant, but a change in darkness.

At the margin of a grove, where the bright crust gives way to black-violet growth, there are films thin enough to be mistaken for stains. They occupy the sheltered sides of crystals and the hairline cracks between mineral plates. In full light they lie flat and almost inert. Toward evening they lift thousands of microscopic vanes, exposing red undersurfaces to the cooling air. Charge leaks through them into the ground. By dawn they have crept a fraction farther across the stone.

These films are the country’s pioneers. A landed flake, dormant perhaps for centuries, opens into a mesh of conductive fibers. It does not seek moisture. It tests the rock electrically and chemically, dissolving a few grains at a time and rejecting most of what it finds. Around itself it builds a dark nodule containing salts in precise proportions. The nodule is root, stomach, memory, and refuge. If the exposed film is scoured away, the nodule waits and tries again.

Many never grow larger than a hand. The successful ones become provinces.

What looks from afar like a single crownplant is less an individual than a long negotiation among generations. Its central knot may be older than surrounding cliffs, but most of its fans are young. New branches arise where buried fibers encounter a useful seam. Old branches, exhausted or badly oriented, are abandoned and mineralize in place. They remain as ribs beneath the living canopy, so that a mature crown supports itself upon a history of its former shapes.

The fans are extraordinarily sensitive. Each consists of interlocking scales, dark on one face and reflective on the other, with narrow channels of ionic fluid between them. Minute changes in atmospheric voltage alter the spacing of the scales. One edge tightens, another loosens, and the fan turns. Because neighboring crowns share the same ground currents, their movements are coordinated across whole groves. The wave that passes over them at noon is not signaling in any deliberate sense. It is a common answer traveling through connected stone.

Yet there is variation within the movement. Some crowns turn early. Some resist until the gradient becomes strong. Some retain old orientations inherited from centuries of growth. A grove therefore possesses something resembling temperament. There are groves that lie close and guarded, their fans overlapping like scales. Others remain open, silver surfaces lifted high, extravagant with light. Along electrically unstable faults, crowns flick and readjust all day, producing a ceaseless dry murmur.

Beneath them lives most of the country’s abundance.

The understory is braided from low mats, beadlike colonies, and thickets of brittle threads. There are red combs that harvest the charge shed by larger fans, pale nodules that feed on tarnished tissue, and translucent sacs full of slowly circulating salts. Some organisms grow nowhere except on the dead ribs of ancient crowns. Others nest between active scales and are carried outward as a branch divides.

The closest equivalent to decay is performed by tiny mineral borers. They invade damaged tissue along microscopic fractures, separating useful carbon compounds from spent salts. Their bodies are chains of flat cells that can disconnect when disturbed and crawl independently by exchanging charge with the surface beneath them. A tarnished branch may host millions. Seen closely, its dull edge is busy with advancing and retreating lines.

The grove does not rot. It is taken apart, sorted, and rebuilt.

Among these growths move the ceramic grazers.

A grazer is low and broad, with six to twelve load-bearing limbs arranged beneath overlapping plates. The number changes with age. Young animals add limb pairs as they widen; very old ones may seal disused limbs inside the armor, where they remain as hollow mineral casts. Their plates are cream, gray, or faintly pink according to the grove in which they fed while growing. On the windward side they become polished. On the sheltered side they carry dark dust, spores, and colonies of hitchhiking films.

The head is scarcely distinct. A forward crescent of sensory plates rests close to the ground, reading voltage differences across the crust. Behind it is a rasp made not of teeth but of replaceable crystal blades. The animal presses these against fresh mat growth, vibrates them, and lifts away a layer only a few cells thick. It feeds without visibly diminishing the landscape.

Its pace is difficult to perceive. A feeding animal may hold one foot raised for an hour while currents in the ground resolve into a pattern it can trust. It may take a day to cross its own body length. When the atmospheric charge shifts abruptly, every joint locks. Dust gathers along its back. Chemical bloom may feather over its plates. A traveler could take it for an unusually symmetrical stone and return days later to find only a shallow trail of polished marks.

Grazers do not gather socially, but their paths converge. The youngest growth is found along particular electrical boundaries, and so several animals may advance upon it from different directions. They stop before touching. Their sensory fields overlap; each registers the other as an obstruction in the ground’s current. For days they may remain opposed, neither willing to cross the disturbance. Eventually one turns aside. Such meetings are among the fastest dramas of their lives.

They reproduce rarely. Two adults leave packets of patterned mineral-organic scales in the same sheltered fissure, sometimes years apart. If the patterns are compatible, the packets join and assemble a small, soft grazer within a ceramic shell. It emerges no larger than a coin and spends its first decades under the mats, feeding on fine threads. Most are consumed there.

The hunters that consume them seldom resemble pursuit predators from wetter worlds.

They are articulated strips, flat enough to enter cracks and long enough, when unfolded, to bridge several meters. Each joint is a dark conductive lozenge connected by pale flexible bands. At rest the animal lies among slabs with its sections angled to imitate natural fractures. Fine sensory whiskers penetrate the crust. Through them it feels the rhythmic current produced by a grazer’s moving ionic fluids.

A hunter may begin closing on prey while still hundreds of body lengths away. It advances only when wind, thermal cracking, or a shifting crown masks its electrical disturbance. Often the grazer detects it and locks in place. Then both wait.

The contest can last longer than a chemical bloom. The hunter’s internal fluids thicken. Its outer joints cool to match the stone. The grazer gradually spends the charge stored in its plates. If it moves too soon, the hunter folds forward in a rapid sequence, each segment gripping where the previous one lay. This is one of the few truly sudden motions on the plateau. Even then there is little struggle. The hunter lays itself across the grazer’s armor, finds the conductive seams, and sends a disruptive pulse through them. The grazer’s joints release. Feeding takes weeks.

Not all hunts end with motion. Sometimes both animals remain locked through a seasonal cooling. The hunter starves first and becomes a black line among the rocks. The grazer eventually wakes beneath its mineralizing body and continues onward, carrying fragments of the predator on its shell.

Above the groves drift the high gliders.

From the ground one first sees a flash without an apparent source, as if a piece of the sky had turned over. Then the glider becomes visible: a wide, tense membrane bowed along a narrow leading body, with trailing filaments that alter its charge and keep it balanced in the rising air. The largest span farther than a crownplant’s width, yet weigh less than a ground grazer.

Their wings are not beaten. Muscles would cost too much for too little purchase in the thin air. Instead, the membrane continuously changes stiffness. Sunlit sections expand; shaded sections contract. Electrical fringes along the trailing edge interact with airborne dust. By these minute adjustments the glider remains aloft, descending during the cold hours and regaining its height over warming flats.

It feeds by opening porous bands across its underside. Reproductive flakes, mineral aerosols, and microscopic drifting organisms strike the membrane and are drawn into branching channels. What is useful is absorbed. What is not is arranged into reflective scales, giving the underside its sudden mirror brilliance.

A glider’s body is almost all wing. Its narrow forward ridge contains sensory pits, dormant reproductive tissue, and a chain of dense nodules that serve as ballast. It has no face recognizable from below. Only when one clings under an overhang can its feeding pores be seen opening and closing in slow sequence.

Gliders are solitary because the updrafts that can support them are widely separated. Still, atmospheric circulation sometimes brings several together over a waking province. They spiral at different heights, each occupying a level determined by size and charge. They do not call. They are aware of one another through distortions in the electrical field, and their paths form a loose, revolving geometry. At dusk the geometry breaks apart. Each animal descends toward its own distant shelter.

Their young begin as airborne ribbons. Released from pores near the leading edge, these transparent strips may drift for years while feeding on dust. Most never develop further. A ribbon that passes repeatedly through rich reproductive clouds thickens, folds along a central line, and becomes capable of steering. Only then does it seek the rising air above a grove.

There are smaller fliers too, though they are better described as motes with habits. Some are branching cages of conductive fiber that inflate when charged. Some resemble six-pointed flakes and can reverse which face reflects light, climbing or descending according to temperature. They graze upon the reproductive dust of plants and are themselves filtered from the air by gliders. During atmospheric veils they may become so numerous that the pearl-white sky acquires a fine, restless grain.

The tower growths host a different community.

Their perforated vanes are inhabited by threadlike beings that never touch the ground. They stretch between openings, feeding on current as it passes through the tower. When violet streamers comb the summit, the threads brighten in sequence from base to tip. Excess charge causes them to divide. Their separated ends curl into rings and are carried away, sometimes lodging in another tower many kilometers distant.

Near the summit live small clasping animals armored only on the side facing the sky. They migrate around the flutes to remain in the weak electrical shadow of the vanes. If caught in a discharge, they stiffen and become part of the tower’s conductive surface. Some revive later. Others remain permanently incorporated, and new plant tissue grows around them. Ancient towers are crowded with such embedded bodies, layered through their walls like fossils in a living reef.

The open plateau is not truly lifeless either.

Inside the hollow rolling crystals dwell organisms finer than smoke. A crystal grain may contain an entire colony adhering to its inner surface. Static charge turns the grain; sunlight passes through it; the colony migrates around the interior to remain at a favorable angle. It never contacts the ground directly. When two grains approach closely enough for their fields to overlap, the colonies exchange packets through a brief arc across the gap.

Thus populations can spread across a basin without the grains ever touching.

After a chemical deposition, these hidden colonies escape through pores in the crystals and become the black rosettes of the temporary flats. They uncurl onto the fresh bloom, feed rapidly, and lay down concentric bands of discarded minerals. Different species leave different colors: rust-red, ash-blue, sulfurous yellow, dull metallic green. The rings that remain are records of appetite and succession. One colony removes a compound that poisons the next; another leaves behind exactly the crystal structure its successor requires.

The awakening may last only days, but for the small lives involved it is a complete age. They expand, compete, fuse, reproduce, and retreat. Some lineages experience the long dormant interval merely as a pause in metabolism. Others live and die entirely during the bloom, entrusting their descendants to nodules that will not open for another century.

There are parasites in the ancient central knots of crownplants—slow, elaborate things whose presence may not become apparent for millennia. They redirect a fraction of the host’s current into growths of their own, making chambers that resemble knots within the knot. There they maintain miniature gardens of microbial films. A heavily inhabited crown may orient a few degrees differently from its neighbors or release flakes in the wrong pressure cycle. Eventually one sector fails and whitens. The parasite survives inside the scar tissue and spreads when the crown grows around the wound.

There are also organisms that appear only during the flame-like oxidations.

They exist ordinarily as inert transparent prisms buried under groves. Heat and blue radiance wake them. They unfold into pale, many-branched forms and move through the collapsing vegetation just behind the oxidation front. They cannot endure unaltered living tissue and cannot feed on fully cooled remains. Their whole active world is the narrow region between transformation and exhaustion.

For months they follow that moving boundary. They absorb unstable compounds, mate by joining branch tips, and deposit new prisms in the enriched ground. When the last blue glow fades, the adults stop where they are. Their bodies harden into white lace indistinguishable from the burned grove.

The prisms wait for the next cold fire.

None of these beings is hurried by a heartbeat in the familiar sense. Their pulses are ionic, thermal, electrical. Some have cycles measured in seconds, but the bodies those cycles maintain may move only by millimeters in a year. Others act quickly during rare favorable conditions and spend nearly all existence locked into crystal stillness.

Nor are they silent in an absolute way. Crown fans click as their scales engage. Grazers produce a faint granular rasp. Hunters give off brief static snaps when they fold. Tower colonies tick upward during a discharge. Beneath these is a constant register too low and slow to be heard: current entering roots, migrating through buried seams, dividing among groves, returning to the atmosphere.

At night the living provinces contract into themselves. Silver surfaces turn downward. Red undersides disappear. Grazers settle onto the ground to conserve charge, while predators flatten and become colder than their prey. Gliders hang beneath ledges with their wings pleated into dense reflective bundles. Temporary organisms withdraw into cracks and seal themselves behind films of salt.

For a while the plateau seems to contain only stone.

But the stone is full of distinctions life can feel: one mineral against another, a charged face and an uncharged edge, a degree of warmth retained after sunset, a strain running through a crystal before it breaks. These are the equivalents of scent, water, shelter, and fertile soil. The inhabitants read them with extraordinary patience.

They do not live despite the barrenness.

The brightness, the dryness, the exposed charge, and the endless mineral differences are their abundance. The empty distances are not a failure of the ecosystem but part of its form, keeping each dark province distinct while wind and sky exchange their dormant fragments. Every grove is an island, every crack a possible beginning, every drifting flake a life traveling through years of blue air toward one exact place where the stone may answer it.
