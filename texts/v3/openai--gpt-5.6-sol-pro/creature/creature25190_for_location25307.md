At first, the inhabitants are easiest to mistake for motions of the mountain.

A figure crossing a high bridge becomes a brief interruption in the railings. Orange-clad workers moving along an exposed pipe resemble beads traveling a wire. Gardeners vanish under banana leaves; lift crews descend in cages with their tools hanging outside; children on distant stairs are only flashes of white cloth and polished buckets. Even the largest gatherings disperse into the architecture. There are too many levels for a crowd to remain a crowd for long.

Closer in, the people are everywhere.

They live in rooms fitted between trusses, in old foremen’s offices, in apartments built through the hollow shells of generators. Their homes are narrow where columns dictate narrowness and unexpectedly spacious where an obsolete machine has left a void. A bedroom may curve around a ventilation drum. A kitchen floor may be the plated top of a sealed gearbox, still faintly warm. Some households have windows onto gardens and open air; others look through interior galleries toward lamps and passing freight.

No two walls are quite parallel. No room is entirely still.

People grow accustomed to the vibration before they learn to walk. Infants sleep in cloth cradles hung from beams, swaying with the deep mechanical pulse. Cups have broad bases. Shelves possess lips. Doors are latched even in calm weather, because pressure changes in the ventilation system can fling them open. Families know which rattles are harmless, which mean rain, and which require someone to put down dinner and go looking for a wrench.

They tend to be sure-footed, though not fearless. Height is not treated as trivial. Children are taught early to test railings with the heel of the hand, to keep three points of contact on ladders, to tie loose clothing near fans, and never to step backward without looking. They learn the difference between a deck that merely rings and one whose note has changed. A child who cannot yet read may still know that red paint means heat, blue bands mean reclaimed water, striped plates conceal motion, and a line of white triangles marks a load path that must never be cut.

Their games reproduce the lessons. They build towers from scrap brackets and compete to see whose structure can hold the most washers. They race seed husks through drainage channels. They play a form of chase in which floors are safe, grilles are rivers, and the person designated as “rust” may only move along seams. On broad plazas they fly kites into the thermal currents, using reels geared down so the lines do not tear their hands.

Few inhabitants think of themselves as living *on* the mountains. They live at an elevation, along a water branch, inside the reach of a particular lift system. Asked where they come from, they name a terrace, an arch, a pump district, or the color and number of a ventilation throat.

“North side” and “south side” matter less than *sunward*, *shadowward*, *upflow*, and *downflow*. Distance is counted in transfers.

A place visible across a gorge may be half a day away.

This has shaped them into patient travelers. They carry folding stools, water flasks, fruit, repair tape, and something to read while lifts climb. At transfer platforms, strangers exchange news without ceremony. Which incline is stalled. Where rain has fallen. Whether the flowering trees on the Ninth Warm Deck have opened. Which freight line is accepting passengers during maintenance. People develop friendships with those who share their daily ascent, though they may know almost nothing about one another’s homes.

Clothing varies by level. In the bright upper gardens, people wear loose fabrics, broad hats, eye veils, and gloves against hot railings. In damp interior districts, garments are close-fitting and treated against mold. Maintenance workers favor layered belts and short coats with breakaway seams, so cloth caught by machinery tears free rather than pulling the wearer in. Nearly everyone has shoes with replaceable soles. The soles are chosen for tile, mesh, oily plate, mossed concrete, or ladder work, and the sound of them often reveals a person’s trade before their face is visible.

The most respected professions are not necessarily the grandest.

Pump tenders have authority because thirst moves upward only through machinery. Drain keepers are welcomed everywhere during the rains. Structural listeners walk prescribed routes with rods, hammers, acoustic sensors, and old notebooks full of comparisons. They tap columns and kneel to press their ears against deck plates. Some use instruments; some claim they can hear delamination through their teeth.

Root stewards mediate between trees and buildings. They know which species pry, which bind, which drink too much, and which can be persuaded to follow channels laid for them. They open planter boxes, trim roots away from membranes, and sometimes order the removal of beloved old trees. This makes them unpopular until the first heavy rain.

There are liftwrights, cable splicers, ceramic patchers, mold farmers, welders, bird wardens, cistern divers, heat accountants, bridge gardeners, insect breeders, and crews whose only work is keeping labels legible. In the lower districts, there are people who map vibration. In the upper districts, there are cloud watchers who predict where a shower will strike by observing swifts and the cooling of sunlit plate.

Many trades are hereditary only in the loose sense that knowledge follows access. A child of pump tenders grows up knowing locked galleries and the smell of overheated bearings. A liftwright’s child knows people across twenty elevations. But apprentices frequently choose otherwise, and households are proud of containing several kinds of competence. At meals, arguments about flow rates coexist with arguments about grafting citrus or tuning relay contacts.

Their society is held together less by ownership than by dependency.

A family may call a room theirs, but the column passing through it belongs to every level above. A garden belongs to those who cultivate it, except that its water is allocated by the district, its shade affects the workshop beneath, and its roots are subject to inspection. Even privacy has structural limits. Access panels cannot be blocked. Emergency ladders cannot be locked. A maintenance crew may legally cross a bedroom if that bedroom contains the only approach to a failing valve.

This produces courtesy, resentment, and endless negotiation.

Thresholds are marked carefully. Bells, curtains, floor paint, strings of shells, and changes in tile announce the passage from public route to household space, even when no door can be installed. To ignore such a marker is considered crude. To deny emergency access is considered monstrous.

Meals are assembled from many elevations. Fruit and herbs come from upper terraces; mushrooms, yeast, and pale vegetables from warm interior farms; fish from reservoir loops; eggs from aviaries and household coops. Grain is grown where broad decks can carry deep beds, though much of it arrives through freight corridors from distant basins. Kitchens favor steaming, pressure cooking, fermentation, and communal ovens that reuse industrial heat. Open flame is rare enough that candlelight feels ceremonial and dangerous.

Water is never served thoughtlessly. Guests are offered a cup, but not an overflowing one. Children are scolded for leaving taps running with a severity reserved elsewhere for lying. After a hard shower, however, the mood changes. People bring out tubs, wash walls, rinse blankets, scrub stairs, and stand laughing beneath authorized spillways. Rain days become half holiday, half labor mobilization.

Everyone watches the gutters.

Their festivals follow maintenance cycles as often as seasons. When a major reservoir reaches its upper mark, districts hang blue cloth from railings. After a bridge bearing is successfully replaced, the closed route reopens as a procession: first the inspectors, then the oldest residents, then children carrying potted plants, and only afterward freight. Decommissioned machines are sometimes given wakes. Their housings are washed, their service histories read aloud, and everyone who depended on them is invited to strike the casing once with a padded hammer.

The resulting sound is remembered.

Religion, where it exists, rarely concerns a creator standing outside the world. The idea of outside is difficult to make sacred. Instead there are devotions to continuance, load, flow, and intercession. Shrines accumulate at junctions: beneath columns, beside pumps, near the counterweights of reliable lifts. Offerings are practical or symbolic—oil in sealed vials, loops of clean wire, first fruit, stamped scraps bearing the number of a repaired home.

Some people pray upward, toward light and rain. Others pray downward, toward the innumerable supports carrying their weight. Most do both.

The old rule—nothing rests on the world; everything rests on something else—is interpreted in several ways. Engineers treat it as an admonition against simplified diagrams. Teachers use it to explain obligation. Priests use it to argue that no life is self-supported. Cynics point out that it also means every failure is inherited by someone below.

The dead cannot simply be buried. There is no earth deep enough to surrender them to.

Some bodies are reduced in high-temperature civic furnaces, their heat returned to steam lines and their mineral remains added to memorial ceramic. Tiles containing a trace of the dead are set into homes, bridges, or public stairs. Others are composted in sealed garden chambers and become orchard soil. Families may eat fruit from a tree rooted in their ancestors, though not everyone finds this comforting.

Those lost into inaccessible depths are mourned differently. A name is painted at the last verified level. Below it, a vertical line descends until the wall or beam ends.

The inhabitants are not all human, and the boundaries between domestic, working, and wild creatures are indistinct.

Monkeys live in loose nations organized around cable routes. They steal tools with bright handles and have learned to trade some of them back for fruit. Certain lift crews tolerate them because monkeys notice damaged cables and agitated machinery before people do. Others regard them as vandals with hands.

Parrots preserve sounds longer than meanings. In remote forested galleries, they repeat alarm tones from equipment removed centuries ago. Some imitate the cadence of public announcements, producing streams of nonsense so authoritative that travelers stop to listen. A few districts train them deliberately, teaching different flocks the calls for smoke, leaking water, or approaching storms.

Cats belong to transformer halls more than to households. Their ears are notched with district marks, and electricians leave food near warm cabinets. Rat-catching dogs work freight levels, broad-pawed and calm around noise. Small deer browse the planted decks; during drought they are guided inward to designated water courts by people carrying branches.

In the wet interior levels live pale frogs, nearly translucent geckos, silver insects that feed on insulation fungus, and blind fish in sumps never intended to contain life. Maintenance workers argue over which should be exterminated and which have become part of the system. Sometimes the system itself has adjusted around them. A filter schedule may exist because a particular midge hatches every sixth week. A ventilation grille may remain unrepaired because swifts have nested there for eighty years and now control insects across three housing decks.

The machinery, too, is often spoken of as though it lives.

Not sentimentally. No one imagines the pumps have human thoughts. But a machine has a history, a temperament, and a web of dependencies. It can be young despite being assembled from old parts, or ancient despite having every component replaced. It can become unreliable in wet weather, resent a cold start, settle after repair, or “look after” a district for generations.

The largest systems have names that outlast languages. Their operators inherit logbooks in many scripts, each generation adding notes in the margins. Some instructions are no longer understood but are still obeyed because abandoning them once caused a failure. A bell is rung before reversing a turbine whose original control room is now a school. Three valves are opened in a sequence no current diagram justifies. A bowl of water is set on a bearing housing—not as an offering, the technicians insist, but because the pattern of ripples reveals imbalance.

Far below the inhabited terraces are others who may or may not belong to the same society.

Lower-level crews are seldom seen in upper gardens. They arrive in sealed lifts wearing pale coveralls, breathing masks, and lamps fixed to their shoulders. Their skin sometimes bears the gray cast of people who live without direct sun. They speak familiar languages with unfamiliar technical words and carry tools made to fit bolts no longer manufactured above.

They come to exchange bearings, medicines, spores, records, and water-quality samples. They dislike questions about how far down they live.

According to them, the sealed levels are not empty. There are warm chambers where old lights still burn, algae farms around condensation trunks, and settlements built on maintenance platforms narrower than upper streets. There are people who navigate by pipe tone and air pressure, who have never seen rain fall directly from a cloud. There are also automatic carriers moving on schedules no one controls and doors that open only when distant machinery enters a particular phase.

Some upper residents believe these accounts. Others think the lower crews cultivate mystery to protect their access routes.

No one doubts, however, that someone lives below the last official map. Waste disappears from certain blocked ducts. Chalk marks change. Unregistered repairs appear on pipes descending through sealed floors. Once in a while, an old lift arrives empty from beneath its lowest listed stop. Its cage is warm. On the floor are wet footprints, red metal dust, or a bundle of unfamiliar herbs tied with copper wire.

Then the liftwrights record the event, inspect the brakes, and send the cage down again.

Life in the hollow mountains encourages neither conquest nor escape. There is always another ridge, but it is made of the same obligations. Expansion means not finding untouched land but reopening rooms, testing supports, restoring water, and discovering what has already taken up residence in the dark.

The people therefore consider habitation a form of joining.

Before a new district occupies an abandoned factory hall, crews spend years listening to it. They map heat, leaks, roots, nests, load paths, old currents, and unexplained noises. They learn what passes overhead and what depends on the floor below. Only then do walls rise, gardens arrive, and children begin giving names to the stairs.

In time, the inhabitants become difficult to separate from the place that contains them. Their orchards cool its roofs. Their waste feeds its soil. Their repairs redirect its stresses. Their songs travel through its ventilation shafts. Their dead become tiles, heat, fruit, and painted names. Animals alter maintenance schedules; roots hold cracked masonry together; machines dictate waking hours and migration routes.

They do not live upon a completed world.

They live inside an unfinished act of support.
