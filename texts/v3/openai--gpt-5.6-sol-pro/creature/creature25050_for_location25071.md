At first, what lives there seems too small to count.

The dimples in the rounded stones are inhabited by pale colonies called, if they can be called anything, the patient mouths. Each is a film only a few cells thick, spread through the mineral rather than over it. They dissolve no more than a grain in a century. The stone passes through them as food, shelter, skeleton, and measure of time. A whole colony may occupy a boulder while showing itself only as a pattern of shallow pits.

They are not rooted. Given sufficient centuries, they cross from one stone to another at the instant the stones touch. Afterward the old colony dwindles while the new one grows, though which is the traveler and which is the descendant cannot be determined. On plains where tides have rearranged the rubble many times, their dimples form enormous interrupted designs: spirals, branching fans, and shapes like open eyes. The designs may be accidental. They may also be the slowest conversations in the sea.

The white growths around the cold lenses are quicker, though only by comparison. Their folded leaves open when nothing warm is near. Inside each sleeping-hand cluster lies a knot of clear fibers that tightens at regular intervals. A cold lens with many thousands of these growths sometimes contracts as one body, drawing itself through the surrounding warm water. In this way it migrates from grove to grove, carrying its winter with it.

Small bell swimmers emerge from the white leaves after a lens has passed through a ribbon forest. Their bells are transparent except for a bronze thread down the center. They have no mouths because feeding belongs to another stage of their existence. In light they pulse and scatter. In shadow they cease to be animals: their bells loosen into shining dust, and the bronze threads fasten themselves along the veins of ribbon leaves.

Years later, when two ribbons touch and exchange color, the threads pass between them.

Thus the bell swimmers may be the wandering thoughts of the groves. Or the groves may be their immense, sessile bodies.

Many creatures in the sea are divided this way. Movement belongs to one form, hunger to another, memory to a third.

The flat browsers on the stone terraces resemble soft disks of slate, their edges pleated and their upper surfaces furred with whatever grows nearby. They are nearly impossible to distinguish from mats of fan moss until they begin to move. Beneath them is a ring of rasping tissue that turns continually, polishing clean spirals into the rock. They do not eat the moss they remove. Instead, they expose old stone so the patient mouths beneath it can breathe.

When a browser reaches the end of a spiral, it folds inward and hardens into an upright pillar. Rows of rounded stones crossing the plains are often sleeping herds. During clear-water tides they wake all at once, soften, and continue their grazing in a direction that was not previously available.

Their young are not born from them. They condense wherever a spiral track has remained unbroken for one hundred tides.

The bladder vines support a more elusive life. Some of their bladders contain ordinary gas; others contain a dense silver vapor; still others hold recollections stripped of any owner. A bladder touched carelessly may release the sensation of crossing a plain that no longer exists, or of opening leaves under a sun older than the sea. These memories enter through skin and remain for days. Larger bladders can impose habits on nearby animals. Bell swimmers circle them. Browsers pause as if listening. Ribbon groves bend toward places they have never grown.

Something tends these vines.

It is rarely visible as a whole. A dark frond folds against the current; several root ends detach from stone; a cluster of gas bladders sinks rather than rises. For a few moments these parts assume a shared outline, tall and narrow, with too many joints and a hollow where a face might be. Then the current separates them again.

These are the walkers of the drowned paths.

A walker has no permanent substance. It borrows a body from whatever region it inhabits: ribbon fiber on the plateaus, glasswood joints in the forests, mineral fans near warm springs. Its only indispensable part is an empty space enclosed within the borrowed material. That hollow is the walker. The body around it is temporary scaffolding.

Because distance increases in darkness, walkers travel by dismantling themselves at the edge of sight and assembling again elsewhere. The long smooth paths are not pressed by feet. They are places where the same intention has passed repeatedly, persuading silt, roots, and stone to remember a route.

No living thing can cross the same clearing twice by the same way, so a walker must continually revise itself. One journey may take it over the drowned ground; the next, along the underside of a ribbon; the next, through the memory inside a bladder vine. Paths that end at abysses often continue beyond them in this last fashion. The missing section exists only as something recalled.

Walkers enter the fitted-stone chambers one at a time. There they shed their borrowed bodies outside the doorway and flow in as a distortion in the water. The bowls are their instruments, or their eggs, or perhaps their elders.

Each bowl contains a small, different sea because the walkers keep it from joining the greater one. They tend distinctions. They preserve a coldness from being warmed, a brightness from being dimmed, a species of quick silver animal from being persuaded into some slower shape. They do not preserve objects so much as conditions.

A walker may spend centuries adjusting one bowl by the width of a mineral grain. It waits for a distant sound that has not yet occurred, then turns the bowl slightly so that, when the sound finally happens, its note will enter the contained sea and become weather there.

The chambers were not built with tools. Long ago, the walkers taught roots to repeat the idea of a wall. They placed rounded stones where the roots would grip them, then repeated the arrangement until repetition made it real. The roots have since died or changed species, but the rooms continue to remember enclosure. Air occasionally gathers in them because enclosure is one of the things air mistakes for dryness.

Walkers do not speak to one another nearby. Close sound is too unreliable. Instead, each deposits a small noise into a shell, a hollow stem, or the joint of a glasswood trunk. The noise travels for years, enlarging as it goes. When it returns as a note that passes through the whole basin, every walker hears it.

A single note may be a name. Two notes may describe a route. A sequence of seven, separated by centuries, is said to mean that a shore has changed its water.

By this method their conversations outlast forests.

The glasswood itself is alive in a more concentrated fashion. Young trunks are ordinary colonies of branching cells, but as silica enters them and they become transparent, something begins to look outward from the amber beads of sap. The beads stop under scrutiny because attention hardens them. Left alone, they resume climbing, pausing at joints as though making decisions.

Very old glasswoods contain thousands of these beads arranged through kilometers of linked roots. Such a forest can anticipate a clear-water front decades before it arrives. It darkens some trunks, empties others, and grows delicate resonant chambers beneath the bark. When the delayed thunder of a future shell reaches it, the whole forest rings without sound.

Walkers avoid cutting glasswood because a severed trunk forgets slowly. As it slumps into gel, its amber beads continue following the former branches, rising through shapes that are no longer there.

Far above, below, or across the drowned plains moves the threadlike animal.

It is not one of the sea’s scarce temporary fauna. It is older than the distinction between animal and route. Its fins are broad, slow folds in the water, often mistaken for clear-water currents. Fine rootlike structures trail from its body and comb drifting spores from the sea. These structures absorb almost nothing. Most of what they collect is planted elsewhere.

Its head is a small blunt wedge bearing neither eyes nor jaws, only a smooth field of pores. Through these it senses gradients of age. It follows young water into old stone and old water into new forests. Because reflected things are older than the things reflecting them, it is frequently seen swimming beside its own reflection, using the elder image as a guide.

Its body does not occupy continuous distance. Sections of it are joined by intervals rather than flesh. The head may pass through a ribbon grove while the middle crosses a mountain horizon away, and the tail remains coiled around a warm spring from an earlier century. Injury cannot travel reliably along it. Nothing hunts it because no predator could determine where the animal ends.

At uncertain cycles it approaches a stone chamber and places its head against the doorway. A walker emerges carrying a single air bladder. The bladder is released into one of the creature’s pores, and the creature departs bearing a memory that may be delivered to the farthest waters.

The migrating abysses may also be alive.

Walkers treat them as animals, though not as persons. Ribbon groves respond to them as they do to grazers. The thread-creature gives them a wide berth. Along the edge of an approaching depth, patient mouths close their films and glasswood draws its amber sap into the roots.

An abyss has no body separate from its effect. It feeds on duration. Where it passes, matter remembers all the years it might have survived and assumes the shape those years would have given it. Mountains flatten. Leaves become soil. Chambers open along their oldest weaknesses. A living thing caught inside may emerge unchanged in substance but exhausted in possibility, unable ever again to flower, molt, divide, or choose a new direction.

Yet abysses leave fertile ground. Walkers sometimes follow behind them, planting bladders full of first memories: the first opening of a leaf, the first pressure of a root against stone, the first distinction between warm and cold. From these recollections, new ecosystems begin.

Near the living-solid shores dwell the most numerous inhabitants of all, though none can be separated from the wall.

The interlocked leaves, roots, transparent trunks, mineral towers, and slow colonies form a single porous intelligence extending farther than any clear-water interval has revealed. Sap serves it as blood. Currents through its capillaries serve it as breath. Browsers move within it like mobile organs, and bell swimmers carry impulses between regions too dense for roots to cross.

It thinks by altering passage. A current admitted through one channel and denied another becomes a decision. A pulse of brown-green light is either a question or the awareness of injury. Its thoughts may require thousands of years to travel from one extremity to the other.

The walkers may be fragments that detach from this shore-mind when it needs to act at a distance. Or the shore may be what walkers become when they have accumulated too many memories to remain hollow.

Beyond it lies the darker water, forever falling without descent. Sometimes the wall opens a capillary into that water. Something presses back.

No inhabitant has crossed entirely. What returns are unfamiliar spores, clear roots that cast red shadows, and small chambers already fitted together, drifting empty through the dark. The walkers gather around these objects but do not enter them. The long animal turns away. Even the migrating abysses thin and disappear at their approach.

Inside each foreign chamber is a shallow bowl.

The bowl is not level.

And in its thumb-sized surface, under a hard direct sun unknown to the upper sea, figures can sometimes be seen standing on a dry shore, looking up.
