The people of the valley tend to be mistaken for workers, especially at a distance.

They are almost always carrying something: a hooked pole, a coil of narrow hose, a case of testing vials, a basket of greens, a child, a folded warning screen, a length of pipe balanced between two shoulders. Their clothes are dark with use and pale at the seams from repeated washing. Waterproof aprons are worn over school uniforms, office clothes, ceremonial coats. Most adults keep gloves clipped to the belt and a small lamp at the throat. Soles are soft, ridged, and frequently resoled. Bright colors occur in cuffs, cap bands, shoulder patches, and stitched tabs indicating which kinds of line the wearer is permitted to open.

They walk with the slight forward inclination of people accustomed to ramps. On level ground many seem about to climb. Their calves are strong; their hands are marked by cold water, oil, plant tannins, lime, and the fine cuts left by wire mesh. Older residents often have one shoulder lower than the other from decades spent moving through sloping passages and carrying loads on stairways too narrow for carts.

No one goes far without touching the valley.

A palm passes over a pipe to judge its warmth. Knuckles sound a railing before weight is put on it. A thumb wipes condensation from a gauge. Children trail fingers along walls and learn to distinguish harmless seepage from the silky mineral film that forms before a joint begins to fail. People pause in conversation when a pump changes cadence. They look up when drops strike leaves in the wrong rhythm. Even those whose work is done at desks or benches acquire this peripheral attention. The valley is too interconnected to belong only to specialists.

There are specialists nonetheless, and many of them.

Water readers travel the upper cisterns before dawn, opening narrow inspection doors and lowering mirrored floats into the dark. Root cutters crawl beneath orchards to keep willow and fig from entering drains. Load clerks maintain layered maps of weight: masonry, stored goods, soil saturation, snow, temporary crowds. There are hinge crews for movable facades, cable splicers for the inclines, moss keepers, fungal inspectors, tramway clearers, heat stewards, night irrigators, and the quiet technicians who listen to bearing housings through metal rods pressed against the bones behind their ears.

Some trades are so local that they have no name beyond a particular district. One family may be responsible for the seven valves under a bathhouse because their grandparents rebuilt the chamber after a collapse and no subsequent drawing has captured what they did. A woman in a lower dye quarter may be the only person who knows which abandoned flue clears the school basement during east rain. People speak of such knowledge as if it resides partly in the person and partly in the place: she remembers the flue, but the flue also remembers her.

Work is organized less by profession than by custody. A person may operate a milling machine during one shift, prune roof fruit after supper, and spend two nights each month on flood watch. Adolescents begin with rounds that require observation but little intervention. They count drips in marked corridors, clear leaves from grilles, carry samples to public testing rooms, and replace the small strips of cloth tied near vents to show the direction of airflow. Their first tools are usually brushes. Their first authority is the right to close a footpath.

That right is taken seriously. A narrow bridge can be barred by a twelve-year-old if its vibration feels wrong.

The inhabitants do not hurry in the manner of people who expect a clear route. They move steadily, leaving room for interruption. A journey across the valley might involve three lifts, a borrowed bicycle, a freight cradle, and a detour through a laundry gallery because the usual stair has become part of a spillway. Appointment times are given with tolerances: before the second release, after the orchard lamps go out, during the uphill cargo interval. Lateness is forgivable if accompanied by useful news.

Directions are spoken as sequences of physical conditions.

Follow the cold pipe until it divides. Take the stair that sweats. Cross where the brick changes color. If you smell yeast, you have gone one court too low.

Children learn these instructions before they learn formal maps. In games, they blindfold one another and identify districts by echoes, floor tremors, drafts, and odors. They float bits of peel through gutters and race along galleries to see where they emerge. They build miniature retaining walls from broken tile and wet soil, then argue over whose failure was caused by rain and whose by bad construction. On school roofs they maintain plots of beans and hardy medicinal plants, but lessons are interrupted whenever maintenance requires the space. No one regards this as interruption for long. Watching a greenhouse roof fold into storm position is considered instruction.

Their speech is full of hydraulic and structural terms used for ordinary life. A secret that has become too widely known has overtopped. A stubborn person is said to be carrying dead load. A household that takes in too many relatives is running above marked capacity. A quarrel may need a bypass, a pressure release, or simply time to settle. Praise is often practical: well seated, clear-running, good under weather.

Promises are rarely made forever. They are made for as long as the wall holds, until the next inspection, through this wet season, while there is heat in the return pipe. This does not make the people faithless. On the contrary, they distrust permanence because they understand the labor hidden inside it. To say that something will be maintained is a stronger vow than to say it will endure.

Households occupy whatever enclosures can be kept warm and dry without obstructing necessary access. A home may be three rooms inserted between a lift shaft and a reservoir wall, with windows opening into a greenhouse rather than the air. Another may curve along the upper gallery of an old assembly hall. Floors rarely align. Tables have adjustable legs. Cupboards are fitted around braces and conduits; beds fold upward when inspection panels must be reached. Many rooms contain one architectural feature too large to ignore—a capped drive shaft, a brick pier, an obsolete valve wheel—and these become shelves, clothes racks, shrines, or seats.

Nothing indoors is quite still. Water ticks in heating lines. Glass clouds and clears. A suspended cup trembles when freight passes several levels below. Families know the cycles of their rooms: when the wall warms, when the air reverses through the vent, when the filtration backwash makes the crockery faintly clink.

Possessions are compact and repairable. Good boots pass between siblings and are rebuilt until none of the first pair remains. Cooking pots carry riveted patches. Coats are cut down, widened, waxed, and relined. Furniture that cannot pass through a stairwell is assembled where it will stay and may remain after several generations of occupants have gone. The valley’s few lavish objects are often made from waste that demanded too much skill to discard: lamps of patterned safety glass, bowls turned from cable drums, intricate screens woven from stripped copper, toys with tiny working gates and lifts.

Meals are moist, hot, and strongly flavored. Mushrooms, cabbages, beans, sour greens, roof apples, dark bread, preserved roots, soft cheeses, canal herbs, and dense broths appear repeatedly. Grapes from the warmer terraces are made into sharp wine. Fish is uncommon enough to be ceremonial, and meat rarer still. Tea is brewed in many strengths from leaves and bitter stems grown near warm-water lines. Workers returning cold from the upper levels drink salted broth from wide cups held in both hands.

Food is rarely described as local to the valley as a whole. It belongs to an elevation, a water circuit, a roof exposure. Lower mushrooms are said to taste of iron. West-wall apples keep longer. Sorrel grown above the old turbines is prized because the residual warmth brings it early. Arguments about these distinctions are affectionate and inexhaustible.

Privacy exists, but not invisibility. Walls carry sound, pipes cross dwellings, and access crews may arrive at any hour. Residents announce themselves by tapping prescribed patterns before opening service doors. People learn not to comment on what they glimpse through a dismantled facade: a family eating supper, someone washing their hair, an old man asleep beside the humming casing of a pump. Curtains are common, but so are peepholes, mirrors, speaking tubes, and small shared windows between adjacent kitchens.

Most social life occupies transitional spaces. People exchange news on landings while waiting for lift cages. Meals are sold from counters built into retaining walls. Courtships develop on inspection rounds because two people may spend hours walking the same wet route with no need to face each other. Public debates happen in bath queues, tram shelters, seed houses, and the covered galleries above markets. A person may be known across half the valley without anyone being certain where they live.

The baths are among the few places where rank becomes difficult to display. Waste heat feeds long tiled rooms at several temperatures. Boots, patches, tools, and colored work tabs are left outside. Inside, bodies show the valley’s actual cost: rope scars, missing fingertips, burns from steam leaks, knees enlarged by stairs, pale bands where protective cuffs have kept out the sun and wet. There are old crush injuries and new surgical braces. There are also bodies made powerful by climbing, lifting, and repetition, though strength is admired less than endurance and judgment.

The most respected people are not those who command the largest systems but those who notice early.

An elder who smells insulation heating behind plaster may save a district. A child who reports a new crack may prevent a terrace from slipping. A gardener who recognizes the wrong shade of green in a reed bed may stop contamination before it passes into wash water. Public records name major repairs, but oral memory preserves the first observer: who felt the tremor, who heard the hollow place under the paving, who saw the water turn cloudy at dawn.

Governance grows from this culture of noticing. Councils meet by watershed, load path, transport line, and heat circuit, their territories overlapping untidily. The same household may answer to five assemblies. Decisions are written on ceramic boards, waxed cloth, metal tags, and digital displays, but they are also carried verbally by route stewards who explain not only what will change but what signs would mean the change is going wrong.

Arguments are constant. People dispute which roofs may take more soil, whether a school can surrender heat to a seed house, whether a market’s foot traffic exceeds the old hall’s safe load, whether an orchard is worth the water it holds. Meetings may last until the lamps dim automatically. Yet everyone understands that disagreement is part of maintenance. Consensus achieved too quickly is treated with suspicion.

Punishment usually takes the form of restricted trust. Someone careless with a valve may lose access to marked systems. Someone who conceals a leak may be barred from custodial work and required to accompany repair crews without touching tools. Theft is judged according to consequence more than value. Taking a basket of fruit is one offense; taking a length of correctly graded cable and replacing it with a weaker one is almost unforgivable.

The dead remain within the valley’s circulation, though not sentimentally.

There is too little ground for burial. Bodies are burned in high-temperature industrial chambers during scheduled clean cycles, and the mineral remains are sealed into small tiles. Some tiles are set into household walls. Others are added to public stair risers, reservoir parapets, or the interior of repaired arches. Names are stamped on the hidden face as often as the visible one. The point is not display. It is incorporation.

A common mourning phrase means: their weight is with us.

Memorials are often acts of continued function. A dead cable tender may be remembered by a bench fitted with drawers of emergency clamps. A teacher’s name is given to a seed archive. After a root keeper dies, their pruning hooks may hang in the greenhouse where they worked until rust makes them unsafe. Then the metal is melted and recast.

Birth is marked by water. A small sealed flask is filled from the household’s current supply, labeled with its circuit and quality, and stored until the child is old enough to test it against the water then flowing from the same tap. Differences are expected. Sediment, mineral content, temperature, even the source reservoir may have changed. Adults say this teaches the child that continuity is not sameness.

There are few animals, but those allowed to remain are intensely known. Small roof sheep wear soft foot coverings on vulnerable surfaces and are moved according to moisture readings. Cats are kept in grain stores and machine archives, where they sleep on warm cabinets and are prevented from entering bird-sensitive greenhouse sections. Dogs are uncommon; stairs, lift cages, and crowded passages do not suit them. A handful of patient, low-built breeds assist tunnel crews, locating warm air leaks and people trapped behind slips.

The bats in the lower ducts are protected by regulations older than some districts. Residents count them at seasonal emergence and argue about whether their decline indicates chemical imbalance, altered airflow, or merely a change of roost. Moths caught inside workshops are cupped gently and released near designated lamp gardens. Even nuisance life is catalogued. In a place with so little uncontrolled ecology, accident carries a kind of prestige.

There are also inhabitants no census records.

Pale fungal threads spread through old insulation and fruiting beds, joining structures that were never designed to meet. Moss colonies are moved from wall to wall in damp slabs, some descended from growth first cultivated generations ago. Microbes travel the water loops, welcomed in one tank and destroyed in the next. Reed beds pulse with larvae. Roots enter cracks, withdraw when cut, and return by another route.

Maintenance crews speak of these lives as coworkers, livestock, trespassers, patients, and weather, sometimes in the same conversation.

And beneath that practical language runs another belief, seldom stated plainly: that the valley itself has acquired habits.

It prefers certain channels. It sheds water from one slope and hoards it in another. Repairs that seem sound fail repeatedly, while improbable structures remain balanced beyond every forecast. Old pipes knock before pressure changes reach the gauges. Unused lift cages sometimes rise a few centimeters at night as distant loads shift through the cable system. Doors swell shut in wet months and open again when upper reservoirs are drained, as if responding to events several districts away.

No one calls the valley alive. That would be imprecise. Life, to them, is not a single quality but a set of obligations: to consume, excrete, grow, damage, respond, depend, and be depended upon. By that measure the distinction is not useful.

During storms, people move into readiness without much instruction. Children are collected into rooms above known flood lines. Market awnings become diversion sheets. Bakers shut ovens to free heat capacity. Bathhouses lower their pools. Gardeners cut drainage trenches through beds they have tended for years. Workshops stop production and uncover emergency pumps. Those not assigned elsewhere stand at windows and listen.

When a warning bell travels down the slopes, other bells answer—not in unison, because sound takes different paths through the masonry, but one after another, level after level. Lamps change color behind wet glass. Gates turn. The inhabitants take their places beside grilles, valves, braces, hoists, and doors.

Then the rain enters everything.

Water runs over roofs, through soil, along leaf ribs, beneath paving, behind tile. The valley grows heavier by the minute. Its walls tighten against their bolts. Reservoirs rise. Cable lines alter pitch. Thousands of people watch separate gauges and feel separate tremors, each responsible for a fragment small enough to understand.

From above, almost none of them can be seen. Only lamps move through the foliage and mist. A stair closes here. A spillway opens there. Warm windows appear and vanish behind sheets of runoff.

Yet it is they who keep the slopes from becoming ruins, and the ruins from ceasing to be a town. They do not conquer the valley or preserve it unchanged. They enter into its endless corrections.

They live there the way mortar lives between stones: under pressure, largely hidden, carrying more than can be seen.
