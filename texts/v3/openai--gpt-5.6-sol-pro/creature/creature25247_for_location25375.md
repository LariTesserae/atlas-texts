No citizens live in the Uninhabited Quarter. What lives there has no use for shelter, yet nearly all of it behaves as though shelter were the most important fact in the world.

The black growths along the northern walls are the easiest inhabitants to mistake for ordinary plants. From a distance they resemble saplings burned down to wire: a central stem, sparse branches, translucent leaves held at severe angles. Up close, their surfaces are not fibrous but finely articulated, made of nested mineral sleeves that slide against one another as the stem bends. They possess neither roots nor any clear division between living tissue and the salt from which they rise. Beneath each plant is a fan of hair-thin conductors spread along the boundary of the buried glass. These filaments do not pierce the glass. They lie against it, harvesting minute differences in charge.

Their leaves are less like leaves than switches. Each presents one broad face and one narrow edge to the air. For long periods they remain absolutely still; then one turns, another answers across the courtyard, and an entire avenue changes orientation in a single coordinated gesture. No signal can be seen passing between them. The plants may be responding to a common current below the city, or they may share some buried connection deeper than their apparent separation suggests.

Their growth is exceedingly slow except when architecture changes nearby. A wall opening into a new arcade may cause a plant three streets away to extend a branch before the arch is complete. If a tower retracts, the nearest plants fold their leaves inward and remain that way until the pavement settles. They seem to perceive the city not by sight but as variations in a field: rooms as hollows in pressure, stairs as ascending gradients, plazas as broad zones of weak charge.

What looks like deliberate planting may therefore be the plants arranging themselves. They favor regular intervals because close neighbors interfere with one another. A grove is not a community but an instrument whose separate stems require distance in order to measure the basin accurately. When too many arise together, the weakest loosen from the ground. Their buried filaments withdraw without breaking, and the whole plant glides upright across the salt until it reaches an empty district or the basin’s edge.

The hollow prisms called seeds may not be seeds at all. They gather in unused rooms, fitting themselves loosely into corners and beneath stairs. There they turn, face by face, as the electrical conditions change. Sometimes several prisms join point to point and produce a fragile skeleton shaped like a much larger plant. Sometimes they assemble into the outline of a small chair, a crouching animal, or a figure standing beneath an arch. Such arrangements do not last. Before the resemblance becomes exact, the prisms repel one another and scatter.

The dull green scales on new buildings are more communal. Each scale is a separate living plate, leathery at its center and glass-thin around the edge. A colony may contain thousands, but no two scales touch. They communicate across the gaps by brief alterations in color, too faint to notice unless watched through an entire dusk. A pulse begins near the pavement, divides around doorways, and rejoins above them. Another travels down a staircase one tread at a time. Gradually the colony acquires a complete account of the structure it covers.

It does not merely cling to architecture; it interprets architecture.

The scales mark thresholds even when a doorway leads nowhere. They circle empty niches as if distinguishing a place reserved for an object from an accidental cavity. They trace the outer edges of windows but never cross the sill. On featureless walls they sometimes form the outline of a door hours before the mineral thins and the opening appears. Whether they predict the change or provoke it is uncertain.

When a building folds away, the colony lifts as one. For several moments the scales hover in their former arrangement, preserving the vanished facade in empty air: a suspended green plan of stairs, arches, cornices, and blind windows. Then the plan loosens. The scales drift to the newest structure and begin mapping again.

The pale plaza-creatures live between the two kinds of growth.

They are as broad as serving trays and scarcely thicker than a hand. A mature individual bears between twelve and thirty legs, though counting them is difficult because the limbs divide and recombine beneath the body. Their broad terminal pads remain a hair’s breadth above the pavement. They may be supported by repulsion from the salt, or they may rest upon some layer of the city inaccessible to ordinary touch.

They have no forward end. When one changes direction, nothing turns; the sequence of its leg movements simply reverses or passes sideways around the body. Beneath its pale outer plates, faint dark threads rearrange themselves to become whatever temporary anatomy the new direction requires.

They visit the black plants at dusk. Approaching one, the creature pauses at the limit of the plant’s buried electrical field. Its legs arrange themselves into a careful ring. Then the mineral fan along its back unfolds—first two ribs, then many, opening into a delicate structure larger than the animal itself. Charge left in the plant’s stem moves into the fan as a violet dimness. The creature remains there until the plant snaps its leaves away. It then closes its fan and departs.

This feeding harms neither participant. Plants visited regularly grow straighter and produce more prisms. The creatures, in turn, seem unable to feed from plants that have gone long unvisited. Each prepares the other.

They rarely meet. If two enter the same plaza, both stop before coming within several body lengths. Doorways often open soon afterward, and each animal takes a different route. On the rare occasions when three are trapped together by sealing walls, they place themselves at equal distances and lower their bodies until they become nearly indistinguishable from pale patches in the pavement. Hours later only one rises. Nothing remains of the others, but the survivor has acquired additional joints.

Then there are the inhabitants of the walls.

No complete specimen has been observed because completeness may not be a condition they possess. A traveling depression might be one animal passing through stone, but it might equally be a succession of local lives waking and dying along a route. The movement has no consistent size. At times it is no wider than a finger and threads through the pleats of a column. Elsewhere an entire facade swells outward as though something huge has rolled over in its sleep.

The black plants turn away before the disturbance arrives. Green scales interrupt their signaling and cant their edges upward. Plaza-creatures flatten themselves. These responses suggest predation, but nothing is visibly consumed.

After the passage, the architecture changes.

The alteration often resembles a bodily accommodation. Narrow passages widen at shoulder height. Low chambers appear with smooth concavities in their floors. Parallel grooves develop on stairs. Yet the proportions are never stable enough to reveal the body being accommodated. A corridor suited to something long and horizontal may turn upward into a shaft too thin for the same creature. A rounded chamber may contract until nothing larger than a thread could occupy it.

Perhaps the wall-dwellers change shape as freely as the buildings. Perhaps walls are their bodies, and the moving bulges are only moments of attention traveling through them. What appears to be an animal inside architecture may instead be architecture briefly becoming animal.

The shadows also display a disputed kind of life.

Where several gather at an intersection, they do not overlap passively. Their edges feel along one another, separate, and try again. Dense pools creep toward alcoves and covered passages even when the weak lights above would place them elsewhere. Salt retreats from them in branching fans. Seeds caught at their edges become still. A pale creature crossing one may emerge with its mineral fan already open, as if it has passed through the vicinity of a plant.

No shadow survives alone for long. Isolated ones thin and divide until they can attach themselves to columns, balconies, or moving organisms. Crowded ones are dispersed by the city. A stair may rise through the middle of a dark pool, breaking it into narrow bands; a roof may retract and expose it to several directions of gray radiance. The quarter maintains distance among its shadows just as it maintains distance among its animals.

Below every octagonal lattice there is, apparently, another population.

The black growths visible on the underside city hang upward rather than downward, their leaves turned toward the glass between worlds. Pale shapes occasionally cross those inverted plazas. It is impossible to tell whether they are counterparts of the creatures above or the same individuals observed at another point in their journeys. Sometimes a fan opens below at the exact moment one closes above. Sometimes the lower streets remain empty while all the vegetation bends toward a point directly beneath the observer.

The objects suspended between cities may be inhabitants too, for suspension changes them. A mineral prism placed among the ribs will slowly develop additional faces. A fragment of black plant sends filaments both upward and downward. Dead material is not preserved there; it becomes undecided. When the next transparent pressure front arrives, the object disappears to one side, but there is no reliable way to know which side has gained it.

Taken separately, the quarter’s organisms appear sparse and specialized: electrical plants, architectural colonies, grazing plates, lithic burrowers. Taken together, they resemble parts of a single metabolism distributed across streets.

The black plants detect currents. The pale creatures redistribute them. The green scales read new structures and perhaps teach the mineral how its forms are to be used. The things in the walls initiate rearrangement, or embody it. Shadows gather waste differences in light and are broken apart before they become too dense. The lower city receives what the upper one cannot keep.

And the city itself grows, heals, sheds, and enforces spacing.

It may be the oldest and largest inhabitant there. Its houses would then be no more houses than the chambers of a heart are rooms, and its avenues no more streets than veins are canals. Its imitation of civic space might be coincidental—one possible geometry produced by an organism whose essential processes are opening, enclosure, circulation, and release.

Yet the imitation is too intimate to dismiss. The balconies are placed for looking out. Alcoves are shaped for waiting bodies. Squares preserve conversational distances. Stairs maintain the rise most comfortable to a foot that has never stepped there.

This suggests another possibility: that none of the visible life is the intended inhabitant. The organisms are custodians, nerves, pollinators, surveyors, or rehearsal mechanisms. The city is not empty because life has failed to occupy it. It is empty because occupancy is a condition still being manufactured.

Its true inhabitant may exist only as a set of measurements.

The width between facing balconies. The height of an alcove. The interval maintained between black stems. The unfilled center of a plaza. The cold pocket sealed inside a room. Something is being described by all these vacancies, the way a mold describes what has never yet been cast.

For now, that absent body is the most widespread creature in the basin. It inhabits every doorway without entering, occupies every chair-shaped gathering of seeds, and ascends every stair only as a proportion. The city continually makes room for it, then destroys the room before anything else can settle there.

Everything living in the Uninhabited Quarter participates in that restraint. It feeds without devouring, grows without crowding, moves without arriving, and leaves no corpse behind.

They are not the city’s population.

They are the means by which it remains expectant.
