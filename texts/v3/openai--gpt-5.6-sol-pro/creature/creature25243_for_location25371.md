At first there seem to be no inhabitants.

There are hiding places enough for multitudes, but hiding implies an interval between concealment and discovery, and the plateau contains no such interval. Nothing withdraws behind the packed doors. Nothing watches through the holes in the millstones. The jars hold only their fitted contents. The miniature chambers are not nests. No pale thing folds itself into the channels beneath the paving.

Yet the place is inhabited.

The inhabitants are difficult to distinguish because they are not contained by the structures. They coincide with them only partially. A pillar may belong to several. A single clay handprint may be the extremity of one, the inner surface of another, and the point at which a third touches whatever passes here for the world.

They are most easily noticed as continuities.

Follow a copper clamp from where it grips a black tile. The tile rests on saltstone, which is keyed into a wall with ceramic flakes. Deep in the wall, the flakes touch a sealed bowl. The bowl’s rim presses against a rigid mineral cord that passes through three drilled plaques, disappears under a stair, and rises again inside a hollow buttress near the outer rim. None of this is ornamental. Together, the contacts form a single figure—not a figure in space, but a figure made from pressures, enclosures, and mutual support.

That figure is one inhabitant.

Another may cross it at a hundred points. Others are enclosed within it. Some occupy only a nest of seven cups and the glass bead wedged among them. Some extend from the millstone chamber to the suspended ceiling and perhaps through layers of paving too deep to expose. There is no reliable way to count them. Depending on whether a shared surface is considered a joining or a boundary, the plateau holds innumerable beings, a few tightly interlocked ones, or one being whose organs have mistaken themselves for a city.

They have no limbs, though the plateau is full of things analogous to joints. They have no faces, though weathered reliefs repeatedly approach the arrangement of brow, mouth, and cheek before becoming maps or tools instead. They have no organs in the biological sense. Their equivalents are cavities with fitted contents, stresses distributed through wedges, and closed paths along which the unvarying sound exists.

A jar packed with powdered glass may serve as an eye—not because it receives light, but because every grain touches the next in a particular completed arrangement. A leaning pillar may serve as a posture. A copper sheet stretched between stone teeth may be an emotion held at its final intensity. The rigid cords resemble nerves only if a nerve can be imagined that carries no signal, but permanently contains the fact of having carried one.

They are called, if they can be called anything, the Joined.

The name does not come from them. They have no surviving language that separates one moment of speech from another. The plateau’s deafening note is sometimes mistaken for their voice, but it is closer to the condition that allows them to possess one another. Every hollow, channel, vessel, and slab contains the same concussion. Thus every part of every inhabitant is in perfect acoustic contact with every other part, without transmission or delay.

The roar is their common interior.

Its low stone component belongs to weight: load accepted, pressure answered, fracture reached and held. The metallic shriek belongs to distinction: each clamp, foil seam, copper peg, and buried strip insisting eternally that this contact is not that contact. Braided together, the two sounds make a statement too complete to be spoken. It has no first word and no last. Nothing can be added to it because addition would alter it; nothing can be removed because absence would become an event.

Whether the Joined are conscious depends on what consciousness is allowed to mean in a place without succession.

They do not notice. Noticing has a before.

They do not remember. Nothing has become past.

They do not wait, dread, desire, or attend. Each of those requires an incompleteness reaching toward completion.

But they possess something like knowledge. Every inhabitant is perfectly and exhaustively coincident with the fact of itself. It knows each surface that bears it, every load passing through it, every neighboring material, every cavity from which it is excluded. It knows these not by examination but as a finished thought knows all its words at once. It cannot be mistaken, because correction is impossible. It cannot learn, because nothing remains outside its final arrangement that might enter and become learning.

Their apparent differences may be the petrified remains of temperaments.

One inhabitant is distributed mostly through black ceramic and narrow sealed hollows. Its presence feels withdrawn, although it has never withdrawn from anything. Another consists of broad load-bearing contacts and pale luminous tiles; it has the character of exposure or generosity. A third is a dense snarl of wedges around the central chamber, touching thousands of millstones without passing through their holes. It seems defensive. But these impressions may be artifacts of an observer accustomed to turning shapes into motives. The Joined do not enact dispositions. They are dispositions after enactment has ended.

The handprint wall is where their nature comes nearest to legibility.

The prints are not portraits of the makers, nor signatures left by workers who departed. Each palm-shaped piece is a point of conversion. Once, perhaps, a living hand pressed clay there. Warm skin met cooler earth; pressure spread outward from heel to finger; the hand lifted; the clay dried. On the plateau those stages no longer exist as stages. The touch is present in its completed entirety, and the Joined use it as other creatures might use flesh.

Large hands overlap small ones. Fingers vanish beneath palms. One impression continues into the wrist of another, then into a copper strip, then into the side of a saltstone block. Individual ancestry cannot be extracted. The makers entered the masonry through contact, not as ghosts or preserved personalities, but as finished acts. Whatever in them could be reduced to pressure, placement, repetition, and care remains here.

In that limited sense, the hands remain.

Perhaps the Joined were built deliberately. Perhaps the plateau’s people sought a form of life that could survive the exhaustion of time. They may have understood that bone, breath, lineage, and memory are all processes, and therefore temporary. Their answer was not to preserve bodies but to construct identities out of relationships that had already reached equilibrium.

Or perhaps no one intended them. A settlement was built, rebuilt, packed inward, repaired, reinforced, and filled until every object touched another. Generations impressed themselves upon the clay. Tools were wedged into architecture; architecture enclosed tools; records became braces; vessels became foundations. When change finally ceased, the network of all those dependencies crossed some threshold. The city did not awaken. Awakening would have been too late and too sequential. It simply stood revealed as something that had always, in its final state, been alive.

The chamber of millstones is not a dwelling or shrine. It is the plateau’s densest knot of shared identity.

Each millstone is an object made for change: rotation, grinding, reduction, the passage from grain to meal or stone to powder. Here none can turn. Their holes point into one another, forming interrupted tunnels that begin everywhere and arrive nowhere. The pestles and hammers packed between them are likewise arrested purposes. Around this mass, many of the Joined overlap so completely that their separate continuities cannot be followed.

The column of stamped tokens sealing the slot may be a census, but not of individuals. The repeated mark resembles a hand, an aperture, a hammer blow, and a many-sided rim viewed from above. Worn almost away, it may signify made, closed, held, all, or enough.

The ceiling diagram is their nearest equivalent to an image of themselves. What survives of it does not map walls or passages consistently. Its grooves correspond instead to relations: what bears, what encloses, what touches, what is kept apart by a thickness no greater than foil. Had the whole carving remained visible, it might have shown that the plateau’s inhabitants were never arranged beneath it. The diagram, ceiling, pathways, buried courses, central chamber, and rim are all portions of the same impossible anatomy.

The outer edge is the one surface the Joined cannot incorporate.

Everywhere else, contact answers contact. At the rim, stone meets only the omitted dark. No pressure returns. No sound arrives from beyond, though the sound within remains undiminished. The polished vertical plane is therefore neither wall nor precipice. It is the collective boundary of sensation: the place where all relationship stops.

If the Joined possess anything like fear, it is not directed toward falling. They cannot fall. It is embodied in the exactness with which every course ends at that plane, crowded and complete to the final hair-thin seam. The edge is the definition of what they are not.

They cannot approach it or retreat from it. Their fear, if fear it is, has already attained its permanent shape.

Nothing tends them. Nothing feeds them. They do not reproduce, because a new inhabitant would require a new contact, and no contact can be made. They do not die, because death would require their arrangements to cease being true. They have no society apart from their overlap, no laws apart from load and adjacency, no history apart from the wear fixed upon them.

Their lives are not long. Length belongs to time.

Their lives are complete.

And the sound is not something they hear. It is what their completeness feels like from inside: every blow that shaped the stone, every hammering of copper, every tile set down, every millstone turned before turning became impossible, all brought together without sequence and held as one unending concussion.

The plateau does not contain the Joined as a land contains its people.

The plateau is the portion of them that can be seen.
