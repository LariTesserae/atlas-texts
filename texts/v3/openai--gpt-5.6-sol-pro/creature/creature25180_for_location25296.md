### The Inhabitant of the High Cistern

At first, the cistern appears to contain a multitude.

There are the little blind swimmers that crowd the ceramic screens. Individually they are colorless, but a gathering of them takes on the diluted pink of milk mixed with blood. They swim in groups of several hundred, all turning together without collision. Their flattened heads contain no eyes and very little brain. Along each flank runs a row of pressure pits, sensitive enough to detect the displacement of a falling grain of sediment.

They are called cutmouths, for the sideways opening of the jaw. The mouth has no teeth. Its lips are lined with flexible combs that strain the gray mineral film from the water. When alarmed, a cutmouth folds its fins tightly against its body and drops. A whole school may vanish this way, not by fleeing into darkness but by becoming a brief shower of pale bodies and settling among the shells below.

Cutmouths are hatched inside the perforated bricks. Their eggs are soft, adhesive, and almost transparent. They are placed in rows along the inner walls of holes too narrow for the jar-hunters to enter. Newly hatched young remain there for weeks, moving no farther than the length of their own bodies, licking the film from the ceramic with tongues like white threads.

They are the most visible creatures in the cistern, and perhaps the least important.

The ceiling shells are older in appearance. They have no obvious head and seldom move once fixed. Their lower surfaces are soft and pleated, their upper shells broad, thin, and shaped to overlap. Water passing beneath them sets their feeding folds trembling. The finest suspended particles are caught there: microbial cells, ceramic powder, fragments of flesh, metal salts.

A ceiling shell begins life without a shell. It crawls upward on seven temporary legs, each no more substantial than a strand of mucus. When it finds a suitable hollow, it eats away the softened face of the brick and mixes the grains with secretions from its back. Its shell is therefore made partly from the cistern. Red brick produces rust-colored shells; bone-lime mortar gives white ones; creatures settled on black glass become glossy and dark.

After attachment, the legs are absorbed.

Generations accumulate in layers. The living occupy the exposed undersides. Above them lie the shells of their predecessors, mortared together by mineral film until animal, tomb, and ceiling become indistinguishable. In some galleries, these deposits are several feet thick. They preserve the shapes of vanished coffers better than the original masonry does.

Among them travel the long-bodied grazers.

These have been mistaken for worms, though each bears a fine internal frame composed of hundreds of linked rings. They do not undulate. Instead, the rings shorten one after another, drawing the creature forward with such smoothness that it seems to pour itself across the wall.

Around the mouth is a fan of feelers. Some are chemical organs, tasting copper and dissolved iron. Others are delicate fingers used to test cracks. A grazer encountering fresh mineral film opens a circular rasp concealed in its throat and leaves a clean path through the grayness.

The paths are rarely random.

Seen over months, they form branching figures across the walls: lines that follow warm currents, curve around breeding holes, and converge on seams where copper cramps lie beneath the brick. The grazers return to these routes even after the film has grown back. Older individuals may follow circuits hundreds of body lengths long, pausing at the same sockets in the same order.

No one can say whether the pattern is memory, instinct, or something impressed upon them by the layout of the cistern.

The jar-hunters feed upon the cutmouths. They are not much larger than their prey. Their apparent size comes from the feeding veil: a loose, leathery throat that can be extended from the body and spread around the inside of an urn or drain.

A settled hunter anchors itself by means of hooks driven into old resin. Its veil darkens until it resembles waterlogged hide. It may wait so long that ceiling shells attach to its back. When cutmouths gather, attracted by the film growing along the jar’s interior, the veil contracts. Water and prey are drawn through a ring of inward-pointing spines.

After feeding, the hunter may not move for a year.

Its young develop inside the undigested skeletons of its prey. When released, they do not resemble their parent. They are minute, many-legged things that crawl into the sediment and consume copper scales. Only after several molts do they seek a jar and begin growing the throat.

The largest mobile animals are the cloaks.

They hang in the deep gallery and in the central shaft, translucent and nearly without structure. Each consists of two thin sheets of living tissue held apart by clear jelly. Dark threads branch through them. These threads carry neither blood nor nerve impulses as such, but slow waves of chemical change.

A cloak may take an hour to contract.

The movement begins at one corner and passes diagonally across the animal, gathering its broad body into folds. Water is expelled from between the sheets. The cloak rises perhaps the height of a hand, opens, and begins sinking again.

Small swimmers shelter within its folds. Grazers cross its surface and feed there. Jar-hunter larvae lodge in its threads. A healthy cloak can carry an entire moving community, though whether these passengers are parasites, offspring, or organs remains uncertain.

The oldest cloaks are scarred with lines of dark mineral. They drift against pillars, chains, and the edges of screens, wearing themselves open. The tears do not close. Instead, the severed edges thicken, curl inward, and slowly become separate animals. A large cloak may divide over several centuries without any single moment of reproduction.

None has ever been observed to die intact.

When too damaged to remain open, it collapses around its passengers and sinks. Scavengers enter it. The jelly dissolves. The branching threads persist longest, settling on the floor like black lace.

There are other forms: shell-borers with drill-shaped mouths; soft grubs that inhabit the warm cavities behind copper plates; tiny jointed things that live only within the feeding fans of grazers; white knots that pulse inside the sediment and surface once in a generation.

The names imply species.

That may be an error.

All the larger creatures contain, somewhere in their bodies, the same five-part structure: five hooked granules arranged around a hollow center. In cutmouths it lies behind the throat. In ceiling shells it is dispersed through the mantle. The jar-hunter carries it at the base of its veil. In a cloak, millions of the structures occur along the dark branching threads.

The granules are made from iron, copper, calcium, and a black substance derived from the old glass. They are too regular to be accidental and too small to have been shaped by any hand.

The cells of the gray film contain them too.

It may be that the film is not merely the base of the cistern’s food web. It may be the youngest and most numerous condition of the same life that later crawls, swims, hunts, hangs from ceilings, and drifts through the shaft.

The transformations are difficult to follow because they are rarely confined to one body. A cutmouth swallowed by a jar-hunter is not wholly digested. Some of its cells enter the hunter’s veil and become part of it. The hunter’s larvae, descending into the sediment, exchange tissue with the white knots. Ceiling shells release dustlike bodies into the current, and these settle inside wounded cloaks. Grazers leave strips of their feeding fans in certain wall grooves; months later, new cutmouth eggs appear nearby.

Parent and prey cannot always be distinguished.

The cistern’s life may be a single lineage broken into temporary shapes. It feeds upon itself, sorts itself through screens, shelters within itself, and carries its substance from wall to wall. Predation moves material from a swift form into a patient one. Scavenging returns it to the film. Death is less an ending than a passage through another mouth.

If there is one inhabitant, it has no central body.

Its memory, if memory is the word, lies in repeated routes and inherited appetites. It knows the temperature of particular bricks. It knows which urns collect the richest current and which grates lead to cold chambers. It knows the long interval between the deep notes of the mountain. No individual knows these things. Individuals are replaced. The knowledge persists in where eggs are fixed, where shells thicken, where grazers turn, where cloaks allow themselves to sink.

The architecture gives this distributed creature its enduring anatomy.

The central shaft is its open throat. The screens are its sieves. The galleries are loops of intestine through which water takes several days to pass. Copper cramps and iron brick provide the mineral differences from which its smallest cells draw nourishment. The porous jars damp sound as flesh dampens shock. The nested walls function like successive shells.

Even the sediment is alive in this broader sense. Buried material is worked, dissolved, swallowed, and lifted again. A copper scale fallen from the ceiling may enter the film, pass into a grazer, be taken by a shell-borer, settle in a cloak’s branching thread, and finally return to the wall in the shell of a ceiling animal. Such a circuit may require a thousand years.

The oldest living thing is said to occupy the lower portion of the central shaft.

Nothing marks its boundary. There is only a region where the clear water becomes slightly denser and the orbiting schools refuse to descend. Long threads rise from below, thinner than hair and many yards in length. They do not wave with the current. They maintain a broad spiral corresponding to the arrangement of the worn pillars.

At intervals, a thread touches one of the circling cutmouths.

The swimmer stops. Its fins open. For a moment it hangs as if fixed to an invisible surface. Then it either joins the descending spiral or returns to its school.

During the cistern’s deep contraction note, all the threads draw taut.

The response passes outward. Cloaks fold. Jar-hunters close. Grazers flatten against the masonry. Ceiling shells seal their feeding pleats. The cutmouth schools settle into the coffers.

For several minutes there are no separate movements anywhere.

In that stillness the cistern does not seem full of animals. It seems occupied by one immense, interrupted animal whose soft parts have been distributed through every gallery and whose hard parts were built for it before it was born.

Then the note fades.

The threads loosen. The cloaks reopen. Mouths begin their patient work. The little swimmers rise from the masonry and gather again around the shaft, pale as filings around a hidden magnet.

The inhabitant resumes moving through its many bodies, never seeing the sun, never breathing, never departing, and perhaps never having understood itself as anything smaller than the warm dark sea that contains it.
