At first the inhabitants seem less like a population than like errors in the water.

A clear disc closes itself and is gone. A jointed speck moves against the grit. Something that appeared to be a chip of wall turns over, exposing a soft underside crowded with feet. Even after hours of watching, the eye continues to mistake animals for debris and debris for animals. This is not only camouflage. The same currents have shaped both. Stone and shell are planed to the same curves, polished to the same pallor, sorted into the same hollows. Whatever lives here has entered the ruin’s grammar.

The smallest swimmers are almost without bodies. They are loops, combs, veils, strings of connected bladders. Most are transparent enough that only their feeding structures can be seen: black grains moving through a clear canal, a wheel of hooks opening and shutting, a haze of mineral needles suspended inside living tissue. They do not propel themselves continuously. They wait folded in the lee of a ridge, then spread when a useful current arrives. Some become sails; some become funnels. A few turn their entire bodies inside out to feed, exposing a sticky inner surface until it is dusted with eggs, shell fragments, and edible salts.

Many are not single animals for long.

Beadlike drifters join when the water grows violent, fastening mouth to tail until they form cords several meters long. The cord bends around pillars without breaking. Each bead feeds for itself, but impulses pass through the chain, so that danger makes the whole assembly contract at once. In quiet water the beads release one another and scatter. Days later they may join different chains. There are no permanent colonies, only temporary bodies recruited by weather.

Among them move the clickers: pin-sized creatures enclosed in paired shells. They feed by striking their shell edges against the pavement, dislodging particles with hundreds of rapid impacts. A large gathering sounds like porcelain cooling. Their shells are not symmetrical. One side is flat and densely ridged; the other swells into a hollow cup. When currents strengthen, they flip flat-side down and seal themselves to the floor. When caught loose, they tumble cup-side outward, allowing the water to sort them into sheltered courts.

They hatch in enormous numbers after a chamber breach. For several hours, a fresh sediment cloud may glitter with their larvae. Almost all are eaten before acquiring shells. Those that survive remember, in some chemical manner, the mineral composition of the chamber from which they emerged. At maturity they try to return to that composition, though by then the source may have been dispersed across kilometers. Their migrations lead them into old fractures, abandoned sockets, and the digestive cavities of animals that mimic the taste of broken stone.

The wire animals hunt them.

These are living filaments, sometimes no thicker than hair and sometimes long enough to cross an avenue. Their heads are indistinguishable from their tails until feeding begins. Then one end opens into a narrow slit and passes over prey like a sleeve. They travel through pores too small for any rigid skeleton, pulling the rest of the body after them in slow waves. Most carry loops of dense mineral inside themselves. By shifting these weights from segment to segment, they can anchor one portion of the body while the rest streams in the current.

A wire animal may spend years threaded through the same district. It emerges from one hole, crosses open water, and enters another, seeming to stitch the pavement. Its full extent is rarely visible. Individuals are known by the patterns of old injuries along their bodies: clouded sections, knots of scar tissue, places where swallowed grit has become permanently enclosed. When a wall collapses, dozens may be exposed at once, whipping soundlessly in the silt like the roots of a plant that never existed.

The six-limbed crawlers are easier to recognize as animals, though only when they move.

Their upper shells grow in the colors and textures of the surface beneath them. A crawler that lives on smooth plaza scales becomes smooth and faintly blue. One from a rusted fracture develops branching orange lines. Those inhabiting relief-covered chambers raise false cups and spirals across their backs. The likeness is produced slowly. They scrape the stone, pass its minerals through chambers in the gut, and deposit them onto the shell in thin translucent layers. An old individual is therefore a record of its route. Its newest growth matches its present dwelling; beneath it lie buried patterns from every district it has crossed.

They walk on six long legs arranged around the body rather than beneath it. Each leg ends in several hooked digits and one blunt sensory pad. The hooks hold. The pad tastes pressure, salt, vibration, and the faint emissions of stressed secretion-stone. Crawlers often pause with all six pads pressed to the floor. In that posture they can detect a coming structural failure long before any visible crack appears.

Their response is not panic. First they lift the youngest. Then they recover their eggs.

Crawler eggs are sealed into architectural sockets with a paste that hardens almost as densely as the plateau itself. A clutch may remain dormant for decades. It hatches only after a particular sequence of vibrations: low strain in the surrounding wall, a sharp fracture, then prolonged turbulence. Thus collapse is their season. When a dome fails, its inner surfaces may suddenly release thousands of juveniles, each small enough to ride the debris cloud. They spread through the newly opened district before larger feeders arrive.

Adults do not care for them. They do, however, move unhatched clutches away from impending collapse. Several crawlers may cooperate, cutting a socket from a wall and passing the stone capsule from one set of legs to the next. Their cooperation ends as soon as the capsule is secure. They have no stable groups and seem unable to recognize one another except by hunger, reproductive condition, and the mineral odor carried on the shell.

Yet their abandoned egg repairs outlast them. Whole walls are held together by generation upon generation of filled sockets. Some arches contain so many dormant clutches that the distinction between nursery and masonry has disappeared.

Deeper in the courtyards hover the plated mouths.

Their true bodies are pale, muscular mantles shaped like flattened bells. The broad end bears a circular jaw; the narrow end divides into steering ribbons. They hang head-down over shafts, opening and closing with the pulse of vertical water. Anything small enough to enter the jaw is tested by an inner ring of soft fingers. Flesh is swallowed. Useful shell is retained. Stone is usually expelled, though certain luminous fragments are kept for reasons that do not appear nutritional.

Their armor is borrowed. Loose scales, crawler shells, sections of tube, fitted paving chips, even the mineralized remains of their own kind are tucked into folds on the mantle. Tiny gripping organs hold each plate separately. As the animal grows it continually replaces them, seeking pieces of the correct curvature. No two suits are alike. Old individuals may carry fragments from districts far beyond their present shaft, making them moving collections of vanished surfaces.

The plates have several uses. They break the outline of the soft body. They shield it from grit. More importantly, they can be released in patterns.

A mild disturbance causes one or two pieces to fall. Pursuers often follow them, mistaking the descending vibration for fleeing prey. A stronger threat makes the animal discard an entire side, spinning away under the unequal drag. In extremity it releases everything. The sudden clatter overwhelms pressure-sensitive hunters while the naked mantle contracts into a narrow ribbon and is drawn into whatever opening is nearest.

Afterward it is helplessly visible. Its white body carries rows of dark attachment marks where the plates were held. It spends the following days close to the floor, testing fragments one by one. Sometimes several unarmored mouths gather at a fresh collapse. They do not fight. They circle the debris, each waiting for the current to present a suitable piece. Their patience gives the gathering a ceremonial appearance, but there is no order to it beyond weight and flow.

The passage-makers live inside the architecture.

Their tubes can be mistaken for plumbing until one is cut open. The animal itself is a soft, segmented cord lining the entire passage it has excavated. At the growing end, it secretes acids and binds the dissolved mineral into its own walls. At the older end, it closes sections behind itself. It therefore advances by destroying a tunnel ahead and becoming a tunnel behind.

Water passes through pores in the lining. Nutrients are caught by combs too fine to see. Reproduction occurs when two passages intersect. The animals open temporary chambers, exchange packets of tissue, and seal the junction again. Their young begin as knots in the tunnel wall. Eventually each knot dissolves an exit of its own and departs in a new direction.

A large passage-maker may ramify through an entire building. Whether its branches remain one animal is uncertain. Injury causes distant portions to contract simultaneously, but after prolonged separation each portion continues to grow. Old networks contain dead passages, living passages, and regions occupied by descendants feeding through the hardened body of an ancestor.

These animals alter the plateau more deeply than the crawlers. Their tunnels weaken pillars, drain brine pools, join sealed chambers, and redirect currents. They also brace what they hollow. The inner lining is flexible and exceptionally strong. A structure riddled with living passages may survive after most of its original substance has dissolved. It becomes a mineral lattice held together by soft bodies under tension.

When such a building finally fails, its inhabitants are torn into the open water. Lengths of living tunnel writhe among the fragments, closing their wounds. Scavengers descend on them. Enough pieces usually survive to seed every nearby crack.

There are also the hingemakers.

They begin as flat larvae that settle along the matching edges of broken slabs. Individuals on opposite sides send out calcareous threads until they touch. Then they fuse. The resulting colony contracts in response to current, drawing the slabs together; relaxes, and lets them part. At first this movement merely pumps food-bearing water through the colony. Over years the slabs become a working pair of jaws, opening and closing across a fracture.

Some ruined courts contain hundreds of these living hinges. Their walls pulse. Pavement plates lift slightly and settle again. Arch fragments knock together with slow regularity. The colonies have no central body, but neighboring hinges entrain one another through vibration. An entire district may begin moving to a shared rhythm.

The rhythm ends when the colonies reproduce. They release their larvae, detach from the slabs, and disintegrate. The joined fragments separate. A dome that seemed restored can come apart in a single tide, not because the creatures failed but because they completed their lives.

Small fauna know the hingemakers’ cycles. Grazers gather in the pulsing cracks. Predators wait for the reproductive collapse. Crawlers use recently abandoned hinge tissue to seal eggs. The temporary repair of one species becomes the feeding event of another and then the nursery wall of a third.

Above these lives pass the animals that cannot be held in a single view.

They are detected first as changes in behavior. Clickers seal themselves down. Chains divide into solitary beads. Crawlers lower their bodies and align their shells with the current. The plated mouths drop everything they carry. Sometimes even the passage-makers stop pumping, and the water emerging from their pores goes still.

Then a line of pits closes across the pavement.

The pits are not architectural. They are the buried sensory organs of a long-bodied hunter lying beneath the silt, or perhaps only the trailing part of one. Each pit contains a membrane responsive to pressure at a distance. They close in sequence as the animal turns its attention overhead.

A shadow crosses the blue.

The hunter’s body is colorless and only slightly denser than the water. Its outer tissue bends weak light around itself, not enough to make it invisible but enough to erase clear edges. It may have fins; it may propel itself by passing muscular waves down a body too long to see. Observers agree only on the wake. It arrives late, rolling over the structures with enough force to tear animals from exposed walls.

These high hunters rarely descend to strike. They feed by making the plateau feed them. One circles above a current junction until its wake dislodges swarms of smaller life. Another lowers a thin appendage into a shaft and waits for the upwelling to pin prey against it. Some release pulses that resemble the first tremors of collapse. Dormant eggs hatch. Shelter-seeking creatures abandon their holes. The hunter passes through the resulting confusion.

Their young may be the pale loops occasionally found tied around the upper fins of towers. The loops have no mouths. They absorb dissolved tissue through the skin and tighten whenever the structure vibrates. As they grow, they move from fin to fin, always choosing higher ground. Eventually one is found empty, its translucent skin split along its entire length. No one sees what left it.

At the rim live forms adapted to falling.

They cling beneath the polished lip where the plateau pours into the trench. Their bodies are asymmetric fans with grasping limbs on one side and trailing nets on the other. They catch whatever the current carries over the edge. When a large fragment falls, they release their grip and descend with it.

For days they may accompany the fragment into darkness, grazing on organisms shocked loose from its interior. Before the stone sinks beyond survivable pressure, the fans inflate chambers in their bodies and rise. Their ascent can take much longer than the descent. Some return carrying the chemical trace of waters no other rim-dweller enters.

Those that misjudge the depth do not return alive. Occasionally their inflated corpses rise months later, distended and altered, filled with small blind scavengers. The rim fauna gather around them but do not feed until the body touches made ground. Contact with the plateau appears to transform carrion into permissible food.

The pale hollow forms that ascend from deeper architecture bring other inhabitants.

Inside them are animals with no obvious adaptation to open water: soft branching things that collapse when unsupported, knots of mouths sharing a common stomach, shelled forms that crawl only along ceilings. They have lived in sealed lower chambers where buoyancy, pressure, and current follow different rules. Most die soon after reaching the high country. For a brief period they attract intense attention.

Some, however, find a corresponding cavity and disappear into it. Years later the local life may change. Clickers develop unfamiliar shell ridges. Passage-makers begin secreting darker walls. Crawlers lay eggs in arrangements not previously seen. Whether the deep animals survive, reproduce with the plateau fauna, infect them, or merely alter the available salts cannot be determined.

Nothing here possesses eyes in the ordinary sense. There is too little reliable light and too much suspended grit. Bodies read the world through pressure, taste, and transmitted strain. A crawler knows the district beneath its feet more clearly than the water a hand’s breadth above it. A plated mouth senses the approach of a falling pebble across an entire courtyard. Wire animals recognize one another through tiny electrical changes in wet stone. Even the great hunters appear to navigate by the architecture’s response to their own passing.

The blue afterglow is therefore an anomaly to them.

When a wall breaks and shines, inhabitants arrive that have no reason to seek light. Drifters turn toward it despite lacking visible photoreceptors. Crawlers leave feeding grounds and stand motionless on the luminous surface. Plated mouths press retained fragments against their mantles. Wire animals extend from their holes and form loose coils in the illuminated water.

They do not eat there. They do not mate or lay eggs. Predators sometimes pass among them without striking.

For the few hours the glow lasts, distinctions between species become less decisive. Animals that normally flee one another hold position together, all oriented toward the opened stone. The gathering is quiet. Even the clickers cease striking.

One possibility is that every living thing on the plateau descends, however remotely, from organisms that inhabited the made structure while it still grew. The light may once have accompanied the deposition of new walls, the opening of chambers, or the circulation of fluids through a larger living whole. What now appears to be irrational attraction may be an inherited response to creation.

Another possibility is less genealogical. The animals may not be descendants of the builders but portions of their continuation.

The crawlers add mineral sheets to their shells by the same process found in old walls. Passage-makers dissolve and redeposit the architecture. Hingemakers assemble broken surfaces into moving structures. Plated mouths collect and carry its scales. Even the drifting chains imitate, for a few hours, the joining of separate chambers into one responsive body. None of them plans. None remembers. Yet together they perform growth, circulation, repair, and shedding.

If the plateau was once an organism, these may be its freed functions, surviving after the unity that contained them has vanished.

If it was a city, they have become citizens without society: each species preserving some fragment of an old civic act as appetite or reflex.

If it was both, the distinction is no longer recoverable.

There may also be something alive beneath them all.

At long intervals, after no obvious tidal change, animals abandon several districts at once. The departure begins with the wire forms withdrawing into deeper holes. Crawlers unseal viable eggs and carry them away. Plated mouths climb out of shafts. Drifters rise and permit the current to take them. Last of all, the passage-makers close their pores.

The emptied district waits.

A pressure moves beneath the floor—not a single blow, but a long displacement passing from vault to vault. Blind stairways exhale. Silt lifts from joints in parallel lines. The pavement flexes too slowly to be seen and too widely to be attributed to collapse. Sometimes a low knock travels upward from depths where falling debris should never return.

Then the openings reverse direction, the district floods with bitter water, and life comes back.

No body has been observed. The buried movement may be brine shifting through ancient chambers, or a chain of structural failures, or the plateau settling under its own weight. But the inhabitants respond to it as they respond to a creature: early, collectively, and with the precise fear reserved for an old predator.

Perhaps they know only that the floor can wake.

Perhaps, in their pressure-sense, the distinction between architecture and animal was never present. To them the plateau is not a place occupied by life. Its walls strain, its cavities breathe, its fragments migrate, its wounds shine, and its smaller bodies feed and reproduce across its skin. Everything moves on a different timescale, but everything moves.

The animals are what moves quickly enough to be noticed.
