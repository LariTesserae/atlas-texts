At first, the inhabitants are difficult to separate from the work.

A figure on a gallery might be mistaken for a coat hung to dry until it leans out and gives a whistle to someone below. Hands appear between leaves to turn trays, test fibers, clear a gutter. Bare calves pass behind the spokes of a flywheel. Faces show briefly in upper windows, green-lit by the canopy, then disappear into steam. The town seems to operate by itself, attended only at its edges by partial people: an arm feeding bark into a cutter, two feet braced on a sluice gate, a voice somewhere inside a hedge counting the strokes of a pump.

Most who live there are human, though outsiders sometimes speak of them as if the moisture had made them a separate kind. It has not, at least not in any simple way. They are short and tall, spare and heavy, dark-skinned and light, descended from canal diggers, mill mechanics, marsh cultivators, displaced river families, traders who stopped for a season and remained for generations. What they share is less an appearance than a set of practiced accommodations.

They move with a particular care. Not timidity: precision. A resident rarely places a foot without knowing whether the surface is brick, root, plank, grating, or the green skin of water. Children learn the difference before they learn the names of all the streets. They run confidently along narrow coping stones, pause without being told beneath dripping valve joints, and know which rails are hot, which tanks breathe, which glossy leaves leave a rash, and which apparently solid patches of duckweed conceal a channel deep enough to swallow them.

Their feet are often bare indoors and shod outside in sandals or soft boots made to drain quickly. Heavy waterproof clothing is considered useless except in violent weather; it traps heat and sweat and eventually admits water anyway. Most people wear loose trousers or wrapped skirts shortened above the ankle, shirts with vented backs, aprons stiffened by oil, and broad shoulder capes woven from waxed fiber. Sleeves are tied close around machinery. Hair is cropped, braided tightly, or wrapped in cloth, not as a uniform custom but because loose hair near belts and rollers is an old and well-remembered danger.

Everything they carry has a loop, hook, or lanyard. Knives, keys, chalk, spectacles, cups, purses, small ledgers: none are trusted to a pocket alone. Papers are kept between thin boards rubbed with wax. Matches ride in stoppered tubes. A townsperson setting down a tool unconsciously chooses the highest nearby surface.

Their hands show the place most clearly. Fingernails are stained by dyes, bark, resin, iron, or tannin. Palms are crossed by pale cuts from reed and cane. Older mechanics have blackened crescents permanently embedded around the nails and smooth patches on their forearms where years of reaching past turning belts have worn away the hair. Workers in the retting yards smell faintly sour even after washing. Press operators carry the warm odor of seed oil. Those from the dye houses may have blue or brown fingers for half their lives.

Yet the town is not populated only by laborers at machines. There are channel wardens, roof gardeners, tree surgeons, furnace tenders, fish keepers, rope inspectors, mold scrapers, boiler scribes, market weighers, bridge carpenters, midwives, teachers, cooks, and people whose whole profession consists of knowing where water ought to go.

The channel wardens are among the most respected. They walk the settlement before dawn with hooked poles and shallow baskets, clearing grates and reading currents by the drift of husks, petals, and oil sheen. They know every culvert by sound. A hollow sucking beneath a lane tells them that a passage is narrowing; an unfamiliar stillness may bring three wardens running faster than an alarm bell. Their authority crosses property lines. They may enter a yard, cut a vine, break open a blocked drain, or order a workshop shut until its outflow is cleared. No owner likes the interruption, but no one disputes the necessity for long.

Tree workers possess similar freedoms. They climb without spurs, using rope slings that do not wound bark, and carry curved knives, folding saws, grafting wax, and packets of mineral paste. They are part arborist, builder, and surveyor. A tree may shade one household, hold up the roof of another, brace a warehouse wall, and root beneath a communal lane. Its pruning therefore requires negotiation among people who may otherwise have nothing to do with one another. The tree worker listens to all of them, examines the grain, the lean, the fungal marks, the embedded iron, and then usually removes less than anyone expected.

Mechanics are recognizable by the way they listen. Conversation does not stop their attention from moving through the layered noise. A slight change in a bearing’s whine turns a head. A mistimed knock draws the eyes toward a wall as if someone had spoken a name. The best mechanics can cross a factory floor and identify three developing faults without touching a machine. Many are older, because this knowledge takes decades and because every machine in town has been altered so often that no original plan is reliable. Their apprentices draw diagrams, but the masters add qualifications in the margins: *vibrates in dry months; second valve sticks after heavy rain; east bearing runs hot when the fig is fruiting.*

People work in small crews whose members often belong to different households. Kinship matters, but so does dependency. The person who keeps your pump alive during flood season may be more immediately important than a cousin across town. Long partnerships form around machines, roofs, channels, and trees. These relationships have their own obligations: meals carried to a night repair, children watched during a boiler failure, wages pooled when a press stands idle.

Ownership is correspondingly tangled. A family may own a room but not the gallery outside it, a roof but not the tree supporting its western edge, a press but not the shaft that drives it, or a drying rack whose lower posts stand in communal water. Such arrangements are recorded in ledgers kept above the flood line, but just as often they are preserved in memory. Elders recite them with reference to events rather than dates: the year the south sluice broke, the season of white fungus, the flood that reached the third stair, the summer the turbine stopped and the old steam plant ran for eleven days.

Arguments are common, prolonged, and rarely theatrical. Voices need not be raised; shouting wastes itself against leaves and machinery. Disputes are conducted close at hand, under awnings or around oilcloth-covered tables, with repeated measurements and the testimony of those who remember former watercourses. The gravest accusations concern carelessness with shared systems: allowing a roof channel to clog, dumping hot effluent into a fish run, cutting a stabilizing root, concealing rot in a footbridge, or diverting power from a pump during high water. Theft is condemned. Neglect is regarded with something closer to horror.

Their houses occupy whatever space industry has left: upper floors of warehouses, rooms behind shops, long narrow dwellings over channels, clusters of chambers sharing a gallery and kitchen. The distinction between domestic and industrial architecture is slight. A bedroom wall may be warm from a steam pipe. A kitchen window may open over a settling yard. Cradles are slung from hooks rather than set on floors. Cupboards stand on brick plinths. Bedding is aired whenever sunlight penetrates the canopy, and a sudden bright interval fills every railing with blankets, shirts, mats, and opened books.

Inside, homes are cleaner and more colorful than the lanes suggest. Walls are limewashed in pale yellow, dull pink, blue-gray, or green, partly for brightness and partly to reveal mildew. Floors are brick or slatted wood, scrubbed with ash soap and aromatic leaves. Furniture is light enough to lift quickly, except for old chests set permanently on masonry bases. Nearly every household keeps a flood shelf near the ceiling holding documents, medicine, lamp oil, dried food, and one or two objects of no practical value that would nevertheless be rescued first.

Cooking favors things that tolerate humidity and ingredients produced nearby. People eat steamed grain cakes, pounded roots, pickled stems, greens simmered with seed paste, sour broths, smoked channel fish, mushrooms grown on spent wood, and flat breads cooked on iron plates. Press cakes and husks feed small household fermentations. Oils vary from street to street and are discussed with the seriousness other places reserve for wine. Some are grassy and bright, others dark, nutty, bitter, medicinal, or scented by the wood vats in which they settled.

Meals are strongly flavored because the air dulls subtler aromas. Sourness is prized. So are smoke, ginger, pepper, charred leaf, fermented bean, and sharp herbs. At the market, customers dab oil on the inside of the wrist and smell it as it warms. Children are sent with bottles to buy exactly enough for a day or two; large stores turn rancid too easily.

Water itself is divided into many categories. There is drinking water, washing water, boiler water, channel water, cooling water, flood water, retting water, and water suitable only for carrying waste toward the outer ponds. Confusing one with another is a mark of dangerous ignorance. Drinking water is collected from selected roofs during clean rain, then filtered through charcoal and ceramic jars. Children are taught never to drink merely because water looks clear.

They bathe often but briefly, using ladles rather than deep tubs. The constant damp does not make cleanliness effortless. Skin infections, mold, fevers, infected cuts, and lung ailments are familiar enemies. The town’s healers are practical and unsentimental. They use boiled instruments, bitter washes, plant-derived antiseptics, steam inhalations, splints of laminated cane, and medicines purchased from beyond the marsh. Knowledge of useful plants is extensive, but no one who tends factory injuries believes every leaf is a cure.

The elderly often have damaged hearing. Years among belts, hammers, valves, and pumps leave many reading mouths and gestures more readily than speech. As a result, the hand signs of the factory have entered ordinary life. People can conduct simple conversations across a market aisle or through a noisy rainstorm: *finished, danger, wait, food, where, child, upstream, downstream, come home.* Some signs differ by mill. Others are understood everywhere.

Children learn them in school alongside letters, figures, water levels, basic mechanics, and the names of local plants. Schools occupy upper rooms with broad shutters. Lessons pause when a nearby shift bell rings or a barge passes beneath the floor. Pupils keep rainfall charts and practice tying rescue lines. They are taught to swim, though not always in open channels; training pools are fenced off from machinery and current. Their copybooks contain sketches of leaf veins beside diagrams of pumps.

Their games imitate adult systems. They build miniature sluices in runoff, float seed-pod barges, erect sheds from broken tile, and argue over drainage rights. They collect discarded washers and bottle caps as counters. A common chasing game requires moving from dry stone to dry stone without touching “flood,” though in wet months the imaginary flood and the real one become difficult to distinguish.

Adolescents seek the few open places. They gather on the upper water tower landing, on disused crane platforms, or along drying racks after the day’s material has been carried in. Privacy is found vertically. Courtships unfold on roofs beneath dripping leaves, in borrowed skiffs at the marsh edge, or during long maintenance tasks where two people can speak without being overheard. Gifts are small and durable: a carved shuttle, a brass hook, a comb of polished wood, a length of exceptionally soft cloth, a plant cutting from a family courtyard.

Marriage, where it is practiced, is less a joining of property than a renegotiation of space and obligations. Two people may belong to different work crews, sleep in one household, eat in another, and keep tools in a third. Extended families spread through connected upper rooms. Children circulate among grandparents, aunts, unrelated neighbors, and shift-mates. Parenthood is communal not from idealism but because the bells divide the day irregularly and machinery cannot be left merely because a child has woken.

Death is treated with the same concern for space, water, and useful matter. Burial within the town is impossible. Some families use raised cemeteries on the driest ridges beyond the marsh; others cremate in a high-draft furnace maintained apart from the industrial boilers. Memorials are modest and often functional. A person’s name may be cut into a bridge rail they repaired, cast into the housing of a replacement pump, or attached to a newly planted tree. The dead persist in instructions: a chalk mark copied each year, a valve sequence, a grafting method, a warning not to load the western gallery during prolonged rain.

There are ceremonies, though few require silence. At the beginning of the wettest season, channels are cleared in a town-wide labor lasting two days. Work crews compete in speed and thoroughness; kitchens send food out on raised trays; children follow the wardens collecting bright scraps from the grates. When the water is moving cleanly, the chimney-yard bell is struck once for each main outflow. People stand listening not only to the bell but to the answering rush beneath the streets.

Another observance marks the annual stopping of the central shafts. For several hours the largest mills are shut down together. In the sudden absence of their sound, inhabitants appear unsettled. Voices become too distinct. Drips seem enormous. Birds from the outer marsh can be heard among the roofs. Maintenance crews swarm over the silent machinery, oiling, aligning, stitching belts, tightening mounts, while everyone else visits, trades, eats, or sleeps. When the shafts start again, the first full revolution is greeted with relief.

Outsiders often imagine the people worship the vegetation. They do not, exactly. They respect it, bargain with it, curse it, harvest it, and know that neglecting it invites structural failure. A tree that holds up a roof is not sacred in the manner of a shrine, but neither is it merely lumber. It is closer to an old, difficult member of a household: demanding, useful, dangerous when ill, and entangled in every decision.

Some residents do keep private devotions. Knotted fibers are tied above doors. Small dishes of oil are left on high ledges. Certain workshops paint eyes on pump housings or press frames, less from formal belief than from the intuition that anything capable of maiming generations of workers deserves acknowledgment. Before entering a tank for cleaning, even skeptics touch the rim twice. No one jokes while the ropes are being tested.

The town’s animals live by similar accommodations. Narrow silver fish patrol the main channels and gather wherever warm water enters. Lizards haunt boiler walls. Herons learn shift times and arrive when screens are lifted from the settling basins. Bats roost in the abandoned upper flue of the gardened smokestack, tolerated because they reduce moths. There are a few cats, mostly on upper floors, long-bodied and careful, passed between granaries when rodents appear. Dogs are uncommon; there is too little open ground and too much machinery to make their lives easy.

Not everyone stays. Young people sometimes leave for dry cities, attracted by straight streets, quiet rooms, clean foundations, and work that does not depend on the weather. Some return after a year unable to sleep without the two-beat pumps. Others come back with new bearings, electrical training, medical knowledge, legal documents, or ideas for machines that do not require constant repair. Such ideas are welcomed cautiously. Anything installed here must survive damp, root pressure, unreliable current, silt, heat, insects, and the ingenuity of whoever will repair it twenty years after its manufacturer is gone.

Those who remain are not bound by nostalgia. They complain continually. The walls sweat. Cloth mildews overnight. Nothing dries when needed. Metal fails from the inside. A dropped object may vanish into black water. Every improvement uncovers an older problem beneath it. Yet they distrust the fantasy of clearing the plants, draining the marsh, straightening the channels, and rebuilding from bare ground. They know what holds the place together, and it is not only brick or timber. The living roots slow the soil. The marsh takes floodwater. The waste from one process fires or feeds another. The shade keeps tanks from overheating. The channels carry both power and danger. Remove one inconvenience completely and three necessities may go with it.

So they practice continuity rather than preservation. They do not keep things as they were. They keep them working.

At dusk, the inhabitants become more visible. Lamps come on high above the wet lanes, reflected in channels and in the glossy leaves. Workers emerge from behind screens carrying lunch tins, tools, folded aprons. Market sellers cork their bottles and hoist baskets into roof cages. Children are called down from galleries. Somewhere a mechanic remains crouched beside an open housing, listening to a bearing turn by hand.

The shift bell sounds. For a moment, people pause—not all together, but in a stagger that passes through the town. Then the lanes fill with movement: neighbors crossing bridges, crews changing places, food traveling upward, waste traveling out, water traveling everywhere.

Seen then, the population is neither hidden by the settlement nor distinct from it. They are the part that remembers. The trees grow, the brick settles, the iron corrodes, the channels wander, and the machines wear themselves toward silence. The inhabitants carry forward the accumulated corrections: where to cut, where to brace, when to open, what sound means danger, how high the last flood rose, which root must not be harmed.

Without them, the place would not immediately die. It would continue lushly. Water would climb the lower rooms; belts would slacken; tanks would bloom; roots would widen old cracks; roofs would fold beneath their gardens. Within a few seasons the town would become almost indistinguishable from the marsh.

What lives there is what prevents that indistinguishability—not by conquering the growth, but by making daily, exacting terms with it.
