Nothing in the chamber is alive in the manner of surface life. Nothing there spends itself.

The thickets are the largest inhabitants, though it is difficult to regard them as plants once they have been watched closely. Their black-centered stalks have the gloss and segmentation of limbs. Their fan leaves do not seek light; they stand at differing angles as if attending to separate sounds within the rock. Along the membranes run branching channels of green fire, carrying resinous oil in place of sap. The oil is their blood, solvent, food store, and perhaps their means of memory. Exposed, it recoils into them. A severed leaf does not bleed so much as refuse the wound.

Each thicket is a single colony. What appear to be separate stalks share root-knuckles beneath the sulfur clay, clasping the same warm nodules and drawing breath from the same vent. The gardens do not compete or mingle. Their roots approach the borders of neighboring islands and turn aside, leaving deliberate strips of bare stone between them. Thus every thicket possesses a precise circumference, as though planted inside an unseen vessel.

They feed upon the chamber’s exhalation: sulfur, hot metallic vapors, and gases with no surface names. Their substance is built without rain. They are less like vegetation than slow chemical flames given durable anatomy.

The little plated grazers live closest to them. Their six legs end in soft, many-ridged pads that grip the upright leaves without puncturing them. Ivory shields overlap across their backs, each plate carrying fine green stains where the leaf oil has entered and hardened in the pores. They scrape the membranes with crescent mouthparts, raising no visible wound. When disturbed, they flatten themselves so completely that they resemble natural deposits on the plant.

They always seem hungry. They never consume anything.

Under the same leaves hang the raspers, long creatures shaped like dry pods. Their heads are little more than two black pits and a ring of hooks. The hind limbs are extravagantly large, combed with mineral teeth. Lift, close, lift, close: each stroke produces one part of the chamber’s unending chord. Their sound may be courtship, warning, territorial proclamation, or merely the movement by which their bodies remain in time. No pairing has ever been observed. No young appear. Nevertheless, some are small, some mature, and some carry what look like sealed egg chambers beneath their abdomens.

The root-burrowers are blind and warm. They inhabit the hollow joints where plant and mineral meet, striking the interior walls with blunt armored brows. Their knocks pass through the interconnected roots farther than sound should travel. A colony on one side of a garden answers another on the opposite side in exact intervals. If removed, a burrower curls into a tight fist and gives off the smell of heated coins. Its pale hide darkens rapidly in open air. Then attention strays, and it is once more inside the root, sounding its appointed note.

Above them travel the triangular fliers. They have no flexible wings. Each bears three or four rigid vanes of transparent mineral tissue, hinged close to a narrow body. Flight should be impossible for them: the vanes scarcely beat, and their weight is considerable. Yet they pass from stalk to stalk in straight, unwavering lines, as though sliding along tensions stretched through the chamber.

They are the most visibly watchful creatures there. On landing, the whole body inclines toward movement. A hand raised at a distance will cause several to turn simultaneously. Touch one, and its vanes strike together with the brittle clack of thin plates. The resulting alarm sends every flier upward, revealing that their ordered routes are not mindless. They can fear. They can choose to flee.

But the chamber does not preserve their choice.

Among these obvious animals are smaller ones easily mistaken for mineral imperfections. Threadlike things coil in the seams of glass. Clear mites congregate around fresh incisions and appear to lick the displaced dust. Pale, soft-bodied pilgrims travel ceaselessly toward the black bowl, each carrying a hard oval scale upon its back. At the rim they extend clustered mouthparts into the ascending silver substance.

Afterward they depart neither swollen nor altered. Their procession resembles feeding, but it may be maintenance. They may be tending the bowl, measuring it, worshiping it, or being measured by it.

No predator can be confidently identified. Certain hooked shapes lie concealed between stalk joints, and sometimes a grazer recoils before one; but no successful strike reaches completion. Death can occur only through intrusion. Within the chamber’s preferred arrangement, hunger exists without starvation, alarm without escape, pursuit without capture. The ecology has all the tensions of life and none of their conclusions.

Then there are the makers.

There were at least five, if each ceiling print belonged to a different hand. They were human or near enough that the distinction would initially have seemed impolite. They wore bark-soled sandals, braided plant fiber, scraped stone, pierced hide or membrane with bone awls, and harvested the jawplates of grazers for hooks. Their longest fingers were unusually slender, perhaps an individual peculiarity, perhaps a trait shared by a people adapted to reaching between the resinous stalks without touching them.

They did not use fire. They learned to soften and seal with the garden’s warm oil. They roofed their shelters against nothing visible, for there is no rain, wind, or cold. Perhaps the roofs kept out falling fliers. Perhaps privacy retained meaning even where change did not.

The sleeping hollow suggests at least one of them slept. The wing-casings lining it suggest patience: enough casings were gathered to make a bed, though no creature in the chamber is ever seen to molt. The worn tools imply repeated use, although their edges are always newly made. These contradictions gather around the makers more densely than around any animal. They seem to have inhabited the single moment long enough to develop a culture inside it.

Their recurring sign—the open oval pierced by a vertical stroke—may depict the chamber and its central pillar. It may mean opening, division, breath, captivity, or “here.” Its placement is exacting. Glass, clay, root, shell: never the same substance twice. The signs may therefore name not places but categories, a catalog of the materials the garden permits. Or each inscription may be an attempt to prove that a previous inscription had truly been made.

The entrance-less cairn is their most deliberate work. It is too carefully built to be refuse and too sealed to be a dwelling. It may contain a body, though a body here would not remain dead. It may surround an object that must never be directly observed during the chamber’s act of restoration. It may be a fourth shelter, built not for a person but for an absence.

The five people themselves are no longer present.

That fact makes them unique.

Everything else returns: tool, leaf, carcass, wall, frightened animal. If the makers departed through the upper crack, the chamber was unable—or unwilling—to call them back. Perhaps conscious beings are not part of its preferred condition. Perhaps the handprints and tools are not remnants of residents at all, but features generated with the chamber, memories of inhabitants who never existed.

There is another possibility. Near the bowl, speech repeats beneath itself and motion becomes layered. The makers may still be there, held too close to the pillar to be perceived in ordinary sequence. The sandal may wait beside a foot that occupies the same instant from another direction. The shelters may appear empty because their occupants are sleeping between one recurrence of the chamber’s chord and the next. The five handprints may be less a memorial than five people pressing continually upward, their hands visible where the rest of them is not.

Yet the deepest inhabitant is neither plant, animal, nor absent maker.

It is the arrangement itself.

It occupies every creature without replacing its responsiveness. It knows the exact angle of each wing, the tension of every cord, the number of dust grains beside each cut. It does not prevent interference; prevention would require reaction. Instead it asserts one version of the chamber more strongly than any event occurring within it. When observation loosens, that version resumes.

Whether this inhabitant is the silver fluid, the pillar, the buried seed-shaped chamber, or the first instant of its creation cannot be separated. The bowl behaves like an organ. The gas vents resemble breath. The thickets stand like distributed senses, the root-drums like a nervous system, the fliers like impulses passing along invisible paths. Even the open oval and stroke resembles a crude anatomical sign: chamber and axis, body and spine, world and pin.

So the hidden garden may be one creature whose apparent ecology is its sustained thought.

Its plants are the thought’s green architecture. Its animals are recurring sensations. The abandoned tools are the idea of hands. The silver climbing the bowl is a circulation that never completes. And the deafening chord is not the noise of thousands of lives, but one newborn being holding its first note without yet having learned to release it.
