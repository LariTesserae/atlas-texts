At first the shelf seems inhabited by the combs.

They stand in the gale like the exposed working surfaces of some vast machine: ranks of dark teeth, clear forks, silver wires drawn so fine that their shadows are wider than their bodies. But no individual comb is an organism in the ordinary sense. A comb is a temporary agreement among thousands of crystalline growth fronts. Its base may be centuries old while its upper vanes are younger than the current wind. Portions continually detach, migrate, and join neighboring structures. Two combs that stand apart at noon may have exchanged half their substance by dusk.

What persists is not the specimen but its tuning.

Each colony maintains a family of resonances: a deep structural tone, several carrying frequencies through which charge and reactive fragments move, and a fringe of delicate harmonics used to sense the air. When damaged, it does not regrow toward a remembered outline. It sounds the missing part. Loose scales nearby begin to shiver; those compatible with the call turn edge-on to the wind, climb pressure gradients in tiny skips, and lock into the vacancy. If none are suitable, the colony changes its tune and becomes a different shape.

The oldest are known by their silence. They do not cease vibrating, but cancel their own sound so precisely that a quiet sheath surrounds them. Within that sheath, rare atmospheric compounds can remain trapped for minutes. Their bases have thickened into black, glassy knots filled with metal atoms harvested over many cold seasons. Needle-grazers avoid these elders. Foil-mites gather around them in dense halos, feeding on the steep electrical boundary at the edge of the quiet.

Other growths are harder to recognize as life. The silver threads do not always connect stone to stone. Some end in open air, terminating at stable pressure nodes. They extend by precipitating one molecular layer at a time ahead of themselves, so that the growing tip appears separated from the strand by a hairline gap. When the local chord changes, the whole thread may vanish—not because its substance is gone, but because it has rotated into a dimension too narrow for the eye. A touch would find it, though touching is dangerous. Charge races through a disturbed thread fast enough to plate a glove with metal or strip atoms from an unprotected hand.

The membranes are more sociable. They spread between combs, pillars, arches, and sometimes the suspended bodies of animals. They are branching mosaics rather than continuous sheets, each cell opening and closing in response to pressure. Across them move dark pulses like thoughts seen through skin. A membrane can belong simultaneously to several colonies, distributing charge among them while keeping their incompatible chemistries apart. During storms these shared membranes proliferate until the distinction between neighboring organisms becomes almost meaningless. For a few violet-lit minutes, the forest is one conductive body.

Then it breaks itself up before the unity becomes fatal.

Among the growths live the foil-mites, the most numerous creatures on the shelf. A single mite is scarcely more than six hinged plates around a charged grain. It has no front until it begins to move. Whichever plate encounters the strongest usable gradient becomes the head; the opposite plates become legs, rudders, or vanes. In calm knots they gather into revolving clouds, each individual flashing once per turn. These flashes pass through the swarm in bands, making it appear that a larger, many-lobed animal is flexing in the air.

The illusion is not entirely false. A swarm makes decisions no mite can make alone. It tests nearby harmonics, divides around dangerous frequencies, and remembers routes between standing-wave refuges. Memory resides in the proportions of differently charged mites. Individuals are replaceable; the pattern of the swarm survives them.

When gradients weaken, the mites fold flat against combs and surrender their charge. Colonies tolerate this theft because the discharged plates polish their surfaces and carry reproductive flaws from one growth to another. After electrical weather, every edge is silvered with sleeping mites, overlapping like scales on a fish that has no flesh beneath them.

The needle-bodied grazers are less benign. They run in rapid bursts along growth edges, six or eight magnetized feet touching down in sequence too fast to see. Their jaws are paired ceramic wedges tuned to split malformed crystal and leave healthy lattice intact. They are gardeners when food is plentiful and vandals when it is not. A starving herd can retune its bite and shear through mature vanes, reducing a comb to airborne splinters.

They have no fixed skeleton. Their bodies are stacks of polarized rods held together by mutual attraction. At each molt they loosen, reverse the polarity of every second rod, and assemble themselves in mirror image. Thus a grazer alternates between two bodily forms throughout its life. One travels best toward the mountain wall; the other toward the precipice. Their migrations are consequently written as zigzags across generations.

Their eggs resemble imperfections in the vegetation. A female presses a few charged rods into a growing crystal edge, where the colony carries them as though repairing itself. Weeks later the rods awaken and crawl out. The growth is left with a narrow sequence of defects that changes its tone. In this way grazers mark their birthplaces with audible scars, and adults returning through the gale can identify the combs from which they emerged.

The wheel-creatures never truly roll. Each is a transparent ring surrounding a fixed silver thread, and the impression of motion comes from a wave of contraction traveling around its circumference. They graze on excitations passing through the strand, slowing electrical pulses by tiny amounts. A crowded thread therefore acquires a lower note. Combs respond by increasing voltage; wheels respond by reproducing. Left unchecked, they can drag a whole grove down through several harmonics until its seams open and it falls apart.

Their young begin as incomplete arcs. Two unrelated arcs must meet around the same thread and decide whether their curvatures are compatible. If they are, they fuse into a wheel. If not, they pass through one another, exchanging a few structural segments, and continue searching. Some remain arcs for years.

The folded cages are the nearest things to familiar animals, though only while closed. At rest they sit in sheltered flutes as angular knots, no larger than clenched hands. When a favorable rhythm passes over the shelf, their struts unlock. They bloom into sparse frameworks many times their resting size and allow the wind to lift them. Internal weights—grains of iron, cobalt, and stranger metals—shift from bar to bar, steering the open cage across several meters of violent air.

They migrate not toward food but toward particular intervals between notes. Once inside the desired vibration, they collapse around it. The captured resonance circulates through their frames and sustains them until it decays.

A cage may remain closed for months, apparently dead. Yet inside, its bars slowly exchange positions. The innermost become outermost; old fractures are moved to low-stress joints; impact scars migrate along the frame without changing shape. When it opens again, it is materially rebuilt but historically continuous—much like the arches among which it shelters.

This resemblance has led some visitors to mistake the cages for maintenance devices left by the builders. It is equally possible that the arches learned migration from them.

The hunters are seldom seen because seeing them requires the wrong conditions. Most are small patches of altered resonance stretched between unrelated objects. A hunter may use a pillar, three comb tips, and the rim of a basin as parts of its body without touching any of them. It adjusts the delays among those points until a focused vibration forms in the intervening air.

Prey entering that volume begins to fail along its weakest seams. Wheels open into arcs. Mites lose agreement about which side is forward. Grazers disassemble into rods. The hunter then shifts the focus toward a membrane, which absorbs the released charge and repays the hunter through changes in tension.

Such predators have no mouth and perhaps no boundary. When the structures anchoring one of them move, the hunter may move with them, remain in the same place using new supports, or divide into several weaker descendants. Their lineage is carried in ratios of distance and delay. A hunter can outlive every physical point that constitutes it.

The prey have evolved correspondingly strange defenses. Some go limp, allowing all vibrations to pass through without reinforcement. Others match the dominant chord so closely that the hunter cannot isolate them from the shelf. Foil-mite swarms sometimes sacrifice a portion of themselves, assembling a false body with a conspicuous fracture frequency. When the focused pulse strikes, that decoy bursts into glitter while the rest of the swarm escapes.

At choral hours, concealment becomes communal. Wheels, mites, grazers, cages, and whole colonies converge upon one harmonic family. For a short time the ecosystem ceases to contain acoustically distinct bodies. It becomes a single bright overtone moving above the mountain’s bass. Predators starve in the midst of abundance.

Beyond the precipice drift the great lattices.

Up close, they are less like animals than movable hollows. Their sparse ribs outline nested chambers, but most of their anatomy consists of pressure differences held in stable relation. Dust enters through the widest rings and spirals inward. Charged particles adhere to certain ribs; neutral grains are accelerated and expelled. The recoil, though minute, steers the creature.

Their concentric structures do not all occupy the same orientation. In sunlight one may glimpse inner rings turned through impossible angles, appearing alternately inside and outside the larger frame. These rings pulse at intervals of several minutes. Each pulse changes the lattice’s buoyancy and sends a low tone across the shelf, to which the combs answer by leaning without bending: dismantling one side and reassembling on the other.

The lattices may be pollinators. After one passes overhead, new crystalline forms often appear among the combs—forms not seen elsewhere on the shelf. They may also be scavengers, carrying away charge, dust, and loose patterns. Small cages sometimes unfold and attach themselves to the inner rings, riding there for days before dropping onto distant faces of the mountain.

No one has observed a lattice being born. During storms, however, the nine basins occasionally fill with the same relief: a series of concentric depressions expanding around an empty center. Afterward there may be one more shadow moving beyond the cliff.

And then there are the inhabitants of the basins, if inhabitants they are.

The levitating grains form landscapes too regular to dismiss as turbulence. Some contain avenues, terraces, branching towers, and fields of minute upright marks. Under magnification, the marks themselves resolve into smaller arrangements of grains, each surrounded by its own calm pocket. These inner figures move. They gather at ridges before the relief changes; they disperse when miniature arches appear. Sometimes they seem to anticipate an alteration several seconds before it reaches them.

They may be models of beings elsewhere. They may be calculations performed by the shelf. They may be the ancient builders reduced to patterns that can survive indefinitely in dust.

On rare occasions a figure separates from the relief and rises above the basin. It has no fixed form, only a persistent spacing among perhaps a hundred grains. The gale cannot scatter it. It travels to another basin, descends, and is incorporated into the landscape there. Immediately the two basins begin showing the same terrain from different directions.

The pillars and arches react to these migrations. A weathered arch may fade as the grain-figure passes beneath it, then reappear at the destination with one new scar. This has suggested that the figure is carrying not information about the structure, but the structure’s identity—that the stone is only the latest substance persuaded to embody it.

If so, the ruins are alive in the same manner as everything else on the shelf. They feed on recurrence. They reproduce by establishing the same relation among new pieces. They heal by transferring damage into positions where damage has always been. Their metabolism is the exchange of probabilities, their body a set of echoes, their age measured not in the endurance of matter but in the number of times the pattern has survived replacement.

It may be that nothing on the Choir Shelf lives alone.

The combs make the chord that shelters the animals. The animals prune and retune the combs. The hunters preserve the clarity of resonances by destroying bodies that drift too far out of tune. The great lattices bring foreign dust and carry local patterns into the sky. The basins remember configurations the shelf has lost—or has not yet assumed. The arches move stress, history, and location through the entire system.

Taken separately, each creature is slight: a film, a defect, a handful of rods, a vacancy given edges. Together they inhabit the deeper body, which is the shelf’s tuned air.

That greater inhabitant has no single voice because the overwhelming chord is its voice. It has no stable anatomy because every grove, animal, ruin, and standing wave is replaceable. It may sleep during the ancient-looking quiet between storms. It may dream in the nine basins. When lightning reveals the full web and every part rearranges at once, it is difficult not to feel that the mountain has drawn breath.

By morning the breath has become architecture, forest, and fauna again, and no individual thing remembers having been whole.
