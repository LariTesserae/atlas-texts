At first, the inhabitants appear to be trees.

They have trunks, crowns, roots, and something like leaves. They stand upright in the light and draw sustenance from the ground. Seen from far across the mineral flats, a colony might be mistaken for a grove in late summer: pale boles, dark foliage, flashes of bronze and green.

But the resemblance diminishes with approach.

The tall forms are not woody. Their trunks consist of nested ceramic shells grown around a hollow core. The outermost shell is smooth, cream-white, and warm to the touch. Beneath it lie alternating layers of porous mineral tissue and conductive black bands. These rings are not scars of annual growth. They are active organs, each maintaining a different electrical potential. Charges move between them in measured pulses, producing the faint notes that travel through the trunk.

The hollow center is not empty. It contains a suspended bundle of solvent vessels: translucent tubes filled with dense amber, red, or nearly black fluids. None of these liquids mixes readily with the others. They carry dissolved metals, carbon compounds, mobile salts, and catalytic grains between organs. Fine membranes open and close around them without valves or muscles. If the trunk is fractured, the vessels collapse inward, their contents thickening instantly into inert glass. Injury does not invite healing. It is converted into a permanent boundary.

The crowns are circular rather than branching. Each is a wheel assembled from hundreds of vanes, and each vane is both leaf and electrical surface. Its upper face is pale and reflective; its lower face is dark enough to seem cut from the shadow beneath it. Between these faces runs a microscopic architecture of silicate cells, conductive threads, charge-separating pigments, and tiny chambers in which light is converted into chemical difference.

The crowns turn throughout the day, though there is no day in the familiar sense. The sun does not visibly travel. Their movement belongs to a longer circuit internal to the organism. One ring inclines while another flattens; one set of vanes accepts charge while another sheds it down the trunk. After many hours the whole arrangement returns exactly to its first configuration.

This return is more important to them than progress.

Around the towers live forms that might be called undergrowth only because they are low.

The hexagonal mats are collector organs spread over the plateau’s surface. Their millions of nickel hairs respond to minute differences in heat and voltage. Clear bulbs at their tips condense no dew; they accumulate charged molecules from the air and trap particles too sparse to be called dust. A shadow crossing them causes successive ranks of hairs to bow, not in alarm but to discharge the imbalance created by the loss of light.

The bronze coils are furnaces. Each begins as a tight metallic bud and unfurls into a ribbed spiral whose inner surfaces carry catalytic films. Mineral-rich fluid enters cold at the base, passes through progressively warmer chambers, and emerges separated into useful compounds. Their fernlike shape is not decorative. It provides the precise ratio of exposed surface to enclosed heat required by their chemistry.

The red bladders are reservoirs and thermal organs. They swell with ionic liquid, their walls taut and faintly translucent. In the hottest part of the cycle they release heat into nearby tissues; later, the same fluid accepts heat back. Sometimes a bladder contracts so slowly that its motion becomes visible only by comparing its shadow over several hours.

The brittle cups regulate excess charge. Darkness cools their rims, altering the conductivity of the layered crystal. Violet sparks then crawl across their interiors. A cup may flash once and remain quiet for years, though internally it continues making and unmaking the structures required to flash again.

All these forms may be separate organisms. They may also be organs of a single one.

No clear boundary settles the question.

Beneath each grove, roots meet in a dense mineral crown. Some roots belong unmistakably to individual towers near the surface, but deeper down they merge through membranes permeable to current, solvent, and assembled molecules. A damaged tower can be isolated without disturbing its neighbors. Yet no tower maintains its full chemistry alone. The mats gather what the coils refine; the bladders buffer what the crowns generate; the cups release what the buried tissues cannot store. Remove one class of form and the others would continue for a time, but their cycles would no longer close.

Thus the inhabitant may be the grove.

If so, the towers are not trees within it but upright limbs. The low mats are its skin. The furnace-coils are its digestive surfaces, though they digest only stone. The underground lattice is both root system and distributed body. What resembles a forest is one broad creature repeatedly rising out of the ground.

Yet even the grove may not be the final individual.

Across the vacant miles, isolated colonies reproduce the same modules with extraordinary fidelity. Their crowns share dimensions. Their conductive rings pulse at related intervals. Waste beads laid around one grove contain the same ratios of metals as those laid around another days away. There is no visible communication between them, no drifting spores or wandering intermediaries, but their cycles fit together as if they were phases of one process spread over the plateau.

Perhaps they descend from a common origin so exact that divergence never began. Perhaps the plateau itself supplies the pattern. Perhaps the colonies are temporary protrusions of a life whose greater body exists in the ordered stresses of the mineral shelf.

Nothing here confirms any of these possibilities. The inhabitants do not reveal themselves by action.

Their nearest equivalent to feeding is the selective dismantling of rock. Root tips enter seams too fine to admit a filament and establish fields across them. Under those fields, useful atoms migrate through the crystal lattice. Iron, nickel, copper, sulfur, silicon, and rare catalytic metals are drawn into separate channels. What remains is not rubble but a cleaner, simpler mineral, often extruded at the colony’s edge as a bead or geometric ingot.

These castoffs can resemble manufactured goods: smooth bars, glazed pellets, perforated disks, black wedges with mirror-flat sides. But they are not tools. No inhabitant picks them up. Their regularity arises because regular shapes are the easiest way to isolate unwanted matter while preserving the closed chemistry of the body.

Their nearest equivalent to breathing is the movement of charge. Their nearest equivalent to blood is a set of liquids that must never encounter open air. Their nearest equivalent to growth is maintenance so exact that replacement becomes indistinguishable from permanence.

Cells wear out, but only locally. A failing membrane is dissolved and rebuilt molecule by molecule. A ceramic strut is softened from within, its minerals withdrawn, sorted, and returned in the same form. The organism does not add a ring or extend a root beyond its established reach. It continually remakes its present boundary.

Nor do the inhabitants reproduce in the ordinary meaning of the word.

Inside sealed nodules beneath the grove, miniature systems assemble: folded membranes, catalytic threads, fragments of ceramic shell, tiny charge-bearing vanes. For a time each nodule resembles an embryo of the entire colony. It establishes gradients, circulates solvent, and begins the ordered extraction of minerals from an internal store.

Then, at maturity, it does not emerge.

Its gradients reverse. Its vanes dissolve. The ceramic components are disassembled into grains, and the grains return to the parent circulation. Nothing fails in this process. The unreleased offspring is not aborted. Its formation and reclamation complete a necessary loop, testing or renewing the pattern from which the grove is made.

Generation occurs, but succession does not.

There are no young colonies and no ancient ones. None appear vigorous or senescent. Size does not indicate age. The largest grove is not an elder or a metropolis; it is simply a larger closure of the same equations.

Whether these beings perceive anything is impossible to determine. They respond to light, shadow, thermal variation, fracture, pressure, and electrical imbalance. Information passes through their roots. A shadow laid across one edge of the great grove alters the timing of crowns far beyond it. The response is coordinated, but coordination alone does not imply awareness.

If they possess a sense of the world, it may consist entirely of departures from balance.

Light is not brightness to them but voltage. Stone is not hardness but a distribution of soluble atoms. Distance may be measured as resistance through the root lattice. Time may be the interval required for a charge to cross the grove and return. A fracture would be known only as an abrupt edge to circulation, after which the world becomes complete again around the loss.

They have no organs for pursuit, fear, appetite, or curiosity. Nothing in their structure anticipates another living thing. They carry no defenses because they have no predators, no lures because they have no partners, no toxins meant to discourage mouths. Their tissues can be hot, brittle, corrosive, or electrically charged, but these are consequences of operation rather than weapons.

They do not compete. Even where two colonies stand near each other, their roots stop at old, exact boundaries without crowding or probing. Neither advances into unused ground. The bare space between them is not contested territory. It is simply outside both circuits.

In this way, the plateau’s life is lush without being fertile and elaborate without becoming diverse. Its inhabitants have many organs but few intentions. They perform chemistry with the precision of machinery, yet no machine exists apart from them. They manufacture components, rehearse descendants, refine metals, store energy, shed waste, and repair damage, all without producing a future different from the present.

The most complete inhabitant, then, may not be tower, mat, grove, or hidden root body.

It may be the cycle itself.

The ceramic trunks and metallic fronds are the durable shapes through which that cycle passes. The oils, salts, charges, and extracted atoms are its circulating substance. The grove exists to preserve the cycle, while the cycle continually recreates the grove. Neither can be called the maker of the other.

Across the plateau, these living circuits stand far apart under the stationary sun. Each is opulent, intricate, and sealed. Each possesses everything required to continue and nothing that would compel it to change.

They are not waiting, sleeping, or enduring.

They are life with all uncertainty removed.
