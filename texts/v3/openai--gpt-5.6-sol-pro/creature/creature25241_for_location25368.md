What lives in the saddle lives close to surfaces.

Nothing there has much use for height or speed. Roots travel sideways. Animals flatten themselves against clay and stone. Even the trees seem less interested in reaching the sky than in placing their weight correctly upon the mountain. Life arranges itself around seepage, shade, and resonance, attending to differences too slight for an outsider to notice.

The broad-leaved tree in the western clearing is older than its modest height suggests. Its trunk is thick, asymmetrical, and divided low, with waxy folds that hold small reservoirs after rain. Beneath the bark lies a loose, pale tissue riddled with water channels. When the stone under one root begins to lose its tone, the tree lets that root harden and starves it of growth. Elsewhere, where a slab rings deeply, new rootlets gather. Over centuries it has shifted its support across the clearing without moving its trunk, abandoning one side of itself and becoming denser on the other.

Its leaves do more than shed water. Their blunt tips release drops in different rhythms according to the branch they grow upon. On still days the whole crown ticks softly, a scattered pattern of impacts on roots, pools, and hollow rock. The pattern changes as underground chambers fill. The tree answers not consciously, perhaps, but accurately: pores close on branches above a rising cistern, while leaves over drier stone uncurl. Its roots know the mountain by vibration.

The six pale trees are not six organisms in the same certain way. Their trunks rise separately, but beneath the moss their roots meet in a swollen lattice wrapped around a buried shelf. A cut made in one trunk will bead with sap in all the others. They flower rarely, producing small gray blossoms with no scent and almost no visible petals. The shell-backed grazers climb them then, following some mineral change in the bark, and carry dust from trunk to trunk on their damp undersides.

Each tree grows toward the east because the grove is slowly following a low note. The chamber that produces it lies deeper every year, or seems to. New wood forms preferentially on the side from which the vibration arrives. The trunks lean after the receding sound as plants elsewhere lean after light.

The giant fern is younger. Its apparent immensity comes from a single creeping body hidden beneath the soil, coiled several times around the clearing. Only one crown is active at a time. When its fronds age, another crown may rise several paces away, while the first collapses and disappears into the mud. Thus the fern crosses the saddle in generations of itself, leaving behind rings of altered clay. Pale amphibians often wait beneath its newest growth. The fern’s shadow makes the ground lower in the saddle’s peculiar reckoning, drawing a thin movement of water toward them.

The translucent cups at the seep walls are neither plants nor fungi alone. Their clear membranes grow from hair-fine threads inside the stone film, but each cup also contains a colony of minute green cells. By day those cells feed on light. By night the membrane closes and digests what the water has brought: mineral dust, spores, fragments of algae, occasionally a tiny swimmer. A cup that catches nothing for several nights loosens itself from the wall. Curled shut, it is carried by the creeping water until it reaches a darker hollow, where it adheres and opens again.

Their peppery odor is a warning and an invitation. It repels the pale pool dwellers, but the shell-backed grazers seek it. By feeding along the rims, the grazers prune old tissue and carry reproductive grains in the joints of their shells. Whole colonies slowly migrate after them, advancing across the cliff in glistening constellations.

The grazers themselves are smaller than a fingernail. Their shells are not grown in spirals but assembled in overlapping mineral plates secreted from what they scrape off the rock. Orange stone produces rust-colored margins; black film produces a dark central ridge. An old grazer is therefore a record of the walls it has crossed.

They have soft, nearly transparent bodies and many short gripping feet. Most of their lives are spent following the edges between wet and wetter. Their looping paths are not searches for food so much as circuits: each animal returns repeatedly to the same patches, harvesting the regrown film. When a mat becomes too thick, they push loose flakes of stone beneath it. They do not keep these flakes or recognize them later. The useful edge belongs, for a little while, to the act rather than to the animal.

At intervals the mountain’s hum passes through a grazer’s shell and makes the plates chatter. Then the animal stops. Across the cliff, others stop as well. Hundreds may remain motionless through a change of tone, after which they resume their separate loops. This is the nearest they come to gathering.

The pale inhabitants of the pools are longer than they first appear. Most of the body remains buried in russet clay: a narrow torso, four folded limbs, and a heavy tail flattened like a blade. Only the nostrils and, sometimes, the tips of the forefeet show. They have no functional eyes. A translucent skin has grown over the sockets, through which branching blood vessels can be seen.

They hunt by feeling disturbances in the clay. When a soft-bodied swimmer passes overhead, the pool dweller rises just enough to trap it beneath a piece of shale. The motion is abrupt, but afterward the animal may remain in the same posture for hours, one hand resting on the chip. Eventually it swallows the prey, releases the stone, and sinks. It never carries the chip elsewhere.

During storms they emerge fully. The booming below drives them out of the deepest mud, and they walk on their long limbs from pool to pool while water sheets across the clearings. Their skins shine colorlessly except along the spine, where a faint branching pattern darkens in time with the subterranean chord. They breed only on such nights. By morning they have buried themselves again, and strings of clear eggs cling to the undersides of resonant stones.

The black bird does not live in the saddle. It crosses because the rising warm air saves it effort, though it dislikes the sounds below. Its single cry is less a call than a reaction to the hum entering its hollow bones. It never lands. In the delayed reflection of the deepest pool, however, its image sometimes appears to circle once before continuing on. The pool dwellers react to that second, reflected passage more strongly than to the bird itself.

The largest inhabitant is known chiefly by interruption.

It rests in the upper ferns, where broad fronds conceal the shape of anything beneath them. When it moves, it does so rarely and without urgency. Three toes press into the mud, each ending not in a claw but in a wide, sensitive pad. The prints suggest a creature heavy enough to bend mature stems, yet its passage makes almost no sound above ground.

No observer sees the whole body because the animal does not present a whole body easily. A low head may appear beyond one shelf while a ridged back is glimpsed in another clearing, the humid air and folded ground making their connection uncertain. Its hide carries moss, translucent cups, and small rooted ferns. These are not merely growths upon an aging animal. They are part of how it drinks. Moisture moves across the living coat toward channels in its skin, following shade and vibration just as water moves across the saddle.

It feeds infrequently. A crescent bitten from the giant fern, a stripped patch on a pale trunk, a pool emptied of soft clay: these signs appear months apart. More often it lies with the underside of its body pressed against humming stone. Along its ribs are thin chambers that resonate with the mountain. Changes in the subterranean tones pass through them. Before a cistern booms, the creature shifts to higher shelves. Before a whistle ceases, it digs shallow hollows that later fill with water.

Its three-toed tracks are therefore not a trail but a series of listenings.

Whether there is one such creature or several cannot be established. The marks never overlap in age, and the upper ferns never bend in two places at once. Yet tracks differ greatly in size from season to season. It may grow and diminish with its water burden, or younger animals may follow routes so widely separated in time that they seem to be one long-lived presence.

Nothing in the saddle names or commands the rest. There is no keeper hidden under the roots, no intelligence directing the misplaced currents. Yet the lives there fit the mountain’s strange sense of depth so closely that they resemble portions of a single slow faculty.

The trees attend to ringing stone. The fern deepens ground with shade. The cups follow water that climbs. The grazers edit the films on the cliffs. The pool dwellers wait beneath reflections. The three-toed animal listens with its entire weight.

Together they do not master the saddle. They are how the saddle notices itself.
