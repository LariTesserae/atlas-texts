At first it seems that only the plants live there, and not very vigorously.

The grasses in the cracks appear to be separate clumps, the fleshy leaves around the pools separate colonies, the moss a last remnant preserved by shade. The little tree seems loneliest of all. Yet beneath the clay they are joined.

A network finer than roots runs through the old seams of silt and along the buried course of the runnel. It is nearly colorless, lacking the white threads or soft rot of fungus. Where exposed by a footprint, it resembles a mineral sheen: a few translucent filaments laid against one another, wet even when the clay around them is dry. They pass into the tree’s roots, enter the waxy plants without any seam of grafting, and rise into the moss beneath the overhang. Even some of the grasses belong to it. What looks like a sparse community is a single life showing different organs wherever conditions allow.

The tree is its principal body above ground, though not necessarily its oldest part. Its twisted trunk stores years of water in dense, fibrous wood. The horizontal branches are positioned to receive reflected light rather than direct sun, and their leathery leaves turn their duller surfaces upward when the basin becomes too bright. They do this so slowly that no visitor could watch them move. Between one dawn and the next, however, their inclination may have changed.

The fleshy plants are reservoirs. Their oval leaves swell after rain and contract over the following weeks, passing moisture into the buried network. Pale dust on their surfaces is not merely settled limestone. It is salt exuded from within, the residue left after the life has taken what it needs from each measure of water. A damaged leaf does not bleed sap. It closes around the wound, hardens to the color of the surrounding stone, and may remain attached for years as a sealed, useless chamber.

The moss is the part most nearly resembling a sense organ. It lies where condensation first forms and responds to changes too slight for an animal to notice: the cooling of the eastern wall before dawn, the mineral character of a new seep, the pressure of an approaching rain held in the warm air. When wet, its scattered patches darken in sequence from the deepest alcove outward. The sequence is not governed entirely by where the drops fall. It passes through the concealed filaments as a slow chemical recognition.

It does not see the basin. It knows it as a set of gradients.

The black pools are coolness and stored weight. The central flat is warmth. The upper ledges are dryness, except during rain. The southern notch is loss. A footstep is briefly known as pressure, salt, and a little unexpected heat. When the impression fades, the knowledge of it fades as well. The creature keeps no image of the foot that made it and has no organ in which such an image could remain.

Its thought, if thought is the word, proceeds through adjustments. One reservoir closes its pores. A branch relinquishes water. Moss thickens over a seam no wider than a hair. Root tips withdraw from clay made bitter by some dissolved mineral and advance toward another pocket over the course of a season. An entire year may amount to a single response.

This is why it can be mistaken for an equilibrium rather than an inhabitant.

The stonework is older than its present shape but not wholly unrelated to it. The rectangular ruin was made by hands belonging to another order of life: quick hands, capable of lifting and finishing an action in an afternoon. Those builders are gone beyond any memory the basin can hold. The living network found the disturbed soil beneath their threshold, followed the shade cast by their walls, and entered the sediment gathered around the foundations.

The low wall across the runnel may also have begun as their work. Since then, the basin’s inhabitant has continued it without placing a stone. Fine roots slow the silt on one side. Mineral salts bind grains into thin skins. Moss retains each brief passage of water. Over centuries these small acts have persuaded the stones downward and inward, tightening the old construction until it serves the living system better than it could have served its makers.

The slabs around the deeper pools have become part of the same arrangement. Beneath them, protected from evaporation, the transparent network is thickest. It reaches the water through pores in the limestone and draws from below, leaving the surfaces untouched. Nothing bends down to drink. Nothing troubles the amber skin.

It produces neither flowers nor seed. The absence of seedlings beneath the tree is not failure. The entire basin is already occupied by the one individual, and it has no neighboring basin within reach. Reproduction belongs to some earlier condition of the landscape, when water may have crossed the rim more freely and carried fragments onward. Here, enclosed and stable, propagation has ceased to be useful.

Nor is it dying. Portions fail, but the whole does not diminish. A grass clump may dry into black threads and vanish into the clay; years later another rises where a shaded crack has deepened. A branch may become hollow and remain held among the living wood. The moss advances the width of a finger while the succulents retreat by the same amount. Loss in one place becomes persistence in another.

It has no need of animals. Indeed, it has outlasted the conditions that once admitted them. The water contains too little free nourishment for larvae. The leaves offer wax, salt, and fibrous bitterness. There is no nectar, soft fruit, loose bark, or tender growth. Nothing here calls outward with color or scent. The faint odor of old roots is the odor of the inhabitant itself, exhaled most strongly after rain.

If approached as a creature, it offers no recognizable center. Cutting the tree would not kill it. Burning the moss would not silence it. Emptying one pool would cause a contraction elsewhere, perhaps not visible for months. Its body is the relation among shaded stone, clay seams, stored water, and the few surfaces where light can be used.

It does not guard the basin, though its presence makes the basin difficult to alter. It does not erase tracks deliberately, though its manner of living helps the clay settle smooth again. It does not maintain the old walls as a custodian would. It merely accepts every stable arrangement into itself, until survival and place become indistinguishable.

During the brief rain, its separated forms come closest to acting as one. The moss opens. The grasses loosen their blades. The thick leaves draw tight with water. The tree’s fine roots become conductive, and the buried filaments awaken through the whole circumference of the hollow. For an hour or two, impulses pass from wall to wall. The southern notch registers the taste of what escapes. The central flat cools. Every pocket fills.

Then the water withdraws, and the life subsides into its distributed stillness.

From above, there is no sign that the basin contains a being. From within, there is no single thing to point to. Yet nearly every dark patch, every leathery leaf, every damp seam and mineral lip is part of it.

It is not waiting. It has no expectation of visitors, offspring, weather, or change. Its existence is not a vigil.

It is simply the slow coherence of the hollow, alive at the speed with which stone becomes wet and dries again.
