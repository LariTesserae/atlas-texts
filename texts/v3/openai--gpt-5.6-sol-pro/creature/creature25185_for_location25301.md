Nothing in the Blackwater Works lives alone.

The smallest inhabitants make this difficult to notice. They resemble separate creatures because they can be lifted on a fingertip: clear mites with milk-white organs, pinched worms that knot themselves when touched, gnats whose wings have sealed into damp paddles along their backs. Each appears self-contained. Yet when one is removed from the plain it continues to make minute answering movements, flexing toward pulses conducted through the soil. Hours later, others gather in the place from which it was taken. They arrange themselves around the absence.

The warm-bodied gnats live wherever condensation is about to form. They do not drink the water once it has gathered. They fasten their mouthparts to bare metal, shell, leaf, or skin and wait for the first molecular dampness, feeding at the instant when vapor decides to become liquid. Their bodies are round and finely furred, with no visible eyes. Pressure pits ring their abdomens. A hand moved above a colony causes them to turn in sequence, not toward the hand but toward the small compression of air preceding it.

They breed inside the pale grease of captive lamplight.

A hooded lamp left burning for several weeks develops dark specks close to its glass. These ripen into larvae, each curled around a filament of illumination. When the lamp is extinguished, the larvae fall out with the darkness and crawl away. For this reason old pump-house lamps sometimes contain generations of empty skins, all shining faintly but illuminating nothing.

The bacterial mats support heavier life. Their grazers are soft, long-backed animals known chiefly by touch: warm sacks with slow muscular waves passing from head to tail. Their mineral teeth grow in ranks and break continuously against the stone. Broken teeth do not remain inert. In wet ground they continue to rasp, drawing themselves forward grain by grain until they reach a seep rich enough to sustain a new grazer. Whether these are offspring, fragments, or merely teeth behaving as animals is not a distinction the Works preserves.

A mature grazer may be longer than a person. Its back carries shallow impressions of nearby pipes, as though the creature had been pressed against them while still soft. Some bear layouts belonging to pipelines kilometers away. They migrate by following warmth, but they never cross a rail or conduit without laying the full length of their bodies against it first. There they remain, listening through their teeth.

Transparent crabs inhabit the undersides of everything: ledges, valve housings, wader joints, the lower lips of open doors. Their shells are clear enough that digestion can be watched as a dim stain moving through them. They stack themselves in living masonry, legs interlaced, the upper layers supporting the lower against gravity. A colony beneath a gantry may hang many meters down without falling. Individuals at its center become flattened and nearly rectangular. Those at the edge remain quick and sharp-limbed.

They exchange shells rather than grow new ones. The emptied shell is passed inward through the colony until it reaches an animal of suitable size. The oldest shells, thickened by mineral deposit and handling, end up at the core. Thus a large crab colony contains a hard central archive of bodies no longer belonging to anyone. When vibrations pass through the gantry, the archive clicks. The living crabs adjust themselves around the message.

In saturated soil dwell the eels.

They have no fixed thickness. A blind eel squeezed between mineral grains can become narrow as a thread; entering a buried cavity, the same animal may relax until it fills the chamber like an organ. Its bones are small loose elements suspended through its flesh. These collect temporarily into a spine when the animal needs leverage, then scatter when it swims through mud. An eel passing underfoot feels at first like a root tightening and then like certainty returning to unstable ground.

They feed on trapped pressure. Around a pinhole seep, several may coil invisibly beneath the surface and drink the force pushing water upward. The clear dome above them shrinks. Their bodies swell and grow rigid. When startled they release what they have consumed, sometimes with enough force to split marl or open an old service hatch.

Their eggs are harder than stone and occur in seams well below the basin. Some have been found embedded in mineral ridges that have not yet risen. The young inside tap intermittently, answering machinery that will not pass overhead for centuries.

The great waders belong to another order of life, though not necessarily another lineage. Their six legs are black, narrow, and extraordinarily jointed. No two observers agree on the number of joints because some bends occur only during certain phases of a step. The body hangs high above the marsh, a long heat-bearing mass with its head lowered from the front like a sounding weight.

Its mouth never reaches the ground. Instead, each foot tastes what it enters.

A wader plants a limb and waits while delicate membranes unfold between its toes. Brine, mud, living film, metal corrosion, buried gas, and the distant movement of groundwater are sampled in turn. The information travels upward as a sequence of muscular constrictions. Half a day later it reaches the body, which begins deciding where to place the next foot.

By then the ground may have changed. This does not trouble them. They are thought to navigate not through the basin as it is, but through an average of what it has been and will be. Their slow crossings describe routes that seem senseless until, years later, a warm pipeline is laid beneath every place they stepped.

Entire ecologies ride on their legs. Clear crabs mass around the lower joints. Reeds germinate in pockets of damp hide. Soft grazers cling under the abdomen, feeding from waxy secretions. Higher up live colonies that never descend to the plain and may not know the ground exists. They feed on condensation coursing down the wader’s body and orient themselves toward the periodic warmth of its heart.

The heart is not inside the body. It travels from leg to leg.

At any given time one limb becomes hot and trembles with a deep contraction. The shelled passengers on it close themselves tightly. Over the following hours the heat rises through the joints, crosses the suspended torso, and descends into another leg. A complete circulation may take several days. If the animal stands across a spatial fault, the heartbeat occasionally arrives in a limb before it has left the last one. During these overlaps, the wader takes a step.

They do not appear to die. They become routes.

An old wader slows until each step takes years. Mineral accumulates around its feet. Its passengers spread down its legs and into the mud. The body cools, but the heat continues to pass along the path it intended to walk. Eventually only six dark pillars remain, spaced across impossible distances, each warming in sequence. Travelers sometimes mistake these pillars for parts of the Works. It may be that the Works made the same mistake.

The composite animals are quicker, though no one sees them assemble.

One moment there are hundreds of fist-sized bodies scattered over a mud shelf: slick, many-ciliated things resembling organs without owners. The next, the shelf bears a single animal. It may have four legs or forty. It may form a broad low shape adapted to crossing exposed marl, or a tall narrow one capable of reaching a valve wheel. Its skin is the imperfect meeting of many backs. Fine seams open and close as its members trade position.

Such beings are not swarms. A swarm preserves the will of its parts. In the assembled animal, the parts become specialized almost at once. Those in the center pulse as a heart. Those along the ground harden into feet. A few gather at the front and produce a temporary cavity that serves as an eye, though there is nothing visible for it to see. Others extrude tendons across the whole arrangement and are pulled apart when it moves.

The animal remembers injuries that occurred to previous configurations. It avoids a pipe on which none of its present members have been harmed. It favors patches of bacterial felt discovered by members long since eaten. When threatened, it may separate, but the resulting organisms depart with unequal portions of the shared memory. Some know only hunger. Some remember destinations but cannot move. A few retain the fear and nothing else, shivering in place until another assembly takes them in.

These temporary beasts are often found near recently maintained machinery.

They gather around bolts before the bolts are tightened. They climb housings and hold themselves against seams. On occasion, one forms a limb narrow enough to enter a service aperture. It remains inserted for hours while warmth travels through its constituent bodies. After it withdraws, the machinery runs more smoothly. The animal disperses at once, leaving several dead members arranged in the shape of a tool.

Whether this is imitation or employment is unknown.

There are other maintainers.

They are not seen directly, and “they” may be an artifact of grammar. Their presence is inferred from dryness. In a place where every surface weeps, a handprint sometimes appears on a valve casing as an area where condensation refuses to settle. It has too many fingers, or perhaps the fingers were moved while the print remained. The span suggests a hand large enough to encircle a human torso, yet the tightened bolts nearby show fine, precise handling.

These prints have no body heat. They have schedules.

One will appear beside a pressure gauge. Several days later another forms on a distant access panel, though the two structures may share a hidden wall. Then a row of fresh painted numerals emerges beneath the water on the inside of a tank. Afterward, the Works alters its cycle by a fraction. The prints fade only when the task is complete.

Sometimes a service door releases warm, dry air, and something occupies that air without entering the marsh. The condensation around the doorway pulls back from a shape. It suggests shoulders at first, then a rack of articulated arms, then hanging cables, then the broad muzzle of an animal testing the weather. The impression never resolves. Small creatures approach and arrange themselves at its feet. Transparent crabs raise their forelimbs. Gnats turn their pressure pits toward it. Eels stiffen beneath the ground.

No command is audible, but maintenance spreads outward afterward. Shells are cleared from grilles. Mineral teeth scrape corrosion from buried lines. Composite animals gather at junctions. Even the six-legged waders alter their routes.

The dry shape may be an operator. It may be a vacancy into which the inhabitants fit their obedience.

Deep below them all live the slowest creatures, if creatures they are.

The sealed engines contain growths that follow the turbines through their week-long rotations. Barnacle-like bodies attach to the blades and develop in the warmth behind the leading edge. Because a revolution takes longer than their lives, no individual experiences a complete turning. Each generation inherits the blade at a slightly different angle. Their anatomy changes accordingly. Those born during descent are flat and heavily anchored. Those born near the bottom produce buoyant sacs. Those born on the rise open fans into currents not yet present.

Collectively, the colony knows the circle.

Its oldest layer, buried under descendants, retains every orientation as a different mineral band. When the turbine stutters, the animals extend soft feelers into the mechanism and pull. Billions die in the correction. Their shells become part of the blade. There may now be turbines whose original metal has entirely worn away, leaving only the shape of rotation preserved in successive generations of animals.

The pumps, too, have acquired flesh.

Living slurry moves through some pipes with the regularity of circulation. Where a line ruptures, the substance does not spill randomly. It bulges from the opening, forms a translucent lip, and tastes the surrounding mud. If conditions are unsuitable, it withdraws and seals the breach from within. If conditions are favorable, it grows a temporary organ outside the pipe: a filtering fan, a bladder, a bed of cilia, sometimes something resembling a hand. Once the necessary water has been sorted, the organ liquefies and is drawn back into the system.

No specimen survives separation from the network. Removed portions continue dividing into finer structures, as if trying to produce a pipe small enough to contain themselves. Eventually they become a clear moisture that climbs the specimen jar and vanishes into its lid.

Aboveground plants partake in this buried life. The lonely reed stands are joined by roots thicker and more complex than their visible stems suggest. They clasp pipelines, enter cracked conduits, and braid around pressure chambers. Some roots terminate in old sockets. Others pass directly through intact metal without puncturing it. A reed cut at the surface may drip warm condensate carrying traces of copper and medicinal antiseptic.

Their folded leaves are not waiting for sunlight. They shelter delicate inner surfaces covered in raised lines. When the basin’s thermal morning passes, the leaves loosen by a fraction, and the lines alter. The patterns resemble piping diagrams, though they include branches that do not exist and omit conduits known to lie beneath them. Over centuries, a plant may unfold enough to reveal one complete section of the network.

Then it withers, and somewhere underground a valve opens.

Near the estuary are inhabitants less easy to separate from the water.

They gather in bottomless pools and give them temperament. One pool resists entry by becoming shallower beneath a probing pole. Another accepts anything lowered into it but returns the object coated in condensation from a different season. Around certain edges lie rings of shed skin, eel bone, cracked shell, reed wax, rust flakes, and human fabric, all incorporated into one flexible membrane. The membrane contracts when the buried pumps reverse.

Large sleepers are detected there by heat. They do not surface. A pool simply remains warm toward its center, and now and then an immense adjustment passes through the ground. Nearby organisms stop what they are doing. Waders lock their legs. Crabs press themselves flat. The bacterial felt dims chemically, though no eye would register the change.

Perhaps these sleepers are animals. Perhaps they are the submerged portions of the pressure stations. Their dreams, if dreams they are, modify distance. A sleeper turns, and two pump houses acquire the same back wall. It settles more deeply, and an hour opens between adjacent valves. Its pulse causes rain from another year to collect against the inside of a tank.

The creatures of the basin have learned to feed on these distortions. Tiny pale worms line up along shortened distances, consuming the excess nearness until ordinary separation is restored. Wide, lidless mollusks attach themselves where time sags and graze on delay. Their shells show growth rings formed by events rather than years: a falling drop, a completed piston stroke, an egg finally laid around its waiting young.

There may once have been human workers.

The evidence does not consist of bones. Bone is too active here, too readily touched and retouched by water. There are lengths of cloth caught inside sealed machinery, tools worn smooth at places no hand should grip, and cords tied around pipes to warn of temporal hazards. Some knots are familiar. Others can only be tied if the rope’s ends are brought together before the rope itself exists.

A few bunkers contain narrow impressions in the silt where people may have slept. These impressions remain warm. If a person lies in one, the shape adjusts slowly beneath them, not to their present body but to an older or future posture: a missing limb, a bent spine, the curled proportions of childhood. Afterward, for several days, dry handprints may follow that person from station to station.

It is possible the workers did not leave. They may have entered the maintenance cycle by degrees. First they learned to navigate through vibration. Then they ceased using lamps. Their speech shortened as sound failed them, passing into taps on rails and changes in pressure. Condensation learned the salts of their skin. The living slurry learned their touch. Their routes became waders; their gestures became composite animals; their work schedules persisted in prints of dryness.

Or the Works may never have employed them at all. The bunkers, rails, doors, and human-scaled valves may be lures grown by an industrial organism seeking a particular kind of symbiont.

At the deepest level, the distinction between inhabitant and habitat fails.

Every creature drinks water sorted by the Works. Every pipeline bears colonies that guide its flow. Roots tighten joints. Eels regulate pressure. Crabs translate vibration into movement. Waders trace future lines across the marsh. The unseen maintainers coordinate these lives, unless the coordinated lives produce the maintainers. Even predation serves some larger circulation: what is eaten returns as shell, tooth, bacterial skin, slurry, mineral deposit, or a remembered property of water.

When the basin draws breath and every pool deepens, the inhabitants pause not from fear but recognition.

The gnats lower themselves against wet metal. The grazers still their rows of teeth. Eels become rigid threads in the soil. Composite bodies loosen at their seams but do not separate. Far above the marsh, the waders suspend their traveling hearts between one leg and the next. In the sealed chambers, generations of turbine animals open at once.

Something below has made room.

The bottoms of the pools recede. Pressure enters the new depth. Water leans toward it without descending. Across the plain, roots, pipes, nerves, galleries, burrows, and flooded conduits tighten into a single connected sensation.

For that long moment, nothing merely lives in the Blackwater Works.

The plain has one body, distributed among millions of temporary organs. The engines are its patience. The animals are its touch. The water is not its blood but its memory, endlessly sorted according to what it has contacted, what it has forgotten, and what it has yet to become.

Then the upward stroke begins.

The shared body loosens. Creatures return to their individual hungers. A wader completes the fraction of a step. A crab passes an empty shell inward. Somewhere behind a service door, in warm dry air, a many-fingered absence reaches for the next tool.
