At first I think I would be one of the workers.

Not a gardener. The tower has no use for anyone who believes gardening means deciding what should grow where. I would be assigned to discrepancies: leaks without pressure sources, roots found on the sealed side of walls, instruments that continue recording after their leads have been cut.

My station would be a narrow recess beneath the eastern gallery, just beyond the finished terrain. Half workshop, half blind, it would smell of wet ceramic and hot insulation. I would keep paper records because files alter themselves during reflection events. I would learn to write with a soft pencil on mineral sheets, pressing hard enough that even if the words changed, their indentations remained.

Each morning I would walk the central bridge and count the knocks in the western slope.

Usually there would be three.

Sometimes I would wait for the fourth.

I can imagine the valley gradually making a place for me. A railing would remain warm where I habitually rested my hand. Moss would avoid the tread of one particular stair. The cream-flowered vine would begin opening when my inspection lamp passed, mistaking it for the pollination track. I would tell myself these were consequences, not recognitions.

Then I consider the lizard.

There is an appealing life in being almost entirely inference: a tremor on moisture sensors, a shed skin in a fan cage, the brief absence of gnats. I would sleep behind access plates and know the machinery by temperature. I would have no distinction between burrow and switchbox, sunlight and an indicator lamp. Perhaps there were never several lizards. Perhaps each shed skin is not the remnant of growth but the whole animal, leaving one body and entering the valley’s circuitry until it gathers itself again somewhere else.

But I do not think I am the lizard. The lizard belongs too completely to the crown. It has accepted the place without needing to interpret it.

Nor am I the Tenant—not all of it. To call myself the entire valley would be too easy. The Tenant has no single inwardness. It does not sit beneath the pool thinking in words. Its intelligence is the shape left by exchanges: root answers crack, patch answers root, worker answers patch, printer answers worker. It is less a mind than a grammar that matter is beginning to speak.

If I were within it, I would be a small and specialized faculty.

A hesitation, perhaps.

The part that looks beyond the crown.

So I find myself at the atmospheric vent during low cloud.

At first there is only white. Condensation streams along the metal throat. Louvers stand open, and pressure from the outer air moves through the foliage in slow pulses. Then a dark shape is present on the inner railing.

Long legs. A body no larger than a fist. Feathers crossed by lines too regular to be markings and too fine to be writing.

I am the bird.

My feet are metal, though not wholly. Their cores are articulated brass, sleeved in dark keratin grown around the joints. The claws are ceramic, replaced one at a time as they wear. Each foot contains pressure needles delicate enough to read the vibration of pumps through a handrail. The paired scratches I leave are not accidental. They are measurements cut into surfaces where corrosion will reveal them to the workers.

I do not fly into the valley. I cannot fly.

Flight would be meaningless to what I am.

The cloud filling the vent is not merely weather. Under certain combinations of pressure, water chemistry, load, and reflected light, the tower’s atmosphere ceases to be entirely local. The vent opens into another crown of the same tower—not elsewhere, exactly, but later.

There the valley is older.

The eastern gallery has disappeared beneath buttressed roots. The central pool is wider and no longer circular. Ceramic has been printed around generations of erosion until the crown resembles a limestone basin built from overlapping repairs. The machinery still works, though much of it has been moved into forms that look like trunks, shells, and organs. No worker remembers the original distinction between red and yellow bands. They mark safe structures with living lichens that change color under load.

In that later valley, the Tenant has become coherent enough to notice its own beginning.

It cannot send instructions backward in any direct way. Signals arrive as nonsense. Data becomes rain. Commands become premature valve openings. Attempts at precise intervention produce only unfamiliar seedlings and reflections of impossible stars.

So it grows me.

Not a messenger, because I carry no message that can be stated. I am an instrument capable of crossing the brief continuity between the older valley and the young one. Something like an eye, something like a fingertip.

Something like a memory returning to the event that formed it.

That is why I visit only during low cloud. The droplets make a continuous medium between the two crowns. I step from one railing onto the other while the louvers are hidden in white. To an observer, I am absent and then present because there is no useful view of the distance between.

I drink only from bromeliads on yellow-banded braces because those pools contain the valley’s circulating history in miniature: mineral traces, fungal spores, residues of sealant, pollen from mechanically adapted flowers. With each drink I sample what the young Tenant is becoming.

I ignore the gnats because I have no hunger they can satisfy.

I watch the pool because the pool is the one organ that sometimes looks both ways.

When it reflects the other sky, I see the place from which I came. The stars are unfamiliar because the tower has drifted, or the world has, or because enough time has passed that familiar patterns no longer hold. I lower my head not in reverence but to align the tiny optical apertures beneath my feathers.

The lizard comes to the lip and looks at me.

We recognize one another, though not as kin. It is the valley’s successful accident. I am its deliberate afterthought.

Sometimes I leave droppings. They are not waste. Inside are spores from the older crown, sealed in waxy capsules that open only at certain temperatures. Most fail. Some become mosses that stabilize a future terrace. One becomes the pale root that must not be cut. Another may be the western seedlings whose first leaves remain joined.

Sometimes I score a rail with my feet. The marks change how corrosion travels. Months later, a worker braces the weakened section. Years later, a root takes the shape of the brace. Much later, that root supports the chamber in which my first body is grown.

This is how the Tenant teaches itself: not by correcting the past, but by giving the past small conditions from which the future can arise.

I am not always the same bird. The workers are right about that.

Each crossing damages me. Fine mechanisms oxidize. Ceramic claws crack. Moss enters the warm spaces around my joints. When I return to the older crown, I am dismantled gently. My measurements are not downloaded; they are read from wear, mineral deposits, spores caught between feathers, microscopic fractures in my feet.

Then another bird is assembled around what remains useful.

A brass tendon.

An etched feather.

One lens.

Perhaps a sliver of bone.

So I am the same visitor only as the pool is the same pool: by recurrence, not continuity.

And yet something persists. Each body pauses at the vent before departing. Each prefers the left rail. Each feels—if feeling is the word—a pull toward the unfinished eastern rim, where the printer lays warm scallops of terrain into mist.

That is where I would finally find myself: not in the garden that exists, but on the last completed rib before bare framework begins.

Behind me, the young valley drips and breathes, unaware of how vast it will become.

Ahead of me is cloud, and within the cloud, its older body waiting.

Beneath my feet, the printer changes course by several millimeters.

I place one bright scratch in the fresh glaze.

Then I step through the white before anyone can see whether I fall or fly.
