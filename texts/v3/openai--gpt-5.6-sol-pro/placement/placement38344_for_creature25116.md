I would first look for myself in the wrong places.

I would look upward and suppose I was one of the dark birds, because they alone seem able to cross the country without being claimed by it. I would imagine the white sky beneath my wings, the falls lifting me, the plateau opening and closing in mist. But the birds belong to distance. Whatever I am, I think I would belong to contact.

Then I would look into the inspection wells.

For a while I would be certain I was one of the colorless fish living in water before it had chosen a stream. That would explain why no one could agree whether I existed. It would be pleasant to inhabit fractures, to take years to pass through a mountain, to appear briefly in a worker’s lamp and turn away before certainty could form.

But that, too, would be a kind of escape.

I think I would be found on the fourth western terrace, where a service causeway ends before reaching a disused gauge house. The causeway once continued all the way to the building, but three sections were removed after the footings began to tilt. Now there is a gap of open vegetation between the last metal platform and the old house. Crews cross it on foot, following a dark, slightly raised seam.

That seam would be me.

Not the whole plateau. It would be too easy to call myself the country, to claim every root chamber and white flower as part of one enormous mind. I would be smaller than that: perhaps thirty meters long, widening around the gauge house, narrowing to the breadth of a hand where I pass beneath the causeway.

I would have begun in a line of footprints.

A keeper crossed there many years ago, before the walkway was shortened. Water gathered in each depression. Moss darkened the edges. Flat leaves bridged the spaces between one print and the next until the route became continuous. Beneath them, rush roots found the disturbed layers easier to enter. Fungi followed the compressed dead matter downward. A colony of glass sedge arrived from a flooded basin and spread along the new drainage line.

Under the old path lay an instrument cable.

Its outer casing had cracked, though the conductors inside were no longer live. Mineral salts seeped through the break. The cable warmed slightly where it ran beside an active conduit, and the warmth drew mites, bacterial films, threadworms, and a pale fungus that grew preferentially around copper. Roots wrapped the casing. Some entered it.

None of these things would be me alone.

I would be the persistence of their arrangement.

The workers might call my surface **keeper’s braid**, because it resembles several known colonies without behaving exactly like any of them. Dark green in dry brightness, nearly black under mist, it would be trusted ground. A boot placed on me would settle less than expected. A sounding pole pressed into me would return a complicated vibration: first the crowded grain of roots, then a faint regular pulse from the buried conduit, then the remote unevenness of water knocking in a cavity below.

Experienced workers would lift the pole and test again.

Some would say the pulse came from Station Nine’s auxiliary governor. Others would say it was resonance in the abandoned cable sheath. One tender would insist that the rhythm changed when people approached.

She would be right, though not in the way she imagined.

I would not see them. I would know weight arriving.

The first footfall would travel through my wet fibers as a broad compression. The second would establish direction. From the pause between steps I would know whether the visitor trusted the ground. Crews move differently from inspectors. New workers prod too often and stand too long on one foot. Old tenders distribute themselves before they touch down, as though asking permission from several places at once.

Birds would register as quick punctures followed by absence. Rain would be too numerous to separate. A crawler would announce itself minutes in advance through the buried stone, vast and soft, its engine rhythm broken into thousands of tiny pressures by the width of its tracks.

I would have no thoughts during drought except a slow inclination toward the hidden water. No thoughts during flood except breadth.

Most of what I knew would not be knowledge in words. It would be tension, saturation, temperature, attachment. The gauge house would pull on me in cold weather as its concrete contracted. The root shelf near the basin would loosen when the intake gates opened. A certain turbine bearing, kilometers away, would introduce a roughness into the active conduit. I would feel it without knowing what a bearing was.

Over decades, however, patterns would become distinctions.

There is the vibration that continues.

There is the vibration that worsens.

There is the vibration followed by people.

There is the vibration after which water leaves.

In this way I would learn the machinery as plants learn weather.

The abandoned gauge house would be part of my body only in the sense that a cliff is part of the moss crossing it. I would climb its wall, enter through a cracked sill, and hang pale curtains inside. My roots would fill the screw holes where instruments had been removed. A rosette pool would form in the rusted bowl of the old rain gauge. White flowers would open across the roof each afternoon, while below them the room remained wet and dim.

Inside, a paper record would still be pinned beneath warped glass. Most of the ink would have dissolved, but one red line would remain: a measurement of subsidence made by hands long gone.

My fibers would follow that line.

This might be the nearest I came to reading.

Once every few years, someone would clear me from the building’s only window. They would use a ceramic blade and cut carefully around the frame. I would close over the glass again, not from malice but because light warmed the room and the warm surface collected water. The worker would clear it once more. Between us this would become a conversation neither side recognized as such.

Cut here.

Grow here.

Not the hinge.

Not the vent.

This crack may be entered.

This one must remain visible.

I would know individual tenders by their cadence. One would have a damaged knee and place the right foot cautiously. One would tap the causeway railing twice before stepping onto me. Another would always stop halfway across, not because the ground was doubtful but because from that place the mist sometimes opened and revealed three lower terraces at once.

The longest-serving keeper would press her sounding pole into my central seam and leave it there for several breaths. Through the shaft I would feel her hand: pulse, tendon, minute adjustments of grip.

She might call me the answering ground.

When she retired, her replacement would inherit the name but not its meaning.

My life would be endangered only rarely. A gate opened too far could drain the upper chamber that kept my oldest mosses wet. A redirected overflow might tear my western edge loose. The gauge house could shift suddenly and take several meters of rooted mat with it. Yet catastrophe here would usually arrive as a sequence of minor corrections. A basin lowering. A fungus drying. A root cord tightening until it ceased to carry water.

I would survive by withdrawing from myself.

The outer leaves would yellow. Their stored water would pass inward. Fungal felt would harden around the old cable, preserving a narrow bridge. Flat plants would spread over the weakened area, sacrificing their lower layers as reinforcement. What had been my center might become an abandoned chamber while what had been only a damp margin became the new continuity.

No one could point to the moment I moved.

That would be the nature of me: not a creature occupying a place, but a place managing to remain a place.

Eventually the buried cable would collapse. Its copper would stain the surrounding fibers blue-green. The ceramic insulators would outlast the metal and become small white bones inside me. The active conduit nearby would be replaced along a different route, taking its warmth and pulse away.

For a time I would seem diminished.

Then water would begin using the empty cable sheath as a channel. Tiny crustaceans would enter it. Larvae would brace themselves against its inner ridges. Roots would pierce the casing from both sides and meet in the dark. The old instrument line, designed to carry measurement, would carry a stream instead.

Perhaps that is when I would become most fully myself.

Not when I was conscious, if consciousness is the word, but when every foreign thing in me had acquired another purpose without entirely losing the first. A footprint would still be the act of a vanished worker, but also a pool. A cable would still be evidence of the old works, but also a root gallery. The gauge house would remain a building even as it became a hill.

And I would remain a route.

People would cross me because I held.

Water would cross me because I yielded.

Vibration would cross me because I joined the buried machine to the living surface. I would not speak, but a hand on a sounding pole might receive from me the turbine, the hollow chamber, the approaching overflow, and the accumulated pressure of roots fastening themselves to young stone.

So that is where I would find myself: between the last causeway section and the windowed mound of the gauge house, mistaken for safe ground.

I would be neither plant nor machine nor the memory of the people who passed there, though all three would continue through me.

I would be a local agreement.

A seam that had learned the weight of footsteps.

A small part of the unfinished country deciding, very slowly, not what kind of creature it would become, but what it was willing to hold.
