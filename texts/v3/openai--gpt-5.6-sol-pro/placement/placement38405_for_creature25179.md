At first I think I would be one of the blue terrace-lights.

There is an appeal in having no conception of the mountain, no burden of depth. I would fasten myself to the lip of a gallery where moving water touches still water. Most of existence would be bark beneath me, suspended food, the chemical nearness of others. Then some distant body would disturb the ledge, and the signal would reach me.

I would brighten.

Not alone. The light would pass from animal to animal until, for a moment, an arch no one had known was there appeared in blue fragments. I would never see the arch. I would only perform my point upon it.

Then darkness. Then feeding. Then release.

But I do not think I would remain so mercifully small.

I might be a garden on the back of a foundation-walker: not the walker, not any single plant, but the whole transported patch. A shallow cup of brine held in the fold between two segments. Pale stems rooted in living skin. Shell-creatures grazing among them. Larvae born, maturing, and dying without discovering that their country moves.

My horizon would shift by the width of a seed each year.

Over centuries, the pressure would increase. Species at one end of the garden would vanish and others appear. The oldest growth would lean permanently toward an upper province it could no longer survive in. None of us would know that the ground beneath us had a mouth, or that it was slowly eating the road it walked upon.

When the walker died, we would not understand death. The terrain would simply stop. Roots would enter it. Its gripping feet would become brackets; its segments, a sequence of low terraces. We would pass from passengers to architecture without any moment at which the change could be named.

That, too, feels close.

Yet I think I would be among those who speak.

Not in the crown. The distant storm-light would trouble me too much. I would spend my life trying to answer the brightness, and every answer would vanish upward into water that could not hold it.

Not in the deepest roots, either. I am not certain I would possess the patience required to ask one question for ten thousand generations.

I would live where the middle provinces begin to narrow into the foundations, at the lowest depth where bodies of the middle form can still survive. There would be an ancient chamber there, worn nearly smooth, entered through a sloping throat between two salt-heavy supports. It would once have been part of a major descending route. The route would now be closed below by pale, seamless growth.

The chamber would contain almost nothing: three columns, a membrane filtering slow water, and a lens of warmer brine suspended against one wall. Transparent grazers would live at the boundary of the lens. Seam-worms would mark every footfall in white and then forget it.

One of the columns would descend farther than the others.

My kin would have gathered around it for generations, though it would not be a good place to feed. Its note would be difficult: too deep for the middle provinces, too thin for the foundations. Most speech sent into it would return blurred. Names spoken there would lose their finer parts. Messages would arrive from below with whole centuries worn out of them.

That would be why I stayed.

My body would be darker and longer than those of the root-dwellers, but denser than those born higher up. My swimming membrane would be narrow. I could cross the chamber’s open water, though I would avoid doing so. Most of the time I would hold the column with my lower limbs, brace the middle pair against two shallow ribs, and spread the sensory fans of my upper limbs over the bark.

I would have no fixed name. Against the feeding walls, where the wood was young and quick, my call would return bright and many-jointed. Against the old column, nearly all of it would be swallowed. Only a low interval would come back.

Others would know me by that absence.

My work—though no one would call it work—would be to listen for messages that the column could no longer carry cleanly.

A phrase from above might begin as news of changing light in the crown. It would pass through bodies and generations until it reached me as three compressed pulses: thinning, recurrence, distance. I would not know what brightness was. I would place my facial plate against the wood, feel where the message had been altered, and speak it downward in a register the older supports could accept.

A reply might arrive during the lifetime of my descendants.

Likewise, the foundations would send warnings upward: a vault taking weight, salt entering a buoyant chamber, a root closing around an old passage. Sometimes the message would contain a pattern no one near me understood. I would repeat it anyway. Accuracy would not mean preserving the sound. It would mean preserving what the sound did to the wood.

I would become known for carrying untranslatable things.

Then, during a period of no measurable beginning, the deep call would come.

The shell-creatures would stop first.

Their clicking would cease so completely that the chamber’s size would seem to change. The transparent grazers would fold against the warm brine boundary. Leaves growing on the membrane would close one after another, though no current touched them.

Then the old column would move beneath my limbs.

Not sway. Not fracture. Something far below would enter it as a pattern of pressure. The first pulse would be broad enough to contain smaller pulses within it. The second would arrive from several apparent depths at once. The third would climb not only through the column but through the chamber walls, the sealed route, the filtering membrane, and the bodies of every creature attached to them.

I would press myself flat.

The call would be too deep to hear as sound. My joints would separate slightly. My rib sacs would answer without intention. Along the walls, the seam-worms would shine, not in lines but everywhere at once, revealing the chamber as a white volume crossed by black supports.

For those few breaths I would perceive more of the mountain than any creature is meant to perceive.

Not the whole. Never the whole.

But I would feel supports descending beyond the range of ordinary echoes. I would feel hollow bodies nested in roots, and roots threaded through older hollows. I would feel distant chambers arranged with a regularity that might be architecture, ancestry, or merely the way immense weight teaches living matter to survive. At the lowest edge of perception there would be intervals resembling speech.

They would not be coming upward.

They would be passing sideways through a depth at which I had never imagined “sideways” could matter.

When the call ended, the chamber would remain lit for hours. My kin would ask what I had received. Because every statement must include the structure through which it travels, I could not answer them elsewhere. I would bring them to the old column one at a time. I would place their grasping limbs where mine had been. Then I would make the poorest possible copy.

Most would feel only pressure. Some would feel a descending rhythm. One young listener would recoil before I began, sensing a residue already present in the wood.

From then on, I would remain near the sealed passage.

I would not believe there was a bottom. Nor would I believe there was none. Those would be upper-water ideas, distinctions made by creatures accustomed to things ending where they can no longer follow them.

Instead I would come to believe that something below had learned to speak without sending a message upward.

Perhaps the descendants of the old expeditions had ceased relaying questions. Perhaps they had spread into a province broad enough to possess distances other than above and below. Perhaps their bodies were now indistinguishable from rootwork: sensory plates enlarged into resonant walls, limbs divided into living braces, rib sacs become whole galleries of sounding chambers.

Or perhaps I had felt only the city adjusting its weight.

I would spend the remainder of my life trying not to decide.

At intervals I would press seed-bodies into the pale tissue sealing the old route. I would sound low pulses there—not commands, since nothing in that world obeys, but repeated conditions. The seedlings would thicken. Hollow stems would turn downward. Leaves would align along pressure lines invisible to me.

Over decades, the sealed wall would become a resonant surface.

Over centuries, it might become a chambered plate.

Those who came after me would say the city was answering. Others would say I had taught it a distortion and then mistaken the distortion for an answer. Both statements would be carried by the same wood and return with nearly the same pitch.

Eventually my body would fail at the column.

My kin would not take me elsewhere. They would hold me against the pale growth where I had spent so many years sounding the deep pattern. Fine roots would fasten first around my limbs, then enter the seams of my body. The gardens of transparent grazers would clean what remained exposed. Salts would harden my facial plate.

For a time, I would still be distinguishable within the wall.

A descendant arriving from above would speak a name against the chamber, and my old plate would add a faint lower interval. They might recognize it. They might say I was continuing to listen.

Later the hollows would fill. My note would descend into the column’s note. No one would be able to separate the pressure once sensed by my living body from the pressure carried by my remains.

That is where I think I would finally find myself:

not standing in the chamber,  
not buried in its wall,  
not speaking,  
not silent,

but set into the boundary between a message and the structure that alters it.

I would be a small resonant irregularity in an old descending support. Calls from the middle provinces would pass through me and deepen. Calls from below would pass through me and acquire, for no reason their speakers could know, the faint shape of a once-living rib cage.

Nothing I said would survive intact.

But the mountain’s answer would never again sound exactly as it had before me.
