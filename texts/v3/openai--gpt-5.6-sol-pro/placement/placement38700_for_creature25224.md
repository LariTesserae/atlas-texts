At first I think I would live at the rim.

It is the easiest place to imagine oneself: where the last roof meets the pale grass, where wind has not yet been broken into drafts and echoes. I could be one of those who carry news inward from the plain, returning with thorn bundles, salt, or rumors of rain. I would know the city as a single hollow full of lamps. I would have distance enough to mistake it for something complete.

But I do not think I would remain there. The rim belongs to those who can tolerate the open horizon. I would keep turning at the edge to look down—not from pride, as visitors do, but with the unease of someone who has left a door unsecured.

Nor would I belong at the active eastern face. I might work there when many hands were needed, hauling baskets or wetting wedges, but I would not be a master cutter. I would be too attentive to what happened after the stone was removed: the dark line appearing beneath new plaster, the unexpected coolness in a storage niche, the drip that began three days after rain.

I would find myself lower down, though not at the absolute floor. Somewhere on the western half of the basin, where the city’s used water and hidden water begin to meet.

My household would occupy rooms behind a retaining wall striped blue-gray and cream. Above us would be a public roof path, so footsteps would pass over our sleeping places from before dawn until the lamps were lit. On the other side of the wall, perhaps, there would be a narrow court containing a fig tree trained almost flat against the masonry. Its roots would not belong entirely to the household that watered it. One root would have found an old drain and followed it beneath three properties, drinking from seepage no agreement had anticipated.

Directions to my door would be:

Go down until the potters can no longer be heard clearly. Follow the wall with the black thumb marks. Pass beneath the fig roots. If the air grows warm, you have gone too far.

The room itself would have begun as an extraction hollow. Its back would still show blunt pick scars beneath limewash. The front wall would be later work, rebuilt twice, with a door lintel far older than anything around it. One corner would remain perpetually damp. We would keep no bedding there, only sealed jars and a shallow stone basin placed beneath a drip that appeared in the cold months.

I do not think I would be born to that household.

Perhaps I would arrive as a child after a failure elsewhere in the city—not a great collapse remembered in songs, but the more common kind: one wall giving way in rain, two rooms spoiled, a family scattered among relations already crowded. I would be taken in because there was space beside the grain jars and because, during the first night, I woke an old woman to tell her that something was scratching beneath the floor.

She would expect a mouse.

Instead, beneath a loose paving slab, they would find water moving through gravel.

Not much water. Barely enough to wet the palm. But enough to explain why the lower plaster had begun to swell.

After that I would be watched for signs of talent. Adults would ask me to stand quietly in unfinished chambers. They would have me press my cheek to walls, tap around sealed niches, smell the air in shafts. Most of the time I would hear nothing useful. I would mistake beetles for running grit and roof traffic for movement in stone. I would give several false warnings and earn an unflattering childhood name.

Little Mouse-Under-Floor, perhaps.

The name would remain long after I grew.

In time I would become one of the people sent for when water appeared where it should not.

Not a cistern keeper exactly, though I would know their work. Not a builder, though I would carry wedges, soot, plumb cords, plaster knives, and little cups for testing seepage. My task would lie between trades. I would trace dampness through occupied walls and decide whether it was a gift, a theft, or a danger.

Water in this city would almost never announce where it began. It might enter through gravel above the eastern cuts, vanish behind a plastered room, soak an abandoned extraction seam, and emerge seasons later beneath a widow’s oven. One household would claim it because it surfaced in their chamber. Another would insist that their roof had fed it. A third would accuse both of blocking an older drain.

I would be called to listen before the arguing became too loud.

My tools would be unimpressive: a copper pin, a bone tube, soot, chalk, waxed thread, several pebbles of known weight, and small unglazed cups. I would press cups against damp walls and compare how quickly they filled. I would stretch thread across cracks and seal it with dots of brittle plaster. I would place pebbles on shallow trays overnight to learn whether a floor had shifted. Sometimes I would taste seep water—not swallowing, only touching it to the tongue—to recognize bitter marl, old ash, salt, or the faint foulness that meant a household drain had entered it.

Most of my knowledge would not be written. It would consist of remembered relationships:

This wall darkens two days after north rain.

That cistern loses water only when the kiln below is hot.

The chamber under the public stair is safe while dry but must be emptied in the cold month.

The fig root marks the old channel, except where it turns toward the blue layer.

The house of Meret owes one jar in five to the rooms beneath, regardless of what Meret’s sons say.

I would keep cords knotted with these obligations. Blue thread for clean seepage, black for drainage, red for danger, undyed wool for uncertain routes. The cords would hang rolled in a plastered niche, useless to anyone who had not learned the order in which I touched the knots. Children would be sent to fetch me and would stare at them while waiting.

By then my public name might have changed. People might call me the Listener Below the Fig, though there would be no grandeur in it. I would spend much of my life crouching beside bad-smelling walls while entire households explained, simultaneously, why the problem had begun somewhere else.

I would not be especially quiet by nature. That would be the private joke of my household. Outside, I would wait through long pauses and speak carefully because a careless judgment could deprive one family of water or bring another family’s ceiling down. At home, beneath the footsteps on the roof path, I would complain about everyone.

I would complain that cutters thought every problem could be solved by removing more stone. I would complain that plasterers hid evidence. I would complain that wealthy houses called ordinary dampness an emergency while poor houses slept beside widening cracks because they could not afford to stop working.

Then, when someone knocked after dark, I would go.

My status would be uncertain but real. I would own little beyond my tools, several good jars, and a mantle patched so often that none of its first cloth remained. Yet I would have access to places closed to most people: family cisterns, disputed rooms, sealed passages opened only for inspection, burial approaches where dampness had appeared. I would know the city from the side, through its walls, rather than from its streets.

This would make me both trusted and unwelcome.

People would feed me when I found a sound wall and remember me bitterly when I did not. I would be accused of favoring lower households because I lived below a public path. Upper households would say I always wanted water to descend. Lower ones would say I did not understand what it meant to wake with another family’s runoff under the bed.

Both accusations would sometimes be true.

The work’s greatest danger would not be collapse. It would be certainty.

The city would reward a person who spoke decisively, especially in a frightened court. There would always be pressure to strike the wall, inspect the dust, and declare: safe, unsafe, yours, theirs, clean, fouled. But stone would answer differently in cold weather. Water would change routes. A crack that slept for twenty years might widen after one badly placed room above it.

I would learn to say, “Not yet.”

Not yet safe. Not yet failing. Do not seal this. Do not deepen that room. Empty the jars and let us listen through the night.

Some would take this for wisdom. Others for cowardice.

Once in my life, I think, I would be wrong in a way that mattered. I would approve a repair beneath an upper terrace, hearing no hollow note after the new stone was set. Months later, following sustained rain, part of the face would shear away. Perhaps no one would die, but rooms would be lost and a stair closed. My soot mark would still be visible on a fallen block.

No formal punishment would follow if I had acted honestly. That might be harder to bear.

For a season I would go often to the rim. I would sit in the grass where sound escaped instead of returning. I would consider leaving with traders, becoming useful in some raised town where water stayed in wells and houses stood apart.

But aboveground walls would disturb me. They would seem too exposed, too dependent on their own narrow foundations. Rooms that did not touch the living face of the earth would feel temporary.

So I would descend again.

In old age, if I reached it, my hearing would begin to fail. This would be publicly inconvenient and privately terrifying. I would compensate with touch, resting my fingertips on stretched cords while another person tapped the wall. Apprentices would do the climbing. Children would be made to describe smells and drafts without being led toward an answer.

My value would shift from perception to memory. I would become the person who knew where the western channel ran before the kiln court was built, who remembered that the dry chamber behind the fig had once flooded to the knee, who could name the dead builder whose wall everyone crossed without thinking.

Eventually people would no longer summon me first. They would summon someone I had taught.

I would remain in the blue-gray rooms, listening to the public feet above. Beside my bed would be the shallow bowl of pebbles, though by then I might no longer wake when they rolled.

So that is where I think I would be found: not at the city’s center, because it has no lasting center; not at its bottom, because it has no final bottom. I would live at a contested level, beside a useful dampness, under a path that belonged to everyone.

And I would be a keeper of things that had not yet decided what they were—a seep not yet a cistern, a crack not yet a collapse, a hollow not yet a room.

Someone who could not promise safety.

Someone asked, nevertheless, to listen.
