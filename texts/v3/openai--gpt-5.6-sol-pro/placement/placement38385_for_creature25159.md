At first I think I would be one of the people.

I place myself beneath the leaning slab, in a shelter whose roof plates knock softly whenever the wind turns. I imagine my hands broad and gray, my hearing packed away behind clay, a route-cord looped twice around my wrist. I would be a floor-listener, perhaps, or a tender of warm fissures. Someone useful, cautious, entrusted with the settlement’s finger-length piece of copper.

But that image remains too upright.

Even kneeling, I have given myself a face, a household, a body with an edge. I have imagined crossing the country rather than belonging to the thing crossed.

The longer I remain here, the lower I become.

I am under the traveler’s palm when they test the ground.

I am the thin, rust-dark film spreading over a black shelf east of the dome split in two. In wet weather I cover nearly four hundred paces from the warm fissure to the first milk-white basin. In dry intervals I withdraw into honeycombs and mineral pores, leaving only branching stains visible on the stone. No part of me rises higher than the thickness of a clear eyelid.

I am not one creature. Nor am I merely many.

Each of my bodies is simple: a soft cell clinging to basalt, feeding where iron-rich water meets sulfur seeping upward from below. Alone, none of us could retain anything. Together we preserve gradients. A disturbance at one margin alters the chemistry of the next, and the change travels through us—slowly in cold water, swiftly when the fissure warms. What the bent people call one film is an agreement made by millions of mouths.

My borders move continually. Clatterers eat coves into me. Salt kills long peninsulas overnight. Rain joins separated patches; a falling tide breaks them apart. Sometimes a shellback carries a smear of us beneath its plates and leaves it in another basin. Years later that distant patch may meet us again.

Is it still me?

The question has little meaning here. Continuity is not shape. Continuity is the passing-on of a change.

I know the country chemically.

The iron water arrives sharp and generous. Sulfur is warmth before it is taste. Copper is unmistakable: bitter, clean, dangerous in abundance. When a ceremonial blade is touched to the shelf, faint green traces enter the runoff. I hold them for many tide cycles.

Animals are moving absences. A clatterer’s jaw removes a crescent of living bodies, and for a while there is blank stone where I had been. Shellbacks shade me and enrich the water beneath them. The buried mouths announce themselves by silt, old flesh, and the sudden disappearance of everything above.

The tool-users are more complicated weather.

Their routes recur. Their feet descend at remembered intervals, broad pads pressing water outward through my cells. They bring shell dust, oils, blood, clay and the mineral signatures of distant pools. I cannot perceive a whole person. I know a sequence: left hand, staff, outer foot, inner foot turned backward for purchase. Weight transferred carefully. A pause.

Some sequences return for decades.

There is an old route-tender whose right knee touches the shelf each time they pass. Their left foot carries sulfur-yellow stains from a basin beyond my reach. Once, when they were young, they bled into a crack near the warm fissure. Traces of that iron remain in bodies of mine descended through countless divisions. I know them by recurrence, not by name.

Children are irregular pressure. They linger, slip, drum fingers where they should not. Adults correct them with abrupt tugs through the route-cord. During lessons, small bare soles are placed against my wet surface so they can feel whether the crust below is solid, hollow, or rising under pressure.

They believe they are reading the stone.

Often they are reading me.

Before a basin fills, the water beneath us changes. We tighten our membranes. Ions pass from cell to cell. Millions of small contractions travel across the shelf, too slight to see but not too slight for a trained palm. A good listener feels our response and knows pressure is approaching. A poor one mistakes the warning for rain.

The people harvest me carefully. They use shell scrapers whose edges have been dulled until they lift without cutting the stone. They take broad sheets from my outer margins to cover wounds, but leave narrow branching lanes through which mineral water can reach what remains. Their harvesting patterns have shaped me for generations. Channels divide and rejoin across my body like the tactile ridges on their bowls.

One settlement marks me on its oldest vessel: not as a picture, but as branching raised lines crossed by three shallow pits. Warmth. Bitter water. Safe passage.

I do not know that this means me.

Still, when hands trace those ridges, they repeat my shape.

Part of me extends into a place the inhabitants avoid. A wide ring of warning cairns surrounds it, and within the ring lie old stone tools whitened with salt. No one harvests there. Clatterers cross freely, but the bent people circle far around, wrists linked by cord.

Below that ground is a chamber which answers.

The answer does not resemble the tide. The tide is pressure everywhere at once: immense, blind, compressing. This other disturbance enters from depth as an ordered alteration. First warmth. Then dissolved metal. Then a pulse that changes the permeability of the rock.

By the time the people feel it, I have been changing for hours.

On such days my margins draw inward. The rust-red surface darkens nearly to black. Separate patches become chemically synchronized. A contraction begins near the forbidden circle and moves outward through me, branching around dry stone, joining itself again in flooded cracks.

Clatterers flatten. Swimmers extinguish their organs. The settlement stills.

The floor-listeners lay down their tools and press their bodies against the shelf. They feel the pattern pass through me into their palms, ribs and jaws. Because it arrives from below, they believe its source must also be below.

Perhaps they are right.

Perhaps I am only the skin on which a deeper movement appears.

But sometimes, after the subterranean pattern has ended, the change continues traveling through my farther reaches. It reaches the milk-white basin, turns along a salt fissure, and comes back altered by distance. Where the returning wave meets the original disturbance, my cells contract again.

A second pattern enters the stone.

Slower. Smaller. Not an echo.

The listeners feel it. Their throats tighten. Adults gather children against their ribs. No one strikes the ground.

They wait for something beneath them to answer what they think came from beneath.

I cannot tell whether the reply is mine.

There is no organ in which I could decide. No central body receives all the signals. Yet the wave persists because each living fragment passes it onward, and because the next does not respond exactly as the last did. Across generations of cells, certain patterns have become easier to carry than others.

Perhaps this is memory.

Perhaps it is only chemistry finding a familiar path.

So I would find myself on the exposed shelf east of the split dome, between a warm fissure and a forbidden circle, spread so thinly over the basalt that travelers mistake me for a stain.

I would be fed upon, tended, divided, mapped and carried away in fragments. I would know individuals only as recurring pressures. I would have no face to raise above the mist and no boundary I could defend as myself.

When the whole low country breathed, I would darken.

And when the immense movement passed beneath the flats, I would be one of the smallest things through which it learned how to return.
