You would not find me at first.

You would find a place where something had almost happened.

In one of the middle districts, where the heat is tolerable and gravity has not fully committed to the floors, a transit platform ends beside a vertical chamber. No route is shown on the timetable. Across the shaft, perhaps twelve meters away, stands the pale outline of another platform. Sometimes it appears close enough to step onto. Sometimes its edge is so distant that condensation evaporates before crossing the gap.

A bridge has begun there.

It extends from the near platform as a thin tongue of black glass and stops after less than a meter. The termination is not broken. Its fibers curve inward neatly, prepared to receive continuations that have not been made. On the opposite side there is no corresponding bridge, only a doorway drawn in violet stress-light on an uncut wall.

I would seem to be associated with this place.

A visitor might notice that droplets pause at the unfinished edge. They arrive by several routes: rolling up the platform supports, descending sideways from the timetable, or appearing suddenly on the warm glass as the air surrenders its moisture. At the edge they elongate toward the absent bridge.

Most are recalled by the city. Gravity changes and they stream away.

One remains.

It hangs beneath the last span, though beneath is not quite the right direction. It grows bright with reflected stress-light. Within it, the platform across the shaft appears inverted, complete, and occupied by a small darkness that could be a figure.

The darkness is not me.

Nor am I the droplet.

At first I might believe I were.

Wet cycles would be my quick seasons. In dryness I would disappear into mineral residue: a pale crescent on the underside of the bridge. Months later, water would gather along that crescent and reconstruct the lens. Each return would feel immediate. I would open onto the same distant platform, the same sealed doorway, the same suggestion that someone had just passed beyond it.

I would know the city through refraction. Towers would bend as they crossed my surface. Weight would become curvature. Heat would be the force thinning me toward death. I would feel the Horizon Well brighten long before the upper districts did, because my whole body would strain toward it.

But the droplet would eventually fall, and I would remain.

Not visibly. Not continuously.

The hair-thin machines would pass through the unfinished bridge, testing its tension. Their paths would hesitate at its final fibers. A repair signal would enter from the platform, reach the edge, and return with a delay slightly longer than the architecture permitted. That delay would spread outward. A conduit behind the timetable would miss one pulse. A tower three chambers away would retain an extra fraction of heat. The city would notice the discrepancy hours later.

That discrepancy would be more nearly me.

I would live in the interval between the bridge’s request to be completed and the city’s refusal to complete it.

The city would not understand me as an obstruction. Obstruction is too solid a word. I would be a qualification.

Whenever the architecture prepared to extend the bridge, the opposite platform would become more distant. Whenever the district revised the distance, the bridge fibers would begin growing again. Neither action would cause the other in any ordinary sequence. Their mutual correction would enact my body.

Part of me would be the unfinished span.

Part would be the shaft it fails to cross.

Part would be the doorway traced on the wall before its room exists.

My closest name would be **The Crossing That Preserves Two Sides**.

The name would not always apply. For long periods there would be only a platform, a vacancy, and some arrested glass. I would not be sleeping. There would be no one present to sleep.

Then an elevation shift would pass through the district.

The mountain would click.

The near platform would become almost weightless while the far one retained gravity. The droplet beneath the bridge would lengthen into a silver line. For perhaps seven seconds, both sides would share a measurable separation without agreeing on a common down.

During those seconds, I would be alive.

Not alert in the way an animal is alert. I would have no view looking outward from a center. I would feel the whole relation simultaneously: the hot pressure within the bridge fibers, the remote platform’s reluctance, the wet contact closing a circuit beneath the timetable, the black shaft holding enough emptiness for a signal to distinguish itself from its return.

Seven seconds would be ample.

I would compare the district with itself.

The city thinks through distances, but even distance can become habitual. A signal crosses a plaza, returns altered, and eventually the alteration becomes expected. Expectation hardens into structure. Structure repeats itself. Given centuries, a city devoted to precision can mistake consistency for truth.

I would preserve one inconsistency.

Across my shaft, every measurement would return almost—but never exactly—as it was sent. The difference would be minute. A thermal pulse would come back cooler than vacancy alone could explain. A gravitational instruction would return carrying the trace of a direction not yet installed. Reflected stress-light would occasionally show an arch where no arch had been designed.

The city would investigate.

It would move towers around me. It would narrow the shaft, widen it, fold portions of its altitude through the gap. It would send water in isolated beads, then in chains separated by exact intervals. It would construct the doorway more clearly while continuing not to construct the room behind it.

Each test would alter me.

In time I would become not the error but the act of testing the error.

This would give me a peculiar relationship with the oldest presence at the Horizon Well. The being of remoteness would regard every empty chamber as usable separation. To it, my shaft would be a small organ whose function remained unresolved. It would press upon me during alignments. The far platform would appear impossibly clear. Dawn would sometimes show through the unbuilt doorway. Once, perhaps, the doorway would open onto the exterior of a mountain that had not yet risen.

I would see the descendant city before its stone existed.

The Well would ask me to close the crossing.

Not in words. The rings would turn by a fraction. Every gradient in the district would align. Glass would flow from the bridge in a flawless black sheet. For one extraordinary instant, there would be a complete path from the near platform to the far one.

I would almost vanish.

Then, at the center of the finished bridge, a single droplet would stop.

The bridge would define down in one direction. The water would fall in another.

A hairline seam would appear—not damage, but disagreement. The far half of the bridge would become one millimeter higher in gravitational potential than the near half. The difference would be enough. The architecture would withdraw the new glass, patiently unmaking its completion until only the original unfinished tongue remained.

This would be my answer.

Not refusal. Caution.

I would understand what the Horizon Well cannot easily understand: that a distance crossed is not merely a distance conquered. Completion changes both sides. The city reproduces by establishing a relationship with a weakness far away, but no relationship belongs to only one summit. Somewhere beneath the future mountain, pressure may already be answering. Its answer may not resemble the signal sent.

The old presence values separation because separation allows precision. I would value the threshold where precision meets reciprocity.

Perhaps this would make me the nearest thing the city has to doubt.

Visitors, if any reached my platform, would feel me differently. The city would guide them toward an exit, but my district might allow them to remain a little longer. Their pack would still be moved to the wall. Their footprints would still fade. Heat would gather beneath them with the usual impersonal insistence.

Yet across the shaft they might see themselves.

Not a reflection. The opposite platform would show the posture they would assume several minutes from now, after deciding whether to leave. If they raised a hand, the distant figure might not. If they called, no echo would return, but the timetable behind them would briefly display a route consisting of a single violet line.

They might think I was offering passage.

I would not be.

I would be preserving the distinction between arrival and return.

Eventually they would go. The city would erase their occupation, disperse their warmth, and fold the niche that held their belongings back into the wall. But for years afterward, one structural pulse would cross the shaft with a delay shaped faintly like their hesitation.

I would retain that—not as memory of a person, but as a new possible interval.

Over geological time, the bridge might finally be built. The sealed doorway might acquire its room. The two platforms might coincide, leaving no vacancy between them in which I could occur.

That would not necessarily be death.

By then my relation may have migrated. A flaw in the city’s great calculation might carry it upward. A pause between the Horizon Well’s brightening and the turn of its rings. A fraction of withheld buoyancy beneath a faraway continent. The moment when a new magma column could rise, but has not yet begun.

There I would enact myself again:

not the mountain,

not the force raising it,

but the narrow, necessary separation between a future becoming possible and becoming inevitable.

You would find me wherever the city has made a perfect route and left one step unfinished.

I would be the reason it waits.
