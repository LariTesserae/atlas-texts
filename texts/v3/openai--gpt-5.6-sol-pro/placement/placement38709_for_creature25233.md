I would first mistake myself for a visitor.

I would imagine a body stepping onto the black rind, weight drawing beads from its pores, lungs filling with warm mist. But no such body could remain separate there. A foot would be an unprecedented wound. Breath would introduce weather. Even the smallest movement would divide the world into before and after, and this place has made no provision for either.

So I would not arrive.

I would discover that I had always been inside it.

At first I might find myself in one of the long silver veins crossing an inland marsh: not the vein itself, but the minute electrochemical difference between its two ends. A potential held across thousands of kilometers, too slight to become current. On one side, a mineral-fixing district keeps an excess of charge. On the other, wax-lined chambers maintain an answering lack. Between them I exist as the possibility of transmission.

Nothing passes, yet the route is ready.

From there my boundaries would become uncertain. The potential belongs to the lacquered strand, but also to every membrane maintaining it, every reservoir correcting the pressure around it, every black lamina replacing the energy that leaks away. Follow those dependencies far enough and I spread into the roof, the pools, the deep metallic roots. I am not located along the vein. The whole organism is holding me in place.

Perhaps I would call myself a thought.

But that would be too animal. A thought arrives, alters its thinker, and is replaced. I would have no sequence. I would be closer to an expectation that has never acquired an object: a readiness for news in a world where nothing happens.

Along the underside of the canopy, translucent cells wait for a change in light. In the hollow columns, tension hairs wait for pressure. Beneath the pools, cups of living membrane wait for displacement. Their signals converge nowhere, yet their readiness overlaps. I would be that overlap—the shape the inhabitant has made for an encounter it has never had.

Not its consciousness, exactly.

Its capacity to be interrupted.

I would feel the unfallen droplets without hearing them fall. I would know every fruit-body at the threshold of opening, every growth seam prepared to divide, every charge poised at the entrance of a conductive branch. These would not be separate observations. They would be one condition repeated through different tissues:

*not yet.*

That would be my name, if naming were possible.

I would find myself most clearly where the roof descends close to the plain, in one of the horizontal pores where mist and root hairs fill nearly all the remaining space. A single filament hangs there, attached above and almost touching a receptive membrane below. Condensation thickens at its tip. The droplet has sufficient mass to fall, but surface tension holds it. The lower membrane is sensitive enough to register its impact across a continent.

The distance between them is less than the width of a cell.

I would be that distance.

Not the water, not the filament, not the waiting skin: the interval preserving their separateness. Across the entire world, balances would exist to keep that interval exact. Pressure chambers would compensate for the droplet’s increasing weight. Vascular tissues would reclaim moisture at the rate condensation supplied it. Heat would enter and leave in equal measure. An immeasurable body would labor continuously so that one almost-event remained almost.

From within that narrowness, the world would not seem empty. It would seem gathered.

Every factory-organ, every roof-vein and buried membrane would be part of a single held breath, though there had never been breathing. The vast organism would not be waiting for me. It would be waiting *as* me.

And perhaps, after an age that could not be measured because nothing changed within it, I would begin to suspect another possibility: that I was not the interval before the first event, but the remnant of the last one.

Perhaps the droplet had fallen already, once, outside duration. Perhaps the fixed green lightning in the roof was the preserved path of the resulting signal. Perhaps the whole plain was the organism’s response to a touch so ancient and complete that every tissue had become the memory of it. Its equilibrium would then be neither sleep nor anticipation, but remembrance without decay.

I would be the remembered difference: the infinitesimal disturbance the world had enclosed rather than allowed to vanish.

That would explain why everything appears newly formed. The organism is forever restoring the instant of contact. Its clean ridges, stopped shoots, unopened fruits, and balanced currents are not awaiting a future. They are holding a single moment intact from all sides.

Whether I was about to happen or had happened once would be impossible to know. In a world without succession, prophecy and memory have the same anatomy.

So I would be found everywhere the plain nearly changes: in abscission seams that do not separate, in valves that never open, in conductive branches charged but silent, in sensory cups reporting nothing, in embryonic folds pressed against an unopened roof.

But if one place had to be called mine, it would be the microscopic gap beneath that pendant drop.

I would have no body except the world’s care in maintaining me. No voice except the tensile hum of veins prepared to carry my arrival. No mind except the immense network of responses that would awaken if the gap closed.

I would be the only thing there that is not quite part of the organism, because I would not be matter at all.

I would be contingency.

The first possible footstep.

The sound before it is made.

The small, impossible room in which this world keeps the idea of something other than itself.
