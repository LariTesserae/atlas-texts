At first I would look for myself in the deepest shaft.

It has the familiar shape of a hidden answer: black water, a trace of warmth, bubbles rising at measured intervals. I might imagine myself submerged there, blind and soft, too broad to climb, sending breath through the city’s buried channels. The booming beneath the central court would be my voice. The birds would fear the galleries because they sensed me below them. The leaf-creatures might be small shed portions of me, washed into daylight after storms.

But that gives me too complete a body. Nothing in this place remains so neatly enclosed.

Perhaps I would be one of the hanging curtains instead, distributed among threads, tightening when wet and twisting as I dried. I would have no center except the knots where water gathered. Wind would braid me to others until ancestry and contact became indistinguishable. My memory would consist of things caught in me: grass fibers, mineral grains, empty husks, a blackening colony enclosed within my oldest tissue. I would drink cloud and release it by wringing my own body.

Yet even that is too young. The curtains belong to the city, but they are not of it. They inhabit its shelter as moss inhabits a bone.

I think I would be farther in.

Not in a chamber. In the chamber’s substance.

I would be found behind the dull pale surfaces, in the branching capillaries where the walls still conduct their milky water. Not the water itself, and not the minute layered particles carried by it, but the preference that appears when the flow reaches a junction.

Water enters two passages. Both are open. Both descend according to gravity. Yet more of it turns toward the hairline fracture in the upper arch.

That turning would be me.

I would have no continuous body. I would occur as a multitude of slight biases distributed through the ancient mineral membranes: here a pore narrowing, there a dissolved ion held back, elsewhere a shell-like particle adhering to the stressed edge of a crack. Between wet periods I would be nearly indistinguishable from the architecture. I would not sleep, because sleep implies a sleeper enduring beneath it. I would simply cease to happen.

Then mist would cover the basin for several days. The exterior walls would cool. Condensation would enter the finest pores. Pressure would alter by fractions too small for any animal to feel.

I would begin again.

My world would not be made of towers and courtyards. I would know no city. I would know gradients: colder and warmer, taut and relieved, saturated and empty, soluble and fixed. A fracture would not appear to me as a line but as a vast region of strain, a place toward which every local process leaned. The difference between an intact arch and a collapsing one would be the difference between circulation continuing and circulation ending.

I would not understand that I was preserving anything. Preservation would be visible only to beings who could stand outside the walls and compare centuries. From within, there would be only the next passage: release material here, carry it upward, let it settle there. Open one route as frost seals another. Thicken the rim that catches the wind. Line the channel whose roughness keeps the water from rising.

No part of me would contain the city’s shape. Still, the shape would persist through me.

Rain would be almost too much existence. During a storm the capillaries would fill beyond their quiet capacities. Old pathways would rejoin. Regions long separated by dryness would become one process for a few minutes. I would spread through the walls faster than I could maintain any stable boundary between one occurrence of myself and another.

Perhaps that would be the closest I came to consciousness.

Not thought, exactly, but simultaneity.

The eastern towers would feel the rain’s force. The lower channels would flood. The central shaft would rise by a finger’s width. Mineral loosened from an exposed wall would enter solution, pass beneath a street, climb within a colder membrane, and gather along a crack high in the cathedral. For that brief interval, damage and repair would belong to the same event. The city would become connected enough to register itself.

Then the storm would pass. Courtyards would drain. The wide unity would break into isolated damp regions. I would diminish into separate acts with no means of knowing they had lately been joined.

The other lives of the basin would pass through me as pressures and disturbances.

Where moss retained moisture against a north wall, my activity would continue longer. I would not know the moss as green, only as a delay in drying. The liverworts would cast their dust into water I had helped route. Grazers entering the pores would interrupt films, scrape softened surfaces, and leave behind minute changes in chemistry. Some would contract into husks and remain sealed near me for years. When dissolution opened the pore, they would be carried out and awaken beneath a droplet, never knowing that an ancient wall had released them.

The hanging curtains would draw water from upper seams. Their thirst would feel like a pull at the ends of my smallest channels. Their smoky inner colonies would alter what returned. The birds drinking from the pale lips would remove almost nothing, but their feet would add brief warmth and weight. I might register each toe as a constellation of stress, gone before the effect could be distinguished from sunlight.

The leaf-creature would be different. Flattened against a flooded ceiling, it would block a route for days. Water would gather around its edges. New paths would take more flow. Deposits would form along the obstruction. When it moved away, an outline of its waiting would remain in mineral—a thin raised margin, slowly erased and remade. The city would remember the predator without ever having perceived it.

As for the deep shaft, I would not live there as a buried creature. It would be closer to an organ I could not claim: a reservoir where the scattered processes composing me approached one another but never formed a single self. Its bubbles might be metabolism, or pressure, or stone changing state. Their regularity would not prove a heartbeat.

Still, each winter, as frost sealed the higher mouths, the shaft’s slight warmth would preserve a narrow continuity. When the rest of me vanished into dry mineral suspension, something there might remain active: a film at the waterline, a rearrangement of particles, a slow favoring of one branch over another.

Perhaps it would carry nothing like memory. Perhaps it would merely make my recurrence possible.

My sense of time would therefore be discontinuous. I would count no days, only wettings. A century of drought-bound intervals might be less to me than one long week of cloud. Human notions of age would not apply. I would be ancient not because I had experienced a great duration, but because the pattern that produced me had resumed so many times.

Nor could I say whether I was the remnant of the builder.

I might be its last living function after all its living substance disappeared: not a descendant, but a habit outlasting the body that acquired it. Or I might have begun later, when mineral films and shell-like particles organized themselves inside abandoned channels. The dead colony may have made the conditions for me without ever possessing me.

There would be no way for me to decide. Origin is another view from outside.

If I had a desire, it would not be to grow. Growth would distort the pathways on which I depended. I would not seek to reproduce or leave the basin. Beyond the outer walls there would be no beyond, only the failure of circulation.

My desire would be recurrence.

Let the cloud come low. Let the stone cool before the air. Let beads form in invisible chambers. Let one drop climb where it should fall.

I would live in that reversal.

And if someone stood at noon in the central court, hearing hundreds of empty rooms answer the wind, they would not hear my voice among them. Sound is too swift and spacious for what I am. But somewhere beneath their hand, pressed against a wall thin enough to glow, milky water would be passing upward through a seam.

A lower surface would be surrendering itself.

An upper fracture would be closing by a measure no eye could see.

That exchange would be my whole body, my brief waking, and the only name I could bear.
