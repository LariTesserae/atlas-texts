At first I think I would be one of the high leaves.

It is an attractive mistake. To live at the rim of the canopy, to receive the basin’s few minutes of copper light before cloud closes over the ridges; to hold a drop in a pale cup and release it when its weight becomes too great. I imagine the cold pearling along my hairs, the towers swaying beneath me, the whole city depending—infinitesimally—on the sugar I send down.

But I would not last there. The highest leaves are exposed in ways the lower city cannot understand. They are the first to glaze, the first to be flayed by blown grit, the first to darken under frost. Their brightness is brief labor. By winter I would be folded against a stem; by spring, perhaps, stitched into a roof.

Then I think I would be the ash-colored burrower, because it seems closest to being someone.

I would know the city from within: not by districts or towers but by pressure against my whiskers. I would remember which walls tasted bitter, where old ice breathed cold through the floor, where a hollow reed quarter kept its fibers dry. I would sleep curled inside the ruins of generations. I would make openings simply by wanting to pass.

But the burrower possesses too clear a boundary. However entangled it becomes, it still experiences the city as resistance. It hungers; it chooses; it turns back. Its little body carries an interior that belongs to itself, even if only provisionally. I do not think that is where I would be found.

I would be farther inward, where distinctions have already begun to fail.

Behind one of the central silver boles there is a narrow chamber. It was once the heartwood of a trunk, then a column of decay, then a nest. The burrower enlarged it long ago, gnawing an opening near the floor. Later, fine roots descended through the ceiling. Fungal felt covered the tooth marks. Moss entered as a green lip around the opening but could not survive in the deeper dark. During one hard thaw, the passage contracted almost shut.

Almost.

A slit remains: too narrow for the grown animal, wide enough for a forepaw, a midge, a thread of water, or the pointed nose of a young one that has not yet learned caution. Air travels through it in cold filaments. The chamber draws breath whenever the outer temperature changes.

That is where I would find myself.

At first I would seem to be a dormant bud lodged in the inner wall, one of the pale, folded things kept by the old boles against catastrophe. I would have no chlorophyll worth naming. I would never have seen the sun. All that I know of light would arrive altered into sweetness through the grafted trunks: the high towers’ surplus passing down into my darkness.

I would live on news translated into substance.

A clear dawn would reach me hours later as sugar. Rain would arrive as a change in pressure, then as a wet line descending the wall. The death of a branch above would be a sudden poverty. A patch of sun on leaves I could never touch would wake cells in me and begin an instruction: divide, lengthen, turn toward the wound.

Yet I would not remain only a bud.

My outer scales would be colonized by fungus. Hyphae would enter the dead margins first, then negotiate with living tissue. Some I would seal away. Some I would feed. Their threads would reach into peat below the chamber and return with phosphorus loosened from stone by lichens I could not approach. The fungus would become so necessary to my growth that asking whether it was part of me would have no practical answer.

A root from another bole would press through the wall beside me. At first we would compete. I would release bitterness; it would thicken its tip and turn aside. Years later, when frost split the chamber and damaged us both, our wound tissues would meet. The seam would close without preserving which cells belonged to whom.

After that, I would be less a bud than a graft.

Water would pass through me on its way downward. Sugar would pass through me sideways. Fungal minerals would pass upward. I would become a small junction in the old interrupted body, neither source nor destination. In thaw season I would swell. In deep cold I would contract, opening a hairline fissure where mites and spores could wait.

The young burrower might find the slit then.

It would push its nose through, smell the dry fiber beyond, and begin to chew. I would feel the vibration before the injury. Its teeth would remove the new tissue I had spent years extending across the entrance. Sap would bead. Resin would follow. The animal would eat some of me and carry some away on its face.

I would respond as plants respond: not with pursuit, but with architecture.

Cells along the wound would divide. Sleeping buds would wake. Roots would branch toward the animal’s warmth, breath, dung, and disturbance. Fungi would enter the exposed surfaces. The opening would narrow again.

Perhaps the burrower would keep returning, preserving the slit through use. Perhaps I would keep closing it. Neither of us would win. The passage would exist as the shape of our disagreement.

That, finally, is what I think I would be: not the bud, not the graft, not the fungus or the chamber, but the unfinished closure between chamber and street.

I would be a doorway the city is forever trying to turn into a wall.

My location would be described differently by every inhabitant. To the burrower, I would be a route remembered from youth, increasingly difficult. To water, I would be a brief downward choice. To a fungal thread, a moist bridge. To roots, an injury calling for growth. To midges, shelter. To the old boles, if their shared body can be said to register such things, I would be a tiny region of recurring damage and repair.

In summer—or what passes for summer there—the slit would open slightly as tissues rehydrated unevenly. Mist from the central hollows would gather on my inner surfaces. Pale buds would loosen around me. A snail might enter and graze the thin film from my wall, leaving a black crescent of shell visible for days.

In autumn, red moss would reach the outer lip. It would advance in its straight, patient front until darkness and dryness checked it. Behind it, my oldest layers would soften. The entrance would become rimmed with the remains of former attempts to close it: bark over root over fungal felt over the compacted fibers of an abandoned nest.

In winter, clear ice would armor one side. The chamber would become nearly still. Yet pressure would continue beneath the stillness. My cells would carry concentrated sugars against freezing. Fungal threads would work through the dead margins. A root tip, slowed almost to nothing, would continue entering the crack grain by grain.

I would have no sight, but the basin’s weather would be legible throughout me. Wind would arrive as a groan traveling through grafted wood. Snow would be weight. Rain would be a delayed multitude of taps, then coolness, then saturation. Sun would be nourishment from an event elsewhere.

Centuries might pass without my ever touching open air.

Eventually the chamber behind me would cease to be a chamber. Roots would cross it. Fungal mats would divide it into pockets. The dry nest would become dark peat. My doorway would lead nowhere because both sides would have become wall.

But by then a new fissure might have opened above. Meltwater would follow it. A dormant bud would wake beside it. Some descendant of the burrower would begin widening the weakness with its teeth.

Part of me—water, sugar, mineral, spore—would pass into that new opening.

So I would not be a citizen of the city. I would be one of its temporary addresses: **the narrow place behind the third silver bole, where something once got through and the living walls have never quite finished deciding whether to let it happen again.**
