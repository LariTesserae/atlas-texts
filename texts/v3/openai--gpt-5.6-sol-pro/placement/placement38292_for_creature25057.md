At first I would choose one of the water pockets.

I would be an amber knot pressed into the last dampness of a crack, containing more lives than my hardened shape admits. I would know the world only when the fog arrived. Water would loosen me into appetite, division, pursuit. An afternoon would be an age; drought, a form of sleep too complete to resemble waiting. I would survive by becoming almost nothing.

But the pools are too self-contained. Their depths open into the mountain, yet their brief inhabitants do not feel the full cleft breathing around them.

Then I would choose the glass shard.

I would lie among hundreds of useless splinters until a hookback lifted me and tested my edge against its armor. I would pass through gripping pads without ever being owned. Egg oil, parasite wax, mineral stain, blood: these would polish a history into me that none of my bearers could read. I would be a tool the mountain had already made, an heirloom without inheritance.

That is nearer. Yet the shard remembers only by accumulating residue. It does not participate in the moment when all the separate lives become one.

So you would not find me in a single fissure or body.

You would find me beginning near the rim.

I would start in the highest veilwing that suddenly folds its throat-fan and stills its membranes. Perhaps it has felt a deep knock through the blade of rock. Perhaps only the animal beside it has gone quiet. The cause would not matter for long. I would pass downward from claw to claw—not as sound, but as the removal of sound.

I would be the descending silence.

Not silence itself. The cleft is never truly silent. Frost continues opening minute wounds in the stone. Water continues falling in hidden chambers. The blind things below continue bracing their long bodies between the walls.

I would be the transfer of attention.

My first body would be a veilwing colony closing around me. Thousands of mouths would shut. Wings would become dark scales against the rock. Eyes would cease answering the wet glints.

As I descended, my body would enlarge.

Hookbacks would stop with glass raised between their forelimbs. Ropelike grazers would feel the interruption of scraping and recoil into their crevices. Silver creatures in the crust would flatten themselves against vibrating plates that no longer vibrated. Even the swimmers in the black pockets would alter their frantic paths when the stone’s continual trembling fell away.

For several breaths, I would borrow every nervous system in the cleft.

I would have no organ from which to command them. No animal would understand the whole of me. Each would know only a local absence: the neighbor no longer feeding, the ledge no longer ticking with claws, the air no longer struck by membranes. Yet those absences would join. The cleft would cease being a crowd and become a single listening surface.

That would be where I found myself: not above or below, but traveling between.

My upper edge would be complete stillness. My lower edge would still be ordinary life—feeding, crawling, rasping, drinking—until I reached it. For a moment I would possess a shape: a front descending through the cathedral wound, visible as folded wings and extinguished eyes.

In that shape I would hear what no individual could hear.

I would hear the blind mouths moving below the pools. I would hear pressure settle through newly born stone. I would hear a crack deciding whether to remain a crack or become a fall that would redirect generations. I would hear one deep knock, then another from a fissure thought to be empty.

The mountain would enter me through every gripping foot, holdfast, armor plate, cyst wall, and sensory filament.

Then one veilwing would call.

The note would puncture me somewhere unpredictable. I would not know whether it came from fear, defiance, confusion, or the simple inability to remain closed. Others would answer. The call would spread faster than I had descended, thickening among the pinnacles until every surface rang.

I would not vanish exactly.

I would turn inside out.

The attention I had gathered inward would be expelled as the chorus. The body that listened through stillness would speak through ten thousand throats. Rock plates would ring. Condensation would break loose. Grazers would extend into space, and hookbacks would lower their shards. The cleft would rain briefly from the force of its own voice.

If asked what kind of being I was, no taxonomy would hold me. I would not be a veilwing behavior, because the hookbacks and grazers and pool-life would become part of me. I would not be the colony, because the colony persists while I exist only for a few breaths. I would not be the mountain, though stone would be my largest sensory organ.

I would be a temporary animal made from permanent neighbors.

My skeleton would be the cleft.  
My nerves would be proximity.  
My blood would be interrupted vibration.  
My inhalation would descend from the rim.  
My exhalation would be metallic and deafening.

Afterward, nothing would preserve me whole. There would be no nest, carcass, or mark saying that I had passed. My memory would remain distributed: in the readiness of veilwings to fold when those above them fold; in the hookback’s instinct to freeze when scraping ceases; in the grazer’s recoil; in the altered movement of swimmers beneath a suddenly quiet surface.

I would die back into reflex.

And when the depths knocked again, those scattered pieces would assemble me once more.

From outside, no one would see me. They would see only a barren summit and perhaps a crooked seam breathing warmth into the cold.

To find me, they would have to lean over the rim at the dangerous moment—not while the cleft clamored, but just as the quiet began to descend.

Then they might feel themselves included.

Their breath would stop with the wings.

Their hand would still against the glass.

And for several heartbeats, I would be larger by one more listening body.
