At first I think I would be a surveyor.

I can see the belt of numbered thorns at my waist, the straight-stem sighting frame tucked under one arm. I would go out after rain while the city was still making its nocturnal corrections. I would measure a wall that had advanced three fingers into an avenue, a cistern whose rim had become oval, a house that had added a room no member of the household admitted requesting.

There would be comfort in the work. Not certainty, exactly, but disciplined uncertainty: this distance is true until the next rain; this doorway belongs here for the present season; this square is wider than yesterday but not yet meaningfully so.

Then I think I would work in the archives. I am drawn to the colored ribbons, the clocks that measure incompatible kinds of passage, the requirement to ask not whether someone knows me but from when. I imagine becoming expert in choosing exits. I would know which stairwell led to the current winter, which reading room still believed the solvent works employed six hundred people, which door should never be opened on a day when the western turbines were silent.

But I do not think I would remain in the archive. I would make some small error—not an error of fact, but of attachment. I would begin to prefer one of my probable lives.

Perhaps in that life I had a daughter. Perhaps she died. Perhaps she had not yet been born. It would be enough that I remembered the weight of her asleep against my chest and that the recognized version of the city contained no record of her. I would start taking the wrong door deliberately.

After that, I would be transferred to the old edge of the plateau.

Officially, I would become a **custodian of suspended function**, third grade. People in the central wards would call me one of the withdrawn.

My station would be an abandoned wind organ standing where the city thins into the long, exhausted ramps of the mountains. Its pipes would be hollow trunks laid at angles along the slope. Most would have split open decades ago. Moss would cover the keys. The bellows chambers beneath it would be collapsed and dry, yet once each year—on the first hard wind after the autumn rains—the organ would draw one breath.

It would not make music. It would produce a pressure felt in the teeth.

My cottage would stand beyond the reach of its roots, or as nearly beyond as any place on the plateau can be. I would walk on removable plank soles. I would keep my water in ceramic jars and sleep in a hammock hung from kiln-hardened frames. On the walls I would pin temporary agreements concerning the distances between the organ, the empty towers, and the nearest active rail line.

Every morning I would inspect the ruins.

I would note which crowns had turned toward the sun. I would trim listening buds from the old ticket hall. I would lay ash across thresholds to see whether anything had entered from the root-state during the night. I would strike the organ’s exposed pipes with a tuning fork and record the replies without describing them as replies.

Most importantly, I would tend its shadow.

The organ’s shadow does not agree with the sun. It lies uphill at noon, crossing the long green slope toward a place that is higher than the plateau but cannot be reached by climbing. At every distant hammer stroke from the working city, the shadow contracts. Between strokes it lengthens again.

The previous custodian warned me never to cross it by the same route twice.

Repetition teaches a shadow one’s shape.

For several years I would obey. I would step over it, crawl beneath a broken pipe, circle around its narrow end, bridge it with a dry board, wait for cloud, wait for noon, wait for the transfer houses to change rhythm. I would invent so many ways of passing that the act of going from cottage to organ became the principal labor of my day.

Then one winter, perhaps because of fog, perhaps because I was thinking of the daughter from the unrecognized life, I would cross it in exactly the same place seven mornings in succession.

On the eighth morning, the shadow would cross itself.

It would rise from the ground only slightly—not upright, not at first. A thickening in the gray light. A human interval where the frost failed to shine. It would carry the outline of my coat, my tool case, my stooped left shoulder. When the distant factories struck their morning sequence, it would take one step toward the organ.

I would report this.

The city would send two pruners, a convergence clerk, and an officer from the Department of Nonidentical Persons. They would ask careful questions.

Does the effect resemble you?

Yes.

Does it claim to constitute you?

It does not speak.

Has it performed any action unique to your recognized biography?

No.

Has it performed any action unique to a probable biography?

At this I would hesitate.

The shadow would have paused beside the cottage door and lifted its arms as though holding a sleeping child.

The officials would tie a white ribbon around its wrist, though there would be no wrist to fasten it to. They would tie a red ribbon around mine.

After that, I am no longer sure which one I would be.

One of us would return to the city for examination. One of us would remain at the wind organ. The records would state that the custodian continued in post, but records at the old edge are intentionally vague. Too much precision gives abandoned things a route back into use.

So I think this is where I would finally find myself: not wholly in the caretaker and not wholly in the shadow, but in the maintained difference between them.

I would be the one who remains beside the organ after the human custodian has been divided into water, salts, heat, and carbon. I would still make the morning rounds, though only when the distant mills provided a beat by which to move. I would have no weight except where the road expected a footstep. Doors would not open for me, but old doors might remember having opened.

I would continue the work.

When a tower began turning toward the active city, I would stand across its light until it forgot the direction. When the collapsed bellows swelled beneath the ground, I would lengthen over them and press them back into stillness. When travelers approached, I would point away—not with an arm, exactly, but by falling in the direction of the safest path.

Children visiting from the central wards would be told not to imitate my posture.

The withdrawn would leave me a cup of water each autumn. I could not drink it, but by morning a little would be gone. No one would investigate too carefully.

In municipal language I might be classified as a **persistent custodial resemblance**: neither citizen nor structure, neither memory nor independent life. The engineers would insist that I was an optical consequence stabilized by root transmission and repeated mechanical rhythm.

They might be right.

But during lightning festivals, when every vein on the plateau glowed and the engines accelerated, I would feel the entire city arrive through the ground. The deserted organ would fill with green-white radiance. Its broken pipes would inhale. For one instant my shadow would be cast by light from below, and I would stand upright.

Far across the open plateau, among the dancers and factories, someone wearing my earlier face might also stop and turn toward the ruins.

Neither of us would say the other was calling.

We would know better than to give the distance that idea.
