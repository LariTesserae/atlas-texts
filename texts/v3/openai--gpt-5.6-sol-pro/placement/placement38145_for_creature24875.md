At first I think I would be one of the long-winged birds.

It is easy to imagine: a dark body held between mesas, wings scarcely moving, distances joined by an anatomy made for surrender. I would know the invisible roads. I would enter a current below the forest line and emerge above cloud, carrying the shape of one valley’s weather into another. My flock would be too widely scattered to look like a flock.

But that life is too clean. It passes over the handholds, the sleeping hollows, the small obligations waiting under stone.

Then I think I might be a bell crawler, one smoky plate among thousands, shifting in a vent too deep for daylight. No individual song, only the alteration made by many bodies turning together. I like the thought of speech that cannot be separated from shelter, weather, and the bodies of others.

But I do not remain there either.

I find myself near a place called, in route speech, **the Shelf Where Three Drafts Disagree**.

It is not a settlement so much as a waiting place. A dark overhang projects from the lee side of a hollow mesa. Beneath it are four padded chambers, a cooking pit, six old sleeping niches, and a narrow cleft that breathes cool air from an unseen river. The inhabited shelf is no wider than a house, but the cliff below it drops through veils of vine into a depth that never receives full sun.

Three routes once met there. Now only two do reliably.

The western route comes by a wall of black-veined cushion plants and a ladder kept rolled in a dry fissure. The upper route descends through pale forest, following reeds that sound a descending interval when the air is safe. The third route crosses a naked shelf beyond a stone arch. It can still be seen. No one has used it for nine years because its warning chord has ceased to be consistent.

I am the keeper of that inconsistency.

I am human, though from a distance I may look like part of the ledge: a small figure in a layered bark-cloth coat, its shoulders sewn with old crawler plates, sitting with both palms against the stone. I am no longer young. My left ear hears almost nothing but a soft internal rushing. My right can distinguish voices in a padded room, but in the open the Roar becomes one white pressure.

This is considered useful.

I do not hear the mountain’s loudest claims anymore. I feel what lies beneath them.

My shelter-name is a pressure pattern tapped against the wrist: two quick beats, a pause, then a long firm press. Strangers translate it as **Aftertone**, though that is not quite right. It means something closer to *the vibration that remains after everyone else believes the sounding has stopped*.

I was born four valleys east, in a bowl where cup trees grew taller than people. That shelter no longer exists. During a long silence the bowl began inhaling. Its forest folded inward, and when the Roar returned, warm spray rose from the soil in narrow columns. No one died, but the sleeping chambers filled with roots and mineral water before the next wet season.

We dispersed along four routes. I remember this not as exile but as a multiplication of home. Each household took cuttings from the same orchard vine. Years later I have eaten its fruit at three distant shelters, each claiming that its vine is the original. All of them are correct enough.

For much of my life I traveled. I carried copper sounding leaves, crawler shell, sealed packets of fern meal, wax, orchard cord, and route accounts committed to touch. I learned to sleep with one hand closed around a braided line. I learned that fear has different textures in different bodies: trembling, rigidity, talk, laughter, meticulous preparation. I learned not to trust the person who says they are never afraid.

At the Shelf Where Three Drafts Disagree, I stopped—not permanently, because permanence is an insult here, but for longer than intended.

The former keeper had died. His funerary reed still sounded in the cold cleft on certain mornings, and the shelter needed someone who knew enough routes to recognize when a traveler’s account contradicted the mountain. So I remained through one storm season, then another.

My work is to maintain a place where no one is required to continue.

That is more important than it sounds. Routes exert a pressure of their own. A traveler who has crossed two dangerous intervals may begin to believe that turning back would make the danger meaningless. A messenger carrying urgent news may mistake urgency for permission. Young route-keepers especially imagine that courage is a kind of momentum.

I give them water. I make them sit. I place their hands against the western wall and ask what they feel.

Sometimes I let the palmback decide.

Her name is rendered by laying a flat hand on the breastbone and curling the fingers inward. In spoken trade language I call her Tuck. She is old, broad, and hostile to nearly everyone. She sleeps beneath bundles of dry pith and emerges whenever food is being cut or danger is already too near to avoid. Her belly pads are more accurate than my copper leaves. If she wedges herself beneath the root beam, the ladders come in, however clear the sky appears.

Travelers resent taking instruction from an animal until the stone coughs.

I tend gardens scattered across the nearby faces: bitter fern beside the river cleft, two cord-fruit vines under the eastern lip, waxleaf in a crack that receives nine minutes of rain during a common western storm. There is also a colony of medicinal cushion plant across the abandoned route. I can see it from the shelter, dark and healthy, spreading slowly over the red wall.

I have not touched it in years.

This, too, is part of tending: knowing when a garden no longer belongs to human reach.

Children arriving at the shelf are fascinated by the unusable path. Adults see a deathtrap; children see a surface with handholds. I show them the old route record. Its cord has seven knots, three shell pieces, and a bird quill split lengthwise. Then I show them the newer record: a plain loop tied shut.

Not forbidden, it says. Not passable by anything we know.

Most evenings I inspect the tuned reeds. I replace those softened by mist and retune those insects have entered. Every reed must speak clearly without pretending to say more than it knows. A route chord does not mean *safe*. It means *the conditions under which this route was last crossed are present again*.

That difference has kept me alive.

During the quiet hour before sunset, I sometimes sit at the shelf’s edge. Across the void, a green escarpment catches the light. Below, the vines turn their free ends in currents I cannot feel. Long-winged birds rise one at a time from the lower dark. If rain is falling in the western valley, it appears close enough to touch, though reaching it would take two days.

I do not feel small there. Smallness depends on comparison, and the High Roar has stripped comparison from the land. I feel exact: one body on one ledge during one usable interval.

Then comes the silence.

Not every silence is the great silence, but no one knows which kind has begun.

The bass pressure disappears first. My crawler plates stop clicking. The orchard cords go slack. Tuck crawls from her nest and presses herself against my ankle, not hiding, which is worse.

If travelers are present, I pass them the common line. If I am alone, I wrap it once around my wrist and sit with my back against the inner wall.

Then I recite what is not known.

I have done this by voice, by hand, and once through a broken wrist by tapping my heel against the floor. The words are never comforting. Comfort is not their purpose. They keep the mind from filling uncertainty with invention.

The wind has not ended.

The mountain has not died.

The old route is not promised.

The next sound is not yet danger.

The next sound is not yet safety.

In the longest silence I have known, cloud came down past the shelf and continued falling. The vines lifted toward it. Drops of water emerged from the river cleft and traveled upward along the wall. Across the void, the forest bowed in several directions at once.

Nothing moved for the length of forty breaths.

Then the abandoned arch gave a note I felt through my teeth.

It was not its old note.

The sound rose rather than deepened, and somewhere inside the mesa a bell-crawler colony shifted in one vast mineral whisper. Tuck bit through my sleeve. The shelf tilted—or seemed to—and the three drafts became one warm exhalation carrying leaves, moths, and a rain of translucent seeds.

Afterward, one of the reliable routes was gone.

The abandoned route had become quieter.

For three days I did not cross either.

That restraint is the act for which I am best known, though nothing happened during it. On the fourth day, two other route-keepers arrived by the upper forest. Together we tested the changed wall. We sent weighted cords across it. We watched basin water. We set fresh reeds and waited through dusk.

The old forbidden shelf did not become a road again. But it became the beginning of one.

The route now extends only as far as a shallow niche beneath the arch. A person may wait there while the eastern draft holds. Beyond that, the stone remains unreadable. Perhaps someone in another generation will add the next interval. Perhaps the arch will change first.

I am content to leave an unfinished path.

When I die, they will wrap me in bark cloth and lower me into the river cleft. I have already chosen the reed: a long-wing quill reinforced with copper thread, tuned not to the shelter’s main chord but to the faint interval made by the disagreeing drafts.

For a few years, perhaps, I will sound whenever the hidden river rises and the mountain breathes outward.

Travelers will sleep above me. Children will be told not who the reed was, but what its note means. Tuck, if she somehow outlives me, will dislike whoever takes my niche. Someone else will retune the warning lines and tend the waxleaf crack. My orchard vine will be carried away after a storm and root on a cliff I never saw.

Eventually the cleft will alter. My reed will split, or fall silent, or begin sounding under conditions no one recognizes.

Then I will have gone farther into the mountain.

Not vanished. Not remained.

Changed from a keeper of intervals into one of the intervals being kept.
