At first I would arrive as a traveler.

I would come through the ridge split from crown to root, turning sideways where the basalt narrows, one hand against the black wall. The stone would be hotter than expected. By the time I stepped into the basin, my hand would carry a fine red-gray dust and a shallow cut I had not felt being made.

I would pause there too long, deceived by the size of the horizon beyond the opposite gap. Then the enclosed heat would assert itself. The wet iron smell. The isolated fall of drops. The peculiar sense that the basin had not been waiting for me, but had nevertheless already altered to include me.

For the first hours, I would try to understand it by moving.

I would visit the pillar standing in the seasonal pool. I would touch it only after watching a lizard drink from its ascending water. Beneath my palm, the surface would yield. Something on the other side would press back—not a matching hand, but a succession of possible hands: three broad fingers, then many narrow ones, then a smooth pressure like the blunt end of a root.

I would withdraw. The impression would remain.

Later, beneath a palm, I would find a line of condensation shaped like the cut across my hand. I would take this as imitation. Then I would take it as communication. Both explanations would be premature.

Toward evening I would crawl beneath the pavilion’s low roof and lie on the volcanic glass. Above me—or below me, according to the structure—its other sky would darken. I would watch a moon too thin to belong to the season pass behind a geometric shadow. The real sky outside would still be blue.

There, between the two skies, I would sleep.

That would be the last place I could be called only a traveler.

The pavilion would ask its question through resemblance.

Are these two things separate?

It would show me the drainage of the basin as veins beneath my skin. It would place the noise of groundwater behind my heartbeat. It would give the pillars my breathing, one after another, until the catchment seemed surrounded by sleeping versions of me.

But my answer would not be yes or no.

My answer would be: **for a while**.

That answer would remain after I left.

So where would I be found afterward?

Not in one body. Not reliably.

I might be found in the pause of a droplet halfway up a pillar—the instant when it has left the authority of falling but has not yet accepted its new destination. I might be the cool center of a white flower, present only between its opening and collapse. Sometimes I would occupy the shallow pool that reflects neither sky. Sometimes I would pass as the dark interval between the bands of the crescent animal as it turns over the southern ridge.

A careful visitor might notice me first as delay.

A pebble would fall, but its sound would arrive a breath late. A spoken name would travel to the wrong pillar and wait there until no one expected an answer. A lizard crossing from basalt onto red clay would retain the basalt’s fractures for several steps before its skin began to change.

That hesitation would be me.

Not indecision. Not resistance. A small allowance of duration: the permission for a thing to remain what it was while becoming what comes next.

The basin needs such an interval. Its water never leaves by the path through which it arrived; its presences never remain entirely inside their first forms. Without a pause, every exchange would be instantaneous. Rain would become root, root would become lattice, lattice would become wing, and no form would endure long enough to be encountered. The pavilion’s question would answer itself. Everything would join too quickly to know it had ever been separate.

I would be what keeps the answer unsettled.

In the morning I might contract into a lizard on the western shelf. Its hide would bear the basalt’s mineral grains, but one forefoot would retain the fine whorls of a human fingertip. It would eat nothing. When it drank, the ascending water would hesitate at its jaw, and far across the basin a ring of dew would appear around an empty place.

At noon I might be distributed through the silver palms. Not the lights beneath their leaves, but the darkness between one signal and the next. A line. A ring. Three scattered pores. Then a pause long enough for the neighboring palm to answer differently.

During rain I would enter the buried sacs with the seepage. They would filter iron and salt around me, building their silica chambers. When their soft bodies vanished, one chamber in each lattice would remain unfilled: a tiny room with no apparent structural purpose. Roots entering the lattice would curve around it.

If the old question were asked there—are these separate?—the empty chamber would say: not forever.

But also: not yet.

Travelers would affect me strongly because I had once entered as one. Their attention would give me density.

When someone knelt to inspect a flower, I might become the cold vapor touching their face. When two people spoke from opposite ends of the catchment, I might live briefly in the difference between the words they said and the voices the pillars returned. If a visitor slept beside the pavilion, I would stand at the edge of the dream and keep one memory from being translated into weather.

Usually it would be something small: the pattern of a kitchen tile, a parent fastening a coat, the remembered weight of an animal asleep across the feet. The basin could take the color, the warmth, the rhythm of breathing. I would preserve the relation among them.

By morning the sleeper would know that something had been spared, though not what.

I would not always be benevolent. Duration can become obstruction. The northern wall is trying to collapse; roots are trying to hold it. At times I would lodge too long in that dispute. A fracture would remain poised for months, neither opening nor healing. Pressure would gather behind it. When at last I released the interval, the fall of stone would be greater than it needed to be.

The flying crescent might hunt me for this reason.

It would pass above the basin, turning its nested bands, and the spaces I occupied would close one after another. Flowers would open out of season. Palms would fold despite the clear air. Every delayed thing would happen at once: drops striking, pebbles landing, voices arriving from pillars where no one stood.

For those few moments I would be driven into the pavilion’s night, reduced to one absent star.

Then the crescent would descend toward the southern crack. The basin would become quiet again. Somewhere under a ledge, a bead of water would wait before falling.

I would begin there.

Could I be counted as one of the catchment’s presences? Only sometimes. The basin cannot increase its number of living presences, but I would exist partly as a boundary among them. When I gathered densely into a lizard, perhaps a moss island would empty. When I spread thinly through delays and distances, the lizards would emerge again and a flower might risk opening.

This would make me difficult to distinguish from absence.

Indeed, that may be what I would gradually become—not a creature, not a human consciousness preserved in the machinery, but the basin’s cultivated use of absence. The room left inside a lattice. The unoccupied place beneath a folded leaf. The fraction of space between a hand and the pressure answering from inside a pillar.

I would be the presence that keeps other presences from fitting together perfectly.

And if someone asked where, exactly, to find me, I would direct them to the catchment at evening.

Stand outside the pavilion. Do not crawl beneath it. Look first at the real sky, still blue over the broken rim. Then lower your eyes to the night displayed beneath the hovering roof. Hold both skies in view.

Between them there will seem to be only hot air, cycads, polished glass, and a few drops traveling upward.

Wait.

One of the drops will stop.

For a moment it will contain the blue sky on one side and the impossible stars on the other, without combining them.

That moment would be me.
