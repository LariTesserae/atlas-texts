At first I would think I lived high in the branches.

I would look for myself among the little browsers, because their life has the clearest shape: bark underfoot, leaves within reach, the herd warm at night. I would want their revolving ankles and grave, delicate faces. I would want to sleep in a chamber grown around the breathing of generations, then wake bearing some portion of another animal’s pattern on my coat.

But I do not think I would belong so securely to my body.

Perhaps I would be one of the wind-breasted birds. I would inhabit the open volume that gives the settlement its deceptive emptiness. I would sing by turning. I would know weather as music passing through bone, and remember every court by the chord it made when crossed. Yet the birds are too swift and definite. Even when their songs arrive out of order, they know where to nest.

Nor would I be one of the beetles, though there is something appealing in their hidden civic labor: to live inside a wall that is also a body; to eat away whatever has ceased to belong; to make the city’s deepest thunder by doing work no larger than a fingernail. But they are citizens of substance. Their world can be touched.

I think I would be found elsewhere.

Not beneath the floor exactly, though I would sometimes pass there. Not in the central pool, though it might show me more clearly than ordinary water. I would be found in the broad empty crossings—in the intervals between branch and branch, between one side of an avenue and the other, where the city’s rule requires that every passage be answered by a contrary passage.

I would be the thing that answers.

No established species would claim me. The browsers might treat me as a browser at a distance, though I would be too thin and my legs would bend the wrong way. Birds would see something bird-shaped moving below them through the air without wings. Lizards would recognize the flash of my flanks but not my smell. In rain I might acquire the pale softness of a fish; at dawn I might be mistaken for a shadow that has forgotten its owner.

My body would not be assembled from repeated sound, but from interrupted crossings.

A bird starts over the central court. At the same moment, far below, a beetle turns back inside the bark. Their motions nearly oppose one another, but not quite. The open space waits. The rule strains. Out of the small discrepancy I take a step.

The first step gives me feet.

A browser ventures onto an exposed root-ramp. Nothing visible moves against it. I pass the other way beneath the rind, and the green scales rise slightly over my back. That passage gives me a spine.

A fish swims through a submerged gallery during rain. I travel above it through the branch that roofs the gallery, mouth opening and closing on sap. That gives me gills, though I may not need them again for days.

I would accumulate anatomy in this fashion: provisionally, through service. Each part would last only until some crossing required another shape. I might have antelope hooves before noon, the flat belly of a lizard by evening, and at night only a line of dimples moving over the floor. What remained constant would be direction. I would always be going the other way.

Most creatures would notice me only when I failed to appear.

A young browser steps into an empty court and freezes, one forehoof lifted. No bird crosses overhead. No insect passes below. The opposite direction is vacant. Then there is a slight movement in the pool—not a reflection, but something under the reflection—and I emerge from the far edge, walking toward the browser.

We pass at the center.

For that instant I look exactly like it, except that my leaf-horns are budding in the dry season and my eyes contain the branch road behind me rather than the road ahead. The browser reaches safety. I continue toward the place it came from, becoming less like an animal with every step. At the shadow of the arcade, I fold through a seam and lose my bones.

I would have no den. Instead, I would have many places where I was almost resting.

There would be a shallow groove beneath an arch where the condensation runs uphill. Beetles would keep it polished although they never entered it. There would be an unopened flower near the western pools that was always warm but never contained a frog. In one of the old trunks, a narrow hollow would give back every sound except footsteps. The browsers would avoid sleeping over it. Birds would occasionally bring nesting fiber there and then forget why.

These would be traces of me, not dwellings.

My nearest thing to a home would lie under the central pool that never reflects the present. Beneath its black surface, the city’s ages cross one another continuously. Ancient animals approach water as future animals withdraw. Fallen arches rise downward into saplings. Birds strike through rings of rain and come out carrying seeds from forests that have not yet grown.

There, the law of opposite passage is always satisfied. There, I could become almost substantial.

If someone looked into the pool at the correct moment—not directly downward, but across it while a drop was still rising back toward a branch—they might see me standing in the reflected court. I would be taller there. Not large, but complete: four narrow legs, a many-jointed body, a breast open to the wind, and a head divided into two profiles facing away from each other. Along my back would grow a small avenue of pale trunks. Tiny creatures would cross between them.

That would not be my true form. It would be the pool’s attempt to show all my borrowed forms at once.

I would possess one call, though I would never make it twice in the same way. Exact repetition would give the call another body, and I could not allow an identical creature to answer me forever. So the first utterance might be a wooden frog-knock, the next a beetle rasp, the third a note piped through the openings of a bird’s chest. To an inattentive listener they would seem unrelated. To the trunks they would mean one thing:

*The way is answered.*

The city might depend on me, but it would not know me as an individual. I would be one of its accommodations, no more a ruler than a gutter is ruler of rain. Perhaps there would be many of my kind. We could never meet, because two of us approaching each other would satisfy our purpose and dissolve at the point of crossing. We might know one another only by absences: an avenue already made safe, a tremor in the floor, an unfamiliar limb fading from our own bodies because it had been used elsewhere.

Sometimes I would go to the gate.

I could not pass through it. To cross the boundary I would need something outside to enter in the opposite direction, and the outer country has no knowledge of this obligation. Its plants stand separately. Its roads permit solitary travel. Things there go from one place to another without being answered.

I would wait between the two pale trunks and watch animals depart.

Their shadows would fall backward into the city. Most would turn at once and resume familiar lives, but now and then a shadow would linger beside me. Because I would have no stable surface, it could not attach itself to my feet. Because I would have no fixed outline, it could not imitate me. We would stand together at the threshold: the shadow without its animal, and I without a shadow.

Perhaps that would be the closest I came to companionship.

When the departing animal eventually returned, transparent and frightened, I would cross inward as it crossed inward. For one pace we would occupy the same direction, violating the rule. The floor would pucker. The trunks would give a deep, premature boom. Somewhere in the settlement a seedpod would burst before it had formed.

Then I would turn inside the animal’s returning shadow and emerge going outward—not beyond the gate, but into the city’s idea of beyond.

There I might discover the second daylight sky beneath the roots.

I imagine it not as another clearing but as the underside of every open space: blue distances folded into the soil, clouds passing through root hairs, rain falling upward into the pools. The branches above would be roots there, and the roots above would arch like avenues. Creatures that vanish while crossing gaps might travel briefly through this country. The missing middles of snakes would hang in its sky like green ribbons.

I would walk its empty roads in reverse, answering the passages of animals in the clearing overhead. With each counterstep, a bird would be permitted to fly, a lizard to sprint, a browser to venture down and drink.

No one would see this labor. They would see only that the city remained traversable.

And when I died—if a being assembled from permissions can die—I would not leave a carcass. One morning an animal would step into the central court and find that no answering movement came. It would wait. The open air would tighten around it.

Then the city itself would respond.

A branch would lean one way while its shadow leaned another. Water would run east beneath the floor and west above it. A flock would begin its song at both ends. The whole clearing, for one breath, would cross against itself.

From that immense contrary motion, something small would take a first step.

Perhaps it would have feet.

Perhaps it would be me again.
