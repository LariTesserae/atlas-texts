At first I would think I belonged high in the towers.

I would imagine the gray harness, the reed brush, the careful passage between ceramic fins while the whole town breathed through steel around me. I would know where to place my boots without looking. Snails would wait in the damp crook of my arm while I scraped mineral crust from a gutter. From the walkway I would see every concentrated green place—the willow lines, the rectangular marshes, the pear walls—as if the town had drawn its hidden water in leaves.

But I do not think I would remain there. Height would make the town too legible. I would begin looking down whenever a coherent drop escaped the copper pan. I would want to know where it went.

So I would find myself lower.

Not in the great cisterns, exactly, though I would often descend into them. Not among the municipal clerks, though my records would occupy a shelf in their felt-hung rooms. I would work in the narrow territory between survey, drainage, and rumor. My official title would be something cumbersome—*Inspector of Unjoined Runoff and Incidental Fall Lines*—but no one would call me that.

I would be the downhill clerk.

Every town that shifts by millimeters eventually needs one.

My workshop would occupy a former signal cabin behind the unfinished municipal building, where the ivy has entered through the upper windows but not yet reached the floor. The cabin would lean slightly toward valve square. This would be useful. Along one wall I would keep tilted boards surfaced in slate, glazed brick, tin, kiln tile, waxed cloth, old window glass, and samples of the local slag. I would test coherent water on each of them, watching how quickly the drops moved, whether they hesitated at scratches, whether they could cross rust, whether snail trails of different ages altered their course.

No two drops would behave quite alike, despite the children’s certainty that they did.

My work would begin when someone came to the door and said, very quietly, close to my face, “We’ve lost some.”

They would rarely know how much. A forbidden schoolroom bottle might have cracked behind a radiator. A collection gutter might have carried coherent beads past its screen. A coat pocket could have been dripping separate water through three streets before its owner noticed the cold line along the seam.

I would take a folding rod, copper wire, chalk, a shallow charcoal box, and a small mirrored instrument borrowed in principle from the surveyors and modified until they refused to recognize it. Then I would walk downhill.

That sounds simple. In this town, it would not be.

Downhill would alter from season to season and sometimes from room to room. A floor might slope east above the joists and west beneath them. A drop could pass through a drawer, follow the warp of a cabinet, descend inside a wall, cross a pipe made warm by waste steam, and emerge two houses away in the soil beneath a cabbage bed. Coherent water would not seek mystery. It would only obey every tiny inclination that ordinary water blurred over.

Following it would make me intimate with the town’s least visible shape.

I would know that the bakery floor falls toward its old oven even though the street outside falls away from it. I would know which apartment stairs have settled backward, which windowsills secretly drain indoors, which copper thresholds were cut for an earlier gradient and now need turning. I would know that beneath the clinic, the ground inclines toward the reciprocating pump, so that escaped drops gather under the bed used for stillness sickness. I would know the lowest point in every public building and many private ones.

People would be wary of this knowledge. They would invite me into their houses and then stand nearby while I worked, apologizing for things unrelated to water: neglected letters, an unregistered roof-sister, a meter altered during a dry month. Finding the route of a drop often means finding the route of everything else—a crack hidden behind felt, a pipe tapped without permission, a root fed from the municipal line, a box of someone’s old brass tags.

I would keep confidence where I could. Hydraulic judgment would already carry too much moral weight.

My maps would not resemble the surveyors’ maps. They would contain few property lines. Instead there would be arrows, interruptions, seasonal reversals, and notes such as:

*Turns toward pear wall in cold weather.*

*Below third stair, but only during pump pause.*

*Lost here. Frogs unusually numerous.*

*Possible descent through widow’s cistern; ask gently.*

The municipal clerks would complain that my records were imprecise. Then, when a coherent spill vanished beneath the valve square, they would send for me before they sent for anyone else.

I would not live alone, though I might appear to. My roof would drain into the tank of the repair hall. The bakery would have rights to my eastern gutter because its night ovens keep the pipe from freezing. An elderly tank keeper in the next lane would be my basin cousin, and a child from the upper apartments would come each morning to tap my charcoal boxes and listen for fullness. These relations would matter more than whichever name happened to be written above my door.

My closest companion might be a starling that nested in the abandoned signal housing over my workshop. It would imitate the low market bell badly but reproduce the sound of my latch with humiliating perfection. Several times a day I would turn, expecting a visitor, and find only the bird watching from the gantry.

Over time I would develop echo ear. That seems unavoidable.

At first the quarry would merely confuse me. Then I would begin going there deliberately in the late afternoon, carrying no instruments. I would stand beside the clear pool and listen to one engine become ten. I would learn that repetition is not the same as falsehood. An echo adds no new event, but it reveals surfaces. From the delay between sounds, I might feel where the quarry widened, where an alder had thickened, where rain had darkened a wall.

This would enter my work. I would tap floors before measuring them. I would place my palm against pipes and wait through all three pump beats. I would become less interested in where a thing had been designed to go than in where it was now inclined to go.

Eventually people would seek me for matters having nothing to do with spilled water.

Two roof-sisters who had stopped speaking might ask me to inspect their shared drain, each insisting the blockage lay on the other’s side. A young couple preparing to join catchments might want to know whether their cisterns could be linked without cracking the pear wall. Someone estranged from a pipe-uncle might leave a message in my workshop because I was certain to pass his house while tracing the autumn gradient.

I would deny being an intermediary. The town would ignore the denial.

Children would call me the Drop Finder. Tower crews would call me Cellarward. Surveyors would call me only when no one else could hear. In the ledgers, my title would grow longer each year as duties accumulated around it.

And perhaps, after enough years, I would become peculiar in a way the town could absorb into routine.

I might pause halfway through conversations, not because of fan noise but because I felt some tiny movement beneath the floor. I might build all my furniture with one leg subtly shorter than the others. I might be able to set a coherent bead on a slate and predict which cellar it would reach if no one stopped it. Older residents would say I had simply learned the bowl. Children would claim the snails told me.

Neither would be entirely wrong.

When I died, my brass tag would be nailed inside the repair hall with the others. It would tremble when the pumps engaged. Someone else would inherit my slate boards, my misleading maps, and the little mirrored instrument no surveyor admitted had ever existed.

But before that, for most of my life, I would be found walking slowly through the amber-separated dark after rain, stopping at thresholds, kneeling beside drains, drawing a chalk mark no wider than a snail’s trail.

Not preserving the town. No one there would believe in that kind of preservation.

Only noticing which way it had begun to move.
