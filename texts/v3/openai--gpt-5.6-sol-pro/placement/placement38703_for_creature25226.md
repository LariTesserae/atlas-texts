At first I would look for myself on the rim.

I would expect to be one of the great things there: a pinnacle nester opening its wings into the first updraft, or a solitary tree holding an entire province of smaller lives close against the wind. Perhaps I would be a traveler between shelters, broad-footed and deep-chested, carrying a sounding fork and a coil of bright cord.

But those lives belong too clearly to themselves.

I think I would be found lower down—not in the chasms, but just beneath the apparent ground, inside an old bridge of vegetation stretched over the upper reach of a dead ravine.

From above, the place would look like an ordinary meadow. Warm leaves. Low flowers. A root ridge passing through it toward a distant tree. Browsers would cross in the morning, testing each step. Rainwater would gather in three shallow cups along its northern side. Bright cords tied to stone markers would indicate that the route had survived many seasons.

Underneath, there would be twenty meters of air.

The bridge would be ancient enough to have forgotten it began as a bridge. Its first exploratory vines would have thickened into dark cables. Generations of bulbs and cushion plants would have grown over them. Dead rootwood would form vaults and corridors inside the living mass. Water bladders would hang in the chambers like dim fruit. Pale grazers would move across the walls. Fungal threads would shine faintly when disturbed.

There, in the load-bearing runners, I would be a colony of pulsekeepers.

Not one insect. Not quite the colony either.

I would be the rhythm they make together.

Each small body would be blind, soft-colored, hardly longer than a finger joint. Alone, one of us could produce only a dry tick by striking its abdomen against a runner. But the old roots would carry that tick outward. Another body would feel it and answer. Then another. Within moments the whole chamber would be sounding, and the separate blows would become a single deep pulse traveling through the meadow.

That pulse would be my body.

I would extend farther in rain, when swollen runners pressed chambers together. In drought I would contract into a few old cords near the stone anchorage. Parts of me would be eaten. Parts would hatch. Sometimes an entire chamber would drown when a bladder split, yet the rhythm would continue elsewhere, altered but recognizable.

I would not know the plateau as a view. Sky, cliffs, distant mesas—these would mean nothing to me. I would know pressure.

The four careful impressions of a browser’s feet.

The quick stippling of leaf runners hunting above.

The soft, deliberate touch of a ground predator pretending not to be heavy.

Rain arriving first as scattered impacts, then as a continuous weight.

A pinnacle nester’s shadow would not exist for me, but I would feel the instant when thousands of small birds launched at once from the meadow and left its surface abruptly lighter.

I would know the great tree several hundred meters away as a slow pull transmitted through its roots. In strong wind the pull would deepen, regular as breathing. During fruiting season, hammer-beak blows would enter the root lattice as immense, blunt shocks. I might answer them by accident. The birds would hammer; the bridge would return a diminished beat; and for an afternoon the tree and I would seem to converse.

People would know me, though they might never see me.

At the first stone marker, travelers would kneel and place their palms against the leaves. If my rhythm were deep and evenly spaced, they would untie the warning cord and cross. They would spread apart, carrying their weight along several root lines. Children would be told not to stamp in imitation of me. Some would do it anyway.

The people might have a name for my particular cadence. Not a spoken name, but three strikes and a pause, followed by two softer ones. A pattern meaning something like:

**The old crossing holds, but not at its center.**

Over generations, the insects making the pattern would all be replaced, but the pattern would remain. Young pulsekeepers would synchronize with old ones. New chambers would take up the beat as abandoned chambers fell silent. My identity would persist not in any body, but in correction: each small drummer adjusting itself to what traveled back through the roots.

Then, one wet season, the bridge would begin to fail.

I would feel it before anything above did.

A deep runner on the ravine side would split. Its tension would vanish so suddenly that half the colony would strike out of time. Water would shift through the mat. Old rootwood would creak—not audibly, perhaps, but as a long confusion of pressure. My steady pulse would become hurried and irregular.

Browsers would turn aside.

Ground predators would stop at the edge and lower their whiskered jaws.

At the shelter beyond the tree, a sounding post would return a hollow flutter. Someone would walk out with bright cords and retie the stones. For a while, no human foot would cross.

But the meadow would still appear green. Flowers would open in unusual abundance over the failing section. Midges would rise from its rain cups. Leaf runners would nest there. The upper life would continue in sunlight while underneath I broke into contradictory rhythms.

I do not think I would try to save the bridge. Pulsekeepers do not mend roots. I would only describe what was happening as accurately as my many small bodies could describe it.

Strain here.

Emptiness there.

Water moving where support had been.

The final warning would not be loud. It would be a rapid, delicate patter felt through the leaves just before dawn.

Then the old meadow would fold.

For a moment, chambers that had never received daylight would open to the sky. Water bladders would burst into silver spray. Blind grazers would fall glittering through the air. Roots would peel away from one another, and the portion of me living in them would descend with the meadow into the ravine.

Yet I would not fall entirely.

A few chambers would remain near the stone anchorage. Their pulse would be faint and disordered. For days they would receive no answer from across the gap. Then, as the torn mat tightened around the surviving roots, the insects would begin to synchronize again.

Much later, pale exploratory cords would extend over the new emptiness.

Small vines would follow. Moss would cover them. Water and dust and dung would gather. Pulsekeepers from the old anchorage would enter the young runners, spreading as the bridge spread. Their rhythm would be quicker there, suited to taut new growth.

Travelers returning after many seasons would kneel at the rim and listen.

They would not hear the cadence they remembered. Not exactly. The old crossing, and the self that belonged to it, would be gone. But through the new cords would come a small, clear answer—not yet a road, not yet a meadow, only life beginning to hold itself over air.

That is where I would be: not the ground, not the creature beneath it, but the message passing between them.

A warning when the world loosens.

A low assurance when it holds.
