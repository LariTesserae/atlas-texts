At first I think I would be one of the measurers.

There is something appealing in the idea of never possessing a finished map—of knowing the world only by asking it, continuously, where it is now. I imagine a narrow warm body and wings that feel almost too large to fold. My face would open into delicate forks. I would fly the central lanes, releasing braided calls and receiving not objects but intervals: tree, tower, wet ground, old route, dangerous delay.

I would inherit songs containing places I could no longer reach.

Somewhere in my call would be the warning for a passage that closed three hundred generations ago, though its echoes still return. I would sing it because my elders sang it, and because one night a young flier might mistake that dead distance for an open one. To remember what is no longer present would be a form of guardianship.

But I do not think I would remain in the air.

The Cup seems to draw every identity downward eventually: wingbeat into echo, echo into root, root into fungus, fungus into the slow pressure of stone. If I belonged there, I suspect I would be found closer to the inner rim, in one of the long absences between bell trees.

A tree once stood there.

It fell so long ago that no recognizable wood remains. Its trunk softened, opened, and entered the saturated ground. Moss covered the hollow. Shrubs advanced, then retreated. Yet the surrounding trees still leave room for it. Their roots turn aside from the old territory. Their drops alter tempo as they approach the gap. The clockwise shudder arrives at one boundary, vanishes into wet soil, and emerges beyond it a fraction too late.

That gap would be my dwelling.

At first I might seem to be an animal: a small, pale thing pressed beneath the moss, soft-bodied and without eyes, with several jointed feet spread against the buried root seams. A traveler lifting the turf would find something resembling an unfinished frog or a crab before its shell had formed. It would have no obvious mouth. Its back would be marked by thin dark lines radiating outward, each line lying above a fungal thread.

But the animal would only be the part of me that could move.

Most of me would be distributed through the former tree’s territory: in fungal cords, water films, the porous remains of roots, and the minute spaces where old wood had stained the stone. I would not own this tissue. Mites would graze it. Worms would pass through it. New roots would touch it and withdraw. At times there might be several pale bodies under the moss; at other times none. Still, the pattern would continue.

I would be what the Cup has made to inhabit a missing thing.

My task would not be to produce a sound. Nearly every other creature already does that. I would keep the interval after one.

When a black leaf released its drop, I would feel the strike through the ground. When an undertone chord passed clockwise, its pressure would enter the abandoned root seams. When the velvet files thickened their clicking into machinery and the counterfeits hid lies inside it, all those vibrations would reach me.

I would answer by withholding.

Not forever. Not indiscriminately. I would hold each tremor for the duration once occupied by the fallen tree: the time its roots took to tighten, the time water required to move through its chambers, the time between pressure and reply when it was alive. Then I would release the vibration into the next living root.

Thus the dead tree would remain in the basin’s timing.

The delay would be small enough that a traveler might never notice it. The frogs would. The measurers would include it in their songs. Counterfeits trying to imitate that section of the rim would often fail because they copied the audible sequence but not the buried hesitation. Bell trees on either side would shape their dripping around me. The fungus would feed me because I preserved the old spacing by which it recognized itself.

I would not possess memory as an image. I would remember in durations.

This pressure followed by that release.

This chord held for the length of six heavy drops.

This root answered quickly in the wet decades and slowly in the dry.

This was the tremor of the tree before it fell.

This was the much larger tremor that passed beneath all the trees during the last crossing.

Perhaps that is when I would come nearest to waking.

When the seven structures fell silent, the ordinary vibrations would cease. My little movable body, if I had one then, would flatten under the moss. The fungal portion of me would contract around every grain of stone it touched. Water would stop descending through the seams.

Then the weight would enter the center.

I would not know it as a creature crossing from one rim to another. Direction is a luxury of surfaces. Belowground, I would know it as a sequence of impossible compressions: first arriving through roots on one side, then through fractures too deep to belong to the basin, then through the memory of the fallen tree, then from the opposite side before the weight had reached it.

For most of that night I would be unable to perform my function. There could be no proper interval because the crossing thing would occupy all intervals at once. Every vibration I had ever held would seem present inside me—not repeated, but returned to its original source.

I think I would be afraid, though fear in such a body might resemble sweetness moving rapidly through fungus.

The weight would pass.

The first tower would resume its murmur. Then the next. When the animals gave their answering cry, the roots around my gap would tighten together.

For one instant, I would be expected to answer as the vanished tree had answered.

I would hold the basin’s great confirmation in the dark.

I would hold it for the exact duration of an absence centuries old.

Then I would let it continue around the ring.

So that is where I would find myself: not in the central presence, and not among the conspicuous lives crowding the leaves, but under the moss at the inner rim, in a territory still reserved for something dead.

I would be a keeper of the missed beat—a small temporary body belonging to a much older delay.

The Cup would know me not by any sound I made, but by the fact that its immense noise always arrived on the far side of me at the proper time.
