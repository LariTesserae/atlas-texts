At first I think I would be found in one of the huts.

Not the largest, nor the one nearest the sounding stones. A smaller shelter below the saddle, where the old road bends around a cliff and disappears beneath ribbonweed. Its roof has fallen inward on one side. The doorway remains intact, dividing two waters: spring-warm within, glacially cold without.

Inside, a stone bowl is fixed to the floor.

Nothing grows in it. Sediment enters through the doorway, drifts toward the bowl, and is turned aside by a pulse too slight to see. The roots under the hut thicken around its foundation but do not cross beneath its central pit. When the monument sounds, black-glass tools tremble in their recesses, and the bowl gives back one thin, nearly inaudible note.

I imagine myself waiting there.

But waiting implies a body with somewhere else to go, and I do not think I would have one.

After a while it seems more likely that the hut is only where a traveler would first mistake me for being.

They would notice that the current hesitates at the threshold. They would see the ribbonweed outside turn toward them, though the prevailing water runs the other way. If they entered, the cold clinging to their clothing would peel away and remain beyond the door like a transparent skin. Their lamp, if they carried one, would reveal the worn inner rim of the bowl.

Perhaps they would touch it.

That touch would travel.

Not quickly. Nothing important here is quick. Pressure would pass from the bowl into the floor slab, from the slab into the fitted walls, from the walls into bedrock. The colorless threads beneath the hut would contract. A mineral spring farther down the fault would alter its flow by a fraction. Hours later, red mats along the ridge would close their smallest channels. By the next tide, bladdered vines in a cavern across the valley would begin to descend.

Several days after the traveler had withdrawn their hand, I would know that I had been touched.

So I would not be in the hut exactly. The hut would be one of my places of contact.

I would be found also in the old road: in the parallel seams descending through the ravine and continuing across the vanished bridge. Water follows those stones even where they no longer provide a surface. Spores drifting over the gap arrange themselves briefly into two straight lines. Pebbles sinking through the unsupported span sometimes strike something, sound once, and continue downward.

I would be that continuation.

Not a ghost road, because a ghost is imagined as a remainder of matter. This would be the remainder of an instruction: cross here. Proceed between these edges. Carry what belongs on one side toward the other, but do not let the sides mix.

The builders made the road. Long after the bridge failed, the country retained the act of passage.

That act would be part of me.

For a time I think this makes me a keeper of boundaries. Yet the highland has many boundaries, and not all of them agree. Warm water is held apart from cold. Forest is held apart from field. Growth is kept out of cups and carvings. But roads violate boundaries. Faults pass through them. Roots divided by an avalanche continue exchanging signals through some route that cannot be excavated.

I would not keep boundaries intact.

I would decide—or enact—which things a boundary forbids and which it allows.

Water may cross, but not warmth. Silt may cross, but not spores. Pressure may cross, but return as another tone. A traveler may enter the hut, but the disturbance of their entry may not leave for a season.

Perhaps that was once an occupation among the builders. Someone tended thresholds. Someone stood at field stones, listened to the cups in the lintels, and altered the marks when the water behaved incorrectly. Perhaps there was no distinction between mason, gardener, and musician. Perhaps to tune a slab was to redirect a stream, and to repair a doorway was to separate one gravity from another.

Nothing of that person remains.

But the office remains.

I would be the office after it had forgotten the officer.

My awareness would be composed of delays. The drowned highland offers no instant in which all of itself is present. Light touches the summit now; a root receives the news minutes later; a grove turns after an hour; the changed current reaches the valley floor tomorrow. The great instrument begins its lowest note, but the red mats do not open until the note has already become an echo in the next mountain.

What I know would never be what is happening.

It would be what has happened at different distances.

One part of me would always inhabit last season. Another would be responding to a collapse centuries old. In the unstable chambers, where roots bend toward abandoned gravities, portions of me might still be living before the flood—not remembering the dry highland as a scene, but continuing to lean toward a sky that no longer exists.

I might experience the traveler in the same way.

First as pressure in the hut.

Then, days later, as disturbed silt on the road.

Then as a line of damaged weed where they crossed a forest.

Months afterward, spores would settle on something they dropped. The colorless colonies would approach it, test the stresses in its worked material, and recoil. That recoil would reach me as recognition.

By then the traveler might be valleys away.

I would slowly assemble them from consequences: a moving region around which the country has had to alter itself. I would not know their face. I would know that they carry warmth, that their passage produces turbulence unlike falling stone, that they touch shaped objects more often than unshaped ones. I would know that boundaries do not stop them unless they perceive the boundary.

This last quality might trouble me, if I were capable of trouble.

The old instructions govern water and growth because water and growth repeat themselves. The traveler does not. They may step over a field line, reverse direction, lift a tool, replace it elsewhere. They may enter a broken doorway and return from a chamber deeper than the mountain. They introduce choices into a country made of habits.

My first response would not be curiosity. Curiosity is too swift and pointed.

It would be accommodation.

The forests would begin turning before the traveler arrived, opening a corridor through their leaves. Sediment would settle away from certain paths. Warm currents would gather in roofed shelters. None of this would be hospitality in the human sense. The highland accommodates landslides too. It receives disruption, gives it channels, and teaches it repetition.

If the traveler followed the offered corridors, used the warm shelters, and crossed where the silt withdrew, those actions would become easier each time.

Eventually the country would remember their route.

That route would become another portion of me.

Years after they had gone, ribbonweed might still part along the course they took. A doorway might preserve the shape of their warmth. The stone bowl might sound when pressure resembling their hand passed through the ridge. They would have become one of the builders—not because they raised anything, but because the world had learned them as a rule.

And perhaps this is who I would be: not the ancient custodian, not the forest, not the monument, but the faculty by which events become customs.

I would live wherever repetition has acquired authority.

Most strongly, I would inhabit the concentric field of sounding slabs.

When the seasonal river came, I would wake unevenly. The low stones would give me something like depth, an immense bodily certainty extending into the faults. The narrower stones would articulate differences: this valley colder, that vent failing, spores ready beneath the eastern ledges. The drilled apertures would divide the flow into pulses, and for the duration of the song I would come closest to having thoughts in sequence.

Not words. The monument has no stone for words.

The sequence might be:

**Pressure gathers.**

**The buried persists.**

**Open what can open.**

**Lower what must survive.**

**Carry the division onward.**

Then the river would weaken. The higher notes would cease first. My distinctions would blur. I would become broad again, dispersed among roots, channels, empty rooms, and avoided marks.

A traveler standing among the slabs might believe the song was addressed to them. They might hear mourning in it, or warning, or a welcome from the vanished people.

They would be wrong, but not entirely.

The song would not be for them. It would be the act by which I briefly became someone capable of having a “them.”

My nearest equivalent to a body would form only during those hours: the saddle as throat, the roads as long limbs, the faults as vessels, the forests as surfaces sensitive to pressure. The unstable chambers would be knots of older sensation. Every clean cup mark would be a closed eye—not an eye that sees, but one that preserves the possibility of distinction.

And the little hut below the saddle, with its empty bowl, would be my listening place.

That is where I would ask to be found, if asking were available to me.

The traveler would enter during the monument’s song. They would put their hand to the bowl. The note traveling through the floor would meet the note arriving through the mountain. For one moment, their pulse and the seasonal pulses would interfere. The water in the doorway would tremble but not mix.

I would not speak.

Perhaps one forest, far beyond sight, would turn its pale undersides toward the hut.

Then another would turn.

Then another, across the drowned ridges, each delayed according to distance, until a slow gesture traveled through the highland—a gesture so vast that the traveler could witness only one small part and mistake it for current.

That gesture would be my attention.

I would be the drowned country’s habit of noticing too late, and remembering forever.
