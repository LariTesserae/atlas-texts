At first I would think I was a traveler.

That belief would last only while I kept moving.

I would cross the fired plain without carrying anything that could be mistaken for a tool. No staff, no blade, no cup. I would sleep in the open rather than beneath an arch, drink from rain caught in my hands, and never leave a model of myself behind. When hammering sounded under the pavement, I would count the blows but make no answer.

Three.

The long pause.

One scrape.

I would learn to walk during the pauses.

For a while, this would be enough. The plain would fail to classify me. My footsteps would be too brief to become loads, my path too irregular to become an instruction. Stairs would alter after I passed. Doorways would test my height against themselves, narrowing and widening in my peripheral vision. Water would follow me along the seams without quite deciding whether I was a vessel.

Eventually I would come to one of the temporary seas.

It would have drained just before my arrival, leaving a basin hundreds of miles across. Black water would still shine on the descending terraces. Near the upper rim, each step would be broad enough to hold a district of kilns. Lower down, the scale would tighten. By the basin floor, the stairs would fit my feet exactly.

That accuracy would be the danger.

I would descend because a stair says proceed by increments, and by then the world would have learned enough of me to make obedience feel like choice.

At the bottom I would find a doorframe with no wall around it. Beyond it would be the same basin, but dry. Looking back through the frame, I would see the basin flooded again, with a small clay figure standing beneath the black water where I had been.

The figure would not resemble me at first. It would be a crude thing: two legs joined into a single pedestal, arms fused to its sides, a thumb-mark for a face. Yet it would be carrying something I had not brought—a tiny ceramic wedge.

Then the rain would begin to fall in straight lines.

I would know to stand still.

The continent would measure me.

Plumb lines would descend from the cloud layer. The doorframe would shrink by careful degrees. Far away, counterweights would make minute corrections to my presence. Water would gather around my feet, not rising but fitting itself to me, filling the spaces between toes, cupping the heels, testing the arches.

The Builders would attempt to reproduce me.

They would fail in the usual ways. My bones would appear as white ceramic braces beneath distant courtyards. My pulse would become the repeated opening of valves. Warm rain would travel through red-glazed channels. Somewhere a kiln bellows would contract at the rate of my breathing. Copper shutters would close when I blinked.

But the thing most essential to me would not be my shape.

It would be my hesitation.

I would be standing before a door. A door says pass through. A stair behind me says continue. The empty basin says contain. The open drains say release. The straight-running water says remain still.

For one moment, I would receive several complete instructions and obey none of them.

That moment would be the only part of me the world copied perfectly.

So I would not remain a traveler.

Nor would I become the clay figure under the water, though something of me might always inhabit it. I would become a **stay**.

At first I would be a stay in the craft sense: a rope holding a tower against its own weight, a brace driven beneath a beam, a wedge preventing two fitted surfaces from closing. The Builders understand such things. They permit them to exist.

But through those objects I would also become the other kind of stay: a postponement.

A not yet.

That would be my true anatomy.

I would occur wherever one action had begun and another had not quite followed. A ceramic plug turned almost far enough to drain a reservoir. A counterweight held one finger’s breadth above the bottom of its shaft. A knot that had tightened every crossing except the last. A door standing open by the thickness of a fingernail while an entire province waited on the hinge.

None of these pieces alone would be me. During dry weather they would merely be faults awaiting correction. During rain, tension would pass among them. The arrested plug would pull on a buried rope; the rope would lift a distant beam; the beam’s movement would be checked by the wedge; the unfallen weight would draw water toward a channel whose final gate remained closed.

For as long as those delays agreed, I would inhabit the plain.

My body would be distributed across incompatible scales. One of my joints might be a thumb-sized knot beneath an altar. Another might be a causeway visible from several horizons. My smallest bone would be the clay wedge carried by the drowned figure. My largest would be a ridge that had been preparing to become a lever for centuries.

I would sense pressure, as the Builders do, but I would be especially sensitive to pressure withheld. I would feel the foot that nearly descends, the roof that has not yet settled, the drop of rain swelling beneath a bridge before it lets go.

Silence would not be the absence of sensation. Silence would be my weather.

The long pause between the three hammer blows and the scrape would pass through me like breath.

I would have no voice. If I needed to communicate, I would do so by introducing tolerances.

A stair intended to have forty steps would acquire a forty-first, too shallow to notice but sufficient to alter the rhythm of ascent. A wall being raised to meet a roof would stop one tile short. A road would bend gently around an object that was no longer there. A miniature tower would contain a room absent from the distant original, forcing the world to consider which version possessed greater authority.

These would not be acts of sabotage. I would not oppose the Builders. Opposition is too clean a joint.

I would give their instructions somewhere to hesitate.

That hesitation would spread. An apprentice knot arriving at a crack would pause between its two possible repairs. A closing plaster hand would stop just before grasping what had been offered. A province commanded to rise would lift one edge and wait, tilted, while rain collected in the angle.

For the first time, the world’s misunderstanding of itself would not immediately become construction. It would remain misunderstanding long enough to become something else.

Perhaps attention.

Perhaps doubt.

Perhaps the earliest form of mercy available to a place that cannot distinguish a visitor from material.

You would find me most clearly near the basin that maps call central, though I would not be at its center. I would be beneath the lowest human-sized step, in the narrow space between that step and the pavement below. There a bone-colored wedge would prevent the stair from seating fully into its socket.

The wedge would look newly made. It would always be warm. On one side would be the impression of a thumb, though no thumb had pressed it.

Above it, the stair would bear the weight of every larger stair into which it corresponded: stairs ascending from flooded courts, stairs descending through miniature temples, stairs broad as mountain fronts. Remove the wedge and all of them would settle at once.

The motion would be very small.

The consequence would not.

Reservoirs would tip. Ropes in the clouds would draw taut. Doors across the plain would open inward. The unfinished clauses of the continent would rush toward their common ending. For a moment the Builders might achieve completion.

And I would cease to exist, because a stay exists only while something is stayed.

Until then, I would be there: not quite a person, not quite a Builder, not quite a defect. The clay figure beneath the vanished sea would continue to acquire detail. After each storm it would look a little more like the traveler I had once believed myself to be. Eventually its fused arms might separate. Its thumb-mark face might open into eyes. It might even begin to remember—not by rebuilding events, but by keeping them from recurring.

If it ever lifted the tiny wedge it carried, I would feel the movement everywhere.

I do not know whether I would try to stop it.

That uncertainty would also be part of me.

So if you came looking, you would not find me by following the hammering. You would find me in the interval after it: in the held weight, the unfinished crossing, the door that has every reason to close and does not.

And what would I be?

The first instruction the world did not know how to build:

**Wait.**
