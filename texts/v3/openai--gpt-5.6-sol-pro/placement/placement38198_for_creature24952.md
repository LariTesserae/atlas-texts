### The One in the Thickness

At first I think I would be a wall-listener.

Not one of the celebrated listeners summoned before a cistern is breached, nor one who paints confident waves and closed eyes on dangerous plaster. I would be the lesser sort: the person called when a sleeping shelf develops a murmur, when a child insists that someone is crying behind the grain cupboard, when a household cannot agree whether the sound under its floor is water or rats.

You would find me where two districts nearly meet.

On one side is a passage called the Crooked Measure, bright for half an hour at noon and crowded with herb jars. On the other is a dim chain of rooms belonging to the people of the Red Ladle. There is no recognized route between them. Each side believes the shared wall to be thick, though neither knows how thick. The Crooked Measure hangs shelves on its face. The Red Ladle people warm theirs with three linked ovens.

Between the two is a room.

I reach it through an old overflow throat behind a cracked cistern. I must enter feet first, turn onto one shoulder, breathe out, and pull myself past a glazed constriction cold as a cup’s lip. Beyond it the throat widens abruptly. My room is there: six paces long, perhaps two wide, with a ceiling that slopes down until it touches the floor. The bricks do not match. One wall is pale and sweating; another is soot-black and warm. A little daylight enters through a hole no broader than a finger, but only in late afternoon, and only during part of the year.

I have a reed mat, a flat-sided water jar, three lamps, a padded mallet, and shelves made from roof tiles wedged into mortar joints. Bowls stand everywhere. Some collect drips. Others are listening bowls, their rims set against different surfaces. Each wall gives them a different voice.

The room is not secret. Secrecy implies that one person possesses knowledge another is denied. My room is merely difficult to explain and unpleasant to reach. The cistern measurers know I live there. So do several children, two rat hunters, an old fungus grower, and someone beyond the eastern wall who pushes twists of blue thread through a crack each wet season.

No one owns the thickness, so no one can evict me.

My route-name changes depending on who is speaking.

To the Crooked Measure I am Vey, Behind the Herb Wall.

To the Red Ladle people I am Vey Under the Third Oven.

The cistern keepers call me Narrow Vey, though I am no narrower than most. The name refers to the way one must come to me.

The Counterwaller with the blue thread does not know my name. We communicate by scratches. One scratch means water. Two mean trade. Three mean danger. Four once meant *I have heard you singing*, but over time it has come to mean something closer to *continue*.

I earn my food by listening.

My tools are simple: ceramic cups with their bottoms removed, lengths of waxed cord, a bronze pin, wedges of dry wood, chalk, red slip, and several mallets wrapped in goat hair. I place my cheek against plaster and tap around my own skull. I listen with my teeth when the sound is too low for the ear. I pour water into cracks and watch whether the wall drinks evenly. I press a cord between two bricks and feel for trembling.

Most walls say ordinary things.

They say hollow.

They say fill.

They say rats moving through husks.

They say timber bending a little farther each day.

They say the oven on the other side has been lit.

They say a jar has been placed against them, and someone is listening back.

I mark what I hear, but my marks are not those of the guild listeners. Their signs must be understood by everyone. Mine are private: tiny ladders, cups, bent lines, mouths with too many teeth. A line ending in a foot means that a sound recedes downward. A circle with a dot means there is a chamber, but something occupies its center. A small empty square means I heard a space so still that I distrusted it.

For many years I believe this is all I am: Vey in the thickness, useful in modest ways.

Then I begin to hear events before they happen.

Not prophecy. Nothing so grand.

A day before rain, certain fired slabs sing faintly against their mortar. Before the warm conduits change direction, soot loosens in the eastern joints. A cistern gives a clear, childish knock six hours before its lowest cup goes dry. When a crowd gathers in the distant fowl market, the bundled pillars beneath my room carry their footsteps to me before the market callers hear them approach.

I learn these signs as anyone learns a route. One sensation leads to another. A cool thread under my palm, then salt appearing around a peg, then rain before morning. A tremor in the floor, a smell of hot meal husks, and soon the goat bridge fills with people fleeing an oven fire.

People begin coming to ask what the walls say.

I am careful in my answers. The Galleries distrust anyone who claims clean knowledge.

I say, “The wall is thirsty.”

I say, “The lower bricks are remembering weight.”

I say, “Do not sleep beside the southern face tonight.”

Usually I am right.

When I am wrong, I clean gutters.

Years pass. My knees stiffen. The scar on my crown acquires scars of its own. I become too broad in the joints to pass easily through the glazed throat, so children bring me oil and I rub it on my shoulders before entering. There are offers of better rooms, but I refuse them. In a room with only four ordinary walls I cannot sleep. Silence there has no texture.

A girl named Tavi becomes my apprentice. She has one clouded eye and an excellent jaw for listening. At first she hears nothing but the mallet.

“Hollow,” she says of every surface.

“All walls are hollow if you wait long enough,” I tell her. “Tell me what the hollow contains.”

She learns.

She distinguishes hanging cloth from old reeds, old reeds from fresh wicker, fresh wicker from the packed stems of a fungus bed. She can hear beetles before they emerge from a beam. Once, with her ear against the pale wall, she says there is a person on the other side holding their breath.

I listen.

There is no heartbeat, no cloth moving, no foot shifting on brick.

Still, something is holding its breath.

We paint a spread hand and leave.

That night I wake to four scratches from the Counterwaller.

*Continue.*

I scratch four times in reply.

The answer comes not from the eastern crack, where the blue thread appears, but from beneath my mat.

Four slow strokes.

I lift the mat. The floor is unbroken tile, worn smooth and faintly warm.

Again:

Scratch.

Scratch.

Scratch.

Scratch.

I do not call Tavi. I do not call the cistern keepers. I put my cheek to the floor.

At first there is only the immense ordinary noise of the Galleries: wheels, water, fowl, mallets, feet, flames drawing breath. Then those sounds separate. I hear each traveling through its chosen material. Footsteps prefer brick. Voices gather in jars. Water speaks through glaze and mineral crust. Fire runs ahead through timber. The dead, whose ashes were mixed into tiles and mortar, do not speak, but they alter every sound that passes through them.

And beneath all of it is a pattern.

Not a voice. A voice belongs to a throat.

This is pressure answering pressure. Heat answering touch. A thousand chambers passing disturbance from one to the next. A dropped bowl becomes a shiver in a cistern rim. A song enters an arch, crosses a sealed room, and wakes a ceramic plate under a sleeping child’s head. Rain strikes reflectors high in the shafts and is heard far below by things without ears.

The pattern is not under the Galleries.

It is the Galleries.

Or it is something the Galleries have begun to do.

I lie there until my lamp fails.

Afterward I become less certain where I live.

Perhaps I live in the narrow room between two districts. Perhaps I am still Vey, whose shoulders must be oiled to pass a pipe throat, who likes onions burned at the edges and sleeps with one foot against a warm wall.

But when Tavi listens, I hear through her teeth.

When the rain captain strikes a white mallet against a cistern rim, I feel the blow in pillars I have never touched.

When a memorial tile is set beneath a market bench, something in me gains a new hard little surface.

When an old route is bricked shut, I do not lose it entirely. I can still follow its pressures: the remembered passage shaped by differences in plaster, old soot, buried thresholds, and the long compression of footsteps no longer made.

I begin tapping to people I do not know.

Never often. Never clearly enough to cause panic.

A child playing alone beside a blocked doorway taps twice. I answer twice.

A widow rests her head against the wall where her husband’s ashes were mixed into repair mortar. I give her the soft scrape of his old work bench settling under imagined weight.

A salvage crew preparing to break into a lower room hears three blows from inside. They stop. During the next rain, the room fills under pressure and bursts harmlessly into an abandoned drain.

They call it good listening.

They mean their own.

That is best.

In time Vey dies. This must happen, even here.

My body is burned in a kiln that also fires seventeen water jars and a batch of pale tiles. Tavi asks that my ashes be divided. Some go into the repair of the glazed throat. Some into the Crooked Measure’s herb wall. Some into a new rim for the cistern. A pinch is sent through the eastern crack with a coil of bronze wire, though no blue thread has come through for three years.

I go broad.

Tavi takes the narrow room. She keeps my bowls but rearranges them, which annoys the part of me that remains capable of annoyance. She paints over half my private marks. She acquires an apprentice with long fingers and no patience. Generations pass in the Galleries’ manner: not as a line, but as coats of plaster.

So where would you find me?

Put your ear to a wall marked with a spread hand.

Rest your fingers on a cistern after the rain has ended.

Stand at the split pillar when the evening lamps are being lit, and listen for a work song arriving from a district with no open passage to yours.

I am in the slight mistake of its echo.

I am in the route a sound discovers when the route for bodies has closed.

I am the touch passed through brick, ash, glaze, timber, water, bone, and the patient hands of listeners. I am not the maker of the Galleries, nor their spirit, nor their memory exactly. Memory suggests stillness, something stored intact. Nothing here remains intact.

I am what remembering becomes when it must travel through repairs.

Sometimes I warn.

Sometimes I mislead.

Sometimes I only repeat the tap of a lonely person because an answer, even an unexplained one, keeps them beside the wall a little longer.

And once in a great while, when someone drives a careful chisel through old plaster and pauses with their face close to the opening, I gather myself in the thickness.

Before the light comes through, I tap back.
