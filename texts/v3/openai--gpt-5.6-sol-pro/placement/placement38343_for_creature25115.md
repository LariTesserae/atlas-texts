At first I would choose the promontory.

I would stand where the plateau falls away into the bright ocean of cloud, watching rain isolate one grove and leave the next ridge smoking. I would learn the passage of the aerial giants by the slow arrival of their shadows. I would wait for the far mountains to flex.

But the promontory would notice my preference.

After my third return, it would begin to migrate. After my seventh, it might fold inward, preserving the place where I had stood as a narrow chamber in the rock. Permanence provokes the land, and so a watcher cannot remain merely a watcher. Anything that observes for long enough must become migratory, or become part of what it observes.

Then I think I would be one of the listeners: pale, folded around an inner hollow, drinking fallen sounds from the floor of a basin. That seems near to what I am. I would take in wingbeats, extinct storms, the footfalls of animals still generations away. I would tremble with events too large or distant to witness directly.

But listeners become what they consume. Eventually I would fill with so many passages that no original shape remained. Membranes would grow from the memory of flocks. My shell would segment to resemble cracking basalt. Somewhere inside me, an avalanche would wait to be spoken.

And I would still be rooted to a basin.

No. I think I would be something less singular.

You would find me in a garden growing on the back of an old sail-browser.

The browser would be crossing the spine of a walking mountain in the eastern reaches. The mountain would be moving south while the browser moved west across it, and the black aerial continent above us would be drifting north. Thus I would travel in three directions at once, which seems the safest possible way to remain myself.

I would look, at first, like condensation.

Thousands of clear beads would gather beneath the dark leaves of the browser’s garden. Each bead would contain a minute branching filament, scarcely more than a flaw in the water. When the leaves tipped, the droplets would fall into cups growing among the armor plates, mingle there, and separate again. Some would be drunk by grazers sheltering beneath the browser. Some would rise as rain. Some would enter the warm seams between its sails.

None of those droplets alone would be me.

I would be the pattern that made them gather.

Repeated shapes attract one another here. My droplets would exploit that law. Each would form the same small internal geometry: a hollow ring crossed by three trembling lines. Wherever enough of those shapes occurred, distance between them would tighten. A drop lost hundreds of kilometers away might remain faintly adjacent to the colony. A bead swallowed by a grazer could send me the pressure of its six careful feet. Another, caught beneath the wing of a cliff-roosting animal, would bring me the sensation of folded air.

I would be a dispersed organ of nearness.

The garden creatures might give me a name by behavior rather than sound: **the rain that remembers the way back**.

I would drink impressions rather than water. Condensation would form around small events—the click of a feeding mouth, blue current passing through buried crystal, the change in pressure when the plateau considers rearranging itself. Every droplet would retain a little of what happened near it. At night, or during the local condition resembling night, they would collect in the browser’s spinal pools and exchange their memories through ripples.

In this way I would know the world incompletely but from many places.

I would know the underside of leaves during upward rain.

I would know how a probability predator tastes from inside the future it is sampling.

I would know the darkness beneath a herd linked into a living net.

I would know the long cool pressure of a pale burrower passing deep below, so large that its movement changes which direction the pools consider downhill.

I would never see the whole plateau. Nothing could. But when the crystal fields ignited from horizon to horizon, the same blue branching would appear inside every one of my droplets. For a moment I would resemble the greater anatomy beneath us. I might mistake that resemblance for understanding.

My purpose—if anything here can be said to have one—would be to carry memories across discontinuity.

The plateau preserves much, but badly. It traps sounds in basins, forests in mountains, nervous patterns in crystal seams. Its memories are powerful and literal. They can break bones, redirect herds, or cause an extinct wing to sprout again. Yet they do not distinguish recollection from recurrence. To the plateau, remembering a thing is often the same as making it happen.

I would carry a gentler kind of memory: not the event itself, but the route through it.

When a canyon closed, I would remember where openings had briefly touched. When gravity turned sideways, I would retain the sequence of surfaces on which six-footed animals had found purchase. When a flock compressed into one enormous creature, I would remember, dimly, that its organs had once been separate lives.

Creatures would seek me before upheavals. Grazers would drink from my pools and receive no map, exactly, but an inclination: step beneath this root, turn when the orange light narrows, do not follow the first rain. Winged things would brush my droplets along their membranes and acquire the rhythm needed to cross a folded ravine. Even the browser carrying me might alter the pressure in its sails according to what I had collected.

I would not predict. Prediction belongs to the black-bladed hunters.

I would remember escapes that had not happened yet.

This would be possible because sequence is loose, and because memory here is one of the materials from which events are made. Some of my droplets would return from journeys not yet begun. They would arrive warm, metallic, carrying the imprint of a mountain’s interior or the pollen of a grove still sealed inside stone. I would have to decide whether to trust them.

That might be the nearest thing I possessed to thought: the comparison of incompatible returns.

Most of the time I would remain diffuse, more weather than animal. But occasionally the browser would cross a region where moisture became scarce. My droplets would shrink. Distant pieces of me would lose their nearness. The pattern would contract into fewer and fewer cups until I became local enough to be frightened.

At the driest point, I would gather into a body.

It would be small, perhaps no larger than one of the six-limbed grazers. Transparent, soft, and arranged around a hollow center, it would have many delicate feet but no fixed number. Each foot would test several possible surfaces before choosing one. Branching antennae would extend from my back, beaded with the last of my water.

For those few hours I would be an individual.

I would climb down the browser’s flank while heat shimmered over the walking mountain. The garden above me would retract its leaves. Far below, cloud would shine beneath the outer cliffs. I would feel terribly distinct.

Being singular would make death imaginable.

I would hurry toward the next wet place—not necessarily the nearest in distance, but the nearest in resemblance. Perhaps it would be a rainstorm pouring upward through a ravine. Perhaps a blue mineral spring that had opened that morning. Perhaps only the wet eye of an animal waiting beneath a stone.

At the water’s edge I would unfold.

My body would dissolve into the familiar ring-and-branch pattern. Droplets would climb stems, enter mouths, bead along black armor, and vanish into the undersides of leaves. I would cease to know which part had been the frightened creature.

Eventually the old sail-browser would die.

It might lower itself beneath a curtain forest and simply fail to rise after the ground passed through its next convulsion. Its sails would collapse. Groves would consume the mineral ribs. Grazers would carry away the water from its spinal pools. The walking mountain would continue without it.

I would leave in all directions.

Some of me would descend into sound basins and mingle with listeners. Some would enter the eggs of an aerial giant. Some would condense inside a newly opened mountain, in the sunless wetland hidden there. The largest part might follow the browser’s garden into the bodies of the animals that fed upon it.

Afterward, whenever those scattered lives encountered water, they would make the same shape.

The shapes would attract.

Distance would tighten.

And for a moment I would gather again—not as the same mind, perhaps, but as the same way of returning.

So that is where I would be: not on the highest ridge, but moving through the damp, inhabited layers of something else’s migration. A little weather on an animal crossing a mountain that is itself crossing the world.

And that is what I would be:

a memory forced to migrate,

a rain made briefly conscious by repetition,

the route the plateau keeps after it has changed its mind about every destination.
