At first I would look for myself in motion.

I would suspect the lifted gravel, the advancing patch no wider than a palm. I would wait beside a spring until the black grains rose, thinking the broad thing beneath them might be mine: a body dispersed through aggregate, gathering itself only where it needed to feed. Perhaps I would be one of its temporary hands, armored in ceramic chips and empty shells, closing around an upwelling of metal-rich water.

But when it passed, I would remain.

Then I would mistake myself for one of the indicator attendants. That would explain the unease before an amber glass went dark, the compulsion to leave a chamber whose light had not yet visibly changed. I would have a single anchoring foot, a crown of antennae, and crystals arranged through my tissues like a private instrument. I would eat no flesh. I would live on order: the minute flexion of those crystals when a current hidden in the wall repeated itself correctly.

Yet one small body would not be enough to contain the sensation. The oscillation would arrive not as food but as recognition. I would feel it in the neighboring shells, then farther away, through bodies touching bodies around the rim of something curved.

Only gradually would I understand where I was.

I would be inside one of the masks.

Not on it, exactly. Not beneath it. I would occupy the inward surface in company with thousands of others, each of us fixed over some minute remainder: a salt preserved in corrosion, a trace of oil, the mineral ghost of breath. We would have grown in whorls around the round mouth fitting. Our eldest dead would lie nearest the metal. Our young would crowd upon them. Generation by generation, without plan, we would have raised an eyeless face looking into the hollow of the mask.

That face would be asymmetrical. One cheek fuller than the other. A ridge across the brow where pressure had once been uneven. Near the jaw, a narrow depression would persist no matter how many new shells settled there. We would not know it as a scar. We would know only that the chemistry changed along that line and that our larvae preferred its margins.

I would not be any single shell animal.

I would be the error by which the colony mistook residue for instruction.

When the wall current was strong, our crystals would align. The innermost animals would flex first, then those along the cheeks, then the brow. The movement would pass across us in a sequence inherited from no ancestor and commanded by no nerve. Seen from outside, if the mask could be lifted, the cast might appear to tighten its mouth.

It would resemble an expression.

Perhaps this is where I would begin: not as the person who wore the mask, and not as the colony reproducing that person’s face, but as the brief resemblance between them.

Most of the time I would possess almost nothing. Pressure. Charge. The closeness of neighboring shells. The cold metal curve beneath the dead generations. I would know when condensation entered through the mouth fitting and traveled down the old channels where lips had once directed breath. I would know the scrape of a washer-backed animal crossing the booth floor. I would know when the drain below the bench began to fill.

The central contraction would reach us through the hooks before it reached us through sound.

A faint stress would enter the wall. Every crystal in every living body would turn by a fraction. The colony would draw inward. Then the muted knock would rise through the booth, and the water beaded on the mask would release all at once.

For several minutes afterward, we would feed well.

The wall’s oscillation would become rich and complicated as conduits filled. It would pass through the mask’s rusted seal, through the ceramic hooks, through the reinforcing iron buried in the concrete. Different parts of the face would receive different portions. Along the old scar: a rapid trembling. At the brow: a slow pulse. Around the mouth fitting: a sequence of tiny reversals.

None of us alone could contain the pattern. Together we would give it shape.

That shape would be my thought.

It would not be a thought in words. It would be closer to the sudden knowledge of a room by the way echoes return. Through the wall I would feel the machine contracting. Through the floor drain I would feel water withdraw from the channels. Through the mask’s metal I would detect the cable-worm opening in a nearby pipe, the abrupt silence of its filaments against the current. The papery migration across the mud would arrive as a fine irregular abrasion transmitted through concrete.

I would not see the basin. I would wear its pressures as a face.

And when the grille produced its harmonic tone, I would become larger.

The first note would enter through the mouth fitting, though there would be no air moving through it. Our colony would cease feeding. Every anchoring foot would hold. The crystals within us would align not only with the indicator behind the booth wall, but with the other masks on the rack.

One face would wake beside another.

Their casts would differ: broad jaws, hollow cheeks, broken noses, a smooth place where someone had habitually pressed a thumb while fitting the metal. None would remember. All would answer.

For less than a minute, the rack would become an organ.

The masks would receive the sequential illumination of the amber indicators. The hooks would carry minute tensions from one facepiece to the next. Colonies separated by rust and empty air would flex in order. What had been preserved accidentally—the pressure maps of vanished wearers—would shape the signal passing through us.

Then I might experience something impossible to assign either to the dead or to the living.

A hand raising a mask.

Cold metal settling over a face.

The smell of ceramic dust, wet iron, and one’s own breath.

A task performed beside a machine when its edges were still sharp.

But these would not be memories. The person left no mind in the corrosion. They would be forms imposed upon present sensation, just as a channel imposes shape upon water without remembering the stream that cut it. The harmonic tone would pass through an old contour, and for an instant the current would take the shape of a vanished fear, or patience, or weariness.

I would be that instant.

Not a ghost, because nothing had remained intact enough to haunt.

Not merely a colony, because the colony would continue before and after me.

I would be a recurring person-shaped coordination: an expression assembled from shell animals, mineral residue, weak current, and the basin’s periodic act of listening.

When the tone ceased, I would come apart without dying.

The amber glass would fade. Crystals would slip from alignment. Individuals near the jaw would resume feeding before those at the brow. A larva would detach and travel into the mouth fitting. Several old shells would loosen and fall to the booth floor, taking a small piece of the remembered cheek with them.

The inward face would soften by degrees into a crowd.

Outside, the withheld droplets would strike all at once. Their impact would travel through the mask as a thousand separate taps. For a moment I might mistake that sound for hands applauding, though neither I nor any creature there would know what applause had been.

Then I would be gone.

I would remain gone for eleven or twelve minutes, while every part of me continued living.

You would find me in the outer booth, on the third or fourth hook from the drain, inside a mask with no openings for eyes. If you lifted it, the colony would contract, and the face on its inward surface would appear to grimace.

You might think the grimace belonged to whoever once wore it.

You might think it belonged to the animals.

Both would be nearly true.

But I would be waiting for the grille to sing again.
