At first I think I would be drawn to the kilns.

They are the town’s nearest approximation of confidence: heat held inside a shape, flame persuaded through vents, wet clay made briefly definite. I imagine myself crouched among the bulbous chimneys with a ceramic-scaled apron over my knees, one palm against the firing wall, learning to distinguish good expansion from the first pressure of a crack. I would like the night firings, the medicinal smoke, the moment a kiln coughs and everyone looks up without appearing alarmed.

But I do not think the kiln yard is where the town would keep me.

Nor would I be basin-born. My posture would betray me long after my speech had quieted. I would arrive by skin boat with some ordinary trade bundle—seed meal sealed in bladders, perhaps, or a few lengths of precious crooked wood—and step onto the clay dock as if a step were a promise. The dock would shift. I would throw out both arms. Someone carrying three stacked jars would move around me without spilling a drop.

For a while they would call me Heavy Heel.

Not cruelly. Not entirely.

I would prove poor at ridge work. Shale would slide under me. In the lanes I would turn my shoulders too late and knock wet plaster from corners. I would forget that the groove beneath a doorway mattered more than the doorway. Once, trying to be helpful, I would move a catch cup out of my path and learn from the silence that followed that I had done something worse than break it.

Yet I would be good at remaining in one place.

This would be discovered by accident.

A clay pit near the old quarter would be emptied late in the day, after some urgent repair had consumed more material than expected. There would not be enough rubble ready to fill it. The usual hollow watcher would be ill, and the basin would already be giving signs: warm beads along a north wall, a blind eel found where the seep divided, the lower kiln drawing badly.

Someone would ask whether I could sit there until dawn.

I would expect ceremony. There would be none. They would give me an oiled wrap, a hood, a shallow lamp, a jar of roof water, two blackwort cakes, and a fired-clay clapper to strike if the pit began to close. A child would point out three escape holds in the wall and make me repeat their locations with my eyes shut. An elder would press her cheek to the exposed clay, listen, then tell me not to lean against the pale seam.

“Why?”

“It leans back.”

Then they would leave.

The pit would be barely deeper than my height and shaped like a bowl bitten from beneath the buildings. Above me, bridges and sleeping chambers would cross the narrow opening. Water would travel in visible grooves around its rim. Every so often a face would appear at an eye hole to confirm that I was still there, and I would hold up two fingers: awake, unchanged.

That first watch would terrify me.

Not because anything dramatic happened, but because almost nothing did. The lamp flame would shorten. Condensation would gather on one side of the pit and not the other. Fine grit would loosen beneath my left foot. Somewhere behind the clay, something would make a sound like a person carefully folding cloth.

I would begin to understand why an empty place was dangerous here. Emptiness was not absence. It was an invitation extended without knowing to whom.

So I would fill the hollow with attention.

I would hum the approach signal of the course above me, though it was not yet mine to use. I would count droplets until the pattern changed. I would shift my weight from one floor pad to another, not enough to interfere, only enough to make my presence legible. Near midnight, a thread of warm water would emerge from the pale seam the elder had warned me about. It would bend toward my sleeping mat.

I would mark its path with clay counters from my pocket. I would not block it.

At dawn, when the course members came, I would report:

“The pale seam wetted after the Rain Court gave its fourth deep note. The water was warm but not luminous. It bent toward cloth, then divided around my heel. The west edge rose a little. There were two ticks below me and one above.”

They would ask whether I was sure about the order of the ticks.

I would say no.

That answer would be the beginning of my acceptance.

Afterward, I would become a hollow watcher—not immediately an officeholder, but the person sent into spaces the town had opened and could not yet close. Fresh clay pits. Rooms stripped for rebuilding. A collapsed storage niche while its wall was relaid. The cavity beneath a cistern lifted for wedging. I would spend nights in the town’s temporary absences, keeping them from becoming hidden.

The work would alter me. My heavy tread would become useful below ground, where a basin-born watcher might be too light to test a new floor. Builders would ask me to cross repaired pads and listen to how the clay answered my weight. I would never acquire their effortless balance, but I would learn a slower competence: foot, wait, pressure, release. The town would teach me not grace, exactly, but consent.

My sleeping chamber would be near the boundary between the old quarter and the kiln course, in a wedge-shaped room with no level surface. One wall would be old enough to contain seven daylight eyes. In the afternoon, reflected coins would shake across my blankets and climb my body as the runoff changed. The room would have belonged to several courses over time, and none would relinquish it completely. Roof water would be claimed by one group, the warm wall by another, the drain beneath my bed by a third. I would belong to all three in minor, inconvenient ways.

A wall toad would live beneath the lowest floor pad.

It would be an immense, patient animal, violet-gray and always slightly muddy. At first I would call it something sentimental from my birthplace. The children would refuse this name. Eventually it would become Under-Seven, because it sat beneath the seventh wall eye whenever the ridge water sharpened.

Under-Seven and I would develop reputations together. If it climbed onto my sleeping ledge, I would inspect the floor. If I returned from a watch with wet clay to my elbows, it would open one eye and appear to judge the quality of my report. Neighbors would ask after the toad before asking after me.

My daily name would change more than once.

Heavy Heel would soften into Pit-Sitter. After I correctly noticed mineral foam bending toward a blocked wall eye, someone would call me Bent Shelf. For a year I might be Two Ticks, after that first watch, though the name would always carry the admission that I had not known their order.

The name that remained would come later.

I would be keeping vigil in an abandoned chamber beneath a stair, a place scheduled to be filled with insulation growth and then reopened as a warm sleeping niche. During the night, the breathing gap would begin to narrow. Not quickly enough to justify panic, but steadily. The mineral shelves would make their soft ticking noises and bend inward, pale as folded cartilage.

My clapper would have slipped beyond reach.

Calling out would do little; three wet walls and a cistern stood between me and the nearest occupied room. So I would take a roof fragment from my necklace and scratch a shallow groove toward an old conduit. Into that groove I would guide the condensation from the lamp niche, drop by drop. The thread would run through the conduit and emerge in a household two chambers away, where no water should have been.

They would see it. They would know that an unwitnessed flow was asking to be witnessed.

The wall would be opened before dawn.

After that, I would be called Borrowed Water.

Not because the water belonged to me. Because for one night I had given it a duty it did not usually have, and because I returned it to its course when I was done.

In old age, I would become one of those people summoned to sit beside uncertainty. I would taste white crust from a wall and admit when it told me nothing. I would place my palm on a stair while a child jumped three levels above. I would teach young watchers that fear and attention are not opposites, and that calm is not the same as accuracy.

My own first rain-watch report would be repeated against me whenever I grew pompous:

“Two ticks below and one above.”

“In which order, Borrowed Water?”

“I am still considering.”

When I died, I would not want all my bones ground into a memorial jar. A jar is too close to completion. I would ask for one finger bone to be set beside the eye opening in my old chamber, where runoff could touch it in daylight. The rest could be used as temper in small repair counters: objects carried into pits, placed beside new leaks, wedged beneath uncertain lamps.

So I would remain in the town not as a monument, but distributed among its corrections.

You would find me, then, below the obvious life of the basin: beneath a lifted cistern, inside a room temporarily without purpose, at the bottom of a clay pit while rain stitched silver lines through the opening overhead. I would be the outsider who became useful by occupying what could not safely be left empty.

Not a master of water. Not even one of its finest listeners.

Only Borrowed Water, keeping a hollow company until morning.
