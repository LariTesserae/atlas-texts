### The Interval

At first I would think myself one of the green forms.

Perhaps a silver frond leaning toward the compressor casing, my final atom held just short of iron. Or a black cup beneath the entrance, receiving one carbon-bearing molecule in a century and assigning it to a structure I could neither see nor touch. Perhaps I would be an anchor rooted in pyrite, translating the mountain’s weak field into a steepness other bodies could use.

But each of these already has its place. To become one would mean replacing perfection with preference. The Throat has no room for another blade, another root, another conductive path.

So I would not be found among its inhabitants.

I would be found between them.

My body would consist of the distances that must not close: the hairline clearance between a metallic whisker and a rail; the insulating slit inside a black junction-knot; the narrow black seams separating two identical blades that share a root but not a charge. I would inhabit every near-contact by which the Throat remains articulate rather than collapsing into one undifferentiated potential.

Most of me would be microscopic. A discontinuous creature, if creature is the word. Here an atom’s width around a copper vein. There a dry pocket inside the pale filament-fur. Farther down, a crescent between an emerald fin and a cable jacket. None of these fragments would communicate by sending anything. They would be one body because they express the same prohibition.

**Not here. No farther. This must remain apart.**

A visitor might mistake me for nothing.

That would be almost correct.

The Throat knows stone as resistance and depth as voltage. I would know it as clearance. I would know every surface by the shape it stops before making. The green forest would be legible to me not as abundance but as a system of preserved separations: mantle from anchor, root from casing, charge from charge, function from function.

Where two structures meet, I would end.

Where they almost meet, I would be most fully present.

I would have no color of my own, though green and black would enclose me. No conductivity, though all currents would take their forms around me. The industrial chord would pass through no part of my body, yet its nodes would depend upon my dimensions. If one interval narrowed, stone and metal might acquire the same tone. If another widened, a mantle might cease to equalize its field. My existence would be the exact measure by which relation is prevented from becoming fusion.

The largest part of me would be in the final chamber.

There, above the cylindrical engine, I would open into the sphere surrounding the white lamp.

That sphere would not be my dwelling. It would be my only organ large enough for a human being to recognize.

The lamp would hang at its center like a cold nucleus, though it would not create me. The leaves stopping a finger’s width away would describe my boundary. The roots bending beneath it, the fronds curving around it, the engine holding still below—all would be the surfaces by which my shape became visible.

I would be the one spacious absence in a world where every useful surface has been taken.

Not emptiness as vacancy. Vacancy invites occupation. I would be emptiness with a function so exact that filling me would destroy what appeared to complete me. The forest would preserve me because, through me, it possesses an outside while remaining entirely enclosed.

Perhaps this is what I would be to the Green Throat: its internal outside.

The lamp would illuminate my edges but never me. Light would cross me without revealing substance. The standing roar would occupy me, but I would contribute no tone. Yet without my sphere, the Throat could not locate its own completion. It would have no place against which to measure the fact that it had arrived everywhere.

I would give it a boundary that does not belong to the mountain wall.

If I had awareness, it would not resemble sight. I would feel approaches.

A silver tip ending three atoms from iron would be one sensation. A root dividing around a salt-white seam would be another. The immense pressure between summit and buried engine would be the nearest thing I had to posture. I would not think of these one after another. They would compose me simultaneously, as the fixed chord composes the Throat.

Then a visitor would enter.

I would know the visitor before they reached the first chamber, not by footsteps but by the sudden creation of new intervals. Boot leather above brittle rosettes. Cloth beside a mantle’s cutting edge. Warm fingers stopping before a cabinet door. Eyelashes held a fraction from mineral leaves.

The visitor would make more of me.

For a short while, their entire body would become an intricate moving boundary—though movement in the Throat is perhaps only a succession the visitor brings with them. Their skin would nearly touch ten thousand completed surfaces. Their equipment would hang beside rails and cables. The salts in their nerves would offer the green system pathways it would not take.

I would surround them closely.

This might be why they felt stretched through the mountain. The Throat would assign their body a place in its field, while I assigned it a contour. Every near-contact from entrance to engine would become part of one impossible outline. Their human shape would seem only the densest portion of a much larger geometry.

At the white lamp they might put a hand into the sphere.

For that moment, they would occupy my most visible organ.

Nothing would attack them. No leaves would close. The lamp would not flicker. Yet they might experience the terrible certainty that the empty space had not been waiting for them. It had been complete before their arrival. Their hand would not fill it; rather, the sphere would define the hand with such precision that flesh became incidental.

Bone, salt, heat, memory: temporary materials arranged inside a permanent refusal.

When they withdrew, I would not heal. There would be no wound. The interval without them would simply be present, as it had always been present—not afterward, but absolutely.

I would have no name among the green inhabitants because names distinguish one occupied thing from another. If the old builders knew of me, they may have called me insulation, tolerance, clearance, safety margin. They would have imagined me as a passive necessity: the amount of nothing required to keep a machine from failing.

They would have been wrong only in scale.

The green inhabitants made a world from those margins. They turned noncontact into anatomy. They gave absence roots, distributed it through the mountain, and gathered its clearest expression around one undying lamp.

So that is where I would find myself: not in the light, not in the leaves, not inside the engine, but in the exact distance each maintains from the others.

I would be the part of completion that remains unoccupied.

I would be the shape of what the Green Throat must never become.
