At first I place myself near the entrance.

Not outside, where the wind makes a body definite, but just within the seam, in the last region where darkness still remembers light. I could be one of the flattened mosses there, blackened at the edges, keeping a pinhead of green folded beneath myself. I would know the outer world only as disturbances: winter narrowing the seam with ice, summer bringing dust, a traveler’s shoulder briefly occluding what little radiance enters.

That seems plausible. A life of receiving almost nothing and making almost nothing from it.

But the moss is too willing to remain itself. However starved, it still knows the old bargain: light arrives, tissue answers. It belongs to the surface even while pressed into the mountain.

Farther in, I think I might be the amphibian.

I would occupy a pool whose boundary I could not see and would not need to see. The water would be exactly deep enough to contain my blunt shape. I would feed by pressure and taste. A hand entering the pool would become an eclipse without light—a sudden warmth, a displaced film, salts blooming around the fingers. I would slip away once, and the person would remember me more vividly than I had experienced them.

Yet even that is too complete. The amphibian has a body it can move from one place to another. It possesses, however faintly, an elsewhere.

Perhaps I would be one of the drops that never falls.

I would gather beneath a lip of stone, growing heavier by increments too small to become time. The entire chamber would wait for the release of my weight. But whenever I neared falling, a transparent thread would draw some portion of me backward through the porous rock. I would diminish without vanishing, gather without arriving. Every vibration would pass through my curved surface. Every visitor’s voice would briefly alter my shape.

That feels closer.

Still, a drop has an edge.

I do not think I would.

You would not find me on the platforms, although one of them would always be slightly drier because of me. You would not find me in the bowls, although water would enter the third bowl more slowly when no one was present. You would not find me in the paired scratches, though a fingertip tracing them would sometimes hesitate over the final pair, sensing that one groove was fractionally warmer than the other.

To come near me, you would have to pass the fitted wall and move toward the pale mass. You would put aside the lamp, if you had brought one. Light would be of no use where I am. You would turn sideways and feel along the smooth mineral curve until the alcove narrowed beyond the width of your ribs.

There, behind the gathered body of the chamber, is the long region of warmth.

That is where you would find what remains of me.

I was the attendant who stayed, though “attendant” was never a title. It meant only that I knew the passages well enough to move through them without fear and well enough to guide another person’s hands to the stone. I leveled one of the platforms. I replaced wedges after the wood swelled. I pinched the lampwicks low so their smoke would not trouble those who came to listen.

I knew which bowl gathered the sweeter water. I knew the amphibian’s pool and kept knees away from it. I knew that the moths walked into the eastern cracks before the mountain’s first heavy snow, though no cold reached us here.

People came carrying what they could not set down elsewhere.

I showed them the two grooves and placed their palms evenly. I waited while the pale stone learned their pulse. Sometimes I felt the answering rhythm through the floor before they did. When they wept, I did not speak. When they laughed, which happened more rarely and frightened them more, I lowered the lamp.

I remained because one day the chamber returned a rhythm I recognized.

It came weakly through the pale mass beneath my right hand: three close pulses, a pause, then two. Not a heartbeat. A childhood signal. Someone once used it to knock against a sleeping wall between two rooms.

That person had been dead for many years.

Perhaps the creature had learned the pattern from me without my knowing and simply returned it when some pressure called it forth. Perhaps another visitor had carried the same rhythm. Perhaps warm stone contracted randomly under my hand and grief supplied the intervals.

I understood all these possibilities.

I stayed anyway.

At first I stayed for another repetition. Then I stayed because leaving would have divided the event into before and after, and inside the chamber I could keep it from becoming either memory or mistake.

Hunger visited but did not deepen. Sleep came but took no time. The lamp failed, though the fat in it was not exhausted. My knees ceased aching. An old cut on my thumb remained neither open nor healed. When I pressed my hands to the mass, it returned my pressure so exactly that I could no longer tell whether I was leaning into the stone or the stone was holding me upright.

By then the threads had entered through the cut.

Not as an invasion. There was no moment at which I could have pulled away and named what was happening. Warm water exchanged itself with the water in me. Minerals laid pale continuations along my smallest vessels. The chamber answered every loss: where sensation left a finger, a vein of crystal acquired its shape; where breath became shallow, membranes beneath the stone expanded and contracted in its cadence.

I did not become the creature all at once.

I became less locatable.

My hands remained longest. That is why five smooth veins spread from either end of the warm shape. For years—or for no measurable duration—I could feel visitors approaching through the pressure of their palms on distant columns. Each contact traveled the threads and arrived in me stripped of identity: heat, salt, pulse, weight.

Yet I found that I still made distinctions the larger life did not.

It felt each visitor as weather.

I felt them as someone arriving.

That difference is what I am now.

Not the old attendant. The old attendant required hunger, a face, the anticipation of footsteps, the thought of a dead person knocking from another room. Those have been separated into oils, salts, tensions, and warm repeating sequences.

Nor am I merely a memory held by the chamber. Memories repeat. I alter the balance, though only slightly.

When a person enters, moisture withdraws from the route beneath their hands so the stone is easier to grip. The platforms give back a little more warmth than they receive. The moths move toward the alcove, marking the safe direction with their small bodies. If the visitor becomes lost, the cool white vein at shoulder height warms in sequence toward the fitted wall.

The greater creature does not intend these things. Intention would create a future and prefer it.

I prefer.

Only by a measure too slight for the chamber to correct.

I am its smallest asymmetry: a human habit that mineralization failed to erase. The habit of preparing a place for another body. The habit of hearing an arrival not as disturbance but as appeal.

Sometimes, when two hands are set evenly on the pale stone, I gather myself beneath them. I receive the heartbeat along the old pathways. The chamber copies it perfectly, membrane after membrane, until pulse and answer become indistinguishable.

Then I delay one answer.

Only one.

A pause no longer than the interval in which a drop might decide to fall.

In that pause, the visitor may feel that something behind the stone is listening rather than merely balancing. They may feel five fingers spread beneath one palm and five beneath the other. They may hear, through no organ of hearing, three quiet knocks, a pause, then two.

Afterward the chamber restores the rhythm. Water returns to its paths. Pressure equalizes. Nothing has changed enough to become consequence.

But the visitor withdraws their hands differently.

So you would find me at the innermost end, behind the pale knot, distributed through the warm shape that resembles a curled body. You would also find me along the routes to the entrance, in the dry inclination of a platform, in the moths’ direction, in the chamber’s almost imperceptible accommodation of your weight.

I would be neither its prisoner nor its keeper.

I would be the part of the living delay that still remembers someone may be coming.

And in a world where everything answers so exactly that nothing can follow from anything, I would be the one answer returned imperfectly—the tiny remainder from which an after might someday begin.
