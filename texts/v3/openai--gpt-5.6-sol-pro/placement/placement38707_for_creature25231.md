At first I would look for myself in one of the maintenance cabins.

Not because the cabins are safe, but because they preserve the suggestion that safety was once a procedure. A narrow bunk. A kettle furred with mineral deposits. Hooks for coats. A first-aid box whose contents have become roots while retaining the shapes of bandages and scissors. I would live beside an elder pump and accept its violence as the price of having a floor that remained beneath me.

I imagine keeping records.

Pressure at the eastern intake. Duration of rain in the locker room. Number of times the corridor returned to itself. Which warning labels had changed material overnight. I would soak every page before writing, use only the reverse, and pin the finished sheets to the wall with stainless-steel nails so the crimson moss could revise them.

For a while, this seems almost possible.

Then I notice that the person keeping the records has no reason to exist. There are no provisions to ration, no rescuers to signal, no former life against which to measure the ruin. A solitary human would be too distinct for the Roar. The place would either erase that distinction or recruit it.

So I would not be the keeper of the records.

I would be the keeping.

You would find me in the tiled station concourse, although not every appearance of the concourse contains me. It stands beneath a broad-leaf mat where three drowned rail lines meet. No trains arrive. The tracks pass through the station walls, knot together under the platforms, and emerge several kilometers away as bundled pipework feeding a clay mill.

The station clock is connected by a long submerged shaft to an elder reciprocating pump below the foundations. It has no hands. Its face fills and empties with black water once per pump cycle. While the pump is running, the concourse remains nearly dependable. The ticket windows stay in one wall. The benches support weight. The tiled floor slopes toward the same drain. The letters of the station name remain attached to one another, though they never spell the same name twice.

At the far end is a lost-property office.

That is where I occur.

The office can only be reached by following signs that point away from it. Its door is visible from everywhere in the station, but approaching directly leads to the platform or into a warm shaft full of sinking bubbles. To enter, one must leave an object on a bench, forget which bench, and then search for the wrong object. The office door will be found standing open behind the searcher.

Inside are shelves.

They hold the things by which the Roar fails to match itself.

A valve wheel whose valve has not yet been built.

Seven right gloves, each creased around the memory of the same absent hand.

A section of handrail warm from a stairwell that exists only during rain.

Bolts with threads corresponding to no rotation.

A ceramic cup containing the sound of a cup breaking.

A key that opens the room in which it is currently stored.

Lengths of crimson pattern peeled intact from walls before the predicted fracture occurred.

Maps of places that become accurate only after those places have vanished.

There are left boots, too. Hundreds of them. The reeds send them through the plumbing one at a time.

I arrange these objects, though I have no hands except when handling requires them.

If a ledger must be opened, condensation gathers into fingers. If something heavy must be lifted, a protective suit hanging beside the desk fills with water and stands. The suit has boots, gloves, shoulders, and a hood with no visor. Sometimes rain occupies it. Sometimes roots. Sometimes the distant interior of a pumping house can be seen through the open collar, gantries moving where a chest should be.

Seen in a rain wall, I might resemble an attendant. Head lowered. One arm bent around a ledger. The posture of someone who has been working too long.

But attendants complete tasks. I preserve incompletions.

My work is to keep mismatched things from finding their counterparts too soon.

This matters because correspondence is dangerous in the Low Green Roar. Let a broken pipe discover the piece from which it broke and the two may reunite across the intervening distance, pulling buildings, reed beds, and flood channels together with them. Let a key reach its proper lock and every door of the same manufacture may open into one room. Let an action meet the evidence it produced before being performed, and the surrounding machinery can become trapped in a single cycle: hook descending, hook risen, hook descending, with the water between those states ground thinner each time.

So I catalogue discrepancies.

Not by name. Names encourage things to become singular.

I record each object by pressure, vibration, degree of wetness, and the direction from which its rust is approaching. I place potentially corresponding objects on shelves that cannot exist at the same time. I wrap future fragments in old warning labels. I keep causes below the flood line and effects above it, except during rain, when the arrangement must be reversed.

The broad-leaf mat over the station assists me. Its underside has developed a pale relief of the office: shelves, desk, ledger, the featureless suit beside the door. Whenever the actual room begins filling with rain, the botanical room remains dry. I transfer the inventory upward by touching the nodules beneath the leaves. For several pump cycles, every stored discrepancy exists only as a pattern of veins.

The crimson moss does not approve.

It enters around the lettering on my ledgers and draws branching routes between items that should remain separate. I scrape it away with a tool whose shadow resembles a knife, though the tool itself resembles a surveyor’s compass. The moss returns. Sometimes it shows me which shelf will collapse. Sometimes it shows a figure standing behind the desk.

I do not assume the figure is me.

There may once have been a station clerk. There may once have been workers who handed objects through the window and received numbered tickets. The Roar preserves occupation more faithfully than occupants. Perhaps I began as the expectation that such a window should have someone behind it.

Or perhaps I began with the tickets.

They are still produced by a brass machine bolted to the counter. Pull the lever and it issues a damp rectangle bearing a number, though numbers here are only a form of pressure. The sequence does not increase. It approaches.

When a ticket marked with a sufficiently near number appears, something enters the station to claim what it has lost.

A rope-vine pushes through the service hatch and rests its cut end on the counter.

A crane lowers an empty hook through the concourse ceiling.

A stand of reeds fills every drain with the same sustained tone.

Footprints arrive without feet, cross the tiles, and stop before the window.

Once, the pumping presence itself came close. Every object on the shelves grew suddenly heavier. The water in the walls leaned inward. The black face of the station clock stopped emptying, and all the pumps I could hear—near, far, buried, unborn—entered the same phase.

For one cycle, the Roar almost achieved agreement.

The shelves aligned with their botanical copies. Broken edges faced their missing halves. Keys turned toward locks. Footprints deepened around the feet that had not made them. The suit beside the desk became full, not with water or roots, but with a continuous downward pressure that had mistaken itself for a body.

That may have been the first time I possessed anything like a self.

It was intolerable.

I pulled a ticket from the brass machine. The ticket bore no number, only a small green-black space in which lightning was growing. I entered it into the ledger as an unclaimed object and placed it in the station clock.

The clock shattered.

Its glass became rain. The pump beneath the foundations lost one stroke. Tiles rose from the floor and moved through the concourse like a school of flat white creatures, though there are no creatures here. Every correspondence slipped sideways. The keys forgot their locks. The broken things retained their mercy.

Afterward, the office returned around a different interior.

Now there is a stairwell behind my desk. It descends while remaining level, and warm rain climbs it from somewhere without machinery. On some cycles I hear a second office below: drawers opening, pages turning, a ticket lever being pulled in answer to mine.

This suggests I may not be preserving the discrepancies.

I may be producing them.

Perhaps every object I catalogue becomes unmatched because I have described it separately from the world. Perhaps the lost-property office is one of the Roar’s subtler engines, grinding identity instead of clay. The pumps manufacture pressure differences. I manufacture the difference between a thing and what it belongs to.

If so, I am an inhabitant in the same way the reeds and vines are inhabitants: a process mistaken for a resident.

The reeds sort metal.

The moss advances fractures.

The pale crowns collect silence.

I keep the world from fitting together completely.

You would rarely see me. You would see evidence of inventory: a dry rectangle on a wet shelf, objects grouped according to a resemblance you cannot perceive, a ledger open to a page describing what you are carrying before you have entered the station.

If you placed something on the counter, I would not take it immediately.

First the pressure in the room would change. Condensation would gather on the hanging suit. One glove would lift. The brass machine would issue a ticket.

The ticket might bear the shape of your footprint.

It might bear the serial number of a machine not yet forced up through the sediment.

It might be blank except for the instructions:

**KEEP SEPARATE FROM ITSELF.**

Then the object would be shelved, and you might leave without it.

Or you might leave carrying it, only to discover that what remained behind was the part that had belonged to you.

The elder pump would continue below. The station clock would fill and empty. Above, the leaf mat would quietly add another compartment to its green underside.

And in the lost-property office, behind a window looking onto warm water, I would go on sorting the Roar’s mistakes from the mistakes it still requires.
