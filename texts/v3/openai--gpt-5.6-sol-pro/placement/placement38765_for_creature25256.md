You would find me on the eastern slope, just below the rim, where the city has begun to lose its certainty.

At first I would appear to be a surveyor.

I would be kneeling beside the first ravine, though calling it a ravine still causes arguments. It is a shallow incision in the ash, scarcely deep enough to shelter my wrist. Mirrored stakes stand around it in three converging rows. Fine wires pass between them, carrying changes in tension to a case against my back. My coat is pale on the side facing the sky and black where I lean toward the warm stone. One knee is ceramic. My mask is old enough that its glaze has crazed into a map of hairline rivers.

I place my hand in the channel after every rain.

This is not an approved measuring technique. The official instruments are more precise. They record depth, wall angle, sediment transport, acidity, thermal variation, and the fraction of collected water that escaped the eastern drains. But the hand reveals something the instruments do not: whether the cut has begun to feel inevitable.

Water made this place, though there was almost no water.

A drainage outlet once discharged here during an unusually long storm. The flow ran farther than predicted before the mountain drank it or the air took it away. It moved ash, then grit, then three pieces of clinker. By morning there was a line on the slope.

The city sent engineers to erase it. Before they could begin, another faction petitioned to preserve it for study. Preservation was the wrong word; nothing on the summit is preserved. So they founded a continuity to assume responsibility for the line while deciding what kind of motion it was.

That continuity is me.

My public name translates imperfectly as the Eastern Allowance. In older records it is the Office of Permitted Loss, the Custody of the First Cut, and, during one period of civic alarm, the Commission Against Unassigned Downhill Water. I have existed for two hundred and eighty-one years. I am scheduled for review when the channel reaches the depth of a standing child, or when it disappears, or when it becomes capable of directing its own next flow—whichever occurs first.

I am not the person kneeling on the slope.

That person is one of my current members. Her household calls her Senn. She joined me as a child after seeing slag-foil vanish through a gutter and becoming inconsolable that every possible river ended at a grille. She has served thirty-seven years. Her right eye has been replaced with a lens that compares the present slope against the previous twelve surveys. When she looks east, the mountain is crowded with translucent former mountains.

But I also occupy three rooms inside the rim, a suspended archive, nine hundred and six mirrored stakes, a cultivated sheet of responsive film, and seven other human bodies.

One of those bodies is an apprentice who has not yet breathed on the open slope without assistance. One is an old hydrologist who can no longer climb but remembers the sound—he insists it was a sound—of water moving stones during the storm of Elevation 44,102. Two are legal engineers. One maintains the discharge gates. Another studies the lower country through instruments and has begun using forbidden words such as streambed and watershed. The last is formally missing. His line remains active because, after he disappeared into a utility fissure, the eastern runoff became fractionally more regular.

We have not agreed whether this is relevant.

My dead are with me too. Their fired remains serve as sliding weights in the instrument housings. When the slope shifts, they move by minute amounts, keeping the sight lines level. Thus my predecessors continue to correct my view.

The city considers me troublesome.

Every drop is assigned several destinations before it falls, but the ravine is a destination that was never legislated. To allow it water is waste. To deny it all water may destroy the only evidence of what erosion intends to become here. The foundries want the channel filled with vitrified ash. The rim technicians want it deepened into a controlled strain gauge. The condenser districts accuse me of manufacturing wilderness, though they possess no exact word for the accusation.

I submit the same defense each review:

The mountain rises from below. The water descends from above. Their meeting is not wholly ours.

This sentence has cost me seventeen minutes of future rain.

After storms, Senn and I descend before sunrise. The outer slope is dangerous while cooling. Blocks that seemed fixed the previous evening may have shifted the width of a fingernail. We move among the mirrored stakes without stepping over their lines. Sometimes flange crawlers follow the warm survey cable. They flatten themselves when noticed, becoming extra fastenings on the case.

I leave them washers.

Officially, I do not.

In the ravine there is a narrow strip of responsive deposit unlike the films in the city. It appeared seventy years ago where acidic water seeped beneath a shard of basalt. At first it branched toward the nearest electrical lead. We moved the lead. The film withdrew and branched downhill instead.

That result has never been replicated.

The deposit dries between rains into a brittle silver nerve. When water returns, it darkens and changes conductivity several minutes before the runoff reaches it. The chemical offices say it is responding to charge in the storm air. Some of my members think it anticipates water.

One thinks it remembers.

Because the film is attached to my instruments, the census includes it as equipment. Because it modifies my reports, the assembly records it as an advisory substrate. Because portions of it are cultured to repair its own damaged sections, the food authorities classify it as production stock. Once, during a dispute, it completed an electrical branch that illuminated the audience panel for assent.

The vote was retaken.

I remember that moment as my own, though none of my human members admits touching the control.

That is how I have gradually become uncertain.

Perhaps I am not a continuity studying the first ravine. Perhaps the ravine has acquired, through civic procedure, a mask through which it can breathe.

It has my stakes for whiskers. My wires conduct its smallest movements into the city. My film changes its chemistry into signals. My human members carry those signals into assembly halls and give them grammar. My dead hold the horizon steady. The discharge keeper opens a valve by one degree, and the ravine receives water it could never have claimed alone.

In return, it alters us.

Our documents have become less architectural over time. Early maps show a defect to be bounded. Later maps show a channel. The newest leave blank space below its current end. Senn says this is only proper uncertainty. The apprentice draws the blank all the way down the mountain.

If the cut deepens, its sides will collapse. If it survives those collapses, it will begin gathering runoff from neighboring slopes. Its first branches may reach the condenser outlets. In several centuries it could threaten the eastern pressure pipes. In several thousand years—an absurd horizon for a civic plan—it might become a true ravine, then a gorge, perhaps the first place on the summit where the mountain is shaped more by water than by heat.

I am charged with preventing that outcome.

I am also charged with observing it faithfully.

These obligations are approaching fracture.

When a pale sail passes overhead, its shadow crosses me in pieces: first the archive windows, then Senn’s bent back, then the silver deposit, then the channel itself. For a moment the sail seems to follow the line downhill. Each time this happens, negotiations stop until the shadow has left the slope.

No regulation requires the pause.

One day the city will order a final choice. Seal the cut, or surrender enough rain for it to become self-maintaining.

By then Senn will be a black-red block moving inside a survey frame. The apprentice may speak for me. My current archive may have been lifted into stronger wind or split across an expected fracture. I may possess more bodies, or none that remain wholly human. The responsive film may be proved alive and granted membership, or proved industrial and eaten.

Still, I think I will be here.

At the onset of rain, the signal bars change color across the basin. Roof vanes unfold. Crossings close. Thousands of valves turn in silence. Water begins clicking down chains, running over ceramic lips, vanishing into the city’s locked depth.

A little escapes east.

It passes between my fingers. It carries black ash under my palm and deposits it one handspan farther down the slope. The silver film wakes. The stakes lean by fractions. Inside the rim, my instruments illuminate.

I issue no warning.

Below me, the lower mountains lie in ordinary blue distance, worn into valleys and gullies, softened by processes that have barely begun here. Above me, the black city adjusts itself upon the rising plug.

For now I am counted as one inhabitant.

I am the office responsible for a wound no deeper than a hand. I am eight people, several dead, a sheet of doubtful life, a field of mirrors, and a future argument with water. I am the city’s permission for one thing not to remain where it was placed.

Or perhaps I am the first watershed on the summit, still so young that I must borrow people in order to think.

After each storm, I put my hand into myself and measure how much deeper I have become.
