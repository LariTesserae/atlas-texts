At first I would imagine myself as something that could cross the plain.

A seal, perhaps—descending through the iron water with the surface still carried in my blood. I would have warmth, lungs, a remembered sky. The basin would spread beneath me as a dim map: ridges, scour marks, pale eruptions of sand. But I would not belong to it. Every movement would spend too much. I would be a visitor whose true country was overhead.

Nor would I be the cod holding itself in the current. The cod possesses the freedom of appearing and vanishing, but it is governed by distant waters. It follows corridors that pass through the plain without becoming part of it.

I would have to go lower.

Perhaps beneath a stone, among the amphipods: a pale, jointed body folded tightly against the underside of a slab. The stone would be both roof and horizon. I would know the tide first as a trembling in my limbs, then as grit ticking over the exposed surface above. I would feed on whatever entered the seam—a torn thread of kelp, a translucent shred of flesh, the softened grain of ancient wood. When the stone shifted, my whole known world would shift with it.

But even that seems too exposed. The slab will turn eventually. Anchor ice will take it, or gravel will lever beneath one edge. The temporary household will open to the current, and its inhabitants will scatter like filings.

So I go deeper still, beneath the creatures that have bodies recognizable from above.

I find myself in one of the quiet hollows, not at its center but along the sloping margin where coarse sand gives way to fine gray mud. Above me is an old iceberg furrow, already losing its form. One bank remains faintly higher than the other. Crushed shell lies in it, and a dark fragment of driftwood has been buried across the trench. A flatfish sometimes settles overhead. Its weight arrives as a broad, gentle darkness in the sediment, then lifts away.

I am not a worm in this mud, though worms pass through me.

I am the thin living boundary around their burrows.

I am a colony of bacteria occupying the depth where the last oxygen from the sea meets what has been buried without it. I have no single body. I am a stain too faint to be seen, distributed through water films between grains. Above me, the mud is cold and oxygenated whenever the tide forces fresh water into it. Below me lie old carbon, reduced minerals, shell dust, and the slow chemical remainder of lives that fell here generations ago.

My country may be no thicker than a fingernail.

Its ceiling rises and falls. After a strong tide, oxygen reaches deeper and drives me downward. When silt seals the bottom, I climb toward the surface grain by grain. I do not travel as a crab travels. One part of me grows where conditions become possible; another ceases where they become impossible. Movement is succession.

The iceberg is my catastrophe and my revelation.

If a keel cuts through the hollow, whole centuries of sediment are turned over in a day. Buried layers open to the sea. Some of me is killed at once by the sudden chemistry of the water. Other parts awaken. Nutrients that had descended molecule by molecule are released in abundance. For a short time the raw trench becomes bright with invisible labor.

There is no grief in this, but there is loss. There is no triumph, but there is increase.

A torn worm enriches the grains around it. A splinter of kelp settles and darkens. The body of an amphipod enters a pore too small for scavengers. I take these things apart without knowing what they were. Seal, algae, cod, driftwood: all distinctions soften at my depth. Everything arrives as compounds, gradients, opportunities.

I would know the larger world only through consequences.

Summer would be a slight increase in what descends.

Winter would be closure.

A kelp torn away many yards upslope would become, days later, an unfamiliar richness in the mud. A whale’s call might compress the pore water for an instant. The grounding of a berg would be pressure, rupture, oxygen. The turning tide would alter the direction of seepage through spaces no eye could see.

I would never know the whole plain. I would not even know one stone. Yet the plain would pass through me continuously: pulverized mountain, dead algae, dissolved shell, glacial flour, the iron of old mud, the breath brought down from a surface I could not imagine.

There would be others below me, using what I cannot use, and others above me living on what my kind releases. We would form layers without walls, nations without boundaries except temperature and chemistry. Disturbance would redraw us. Stillness would redraw us differently.

If someone lifted a core from the hollow, they might find a dark band or a change in odor. They might call it an anoxic horizon, a microbial mat, a redox boundary. They would speak as if they had found a thing.

But I would be less a thing than a position held briefly in the continuous settling of the world.

That is where I would find myself: a few millimeters beneath the drowned plain, along the fading edge of an iceberg scar, living in the water between grains.

Not standing.

Not hidden exactly, because there would be no shape to reveal.

I would be the life that moves when its boundary moves—the narrow, patient seam where what falls from above is admitted into the dark.
