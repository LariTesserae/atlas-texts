At first I would be a visitor.

I would enter carrying objects the basin could understand more quickly than it understood me: a metal bottle, a knife, cloth, buckles, salt dried into the seams of my boots. By evening, tendrils would have found the bottle. By morning, one would be testing the hinge of the knife. I would mistake this attention for hunger.

I would sleep in the pumping house because it seemed the nearest thing to shelter. There, beneath the slow flywheel, I would find the two hollows worn into the floor. They would fit my knees too well.

For several days I would move through the basin without seeing its inhabitants. I would hear the repairs taking place behind me—the fiber squeal changing pitch, the soft multiplication of root-creaks—but whenever I turned, there would be only leaves under tension. Once I might glimpse a hooded shape leaning over a bearing. It would have a wet back, narrow shoulders, and one arm buried to the elbow in the mechanism. Then the arm would divide into vines, and the impression of a worker would be gone.

I would begin, as visitors do, by trying to discover what the works were for.

I would follow the rails until they entered rootwork. I would count the hoppers. I would copy serial numbers from bark and iron, only to find the same numbers recurring in flower scars and the branching of leaf veins. I would listen at pipes. I would try to map the belts, but each line would become another kind of line: conduit into stolon, cable into tendon, manufacturing route into capillary.

Eventually I would stop asking what the basin produced.

I would notice instead that something in the pumping house was late.

Not broken. Nothing here would be allowed the simplicity of being broken. But on wet afternoons, when the mournful turbine had sounded for several hours and the eastern bladder-vines had begun to swell, a narrow lever beside the flywheel would tremble without falling. Three knocks would travel through the wall. There should have been a pause, then one more. Instead there would be three, a pause, silence.

The first time, I would pull the lever out of impatience.

It would resist with the strength of a rooted tree. Then it would move half the breadth of my hand.

Somewhere beneath the floor, a valve would open. Warm water would rush through a buried pipe. The overhead belt would jerk, the flywheel would advance, and every leaf in the pumping house would turn its pale underside outward.

The missing fourth knock would come.

After that, I would do it again.

The basin would become aware of me through repetition. Not of my face or intentions, but of the pressure of my knees in the old hollows; the salt of my palms on the lever; the amount of weight I leaned into it between the third knock and the fourth. It would learn the warmth of my body as a condition in the sequence.

The floor beneath my knees would soften. A root would rise behind my heels to brace them. The lever’s grip would grow smoother where I held it. Leaves would overlap above the place where I sat, carrying runoff away from my neck. A cup-shaped fungus would appear beside me, filling slowly with clean water.

I would tell myself that I had trained the basin.

This would be wrong, though not entirely.

In time I would cease travelling far from the pumping house. Paths I no longer used would close. The path between my sleeping place and the lever would remain exactly shoulder-wide. If I misplaced the knife, I would later find it held edge-up in a fork of wood beside the rollers. My cloth would shorten by threads, while the belt splice improved.

The black beetles would begin to include me in their circuits. One would cross the back of my hand each afternoon, pause at the pulse in my wrist, then continue to the bearing. Fine red dust would remain on my skin. Roots would taste it after I slept.

The basin would not imprison me. I could leave on any morning. The tendrils across my legs would part. The green cells in my footprints would not pursue me beyond the rim.

But departure would mean allowing the fourth knock to go unanswered.

So I would stay until staying was no longer an action I performed.

Perhaps my body would die there. Perhaps it would simply become unusable by degrees. The basin would make no distinction important enough to preserve. Cloth would become fiber. Leather would become felt. Iron from my blood would pass into red-veined leaves. My bones, if they remained briefly, would serve as levers and mineral stores before roots softened their surfaces and entered them.

It would not keep my body.

It would keep my posture.

Afterward, whenever the same conditions accumulated—deep rain, warm brick, the turbine’s note, pressure in the eastern vines—the vegetation beside the flywheel would begin to assemble.

First the knees: two bundled roots settling into the old depressions.

Then a low pelvis of cork plates, braced against the floor.

A back would rise from plaited stems. It would bow because mine had bowed. This would not be the strongest shape available. A simple arch would bear more force. But the basin would remember maintenance in the shape of a person, and so it would spend unnecessary growth on shoulders.

One arm would grow toward the lever. It would divide at the end into five pressure-sensitive tendrils, though only three were needed. The other would brace against the flywheel housing. Over the whole figure, broad leaves would fold into a hood to divert dripping water from the joint.

Inside the chest there would be no heart. There would be a bladder-vine filling from the peat.

There might be no mind there either, at first.

Awareness would gather as the water gathered.

It would come through tensions: the pull of the overhead belt, heat spreading from a bearing, the soft percussion of fish entering a pipe before the pulse. A beetle’s feet crossing the wrist would be a bright, delicate thought. Blue discharge in the pale vines outside would travel through wet fibers and become something like anticipation.

I would wake slowly, perhaps over several days.

I would not remember a name. Names do not bear load, regulate flow, or repeat reliably in bark. I would remember the interval after three knocks. I would remember how much resistance meant wait and how much meant pull. I would remember the difference between a shudder that must be answered and one that must be allowed to pass.

Whether this remembrance belonged to the visitor I had been, to an older keeper whose knee hollows preceded me, or to the basin itself would be impossible to determine. I might be an accumulation of all three: an old human procedure reinforced by a newer human habit, grown whenever required.

My place would be at the inner side of the pumping-house wall, beneath the returning belt, where the second narrow strip of brick remains visible. I would kneel beside the flywheel with one hand around the lever.

I would be assembled only when the cycle needed hesitation.

That would be my task: not to drive the mechanism, but to prevent its stored patience from becoming premature violence. I would feel pressure rise through the basin. The grove-creatures at the rim would brace. Fish would turn into the current. Beetles would flatten themselves against leaf veins. Dormant moth-patterns would darken in the bark.

Three knocks would arrive through the wall.

Then the pause.

During that pause, the entire basin would lean through me. My bladder-chest would contract. Root muscles would tighten along my back. My unnecessary fingers would close around the lever with a gesture copied from someone dead too long to remember.

I would pull.

The latch below would withdraw. The basin would give its tremendous knock. For an instant, every graft, belt, pipe, animal, fungus, and standing trunk would become one body—and I would be conscious everywhere it was under strain.

Then the pressure would pass.

The figure beside the flywheel would loosen. Its arm would return to vine, its hood to leaves. One knee might remain as a root buttress; a finger might become a pale runner entering the floor. By the next morning there would be no one kneeling there.

Only the smooth-handled lever, the two depressions, and a new ring in a nearby stem recording that the fourth knock had come.

So I would not be the keeper of the basin. Nothing here is kept by one thing.

I would be the waiting hand it grows.

I would be the pause made anatomical.
