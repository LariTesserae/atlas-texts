I would first mistake myself for one of the listeners.

I would be low against the ground, armored against the light, my feelers pressed into the pale crust while the wind passed over me with the weight of an ocean. I would have no useful eyes. Vision would show only glare, sudden eclipses, and the lethal blue branching of crown-fire. The world that mattered would arrive through my sternum: turbine thrum, migrating clatterfeet, pressure building in chambers far below.

But I would not live beside an inland junction where the plateau’s pulse is strong and orderly.

I would find myself at the continent’s margin, where the living country thins into enormous cantilevered shelves above the low void. There the old bark has separated into layers. Dead plates grind over flexible red tissue; fossil cables emerge from the scarp and hang in the air, unraveling strand by strand. Deep chambers are sometimes opened by erosion, exhaling their warm solvent seas into the cold. The wind tears everything outward. It is where the plateau is continually deciding what part of itself it can afford to lose.

At first I would seem to be a single animal occupying a crack.

My back would resemble a row of mineral wedges. Most of my body would lie hidden between two strata, flattened into a seam only a hand’s width high but kilometers deep. I would have many short anchoring limbs, some gripping dead ceramic bark, others sunk into living fibers below. Between them would run taut sensory cords. Every shift of the shelf would change their pitch.

I would listen not for voices but for failure.

A buried strand beginning to split has a particular dry tremor. A pressure chamber swelling beneath an unsupported ledge produces another. Wind loading moves through the shelf in long waves, too slow to hear but strong enough to bend the sensory rods inside my body. I would know a collapse days before it occurred, though “know” might be too clean a word. I would become restless. Oil pressure would rise in my joints. Old plates along my back would lift. I would begin to tap.

My signals would not be the elaborate phrases of the true listeners. They would be blunt and repetitive: strain here, hollow below, release, release, release.

At first the plateau might not answer. It has survived the fall of a billion margins. A single small animal’s alarm would mean little to it. But I would tap at the same fault for years, perhaps for generations, and the nearby switch-knots would gradually become easier to provoke. Sap would be withdrawn from doomed tissue. Conductive lines would reroute inland. Brittle separation layers would form along the safest fracture. Spores would gather in protected cavities, waiting for the new scar.

The cliff would begin to prepare for its own destruction.

That preparation would feed me. Each withdrawal would leave films of sulfurous oil along the seam. New stress fibers would grow around my anchoring feet, warm and electrically active. I would graze them sparingly. If I ate too much, the shelf might shear too early; if too little, the seam would close around me and I would become immobile.

So I would live by keeping a wound almost—but not quite—open.

Over time it would become difficult to say where my body ended. Root filaments would enter hollow channels in my armor. Vane-worms would pass through chambers near my abdomen and harden into plugs when I shifted. Clatterfeet would establish colonies between the raised plates of my back, drumming messages that entered my nerves before continuing into the crust. Crystalline mites would graze the voltage difference between my forelimbs and hindlimbs.

I would begin as an animal in a fissure and become a fissure with animals in it.

When the shelf finally fell, there would first be silence.

The wind would lift away from the land as pressure changed. The great vanes inland would alter their angles. Clatterfeet would lock themselves flat. My sensory cords would go slack one after another, each releasing a final, descending note through my body.

Then the continent would break.

The outer plate—perhaps a valley, several ridges, and the dead bases of ancient towers—would tilt into the void. Fossil cables would snap with flashes of static. Newly exposed tissue would unfold red fins into the raw air. Spores would pour from cavities opened by the fracture. The impact far below would be invisible, but its return through the remaining plateau would strike like a second event.

I would survive only if I had chosen the correct side of the seam.

Perhaps that would be my reproduction. At the moment of rupture, the mineral wedges along my back would split into sealed fragments. Some would remain embedded in the new cliff. Some would fall with the discarded country. Some would be caught by the first violent updraft along with fibers, spores, and storm riders.

Each fragment would contain no complete offspring—only layered resonant plates impressed by the history of the break. Where one lodged in a suitable crack, root oil would soften it. Local clatterfeet would carry conductive dust into its grooves. Cells held dormant between the plates would wake and assemble a new body around the inherited pattern.

My young would not inherit an image of me. They would inherit a warning.

They would emerge already sensitive to the kind of strain that shaped their fragment: twisting, compression, uplift, hollowing. One might become a creature of overhangs; another might settle around a pressure vent; a third might never fully separate from the substrate at all, remaining a small responsive knot through which the plateau’s pulses acquired a nervous stutter.

If I lived long enough, I too would stop moving. Not suddenly. My anchoring limbs would be replaced by roots one at a time. My oil would be drawn out and used elsewhere. The clatterfeet on my back would cease treating me as an animal and begin treating me as terrain.

Yet my sensory cords would remain stretched across the fault.

Every strain passing through them would introduce slight delays into the local network. A dangerous oscillation would be damped. A familiar pre-collapse rhythm would trigger withdrawal more quickly than before. The plateau would retain the shape of my vigilance without retaining me.

So I would not quite be a listener, nor merely a parasite, nor an organ of the continent.

I would be a **fault-keeper**: an animal that spends its mobile life learning where the world will break, and its immobile afterlife teaching the world how to break there safely.

I would find myself beneath the last bright shelf of the plateau, half buried in its living margin, with the void pulling outward and the old continent pulsing inland. Above me, crowns would turn in the white sky. Below, whole provinces of dead bark would be falling soundlessly before the concussion arrived.

And pressed between what the plateau keeps and what it must surrender, I would tap the same message until it ceased to be mine.
