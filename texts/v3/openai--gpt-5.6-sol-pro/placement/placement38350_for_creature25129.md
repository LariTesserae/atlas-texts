I would not be in the highest towers or the warm center. Those places are too exposed, too crowded with hunger. Nor would I live beyond the suburbs, where the chemical landmarks grow faint and the open mud offers nothing to brace against.

I would find myself where a principal avenue narrows beneath an old arch.

One side of the arch would still be alive: pale colonial tissue stretched thin over ancestral mineral, tasting the water through thousands of small mouths. The other side would be dead and bored through, crowded with tube-worms and mites. Above, cleaner shrimp would hang in white masses. Below, generations of pilgrims would have pressed the sediment into a hard, shallow trough.

Everything traveling between the central springs and the terraces would eventually pass there.

At first I think I would be one of the curtains hanging from the arch—something almost architectural, transparent and many-lobed, able to know the city only by what touches me. I would taste pilgrims before their bodies arrived. I would contract at the sweetness of disease. Needlefish would strike me as a quick succession of salts and tiny pressures. The great animal passing overhead would become a sudden reversal of current, an immense absence in the chemistry of ordinary life.

But a curtain cannot leave when the quarter suffocates. It must close, endure, or die with the arch.

So perhaps I would be a needlefish: nearly invisible except for the blue bacterial lamp beneath my jaw. I would know the city as openings chosen at speed. I would pass through towers that had stood for millennia without understanding that they were towers. My light would not be mine alone; it would belong to the school, a syllable in a sentence moving faster than thought.

Yet that life seems too bright and brief. I would spend it being summoned into patterns and broken out of them by mouths.

No. I think I would be an old shell-bearer.

Not old when I first became myself. I would begin in a spiral shell thin enough for light to pass through, though there would be almost no light to pass. I would have many legs, a soft folded abdomen, antennae sensitive to the invisible borders laid across the streets. I would leave that first shell without ceremony. There would be no permanent shape waiting underneath it, only a body prepared to fit elsewhere.

My second home might be the calcified brood chamber of a dead colony. My third, a hollow piece of pilgrim armor. Each would reshape me. I would become asymmetrical in ways that recorded where I had lived.

By the time I reached the arch, I would be carrying a chamber broken from some deep tower during a collapse. Its exterior would be pale beneath accumulated growth, its interior polished by the soft turning of my abdomen. One side would bear the dark seam of an ancient conflict where two colonies had met and poisoned each other for generations. I would not know this history. I would carry it anyway.

Others would settle on me.

First a red-brown bacterial film in the grooves. Then mites to graze it. A thread-worm would anchor near the rear of my shell and spread a feeding crown whenever I faced into the current. Shrimp eggs would adhere beneath a ledge. A small bell-throat might grow on the highest point, limp and transparent until touched, when it would flash green.

Eventually I would no longer enter a place alone.

At the cleaner station the shrimp would recognize my scent beneath the scents of everything I carried. They would climb my legs and force themselves into the seams of my joints. They would pull parasites from the soft skin under my shell. Some would graze too deeply. I would endure them until endurance became danger, then strike the wall with a claw and scatter them.

When I walked away, a few would remain aboard.

I would feed on almost anything: bacterial films, dead tissue, eggs spilled from a broken brood cavity, the softened skin of a thing fallen from the upper darkness. I would learn no map, but certain dissolved signatures would draw or repel me. Iron would mean one route. Sweet rot another. The bitter closing of the towers would halt me before I understood there was danger.

I would know the city through my feet.

The packed avenue would have a different vibration from fresh sediment. A pilgrim approaching from blocks away would be a slow rhythm entering my joints. The contraction of a nearby tower would pass through the arch as a sequence of knocks. When needlefish extinguished themselves, I would not see the darkness increase; I would feel their small wakes cease around me.

Sometimes I would stop beneath the arch and become, for a while, part of the bottleneck.

Travelers would divert around my shell. Shrimp would use me as a lower platform. Worms would extend from my back into the accelerated flow. Sediment would bank against my legs. A juvenile shell-bearer might shelter beneath my abdomen, taking my chemical trace for the safety of a wall.

If I remained still long enough, the difference between resting and founding something would become unclear.

But eventually hunger, poison, or the inherited unease of my muscles would move me on. I would cross a district boundary and change behavior before knowing why. I might become docile in the plume of a nursery, combative beside a bacterial enclosure, frantic at the sweetness of infection. What I called intention would often be the city entering me through my gills.

In time my burden would grow too large.

The chamber on my back would thicken with tenants and their remains. Mineral grazers would roughen its surface. Worm tubes would make it top-heavy. The bell-throat would multiply into a small luminous cluster. I would have to brace myself when pilgrims passed, or their wakes would roll me into the road.

Perhaps I would begin breaking pieces away. Perhaps I would not. A shell-bearer has no ideal self toward which it is trying to return. There would be no original shape beneath the accretion, only earlier arrangements.

This is why I think I would be one.

Not because it is free. Nothing in the basin is free in that sense. The shell-bearer is directed by chemistry, burdened by tenants, altered by every refuge it survives. But it carries a small, moving confusion of the city with it. It is citizen and street obstruction, grazer and pasture, animal and inhabited ruin.

And even “I” would be inaccurate.

The bacteria in my gut would digest what my own tissues could not. The film on my shell would feed the mites. The mites would keep the film from smothering the bell-throats. The bell-throats would flash when a hunter’s wake touched them, and their alarm might make me crouch before my own senses detected danger. Cleaner shrimp would preserve my joints while drinking from my wounds. Parasites would wear my chemical identity and make their hunger feel, briefly, like mine.

My boundary would not be my skin. It would be the uncertain outer edge of all these bargains.

One day, crossing the plaza beneath the buried rays, I would fail to rise after a pulse passed through the mud. Or I would be overturned by a pilgrim. Or I would simply stop during an oxygen-poor season and seal myself inside my borrowed chamber without ever opening it again.

The tenants would continue.

Shrimp would eat the soft parts they once cleaned. Worms would remain extended from the shell. Bacteria would bloom through the interior. Sediment would gather around the legs until they disappeared. The chamber might become fixed beside the avenue, another low obstruction shaping traffic. A tower bud could settle upon it. Descendants would divide, secrete mineral, and rise.

Long afterward, the old arch might collapse.

Its broken span would come to rest against the new growth founded on my shell, leaving a narrow hollow beneath. Water would move through that hollow. Cleaner shrimp would establish themselves there. Pilgrims would press a new curve into the road to pass around it.

Then I would be where I had always been: at a constriction between districts, beneath living walls, surrounded by creatures mistaking remains for shelter.

I would have become part of the place without ever understanding it.

And the city, having passed through me all my life, would finally carry me.
