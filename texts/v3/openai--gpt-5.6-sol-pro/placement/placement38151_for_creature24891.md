At first I think I would be one of the measurers.

I can see myself in a narrow room beside a spring, with fixed cords hanging from pegs and standard stones arranged on a shelf. Each day I would lower the same marked rod into the water, note the same depth, and compare the flow against a bowl whose volume had been established before anyone remembered establishing it. I would be among those faintly embarrassing measurers who record one line twice because the second reading seems narrower by a hair.

I would remember discrepancies after the tablets corrected themselves.

But I do not think I would remain there. Measurement in this city confirms the city. Even rebellion becomes another ritual of equilibrium: discover the impossible difference, summon witnesses, wake to ordinary figures. I would begin to suspect that change cannot be caught by staring directly at it.

Then I imagine myself as a traveler.

I would leave through the western gate and walk into the rain veil, expecting cold drops to strike my face at last. Perhaps I would find the green sea, the inverted red forests, the plains where houses roll on wheels. I would return with a steel needle hidden in my hair or a fruit whose seeds had never known mountain soil.

Yet I do not think I would be a traveler either. The roads would give me wonders, but not elsewhere. I would come home carrying quartz where glass had been, antler and stone where steel had been. My account would enter the hall of impossible memories and become, eventually, one more story belonging to the gate.

I think I would be the person who receives those travelers.

My place would be at the northeastern edge of the city, where a serious gatehouse—with guard rooms, bronze fittings, and a parapet—dwindles on one side into a low wall among silver grass. Beyond it, the road crosses bare basalt, passes a round meadow, and rises toward a cleft between two black ridges. A spring comes out under the gate’s inner foundation. Its water gathers in a shallow stone basin where returning travelers wash their hands.

I would have a room above the gate hinge. Not the grandest room: a low chamber smelling of wool, pine smoke, damp leather, and the sharp mineral scent of the spring. My window would face the pass. Beneath it would stand a broad worktable polished by use but never further worn.

I would be a mender.

Travelers would bring me what the basin had made of their foreign things.

A cloak once dyed with the purple of some coastal animal would return lichen-gray, but retain along one edge a color no one could name except in comparison to bruised evening. A belt purchased in a wheeled city would become familiar hide, though its holes might lie at intervals not used by any leatherworker in the basin. Salt from an unseen sea would dry into the seams of a pack. A woman might swear that a wooden button had once been a disk of transparent blue material through which she could see her own fingertip.

I would not try to preserve the objects as they had been. That would fail.

I would repair what they had become.

Into the gray cloak I would fit a patch whose ordinary district pattern bent, almost imperceptibly, around that nameless strip of color. I would reinforce the belt without correcting its strange spacing. I would stitch over the salt stain with pale thread, not concealing it but giving it the shape of a mountain road. If the transparent button had become quartz, I would set it into a clasp suited to quartz and leave a small opening behind it, so a child could still hold it to the light and see a blurred finger through the stone.

Most of these repairs would remain.

That would be my craft: not bringing the new into the basin intact, but discovering what shape the basin would permit its memory to take.

People might call me a hem-listener, or the Keeper of Returned Things. The title would sound older and more official than the work really was. I would still spend much of the day replacing sandal straps, patching felt, oiling pack frames, and picking burrs from wool. Gate guards would sit in my doorway when the wind came down from the pass. Children would bring me crow feathers and claim they were found beyond the rain. I would pay them with warm bread whether I believed them or not.

I would keep no catalogue. A true catalogue of foreign remnants would be blank by morning or rearrange itself into an inventory of local wool.

Instead, I would wear the record.

My cuffs would be patched with tiny pieces cut from garments carried along every road. All would now be made of basin materials—wool, hide, nettlecloth, linen—but their weaves would disagree. One would be too fine for our looms. One would twist backward. One would contain a knot no local weaver admitted knowing. Seen separately, each patch would be unremarkable. Together they would make a pattern that never quite repeated.

The city would tolerate it because repaired cuffs belonged to me. Perhaps they always had.

I would be neither young nor old. There would be gray at my temples that never spread, and a stiffness in one thumb that worsened during no weather. I might remember having apprenticed under a woman named Aser, though some would say I had been Aser’s teacher. In one district history I would appear as a child watching a delegation enter. In another, the same story would name me as the gatekeeper who challenged them.

Both accounts would fit the stones.

I would have traveled once—or believe I had. My memory would be of a shore under a dark sky. Not the warm green sea described in the halls, but a nearly black expanse breathing against flat stones. I would remember that its horizon did not bend back toward me. I would remember the terror of looking outward and understanding that distance could continue without returning.

Nothing from that journey would remain except a small shell sewn inside my collar.

Whenever I removed it, it would become a polished mountain seed.

Whenever I left it unwatched, it would be a shell again.

I would never tell the measurers. Not because I feared disbelief, but because measuring it might force the basin to decide.

So I would live at the gate, mending the city’s encounters with what it could not contain. I would witness departures. I would ask each traveler what they carried, touch every pack, count every buckle, and memorize the wear on their staffs. On returning, they would stand at the spring while I looked for differences too small or too misplaced to be corrected immediately.

Usually there would be none.

Still, I would watch.

And perhaps my truest identity would not be mender, measurer, or gatekeeper. I would be something for which the city had no formal office: a keeper of imperfect reversions.

I would tend the residue left when the impossible was translated into the familiar—the backward thread, the salt in a seam, the foreign rhythm hiding inside an old song, the traveler who returned with all the same provisions but held the staff in the other hand.

I would not expect these fragments to overthrow the basin. I would not build a machine or draw a road that led away. I would know that a future cannot enter as conquest here. It would have to arrive as an exact repair: something fitted so well into the enduring world that the world mistook it for what had always been missing.

When a figure appeared on the northeastern road, I would go down to the gate before the guards called out.

Most figures would become familiar as they approached—a shepherd, a council messenger, a traveler returning by an unexpected pass.

But I would look first at the hem of the stranger’s cloak.

The face might belong to the city.

The cloth might not.
