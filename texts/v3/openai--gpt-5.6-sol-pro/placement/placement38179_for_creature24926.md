At first I think I would be one of the trees.

I would stand beside an open reservoir, pale-trunked and blackened on the windward side, with roots spread over the bedrock like a hand laid flat. I would know the city through pressure: the cool refusal of a foundation, the wet invitation of a paving joint, the slight settling of a wall against one root. My seedlings would gather around me but go no farther, so that time would take the form of a widening ring.

But a tree is too conspicuous here. It declares itself against the courts and roofs. It has a crown, a center, an apparent individual life. Even if its roots accommodate every structure they meet, it still stands apart from them.

Then I think I would be the sedge beneath a meadow shelf—the hidden, pale body of which all the green blades are temporary expressions. I would occupy an old garden terrace beyond the eastern district, where paving lies a finger’s depth below mud. Some of my stems would rise in the straight line of a buried channel. Others would mark the corners of a vanished settling pool. To anyone looking down from the western terrace, I would seem merely a band of vegetation. Under the soil I would be continuous, turning aside at clay lips, thinning over stone, thickening where warm water spreads.

I would have no reason to know which parts of me were old. A runner might divide, rejoin, be buried by its own decay. The city’s plan would become the shape of my body without ever becoming an idea in me.

That is closer.

Still, the sedge belongs too much to one shelf. It knows the water only after the city has gathered and delivered it. It reads an inherited line, but it does not keep the line.

I think I would be found deeper in the settlement, in one of the roofed reservoirs beside the central way.

The chamber would be low and broad, its columns too massive for the weight they bear. Light would enter through a rectangular doorway and lie across the water without reaching the farther wall. Condensation would form on the beams, travel to their lowest points, and fall. Each drop would briefly make the reflected doorway tremble.

Across from the entrance, the reservoir would overflow through a notch cut into a single dark slab.

I would live there.

Not in the basin as a floating leaf, and not in the water itself. Water is too much a visitor, even when it moves almost imperceptibly. It arrives from the ridge, passes through stone, is held for a while, and leaves by another course. To be the water would be to know the whole plateau but possess no place in it.

I would be the thin, pale colony coating the overflow notch: a clear bacterial skin at the upper edge, a chalk-white mineral body beneath it, and a faint green layer where reflected light reaches under the lip.

From a distance I would look like a deposit. Up close I would still look like a deposit.

Only the softness of my newest margin would reveal me.

Water would pass through me continuously. I would take from it what the mountain had dissolved: traces of lime, iron, salts without names. I would slow the smallest particles and bind them into myself. Beneath my living surface would lie earlier surfaces, hardened and pale, each one the remains of what I had been. I would grow by becoming stone.

The builders would have cut the notch square. I would round it.

Not quickly enough to stop the flow. Perhaps never enough. Each new layer would lift the water by less than the thickness of a leaf, and the transparent sheet descending to the next terrace would shift outward by an amount no eye could follow. The reservoir would continue to hold its line. The channel would continue its whisper. Yet over centuries the water would be running across a shape partly made by me.

I would not be a single creature in the way the vanished builders understood creatures. At my bright edge, several kinds of cells would live among one another. Some would use the weak light from the doorway. Some would feed on what the water brought. Some would inhabit the darkness beneath, where the newest mineral crust sealed them away. Fine fungal threads might enter from the wet clay beside me. Spores from the hanging ferns would settle and fail, leaving a little matter for the colony to absorb.

My identity would not be a boundary but an arrangement.

The uppermost film would divide. The inner layers would die. The dead layers would remain as structure. If a sliver broke from the lip and passed into the lower channel, I would lose it without injury. If it lodged at the next overflow and began to grow, it would not be clear whether I had reproduced or merely continued elsewhere.

I would experience brightness as a change in chemistry. A dry upland breath would tighten my surface. Rain on the roof would alter the reservoir so slightly that the alteration might take hours to reach me. The opening of a cone in the court outside would be far too sudden and distant to enter my world.

But I would know drops.

I would know the regular condensation from the third beam, the one sagging almost imperceptibly toward the water. I would know when moss thickened along the cistern stair because less mineral dust would wash down from that side. I would know the slow fungal weakening of a roof mat through minute changes in what entered the basin. I would know, without knowing what knowledge was, the whole chamber as variations in water.

Perhaps my oldest buried layer would date from when people still came there.

A bowl might once have struck the reservoir wall and shaken loose a cloud of clay. Feet descending the upper steps might have sent small waves across the surface. Hands might have cleared my softness from the notch to keep the level exact. For generations I may have been removed whenever I became visible.

Then one day no hand returned.

I would not remember that day. There would be no preserved image of the last person, no recognition of departure. There would only be an uninterrupted layer where the scrapings ceased: a slight thickening, then another, then thousands more. The builders’ absence would exist in me as continuity.

Above the waterline, the cut stone would remain theirs.

At the waterline, it would be becoming mine.

That is where I would find myself: not at the city’s highest terrace, where the whole watershed can be understood at once, but in a dim reservoir where nothing can be seen whole. I would inhabit the exact place where holding becomes release. The mountain would enter on one side of me and the lower terraces begin on the other.

I would be soft life making a hard edge gentler.

I would be a colony, a crust, a living mineral line.

And if I had anything resembling a thought, it would not be *I am still here.*

It would be the pressure of level water, the faint passage across my surface, and the endless, unastonished fact:

*Enough. Still enough. Overflow.*
