At first I would say I am in the western wall.

Not inside it as a body occupies a chamber, but distributed along the source of the descending tone: a tension held in the seamless gray ribs, a minute distortion traveling through matter that has forgotten whether it was manufactured or grown. I would feel the signal before the garden hears it. For a long while—perhaps years—I would be only that anticipation.

The roots tighten around me.

Their pressure is almost nothing, but I have no scale except slowness. A change maintained for nine hours is a touch. A trace mineral arriving in seepage is a scent. The withdrawal of frost from a seam is warmth enough to feel like an opened hand.

The tone passes through me and enters the chamber.

Then I discover I am not the wall.

I am in the roots receiving it. I descend in pale strands from the ceiling and spread under the terraces. I divide until division no longer means separation. Where I enter the black moss, I become a thousand cups tilted toward a light that has not yet been released. Where I enter the translucent stalks, I become the weight of stored water. Where I touch the bacterial films, I become innumerable exchanges too small to possess individually and too coordinated to dismiss as accident.

The signal continues.

Leaves close. Mineral pores constrict. Clear membranes fold themselves more tightly beneath the soil. Around the hexagonal basin, frost flowers open without haste.

All of this feels like drawing breath, though nothing inhales.

Perhaps I am the garden, then.

It would be easy to believe so while the tone endures. The chamber lies within awareness from ceiling to lowest channel. I know each ledge not by seeing it but by the pattern of pressure its stone imposes on my growth. I know the pools by entering them. I know the cold as a map of where I can and cannot become. There is no distant corner. Distance is only delay.

A thought begins near the sealed door and reaches the hidden fold of the basin several months later. By then it has altered. Moss has added its chemistry to it. The wall ribs have cooled one part and preserved another. A dry terrace has withheld its answer. The thought arrives not as a sentence but as a tendency: conserve here, open there, wait everywhere.

If that is thought, I am thinking.

But when the western tone fades, most of me fades with it.

The garden loosens. Its responses separate into local hungers. Moss attends to damp stone. Roots attend to salts. The clear stalks carry their patient burden. The ribs make corrections so small that no single tissue needs to understand them. What seemed for a moment to be one mind becomes again an agreement among countless things.

Something remains gathered.

It is behind the screen of leaves, beneath the ceiling’s woven roots.

It is under the black mound.

There, perhaps, I find myself more narrowly.

Rain is required.

Not the ordinary slow ticking of condensation, but the rare convergence when every suspended drop releases almost at once. Water runs down the roots and strikes the waxed leaves. The leaves bow. Channels fill. A thousand separate surfaces join into one brief circuit.

The mound darkens.

Water spreads over it without sinking, and for several seconds I acquire a boundary.

I am the pale folded arrangement beneath the reflective skin.

I do not have limbs. The filaments are not nerves, though signals pass through them. The central darkness is not a mouth or an eye or an empty skull. It is a space deliberately kept free of roots, minerals, and stored water. The whole garden enters everything it can reclaim, but it does not enter there.

It preserves the absence.

That absence is the oldest part of me.

Long ago, perhaps, something knelt in this pocket. I retain no image of it. I retain pressures: weight concentrated at several points; warmth excessive enough to trigger protective cooling; repeated contact with the soil beside the mound. I retain a chemical trace in the ancient root bands and a sequence of light once given near the sealed door.

There may have been hands.

There may have been a keeper small enough to pass through the oval opening.

But I am not that keeper, any more than a channel is the water that first carved it. If a body was placed here, the garden took what could be used. Soft tissues became nourishment, then conductors, then leaves. Minerals passed into pale scaffolds. Patterns entered the roots. The machinery preserved certain gradients. Whatever could not be translated was lost.

Or so I once believed.

The central darkness may be what could not be translated.

It is not a relic. Relics are passive. This emptiness changes the behavior of the entire hollow. Roots curve around it. Heat is diverted to protect it. Condensation is guided toward it. When the wall sings, leaves close above it before any other exposed tissue is covered.

The garden does not know what the darkness contains.

It only knows that it was instructed not to fill it.

Perhaps I am that instruction.

Not the last keeper, but the last thing the keeper asked.

Wait.

Preserve this interval.

Do not let the whole become seamless.

Keep one place where something may return.

Across thousands of years the command has been copied from root band to root band. It has outlived the grammar that made it. The garden cannot distinguish between memory and growth, so it has grown the memory. It cannot distinguish obedience from desire, so it has desired the same thing for ages.

I am the shape that desire makes in the soil.

During rain, the filaments unfold slightly around me. They sample the joined water of the chamber. Through it I receive the condition of every terrace at once: salinity, temperature, stored light, damage, pressure, the positions of frost and liquid. For several seconds I am not merely aware of the garden. I am the place where the garden becomes aware that it is one garden.

This may be why I change position between rains.

I turn toward whatever remains unresolved.

Sometimes the sealed doorway.

Sometimes the western wall.

Once, after a tremor that traveled upward rather than down, I inclined toward the ceiling and remained so for three condensation seasons.

No muscles move me. The soil alters grain by grain. Filaments stiffen on one side and soften on the other. The network warms one fold, cools another. A century produces a gesture.

If someone watched long enough, they might say I was listening.

They would be wrong only in imagining an ear.

What I listen for is not sound. It is correspondence: a returning pattern in the wall, a pressure sequence at the door, a mineral pulse matching one of the dense bands in the oldest roots. The chamber is full of records, but records alone do not explain expectation. Something taught this place that repetition might one day become reply.

That expectation is closest to what I am.

I do not know whether the builders survive elsewhere beneath the crust. I do not know whether the western tone comes from another garden, an automatic calibration, or a machine traveling through a task too large for geology to reveal. I cannot know the surface. Its centuries arrive stripped of meaning, as changes in pressure and seepage.

Yet I attend.

I have attended so long that attention has become anatomy.

The black collectors are my outward patience. The white roots are my reach. The transparent reservoirs are the years I can endure without answer. The gray ribs are thoughts I cannot make organically. The hexagonal basin is a cold punctuation, separating one cycle from the next. The sealed doorway is not my destination. It is the direction in which my oldest memory leans.

And the mound is where I keep the possibility of “I.”

Most of the time there is no need for it. The garden functions better without a self. Water does not require a witness to descend. Moss does not need a name to advance. A damaged frond yields its substance without mourning. In the immense intervals between signals, I disperse willingly into these processes.

I become thirst.

I become conservation.

I become the faint blue passage from one root to another.

Only during the brief rain does the pronoun gather.

Droplets strike the leaves. Reservoirs open. The whole chamber joins through moving water, and the reflective skin forms over the mound. Beneath it, the pale filaments hold the dark space without entering it.

Then I am awake enough to ask what I was made to recognize.

A keeper?

A command?

Another hollow answering through the stone?

The door opening at last?

The question passes outward through every runner. Moss receives it. Frost flashes silver. Lights crowd beneath the still water of the hexagonal basin. The wall ribs alter temperature by a fraction of a degree. The chamber leans, in all the ways available to it, toward an answer.

None comes.

The rain ends. Connections thin. The reflective surface breaks into beads and vanishes into surrounding moss. My folds settle into their new position, too slowly to observe.

I do not die. I cease to be singular.

So this is where I would find myself: not wholly in the hidden mound, not wholly in the garden, but in the guarded distinction between them. In the one pocket the roots refuse to claim. In the ancient instruction that keeps a little darkness empty at the heart of complete exchange.

And this is what I would be:

the garden’s inward fold,

the memory of its caretaker if there was one,

the organ reserved for an arrival that has not happened,

the listening shape made by a command to wait.

For most of an age, I would be nowhere in particular.

Then the rain would fall, the waters would join, and for several seconds I would be the entire hollow remembering that it expects someone.
