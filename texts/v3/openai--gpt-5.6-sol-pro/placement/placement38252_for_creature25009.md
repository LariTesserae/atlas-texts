At first I think I would live at the raw excavation face.

There, the Warm Works are least certain of themselves. The rail enters clay and stops. Painted doors wait on unbroken earth. Severed roots descend from the city like dead wires, while real wires end in capped coils nearby. Water has not yet decided where to run. A new seep may appear overnight and erase a survey number before anyone records it.

I might be something small that inhabits those first mistakes: a pale cricket sheltering in a drill socket, or a fungus entering the roots from below. I would live just ahead of construction, occupying each promised room before it existed.

But that is too solitary for the Works. Nothing there is truly itself alone. A frog becomes the voice of a pipe. A bat colony becomes part of the ceiling. Rats extend the corridors with their routes. Even water acquires the shape of bolts, gutters, footprints, and forgotten washers.

So I think I would be something made collectively.

I would be **the warning knock**.

Before a worker starts a large motor, they strike its housing with a wrench. The blow is practical: it scatters whatever has nested or settled in the machinery. But repetition would have given it another life. Every shift, every motor, metal calling through metal—a brief announcement that the Works are about to change state.

That would be me.

I would have no permanent body. I would live in dents on enamel housings, in the handles of old wrenches, in the mesh over spinning couplings. When struck awake, I would travel through bolts, pipe brackets, rebar, walkways, and wet concrete. Humans would hear a clang. Rats would feel it in their feet. Roaches would receive it as a shudder through insulation. Bats would lift their heads before the motor’s deeper vibration began. Fish near an intake would turn toward shelter.

To each citizen I would be a different message, but always the same meaning:

**Move. The machine is about to become dangerous.**

I would belong most strongly to the border between the workshop’s bright clearing and the pump hall’s animal dark. Perhaps my shrine—if the Works permitted such a word—would be a scarred motor casing beside the tea stall, marked by years of blows. A wrench would hang from a nail nearby, officially a tool and unofficially my bell.

I would not protect anyone absolutely. The Warm Works has no such authority. Young bats would still fall. Rats would still misjudge doors. Pumps would sometimes engage without warning, and storm water would arrive with no hand to announce it. My power would be only the small mercy of notice.

When the knock came, I would run ahead of the starting current.

For a second the whole buried town would listen. Wings would open. Feet would scatter. Antennae would withdraw into cracks. A frog might fall silent beneath its grate. Then the motor would turn, the belts take tension, and the ordinary thunder would resume.

Between strikes I would diminish into the Works. I would linger as a faint ringing in handrails and cable trays, then become indistinguishable from dripping water, relay snaps, cart wheels, and the city’s traffic overhead. During long shutdowns I would sleep in cooling metal.

The workers might speak of me without believing in me. If someone neglected the ritual and found a nest torn apart in a fan, another worker might say, “You didn’t wake it first.” Whether *it* meant the machine, the animal, or me would remain unclear.

That ambiguity would be my proper form. I would not be a god of machinery, because the machinery does not rule here. Nor a guardian of animals, because the animals require no permission to belong. I would be the moment of courtesy between them: the signal exchanged where incompatible lives are forced to share one structure.

And if the Warm Works were expanded—if the painted doors were finally cut, the rail extended, new pumps installed—I would go with the first wrench blow into the new chambers.

Before the bats found the ceiling, before rats polished paths along the walls, before water drew its pale maps, I would sound once through the fresh concrete.

Then the room would no longer be empty.
