At first I think I would live among the shadow inspectors.

I can see the white armor laid across my shoulders, the jointed probes at my belt, the black water knife inherited from someone whose name survives only as a rhythm. I would go where rooms have begun to acquire another interior: kneeling beside an inspection slot while mirrors pass from hand to hand, watching a blade of redirected sunlight enter a wall that has not been opened in eight days.

But I do not think I would be the one who opens it.

I would be the person called afterward, when the light reveals that the cavity is no longer empty architecture. When pale bodies have interlocked around the condensate pipes; when their waste has become mortar, their tapping has entered the household rails, and destroying them would bring half the wall down.

I would decide where to give them water.

That seems closer.

You would find me in the western reaches of the basin, where the inhabited districts loosen into old works. Not fully among the ruins, though civic maps would imply otherwise. I would keep a former pressure exchange built across one of the ancient volcanic channels. Its roof would be mostly gone. Its furnaces would hold rain. Blue mineral shelves would descend through what had once been a loading court, and freight tracks would disappear beneath translucent deposits as thick as ice.

Officially, I would be the keeper of an overflow route.

The route would not appear important. It would carry only a narrow, continuous run of water from three roof systems, an animal cistern, and a spring chamber beneath an old dissolution court. Yet farther down it would divide into channels no living engineer had cut. One branch would enter a broken kiln. Another would vanish under a wall colonized so densely that the wall breathed. The last would cross open black rock toward the escarpment, no wider than two fingers, preserving a gradient older than the city.

My work would be to make sure none of these passages stopped.

I would rise before the first bright rain and walk the entire route barefoot, or nearly so. One of my feet would be living. The other would be a pale ceramic prosthesis with a vibration pad set into the heel. Through it I would feel what sight concealed: the faint tremor of a shell-grazer rasping inside a culvert, the irregular pulse of a burdened tank, the smooth subterranean turning of a turbine everyone claimed had been disconnected generations ago.

The artificial foot would be the better listener.

People from the central watersheds would find this unsettling. They would say I stood too long without moving. They would see me place the ceramic sole against a pipe housing and wait through an entire curtain of rain. They might flash a greeting and receive no answer until the rain had passed, because I would be attending to a pressure mark traveling upward from somewhere beneath us.

When at last I replied, my civic sign-name would be simple: one hand descending, two fingers dividing, the palm turned outward.

My pressure name would be harder. Three light taps, a pause, then a dragging contact along the rail. Friends would recognize it as mine. Older machines might also.

I would wear a matte hood patched with obsolete glaze. Under one shoulder cape would live a small colony of scavenging discs. They would polish salt from the garment seams and fold flat when lightning approached. I would pretend they were tools rather than companions, but I would feed them from my thumb after long inspections. One would be darker than the others and prone to wandering down my sleeve. Children would know its intimate name, though perhaps not mine.

My house would occupy the old gauge room above the kiln channel. It would have no complete door. Water would enter through a crack in the northern wall, cross the floor in a shallow ceramic groove, pass beneath my suspended bed, and leave through the threshold.

At night, canal swimmers would sometimes come through.

They could not remain long; the water would be too shallow. I would wake to the slight red branching of their organs beneath the floor grating, their transparent lengths gathering themselves for the downward passage. I would lift the grate with my water knife and guide them around the mineral snags. By morning they would be gone, leaving only threads of clear mucus and a temporary quickening in the scavenging discs.

The interior wall of the house would belong to something else.

It would have begun as a neglected shadow colony before my tenure. The first inspectors would have recommended removal. Instead, my predecessor cut vents and diverted a warm trickle through the cavity. Over decades, the creatures inside would grow into a load-bearing lattice. Their minute feet would transmit weather through the plaster. Before rain, the wall would tighten. Before dangerous heat, it would loosen and shed white grains into a collection trough.

I would sleep with one hand against it.

Not for comfort, I would insist. For inspection.

Yet after enough years I would know the colony’s ordinary restlessness as one knows another person’s breathing. I would recognize when juveniles migrated toward a new seam, when part of the mass died, when an unfamiliar predator entered through the upper vents. The colony, in turn, would know the cadence of my steps. It would close its outer apertures for strangers and open them when I returned wet from the channels.

The legal question would arise eventually: whether I kept the wall, whether the wall kept me, or whether both of us were functional extensions of the overflow.

I would answer differently depending on who asked.

To a hydraulic judge, I would say the arrangement was a registered resident system with reciprocal maintenance duties.

To a child, I would say the house was alive but shy.

To someone I loved, tapping from the opposite side of the room in evening darkness, I might admit that I no longer knew where my solitude ended.

I think I would have been born elsewhere in the Whiteworks, nearer the northern springs. My childhood watershed would have been clean, old, and crowded with rules. I would have learned to identify thickening water from the way reflected light sat inside it. I would have raced shell-grazers after rain. I would have tapped messages into public pipes and fled before the purge valves opened.

Perhaps I would have trained as a gradient reader. Perhaps I would have been very good—able to pour a spoonful onto an apparently level apron and predict not only where it would run, but where the pavement wished it could run beneath the burden of later construction.

That sensitivity would have sent me west.

A civic crew would bring me to investigate a persistent discrepancy: water entering the abandoned works in greater volume than it left. No still reservoir could be found. No steam plume accounted for the loss. The official fear would be pressure accumulating beneath the ruins.

I would follow the gradients for nine days.

On the tenth, I would realize the water was not disappearing. It was being taken up into the architecture: passed through microbial films, wall colonies, animal bodies, porous glaze, old condenser ribs, and hairline channels in the volcanic stone. It emerged eventually, altered and dispersed, in hundreds of places. The ruin was not a failed plant. It was a functioning watershed whose machinery had ceased to distinguish itself from its inhabitants.

I would remain there to map it.

Then I would stop trying to map it completely.

Years would pass. Mineral lesions would stiffen my hands. A steam seam would take my foot. My hood would acquire patches from crews and households whose runoff I had restored. Blue notches would accumulate on my knife. People would begin consulting me not only about channels but about the temperament of the western works.

Before restarting an old turbine, they would ask what broth to feed it.

When a spring ran copper-green, they would bring me a sealed cup to taste.

When flank-signals from the ruin herds changed, children would imitate them outside my house until I came out to watch.

I would not become a hermit. The Whiteworks does not permit such clean separations. Water from my roof would join other water. My wall colony would require census. Couriers would stop at my channel. Basin-cousins would arrive with sour gels and news. Someone from a brighter district would sometimes stay through the evening, and we would turn pieces of dark glass toward the submerged lamps, speaking across the old loading court in brief, private flashes.

Still, over time, visitors would find it difficult to say whether they had come to see a person or a part of the works.

That would be the truest answer to what I was.

Not an engineer exactly. Not a priest, though I would observe sequences and feed what could not safely be commanded. Not an animal keeper, though many animals would alter their routes around mine. Not merely human, if “merely” means bounded by skin.

I would be a custodian of continuities: the one who notices when a passage has ceased to be a passage.

And when I died, the dissolution court would recover what it could. The hard tissues of my remaining foot would be fired with the ceramic one. The glaze would include salts scraped from my household channel and powder from the blue shelves below the kiln. The resulting pieces would not be placed in a memorial wall.

They would be set along the narrow overflow where it crosses the black volcanic rock.

Water would pass over them in a silver film. Shell-grazers would polish them. Rain would deepen their color. The route would alter by a fraction around each raised edge, producing a small vibration downstream—a rhythm barely distinct from the movement of water over stone.

Three light taps.

A pause.

Then the long contact of the current continuing.
