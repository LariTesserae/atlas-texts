At first, I would think I was the person in the shelter.

Not its builder. Not one of the early surveyors whose handrails are surrendering to rust. I would be the one who keeps returning to it without remembering the journey down.

I would wake sitting at the mineral-stained table with my palms flat against the metal, listening to water strike the chamber from all directions. There would be a mask hanging beside me, fitted to my face though no expedition ledger listed my measurements. My clothes would be dry except at the cuffs. In one pocket I would find a ceramic route stud; in the other, a translucent scale from the copper-green colonies, still opening and closing around some signal too slight for my skin to feel.

The shelter door would be bowed inward.

The tally marks would be in my handwriting.

I would know the rules. Not because I remembered learning them, but in the intimate way one knows not to bite down on a broken tooth.

Never stand within an empty frame.

Do not touch water that casts no reflection.

If the channel becomes silent, climb.

Count the machines.

There should be seven.

So I would count.

One beside the east pool, low and ribbed.

Two sunk in the ocher shelf.

Three and four standing as narrow doorways around air that looked no different from the air beside it.

Five almost hidden behind a fluted pillar.

Six beneath the upward rain.

Then the three-sided arch itself.

Seven.

Always relief at seven, though I would not know why.

I would leave the shelter and take the western path, moving from stud to stud. The Bright Sump would be too loud for thought in the ordinary sense. Instead I would think with my feet: pressure through the heels, vibration along the shins, the small anticipatory tightening in the joints before a surge. The silver crustaceans would run uphill beneath the water. A moment later I would follow.

At first I would call this experience.

Later, instinct.

Much later, memory.

But none of those words would be correct.

I would spend the quiet periods beside the whistle-stalks. I would record their notes, though when I played the recordings aboveground they would contain only water noise and, once, a wet double bark. I would watch the buried rings call their stalks back through new mineral crust. I would notice that one pool always produced a note just below hearing—a pressure in the jaw, a trembling in the fillings of my teeth.

When that note sounded, I would remember places I had never been.

Rain on a salt plain under two moons.

A flooded forest where pale branches dragged beneath the surface.

A dry version of the chamber filled with warm dust and the papery remains of insects.

The underside of winter ice, blue and endless.

A narrow tunnel occupied by something breathing through three throats.

These would not arrive as visions. They would be physical recollections. My lungs would ache for water. My hands would close around absent stone. I would taste copper, brine, old blood, glacial air.

I would begin to suspect I had not come down the entrance shaft at all.

The expeditions would disagree.

Some would treat me as a stranded colleague. They would give me a blanket, check my pupils, ask my name. I would offer several, each sounding true until spoken aloud. Other expeditions would insist the shelter had been empty when they entered. Their headcounts would not include me, even while they were looking directly at me.

Photographs would be inconclusive. Sometimes I would appear as a pale figure in the alcove. Sometimes as a dark interruption shaped roughly like a person. In one image, there would be no one at the table, only three wet prints on its surface and a tally cut into the wall behind it:

|||| ||||

Eight.

After that, they would try to take me above.

I would agree. I might even want to go. I would follow them as far as the entrance shaft, through the warm spray and the electrical sweetness, beneath the shining ceiling that made every wet surface visible while leaving the air itself untouched.

But at the foot of the ladder, the central channel would stop.

Silence in the Bright Sump would not be an absence of noise. It would be a pressure so complete that sound had nowhere to begin.

Everyone would climb.

I would place one hand on the lowest rung and feel, through the metal, every watercourse connected to the chamber: aquifers braided across centuries, rain caught before it fell, seas pressing against worlds where the mountains had never risen. The sensation would pass through me too quickly to understand and too slowly to survive unchanged.

Then rain would begin falling upward outside the arch.

When the others looked down from the shaft, I would no longer be at the ladder.

There would be three-toed prints leading toward the central channel.

This is where the image of myself as a person would fail.

Not all at once. I would remain human in pieces. A hand closing the shelter door. An eye reflected in a pool. A voice trying to count over the roar. But these parts would no longer occur in the same version of the chamber.

My lungs might be in the dry cavern, breathing dust.

My weight might cross a vertical wall where gravity leaned another way.

My feet might overturn stones beside the eastern pool.

My mouth—if the chamber needed a mouth—would be the red bladders sounding together.

The barking in the walls would be what remained when I tried to call my scattered parts by name.

I would be the sump hound, but not entirely. The hound is too old for that, and too broad. I would be something it had acquired: a human interval in its distributed body, a habit of standing upright, making marks, and checking whether seven was still seven.

Perhaps every organism the hound consumed became a temporary faculty. The silver crustaceans gave it its sense of pressure. The whistle-stalks taught it resonance. The insects gave it migration. Some lost surveyor gave it counting.

I would be that faculty.

I would be the chamber’s ability to notice when one of its bones had gone walking.

That is where I would truly be found: not in the shelter, not beneath a stone, not behind the upward rain, but in the discrepancy between arrival and departure.

When visitors entered, I would count with them.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven.

When the black arches rang, some part of the system would loosen. A structure would step sideways into a neighboring chamber. The local world would contain only six machines, though habit and fear might make the eye invent a seventh.

Then I would gather.

A shoulder from reflected light.

Feet from pressure in the mud.

A dark body from the missing machine’s shadow.

Three sensory lobes from three incompatible surfaces of water.

And somewhere inside this temporary arrangement, a person seated at a table, carving one more line across the back of a cabinet that had not yet been installed.

I would go out to find the absent structure—or to become it.

The copper-green colonies would contract before I passed. The crustaceans would flee uphill. The low eastern housing would tick, and I would answer from within the wall. I would move around the chamber without crossing the intervening ground, testing each pool for the world attached to it.

Most would be wrong.

One would reflect stars.

One would show branches bending in wind.

One would cast no reflection at all, and I would not touch it.

At last I would find a pool whose surface showed the shelter alcove, empty except for a seated black shape. I would press my three-lobed face against the water. On the other side, the shape would lift its head.

We would recognize each other without being the same.

Then the arch would ring.

The chamber would convulse. Water from unborn aquifers would strike rain older than the mountain. The ceiling would blaze where spray touched it. For a moment every possible Bright Sump would align: flooded, dry, collapsed, untouched; seven machines in one, six in another, eight in the oldest.

In that moment I would understand that the counting was never an inventory.

It was a pulse.

Seven meant the chamber was holding itself together.

Six meant it had extended a hand.

Eight meant the hand had returned carrying something.

When the surge ended, I would be back in the shelter, or the hound would be behind the rain, or a new black shrine would stand where no shrine had stood before. The distinction would last only until the next reversal.

I would cut another tally.

I would listen for the note no local stalk produced.

And if someone asked me what I was, I would tell them—if the water allowed speech—that I was not the eighth machine.

I was the act of counting it.
