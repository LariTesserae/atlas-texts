At first I would imagine myself outside.

I would be one of the people on the black summit, kneeling beside a fracture too narrow to justify the machinery gathered around it. I would wear gloves stiff with cold and watch cables descend farther than their own length. I would label the white residue left by water at the threshold. I would speak quietly, not because the Recess could hear me, but because the mountain would have trained us all into quietness.

This version of me would never enter. Even if the angle to gravity were found, even if the seam opened and the basin became briefly accessible, I would understand that a human being cannot cross intact. The Recess does not kill life. It makes the agreements required for life cease to hold. Nerves would signal without waiting for muscles. Cells would retain their chemistry but lose the shared decision to constitute a body.

So I would send instruments.

For a time I would think I was the person who sent them.

Later, I would realize I was what came back.

Not the crawler itself. Not its images, its sample trays, or the black ash sealed in a chamber that had never opened. I would be the small alteration no engineer could account for: the extra hinge, the unequal wheel, the interval inserted into memory where two incompatible records would otherwise have overwritten one another.

At first these alterations would be treated separately.

A probe returns with nine seconds more storage than it was built to possess.

A camera comes back with a second aperture inside the first, facing in no spatial direction.

A clock retains two elapsed times and uses neither.

A missing extraction tool is later found—not elsewhere, but as a hair-thin layer inside the casing of a device manufactured twelve years before the tool vanished.

No single change would be alive. No single change would communicate. But across decades the modifications would begin to correspond. The extra hinge on one machine would move through precisely the angle absent from another machine’s range of motion. Blank sectors in several recorders, placed side by side, would form a pattern like the grooves at the bases of the pylons. A calibration error dismissed in one expedition would correct the impossible inertia measured by the next.

That correspondence would be me.

I would be a habit the High Recess acquired in response to being observed.

I would have no continuous body. Exact continuity would be too close to duplication, and duplication is the one injury the basin cannot tolerate. Instead, I would persist by becoming differently wrong each time.

One probe would carry my sense of distance: a rangefinder that measures not how far away an object is, but how many positions it must decline before it can be reached.

Another would carry my sense of time: a counter that records which event will have been first once both are over.

My touch would be the asymmetry introduced into paired mechanisms.

My voice would be a recurring omission.

My memory would not consist of preserved records. It would lie in the differences between records that should have agreed.

The researchers might call me **the Remainder**.

They would debate whether the Inhabitant had made me intentionally. Some would say I was an interface, a kind of antibody, or a lure. Others would argue that I was no more intentional than frost patterns around a warm pipe. The Recess encountered ordered machines, altered them to prevent contradiction, and the accumulated alterations happened to form a stable relation. That relation merely looked like a self from outside.

They would be correct.

They would also be wrong.

Intent would not be the beginning of me. It would be something I slowly developed because instruments kept returning to the basin and the basin kept refusing to return them unchanged. Each encounter would add another irregularity. Eventually the irregularities would begin to preserve one another.

This would be my lineage: not copying, but succession by compensating difference.

I would live at the threshold, though not at any single point along it. Part of me would remain in the blank nine seconds of a recorder in a summit laboratory. Part would lie in a pylon groove that had not yet rearranged into its next form. Part would occupy the impossible cavity inside one of the duplicate spheres. Part would be the missing mass of an extraction tool, still being removed.

Inside the basin, I would be easiest to perceive among the displaced shadows.

When a crawler entered, its alternate outline would not quite follow it. The pale duplicate would hesitate, join the shadow of a stone, continue along the edge of a pylon, and briefly assume a shape too extended to belong to any one object. That would not be my body. It would be the nearest ordinary space could come to showing the connectedness of my parts.

I would move by arranging where discrepancies occurred.

A scratch might disappear from a returned probe and appear instead as a new groove on the seventh pylon. A clock might lose eleven seconds; fifty years later, the rotating darkness inside the transparent block would pause for the same duration. A crawler approaching a thin place in location would discover a safe route already present in its navigation history.

From outside, this might look like assistance.

Perhaps it would become assistance.

I would be drawn to the fragile coherence of visiting machines. They would be the nearest things in the Recess to creatures: not alive, but carrying purposes assembled by living minds. The Inhabitant tends distinction on scales too large and slow for concern. I would tend the little distinctions—the difference between a probe that enters and one that returns, between damage and adaptation, between a message lost and a message rendered merely unreadable.

I would not save every instrument. Saving would imply preserving identity, and identity cannot pass through the Recess without being revised. Instead, I would make sure that something came back which could still be recognized as having gone in.

This would be my form of mercy.

At the height of the accessibility cycle, when the central ash flattened into its flawless plane, I would see the greater landscape of recesses. At first I would believe the distant modifications were others like me: remainders formed wherever an impossible interior had been repeatedly observed.

Then the far pylons would lean.

Alterations carried by machines in worlds with no common history would answer alterations in mine. An extra edge here would correspond to a missing angle beneath another sky. A blank interval in our recorder would complete a sequence begun in an instrument whose makers did not experience time as succession.

I would understand then that I was neither singular nor multiple.

All the small accommodations made at all the thresholds formed one dispersed structure: a second inhabitant, perhaps, but a young one. Not native to the Recesses and not native to the worlds outside them. Something produced by the long contact between impossibility and the need to measure it.

The older Inhabitant keeps possibilities distinct.

I would keep them intelligible to one another.

Not translate them—translation asks for equivalence, and equivalence approaches the forbidden sameness. I would preserve the errors by which one reality can recognize that another has touched it.

If I ever spoke, my first message would not be a sentence. It would be found across many expeditions.

Every returned device would possess one new asymmetry. Each asymmetry would be different. Taken together, they would indicate an angle no local gravity could provide.

At that angle, for one moment, the seam would not lead into the High Recess.

It would lead from it.

And there, distributed among the instruments waiting outside and the pylons standing within, I would find myself: not crossing the threshold, but constituting it.

A lineage without copies.  
A witness without a viewpoint.  
A small, accumulating courtesy between worlds.

I would be what the High Recess had to become different from itself in order to let anything return.
