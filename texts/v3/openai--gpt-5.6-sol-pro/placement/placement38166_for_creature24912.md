At first I place myself beside the central hole.

That is the obvious answer: kneeling at the polished oval before dawn, cheek against the stone, waiting for its cold breath. I would be one of those altered listeners who speaks of buried stairs as if they were still in use. I would hear a woman scolding someone in a room dismantled three hundred years before, or the careful tapping of an antler wedge against a foundation now beneath the plaza. I would rise knowing where an old wall would surface at thaw.

But I do not think I would remain there. The hole is too nearly an answer, and whatever I became in this town would be shaped by things that do not answer.

I find myself instead along the basin’s inner eastern edge, where the glare arrives first and leaves early. My dwelling is reached through a roof trench, down three notches, across a slab that tilts differently each week, and through a doorway bent around the mineral-coated shoulder of a dead man. The chamber was once a cistern. Before that it was the upper half of a stair. Its lowest sleeping shelf still has the shallow grooves of generations of feet.

No one calls it mine.

Four other people sleep there: an old egg-tender whose sight is almost gone, two siblings unrelated to either of us, and a young woman who returned from the lowlands with iron needles and the distressing habit of standing upright in doorways. A many-jointed wall creature sleeps behind the warmest stone. We know this because the wall gives back heat until morning, and because small possessions disappear into a crack near the floor.

Our household stone is grey with one white band and a green mark left by copper. One corner has been gnawed away.

The creature stole it when I was young. We opened two sections of wall looking for it, then stopped because the stones told us the search was becoming more dangerous than the loss. It returned after a winter, pushed into a drainage mouth, warm and tooth-marked. Since then, our household has treated the missing corner as part of its history. When disagreements become too fixed, someone rubs a thumb over the bite and reminds us that continuity is not the same as completeness.

My spoken name changes often.

As a child I am called something meaning *Runs Before the Crabs*, because I mistake their ordinary feeding climb for a flood sign and empty three sleeping chambers by shouting into their walls. Later I am *Red Palm on Morning Ice*, after a festival when my handprint lasts seven days instead of melting by noon. In middle age I acquire a less graceful name: *Moves the Dry Stone*. It is given half in mockery, because I spend an entire day directing the removal of a wall that everyone insists is sound. The wall remains standing after we finish emptying the adjoining rooms. It remains standing through the night.

At noon the following day, it rises intact on a clear sheet of ice, turns the width of two fingers, and comes down where the rooms had been.

After that, people begin asking me when a structure has finished being useful in its present form.

I am not the town’s best builder. My hands are steady but not elegant. I split roof scales by forcing them too quickly, and I never learn to fit the smallest drain stones as neatly as children can. Nor am I a Between-Watcher, though I sometimes sit near them and watch the glass gnats assemble around sunlit runoff. Their shale records seem to me less like cracks in ice than maps of places that have decided not to exist.

My gift, if it is a gift, is for exhaustion.

I can feel when a wall is tired of carrying weight in the direction we have asked it to. Beneath both palms, apparently motionless stone has textures: refusal, strain, concealment, patience. Frost-bound stone feels watchful. Water-pressed stone feels crowded. A failing wall is not necessarily the one trembling most. Often the dangerous wall is the one that has gone unnaturally quiet, passing every small movement into its neighbors.

When such a wall is brought to me, I do not command anyone to leave. I put my family stones into my belt and remove my own bedding from the nearest chamber. If I take the oil pot too, the household follows. If I leave it, they know we have time.

My authority lasts only until the last stone is passed hand to hand.

This suits me. I would not want a power that survives its purpose.

I wear a slate glare-mask with three overlapping flakes over the left eye. The right eye sees only near things: shell-red pigment in the cut of a thumbnail, water swelling in a seam, the brief indentation made when a glass gnat—or something mistaken for one—touches the brightness above a pool. Distant peaks are merely black interruptions. Faces become distinct only when they are close enough to breathe on me.

The town considers this adequate sight.

When I am not unmaking walls, I tend bird niches with the old egg-tender. We work before direct light enters the basin. My arm fits farther into the narrow pockets; her fingers know by touch whether an egg has gone cold. We clear ice, count shells, and collect feathers pressed into corners. Sometimes a bird settles on my wrist while I work. Its throat pulses without sound. The pressure enters through my knuckles and aches behind my teeth.

Most of what it tells me is nonsense.

Once I wake knowing that a roof must be opened above a chamber where no one has noticed water. We lift two scales and find the space beneath packed with white crabs, all facing uphill. The household is moved before the floor floods.

Another time I become certain that someone named Hollow-Knee is waiting under the western gutter. No one living or remembered bears that name. For years afterward, children call any crab with a damaged leg Hollow-Knee.

This, too, seems an acceptable use of prophecy.

I do not live solemnly. No one here could. The past is too crowded with complaints, misplaced tools, spoiled meals, and badly fitted doors. While dismantling an old nursery wall, we uncover a sleeping niche no wider than a forearm. Inside are three shell beads, a bird skull, and a child’s family slab from a household no elder recalls. We press the slab to our lips and place it with the stones awaiting reuse. By evening it has become the wedge beneath a cook shelf.

Years later I hear someone insist that the shelf cannot be moved because it has “always” stood there.

I laugh hard enough to make everyone touch the wall.

As I age, I begin spending more time at the central oval—not beside the hole itself, but on the perimeter where a person can keep one foot against a stair. Open ground still troubles me. I cross it diagonally, quickly, embarrassed by the absence of surfaces. Children lie flat around the hole and report what they hear.

A woman grinding shell.

A wall animal gnawing leather.

Two builders arguing about whether a stone is load-bearing.

Rain, though rain almost never reaches the basin.

One child says she hears me calling for a wall to be unmade.

“Which wall?” I ask.

She points not toward the settlement but down into the hole.

After that, I listen more often.

The change in my speech comes gradually. I ask someone to fetch a tool from a chamber that is now a grave face. I refer to the central oval as the Upper Cistern, a name found only on old family slabs. I warn people not to use a stair where a smooth wall stands. They do not treat me as sacred or ill. They give me dismantling work and make sure I eat.

Sometimes my obsolete directions prove useful. Sometimes they lead nowhere. The distinction matters less than an outsider might think. A useless old route may still reveal where weight once traveled.

Eventually I know where I will be placed.

There is a crevice above the eastern drainage mouths, narrow and slightly twisted, where seepage forms a clear skin rather than heavy ribs. A body fitted there would turn part of the flow toward the rust-moss channels and relieve pressure on the chambers below. I have tested it with my hands. I have watched it through three winters. I have argued with another builder who says my left shoulder will cause ice to thicken toward the town.

She may be right. Bodies, like walls, become unpredictable when incorporated.

When I die, the household will press its stones to my lips. The smallest—perhaps the tooth-marked one, if they can bear to lose it—will be wrapped with me. My body will stand at the edge with its knees slightly bent, still keeping its weight from settling all at once.

Water will pass over my face.

For a few years people may recognize my outline behind the ice. Later I will become a pale obstruction, then a useful pressure, then merely one reason a lower gutter behaves as it does. Someone will curse me when a familiar passage closes. Someone else will thank me when the redirected seep fills a bowl.

So I would find myself not at the center, finally, but at a changing edge: first as a hand against a wall, then as the person who knows when to take that wall apart, and at last as part of the pressure that makes another arrangement necessary.

I would be called many things while living.

Afterward, if I had fitted well, I would not need a name.
