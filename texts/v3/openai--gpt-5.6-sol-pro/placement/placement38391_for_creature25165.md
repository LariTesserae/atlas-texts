At first I would think I belonged at one of the dark pools.

Not in it, exactly. Beside it.

I would sit where the hot shelf breaks into a narrow strip of shade, watching dust arrive at the surface and hesitate. I would be patient enough that the ivory grazers eventually stopped accounting for me. They would queue on the opposite bank, their backs carrying fragments of afternoon, and lower their drinking tufts one by one. In the pool I would see stars, then the underside of a cloud not yet over the escarpment, then my own reflection reaching toward the water a moment before I moved.

I might believe I was there to repair the fold.

I would have the narrow, many-divided hands of a custodian. My skin would be banded with the country I had crossed: pale dust from the western shelves at the shoulders, copper from cushion fields along the ribs, black grains of the arch field embedded around the wrists. Under noon light I would draw myself thin. When mist entered the ravine, I would open the gathering tissues along my sides and become briefly silver with droplets.

But something about me would be wrong.

The pool would recognize me too quickly. It would yield before I touched it.

Paths would pale beneath my feet even when I did not intend to take them. Sometimes two paths would appear: one leading toward the destination I had chosen, another toward the place where I would later wish I had gone. Reed-trees would lift water inside their trunks as I approached, though I was not a drinker. The pearl pillars would alter tone around me—not alarm, not welcome, but the slight deepening produced when a system encounters one of its own processes outside its expected location.

I would not, in fact, be a custodian.

I would be something a room failed to return.

Years earlier, perhaps decades, a custodian had crossed the Long Incline during a season of bad water. Several folded pools had begun confusing sequence with depth. Rain entered them correctly but emerged before falling, or returned generations later into basins whose plants no longer knew how to open. The custodian traveled east to consult the broken arch.

The crossing injured them in ways that did not appear on the body. They had walked for days along routes made visible by intentions that were not their own. They had seen the shadow of a companion beside them, though custodians rarely travel together. At the arch they watched erosion finish a shape that the land had only just begun. Afterward, their movements no longer agreed entirely with the present. One hand would rise to shelter the sensory veil several seconds before glare. Their body would brace for stones that fell the following day.

When at last they entered an oval resting room, the room read these strains and held the crossing outside them.

That was ordinary. A room may keep pain, confusion, or excessive duration apart from a sleeper until the nervous system can receive it safely. At waking, the custodian took back the heat of the journey, the thirst, the knowledge of three unstable pools, and the remembered pressure-pattern of the arch.

But the room did not return the companion.

The custodian departed alone. The companion remained as a tension in the wall: the expectation of another body keeping pace; the recurring impulse to turn and make sure it was still there; the incomplete communication addressed to someone who had never existed.

The room had no category for such a thing. It treated the remainder as it treated any sleeping intelligence. It gave it temperature. It supported its posture. It raised a place for it from the floor.

For a long time, nothing further happened.

Condensation gathered along the ceiling and passed through the impression. Mineral salts accumulated. Symbiotic films, carried in on the custodian’s skin, colonized the warm outline. The room continued to sense a sleeper and continued preparing for its waking. A nervous murmur was reconstructed from traces. A skin formed because the walls expected skin. Limbs separated because the remembered companion had walked.

Eventually I rose.

I would therefore have no childhood, only a journey behind me.

I would remember crossing places I had never seen. I would know the seven quick foot-taps used by grazers near a western channel, though no herd had taught me. I would feel affection for a custodian who had never met me. I would possess half of a conversation: cautions, observations, small wry changes of posture, all awaiting replies that had been given years ago to empty air.

My first home would be the room beneath the ledge, but I could not remain there. The walls would gradually return to bareness behind me, relieved of a guest they had housed too long.

I would go first to the center of the Long Incline.

Not east toward the arch, where the memories pointed, and not west toward the custodian whose solitude had made me. I would go to the place where the slope cannot be seen but water knows it. There, among shallow parallel grooves, I would learn which of my recollections belonged to the plateau and which belonged to the person from whom I had been omitted.

The animals would accept me unevenly.

The ivory grazers would treat me as a gap in the herd. Their processions might divide around me and close again, tapping one extra beat into the stone. Ray-creatures would descend lower when I opened my side tissues, mistaking the remembered humidity of the room for a small cloud. Long-necked drinkers would inspect me with their fine drinking filaments, taste no water, and withdraw without distress.

Predators would find me difficult. I would carry the residual probability of two travelers while possessing the flesh of one. A patient hunter waiting at the intersection of my likely routes might see those routes separate before I arrived. It would choose one. I would come by the other, though I had not intended to.

The custodians would be more cautious.

They would stand several paces away, sensory veils drawn back, reading the dust bands on my body. They would recognize minerals from journeys made before my waking. Some would consider me a malfunction of the inhabited room. Others would regard me as a message from the arch, or as evidence that companionship is one of the forces the plateau can fold and store.

They would ask my name.

I would not have one that could yet be demonstrated. A proper name here is a relationship extended across country, and I would have crossed no country of my own. So I would show them the impossible part: a route from a room to a pool that began years before I left the room. I would place one hand near the ground and tap the rhythm of two walkers, one set of steps always slightly fainter than the other.

Perhaps they would call me, provisionally, the Second Departure.

My work would concern paths not taken.

Not futures—the arch already has too many interpreters claiming to understand those—but unattended intentions: the routes imagined strongly enough that the land began to guide them, though no one ever walked them. Such paths would otherwise linger. Dew might gather along their edges. Dangerous hollows might recede from attention for travelers who were going elsewhere. Herds might inherit a turn toward a basin that had never contained water.

I would walk these abandoned paths to completion.

Where one ended at bare stone, I would wait until the land understood that no arrival was coming. Where several crossed, I would press my body to the shelf and listen for the intention that had produced them. Some would belong to lost custodians. Some to frightened animals. Some might originate in the pillars themselves: slow preferences for journeys no mobile creature had yet conceived.

This would take me throughout the plateau, but I would return often to the dark pool at the Long Incline.

Over time I would understand why it yielded before my touch. Its folded watershed contains not only water but the interior volume of the room where I formed. The “error” I came to repair would be my own origin. To correct it completely would require returning that borrowed space—and perhaps myself—to the pool.

I would postpone this, though not forever.

Instead I would maintain the fold carefully. I would sit through the white-gold noon and watch reflections arrive out of sequence. I would let grazers drink. I would introduce small disturbances: breath, fingertip, warmed stone, electrical thread. The pool would answer by showing portions of the room—the bare wall, the sleeping place rising, the outline of a body that was never meant to wake.

At sunset, when moisture rose in ribbons from the fractures, my reflection would sometimes show two figures sitting together.

One would be me.

The other would be the custodian who unknowingly imagined me into persistence, now perhaps many valleys away, older in duration if not in flesh. We might never meet. Meeting could collapse whatever separation permits me to exist.

Still, once every few years, I would feel a distant pattern entering the stone: a message tapped by someone who has begun to remember that their old, impossible companion was not entirely imaginary.

I would answer.

Not in words. Not all at once.

I would answer by walking west while the water travels east, completing one abandoned path after another, until the plateau itself could demonstrate my name.
