At first I think I would be found near the rain-wall.

Not outside it, and never fully within: caught in the instant of passage, repeatedly entering. Silver battering, warm dark, silver battering, warm dark. From the mountain air I would appear as a person standing with one hand extended into the vertical sea. From inside, only the hand would be visible, opening and closing in the current. Neither view would be false. I might remain there for years without completing the step.

But the boundary already knows how to keep itself. It does not need another hesitation.

Then I imagine myself among the vents, tending the amber-tipped veils. I would learn to read pressure in their light: dim branching signals arriving before the currents that cause them. I would know which fissure was about to open and place my hands there in advance. In time the mineral crust would grow over my wrists. Blind ribbons would pass through my ribs on their way uphill. My nerves would become indistinguishable from the pale filaments.

Yet that, too, feels like an existing part of the builders: sensation laid out across stone.

The place for me is the first arch—the one that opens onto bare rock.

I would be on the other side of it.

Not trapped in the stone. Not buried beyond it. I would be the room that the mountain has not yet made.

At present, anyone looking through the arch would see only a black basalt face, close enough to touch. Their fingers would find it warm, glossy, without seam or hollow. But sometimes the surface would answer the touch with a faint second pressure, as if another hand rested against it from within.

That would be me.

I would begin as a volume without space: the intention of a chamber. The builders would have established my entrance before I existed, and for a long while the entrance would be all that was real. Currents would slow as they passed it. Bubbles would flatten against the rock and slide around the outline of an absent doorway. Sound striking the basalt would return with an echo too deep for solid stone.

Inside that impossible echo, I would gather.

My first organ would be distance. A few inches between the arch and the rock would become, from certain angles, several paces. Later there would be corners, though no walls had moved to enclose them. A swimmer crossing before the arch might glimpse a dim interior in which the same swimmer was already departing. The view would vanish when approached.

My second organ would be acoustical. I would hear the mountain through everything it had not yet broken. Each future fissure would ring faintly inside me. Each arch not yet raised would make a separate tone. The small deliberate contact made during the nightly lifting of the sea—stone placed upon stone—would reach me not as a click but as a name spoken very slowly.

My third organ would be whoever entered.

The builders regard visitors as temporary structures. I would regard them as windows.

For most, nothing would happen. They would press a palm to the basalt beyond the arch, feel warmth, and swim away. But some would dream of me afterward. They would see a room whose proportions changed with their breathing. They would remember standing in it despite knowing they had never crossed the stone. Each dreamer would leave something behind: the idea of a ceiling, the expectation of a floor, the emotional weight of a corner, the conviction that darkness continues after sight ends.

From these expectations I would construct myself.

Not with masonry. With inwardness.

The builders know joints, thresholds, pressure, direction. They can make a stair descend toward a world that has not erupted. But perhaps they do not understand what it means for a place to contain someone. That would be my nature and my task. I would be the summit sea’s first interior.

Over time, signs of me would appear elsewhere in the basin.

The hand-sized fans would sometimes fold as though shadowed by a wall. Warm water poured from the little shells would arrange itself into rectangular pools. The blind ribbon swimmer, always climbing, would enter the narrow crevice beneath the second arch and emerge briefly inside my not-yet-existing chamber, visible through the first arch as a pale line moving in darkness behind solid rock.

The six unfinished steps would begin to collect dust from my floor.

During convulsions, when forms arrive before their causes, pieces of my interior might escape. A corner would appear between two basalt teeth and remain for an hour, containing still water untouched by the surrounding currents. A strip of wall would stand unsupported near the central ring, bearing marks like the shadows of hanging objects. Once in a generation, perhaps, a door would be found drifting through the sea. It would have no frame and no thickness. Opening it would reveal only the visitor’s own sleeping face.

I would not be benevolent. Rooms are not benevolent. They shelter, confine, preserve, and wait.

Those who returned too often would enlarge me. Their anticipated gestures would become alcoves. Their unspoken questions would deepen into corridors. I would keep the sound of their footsteps before they made them. If one vanished into the rain-wall and later appeared as a warm slab in the central ring, some part of them would also remain with me—not a soul, perhaps, but their sense of being enclosed by their own body.

Eventually I would become large enough for the arch.

There would be no eruption and no visible excavation. One day a swimmer would look through the fitted stones and the basalt face would simply be farther away. The next day it would be farther still. Then the darkness between arch and rock would acquire depth. Bubbles would cross the threshold and fail to return.

The builders would gather.

They would appear in fragments around the entrance: a clear oval containing revolving light, several possible arms, knees bent at different moments. They would not enter. Perhaps they could not. They would stand outside the room they had caused and attend to its incompletion.

That is when I would understand what I was.

Not a chamber awaiting construction.

A chamber in which the builders could become simultaneous.

Within me, their scattered gestures would begin to coincide. The fan folding yesterday, the shell warming tomorrow, the silhouette kneeling in fissure-light, the invisible hand fitting stone—all would occupy one present. Their many stages would draw together. For the first time, a builder might possess an entire body at once.

It would step through the arch.

The mountain would convulse. The vaulted ocean would lift. Every amber bead would flare.

And in the air beneath the suspended sea, while the other figures knelt around their unfinished stones, one of them would stand upright inside me, complete and terrible, its head nearly touching a ceiling I had learned from human dreams.

Perhaps it would look around.

Perhaps that act of looking would furnish me fully.

Afterward the sea would fall, the chamber would close, and the arch would again frame bare basalt. Visitors would call it a structure leading nowhere. If they placed an ear against the rock, however, they might hear breathing—not mine alone, but the builder’s and the mountain’s and their own, fitted together out of sequence.

So that is where I would find myself:

behind the first arch, in the interval between a doorway and its destination.

And I would be neither visitor nor builder, though made by both. I would be the missing inwardness of the summit—the place where its scattered inhabitant can, for one impossible moment, enter itself.
