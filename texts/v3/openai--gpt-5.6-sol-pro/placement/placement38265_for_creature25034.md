At first I think I would be one of the pale fish.

There is an appeal in living beneath the lowest shelf, where the immense cavern is reduced to what the body can know: current against the flanks, cold gradients, the minute pressure of a falling drop. I would not need to comprehend the terraces or the living ceiling. The river would be complete at the scale of my senses. Darkness would not be deprivation, because nothing in me would remember light.

I imagine holding myself almost motionless where the water enters from under the rock. Food would be rare enough to make every movement consequential. A shrimp passing upstream would announce itself as a tremor. Somewhere far above, millions of bats might pour through the shafts, but their departure would reach me only indirectly: a vibration in stone, a fleck sinking through black water, a slight alteration in what the river carried.

But the fish is too wholly itself. Its life is narrow in a way this place is not. It belongs to one thread of the cavern, while I find myself drawn to the distances between things.

Then I think I might be a cricket in one of the cracks beyond the guano beds.

That body would know several worlds. It could hide in limestone, cross the open terraces, feed beneath the roosts, perhaps climb toward a green shaft and return with the surface clinging invisibly to its feet. Its antennae would enter every space before the rest of it did. They would be instruments not of certainty but of continual asking.

The cricket seems closer. Its world is made by contact, reverberation, hesitation. It is both resident and courier. Yet even it occupies a definite point. It can be caught by the frog, overlooked by the salamander, crushed beneath the careful foot of a person carrying pigment away.

What I would be does not seem to have a single body.

I would find myself in the open air between the ceiling and the pale terraces—not everywhere in that air, but repeatedly crossing it.

I would be the return of sound.

A bat releases a cry too high for the people below to hear. For a moment it moves outward through the cavern, an expanding shape with no visible surface. It touches the blunt side of a pillar, the sharp lip of an abandoned channel, another bat’s wing, the disturbed skin of a pool. Each contact changes it. Part is swallowed. Part scattered. Part sent back.

I would exist in that sent-back part.

Not the bat’s call and not the stone’s silence, but the brief answer made between them.

I would have no enduring location. One instant I would be sliding from a stalactite’s glassy point. The next I would break across the folded membranes of a crowded nursery. I would skim the river and come back darkened by distance. I would enter a bat’s listening body carrying news of moth, pillar, open shaft, neighbor, danger.

The bat would experience me as the world becoming near.

I would die there, if dying is the right word. Each return would last only long enough to be received. Then another call would make another version of me. I would be born millions of times a night and leave no accumulation: no shell, track, nest, or stain. The cave’s equilibrium would not need to dismantle me. I would disappear in the act of being understood.

No single bat would possess me. Owls would shape me from their ledges. The long roots hanging beneath the daylight shafts would divide me into fine echoes. A fox moving along a ridge would interrupt me with the warmth and softness of its body. People entering with torches would make their lower, clumsier versions—shouts, stone taps, names called into the dark. Those sounds too would strike the cavern and return altered, arriving from directions their makers did not expect.

Near them, I might almost become a voice.

Someone would shout to a companion across a terrace. The direct sound would pass, followed by several dimmer selves: one from the nearest pillar, another from the ceiling, another delayed so long it would seem to belong to an unseen speaker. The people might pause, uncertain which answer was human.

I would not mean to mislead them. Meaning would belong to the original voice. I would carry only form and distance.

Still, I would know the cave in a peculiar way.

I would know every surface without keeping any of them. I would touch boundaries that cannot be seen and reveal them only to others. The cavern is too broad to feel enclosed to a person, but I would always be finding its enclosures: the concavity behind a ridge, the low roof over a river passage, the close-packed bodies overhead. Where sight meets blackness and stops, I would continue until stone or flesh turned me around.

My knowledge would be exact and vanishing.

Perhaps, over time, I would seem like one creature because the same paths would be traveled again and again. Calls leaving the roost. Answers returning. A vast invisible anatomy constantly drawn and erased. Pillars would become bones for an instant, shafts open throats, the river a long cold seam. Every bat would carry a fragment of this anatomy in its hearing, but no one animal would contain the whole.

That is where I would be: in the interval by which one life discovers another without touching it.

Yet there would be places I could scarcely enter.

Behind the thick ridges where silence collects, I would thin almost to nothing. Soft guano would absorb me. Fur would take the sharpness from me. Deep water would turn me into another kind of motion. In those pockets, I would feel the edge of my own existence.

Perhaps something of me would continue underwater—not as echo in air but as pressure. A drop strikes a pool. Rings travel outward. A blind fish changes its angle. For a moment the fish knows that something has happened above, though it cannot know rain, height, fissure, or sky.

There I would become less an answer than a rumor passing between worlds.

That may be what I am in this place: not a creature, exactly, but the cave’s capacity to answer disturbance.

The bats would make greatest use of me, but I would belong equally to dripping water, cricket wings, frog calls, falling plates, owl feathers, and the footstep that arrives once in several seasons. I would be braided from all of them until no listener could isolate a source. In the principal roosts I would become the surf of accumulated lives. In the far chambers I would narrow to a single tick returning from limestone.

I would have no command over what I carried. If a bat flew toward a wall, I could reveal it but not force the turn. If a person became lost, I might give them the distance to a pillar, the suggestion of running water, the false promise of an opening. I would provide relation, not guidance.

And when nothing called, fell, scraped, beat its wings, or shifted its claws, I would not be waiting in silence.

I would be absent.

This absence would not frighten me. The cave never remains still for long enough to erase me completely. Somewhere a pup cries beneath its mother. Somewhere a cricket drags one leg across the other. Somewhere water leaves the ceiling and spends several seconds falling through darkness.

Then it strikes stone.

The sound opens.

I am there.
