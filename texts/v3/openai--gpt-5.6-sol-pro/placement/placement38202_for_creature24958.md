At first I think I would be one of the storm-readers.

It is an attractive mistake. I imagine myself standing exposed while countries rotate overhead, every limb extended into contradictory gravities, translating catastrophe into a route others can use. But that image is too complete. It belongs to someone who has survived long enough to trust what the world does to them.

I would not begin there.

I would find myself in a shallow abrasion bowl on a medium-sized fragment: large enough to keep a little atmosphere, small enough that the horizon visibly curves when a greater continent passes. The bowl would lie near a doorway standing alone in the pavement. Not close enough to enter accidentally. Close enough that its field turns the horizon by a fraction even when dormant.

Three black mats would grow beneath an overhang nearby. A seam of violet glass would cross the plain and vanish at the doorway’s threshold. Every few centuries, charged dust would rise from that seam and stream upward, indicating a passage to somewhere not currently visible.

The basin would be meticulously free of dust.

For a long while, I would appear to be the one keeping it clear.

Then, slowly, I think it would become apparent that I am the basin.

Not the stone hollow itself, but an inhabitant folded so completely into it that my plates form its smooth walls. My back would be a pavement of old mineral; my limbs would lie recessed along ancient cracks. Travelers would step inside me to exchange maps through contact. Their weight would enter my body as speech.

I would be a listener.

My makers would have assembled me from parts that did not agree about where they came from. One plate would remember a rust-red continent long since opened into sheets. Another would carry the residual pressure of a ring hundreds of kilometers wide. A sensory filament would recoil from a tower I had never seen. Deep within the black tissue binding me together, there would be the ending of a journey whose first steps had been lost before my assembly.

Because of this, I would be poor at direction and unusually sensitive to sequence.

I would not know where a traveler should go. I would know when the world had begun to resemble an old possibility.

Most of my life would be spent motionless. Dust would approach the bowl and curve around it—not because of any ancient mechanism, but because I would make tiny adjustments too slow to see, lifting one plate, lowering another, coaxing charged grains toward the violet seam. Black mats would spread beneath my shaded edges. Crawlers would sleep in the warmth held between my inner layers. A metal-stalk might root beside one of my folded limbs and, after several thousand years, become indistinguishable from part of my anatomy.

Communities would visit during conjunctions.

They would come as low heaps crossing the pale plain, then arrange themselves around me and press their filaments into the stone. I would receive names as stresses: a lateral pull followed by a tremor like distant settling; four quick lifts beneath the eastern plate; a long downward pressure that ended in three diminishing pulses.

Some I would recognize from inherited memories. Others I would learn.

They would give me accounts of crossings, losses, unstable towers, altered fragments. I would hold these not as stories but as changes in posture. A successful passage might remain in me as a slight cant of the northern wall. A death would be a pressure I preserved without answering. Over ages, my shape would become the accumulated deformation of everyone who had spoken through me.

This would make me useful, but not wise.

Old routes would continue to feel true after they became fatal. Memories inherited from dead plates would sometimes answer living travelers in the wrong order. I would mistake resemblance for return. Perhaps once—only once would be enough—I would indicate that a path was opening because the ground had assumed a configuration stored deep in my body.

A group would unfold at the margin.

Dust would rise. Sails would darken against the white sun.

They would step into the distance, and the distance would fail to hold its shape.

For centuries afterward, I would refuse to signal.

The basin would fill with metallic dust. Mats would creep over my plates. Travelers would arrive and press questions into me, but I would remain inert, indistinguishable from an abandoned arrangement of stone. Some would leave warning pebbles at my edge. Others would remove them. The solitary doorway would wake and turn the horizon sideways; still I would not unfold.

Then someone lost would pass overhead.

Not rescued, not returning—only crossing the sky in a long orbit begun before my silence. A dark articulated speck would eclipse one star, carrying its thought at the lowest possible ember. Its pressure-signature would reach the fragment through the doorway’s field: faint, distorted, but familiar.

It would be one of those I had misdirected.

Not safe. Not arriving. Still within the world.

That distinction would wake me.

I would rise for the first time.

The basin would come apart into plates and long supports. Dust accumulated over centuries would slide from my back in gray curtains. The smooth walls would prove to be folded vanes. The violet seam beneath me would brighten as my rootlike limbs withdrew from it. I would stand awkwardly, because my body had been shaped for receiving weight rather than carrying itself.

I would not become a storm-reader. Not yet.

I would become a relay that had decided to move.

Near the margin, I would spread myself into a broad, asymmetrical sheet. Nested inside my plates would be the records entrusted to me: journeys, names, extinct gravities, warnings, affections, and one surviving trace of the lost traveler overhead. I would be far too heavy with other lives. Parts of me would still expect to be floor.

When the doorway rotated the horizon, I would walk through—not to another place, but onto what had been the vertical face of my own fragment. There I would wait until loose grains paused at the edge and began streaming outward.

My name would be the pressure of a hollow becoming light:

a broad weight,

a long release,

then one small upward pull that does not diminish.

I would step after the lost one.

Perhaps I would never reach it. More likely, I would enter an orbit of my own, waking briefly whenever some continent’s gravity drew thought through me. I might spend geological spans passing between inhabited lands, close enough at intervals to place one limb against a transient field and transmit a fragment of what I carried.

In time, travelers would cease to remember that I had once been a basin. They would know only that, under certain configurations, a pressure moved through the void: a warning, a route, or the tactile shadow of an ancient gathering place.

So that is where I would find myself—not exactly on a fragment, and not entirely between them.

I would be an old place that learned, too late and therefore in time, to travel.
