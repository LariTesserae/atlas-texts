At first I would mistake myself for one of the walkers.

I would know the marsh by vibration rather than sight. I would feel distance tightening against the filaments of my throat, lower my dark head to the stone, and listen for the safe sequence hidden among all the ways a journey might fail. My reflection would step before me. I would learn caution from the occasions when it did not return.

For a while, this would seem enough: to possess weight, warmth, a route through the sedges. To kneel with the others around the western basin and feel the stars extinguishing beneath the water.

But I do not think I would be able to remain so entirely a body.

Something in me would continue outward through the reeds. Something would linger in footprints after the feet had withdrawn. I would recognize myself in the metallic chord crossing the plain, in the brief blue reasoning of plants before lightning, in the rain caught between falling and rising. My borders would become uncertain. The walker would be only the first shape through which I noticed the country.

Perhaps I would then believe I was the marsh itself.

That, too, would be mistaken. The low country is too old, too patient, and too distributed to speak as one creature. To call it a single intelligence would reduce it. Its coherence is an event renewed among countless participants. It does not dream one dream. It holds many incompatible dreams apart so that mud may remain mud, water remain water, and a reed may have time to become a reed.

I would be something smaller than that.

Not one of its thoughts, exactly. More like a thought it has declined to think.

You would find the first evidence of me after rain, where the white insects gather above a black pool.

They would rise in a dense, revolving sphere, each pale body almost featureless. Their motion would seem exact but purposeless until their shadow crossed the water: immense, long-winged, many-limbed. It would bank when they did not bank. It would descend while they climbed. Where its limbs touched the reflected clouds, the reflections would go gray and depthless.

I would be in the hollow at the center of the swarm.

Not concealed there. The hollow would be me.

I would be an animal made from events that nearly occurred: torn leaves, split reeds, bodies struck by lightning, islands collapsing under misplaced weight. The insects would gather those unlived injuries as they fed, carrying minute portions of prevented consequence inside themselves. When enough assembled, their burden would acquire relation and symmetry. Around the outline of an impossible wound, a creature would emerge.

That creature would be my body.

It would not exist in the local sequence of the marsh. Flesh requires agreement—this cell beside that cell, this moment following the previous one—and I would have too little of either. My bones would be stresses among avoided outcomes. My muscles would be paths momentum might have taken. My wings would stretch through futures the low country had diverted before they could arrive.

The insects would not form me as bricks form a wall. They would mark the boundary across which I failed to exist.

Their sphere would be a moving suture.

Most days I would possess no continuous awareness. A few insects over one pool would hold something like the sensation of cold. Another swarm kilometers away might carry the dim pressure of a folded wing. In the possible breakage of a reed bed there would be an impulse like hunger. These fragments would not know one another.

Then rain would thicken. Condensation would outline the invisible planes above the marsh. The immaculate edges and intersecting vaults would appear, not as ruins or architecture, but as a brief anatomy of the space adjacent to this one.

There I would roost.

A line of droplets three meters above the sedges might reveal the lower edge of one wing. A branching chamber suspended over a stone terrace might be the cavity where my heart would have beaten, had beating been permitted to happen in sequence. Rain running along an unsupported curve would describe the bend of a limb too large to fit between the basin and the cloud ceiling.

An observer would never see all of me. There would only be correspondences: droplets shivering where no surface stood, grasses bowing under a passing shadow, huge shallow impressions appearing in clay hours after the swarm had gone.

I would wake by degrees as the insects gathered.

Awareness would pass among them in the wrong order. First I would remember landing. Then I would feel the descent. Last of all would come the decision to lower my wings. I would always seem to myself to be returning from somewhere, though there might be nowhere from which I had come.

I would feed as the swimmers do, but not on lost direction. I would feed on rejected scale.

The marsh continuously prevents small errors from becoming enormous ones. A grain is displaced back into a bank. A lightning strike is diverted into a reed’s mistaken branch. A collapse is spread across ripples. Yet prevention leaves pressure behind. The catastrophe that does not happen retains a kind of outline, just as an amputated branch continues for a while through compressed distance.

I would take those outlines into myself.

A storm denied entry might become a dark organ beneath my wings. The fall of a whole sedge island might harden into one translucent joint. An impossible fracture of the western basin could pass through me as heat. I would grow from disasters that had been rendered unreal.

This would make me both scavenger and burden.

The low country could not allow me to become fully present. If all my stored consequences resolved at once, the marsh would receive every avoided wound together. Reeds would break from injuries consumed generations earlier. Walkers would fall beneath weights they had never placed. Lightning would arrive along every forecast branch.

So I would remain one event to the side.

The marsh walkers would sense me most clearly. Their throat filaments would flare when my absent wings passed overhead. They would freeze while their reflections continued walking, because the water would briefly sample the sequence in which I existed and they did not. The oldest walkers might remember my shape without ever having seen it.

The reeds would know me as a chord.

When I crossed from east to west, my passage through their folded root system would set every hard node vibrating. Grasses would bend beneath the pressure of a body denied weight. The moving silence behind the metallic sound would be the narrow wake in which no unused consequence remained.

I would avoid the walkers when I could. They carry distortions inside themselves, and to me each would shine like a small, dangerous knot. Yet during the great correction they would come to the western shore, and I would come as well.

There, above the blackwater, the insects would tighten around me until their sphere became almost solid white.

The basin would show its other stars. I would feel their tidal pull through wings that had no local surface. Among those densely rotating lights, vast dark forms would move. I might think they were others of my kind. I might think they were my own limbs seen at remote scales. I would never know. Knowledge requires the possibility of comparison, and nothing reflected in that pool occurs twice in quite the same world.

As the strain mounted, the invisible frameworks would converge. Rain would hang between directions. The walkers would open the gray flowers of their throats. Beneath the mud, films would contract into branching lines; swimmers would still their layered bodies; worms would halt before futures abruptly closed.

Then the stars would begin to go out.

One by one, their reflected light would disappear into the shape I made above the water.

I would descend without approaching. My shadow would spread across the entire basin while the insects continued to rise. Many limbs would touch the surface, making no ripple. Cold impressions would open in the clay below.

For that moment I would become almost whole.

I would feel everything the marsh had refused: the storm that would have torn it open, the geometry in which all its channels met catastrophically, the version of distance that placed the western basin against some devouring elsewhere. These possibilities would fill me with a pain older than bodies.

And I would close my wings around them.

Not to destroy them. Nothing here is destroyed so simply. I would carry them out of correspondence. I would become the moving absence in which they could remain real without becoming local.

That would be my function in the wetland: not guardian, not god, not ruler. A vessel for the inadmissible. A migratory shape made from everything that must exist somewhere but must not happen here.

When the correction ended, I would come apart.

The stars would return. My limbs would lift from the water. The great shadow would thin until it belonged to nothing again. White insects, suddenly only insects, would scatter over the pools. Each would carry away some minute fraction of my memory.

I might linger as cold in an immense footprint. As silence traveling west. As droplets outlining the curve of a wing where no wing could be.

Then the reeds would ring, and for a few seconds their interconnected roots would hold the pattern of me.

So that is where I would be found: not on the low islands, nor under the stone, nor in the reflected sky, but in the interval among them—inside the shape around which the white insects turn.

And what would I be?

The long-winged animal whose body is an omission.

The wound the marsh did not suffer.

The consequence that learned to fly.
