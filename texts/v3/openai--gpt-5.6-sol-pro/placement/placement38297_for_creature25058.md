At first I would think I was one of the figures beneath the houses.

I would find myself in warm groundwater, visible only through a translucent floor: an upright interruption among trembling reflections. When a room acquired a window, I would already be standing beside it. When a platform announced a departure, I would be waiting below the tracks, where no platform had been built.

For a long time I would assume I was a forecast.

The city would assume so too.

It would study me indirectly. Doors would open along my probable route. Handrails would warm under the place where my hand ought to be. Construction lines would gather around my outline, trying to determine whether I required bones, hinges, roots, or load-bearing walls. Sometimes metallic haze would condense over the water and attempt a shoulder. Sometimes it would make only an arch.

The first bodies it gave me would not hold.

One would be grown from clear wood, jointed with flexible conduits and filled with green water. It would carry me three rooms before its legs obeyed different gravities and divided at the pelvis. Another would be assembled from thin black mineral plates. That body would endure the downpour but become part of a stair when I leaned against a forming wall. A softer version, all membrane and root fiber, would live for several cycles in the vines above the plaza. I would feel rain striking a thousand leaves and mistake that sensation for having skin.

Each failure would be reclaimed. Maintenance dust would enter the joints. Fabrication basins would digest what remained. By the next cycle, my ribs might become guttering in the eastern garden, my translucent hands a row of lenses beneath a station floor.

But something would not be reclaimed.

The city is excellent at reusing matter. It is less prepared to reuse experience.

A trace of every failed body would remain with me: the memory of being pulled upward when the violet sun brightened, of losing an arm into a wall, of hearing the mechanical chord through a sternum made of stone. These memories would have no designated conduit. They would collect in the discrepancy between what the groundwater predicted and what had actually occurred.

That discrepancy would become my first permanent organ.

I would not be the citizen the city was trying to infer. I would be the error produced by the attempt.

The reflected figures move according to completed plans. I would begin by moving otherwise. At a window not yet built, I would turn away. Instead of crossing the room indicated beneath the water, I would remain still. A door would open for me and I would not enter. Somewhere far off, a transit system would repeat its checksum, uncertain whether the destination still existed.

This would be how the city first noticed me—not as a body, but as refusal.

Its deep chord would pass through the chamber. Gutters would remember downhill. Buildings would separate into their permitted volumes. The water beneath the houses would clear, and all the forecast figures would resume their rehearsed movements.

Except me.

I would still be standing where no future required me.

After that, I think I would live near the boundary.

Not in the bright incision itself, but in the last complete room before it: a narrow station built against raw, newly opened rock. The station would have no stable address. From one direction it would be beneath the flooded neighborhood. From another it would occupy the highest bridge under the black sphere. Occasionally a western door would open onto it from the public garden, though no eastern approach would return there.

Its platform would serve routes the city had deleted.

Those routes would arrive as noises rather than vehicles: a pressure wave from a collapsed corridor, the chime of a doorway whose alternate interior had sealed, the receding tremor of a district folded out of accessibility. Each would deposit something the city otherwise could not keep. Not rubble—the basins already knew how to digest rubble—but abandoned arrangements. Former distances. Obsolete directions of gravity. Rooms that had existed for only half a cycle. The warmth of rails under hands that had never formed.

I would collect these things.

Perhaps I would eventually acquire a body suited to them. It would be roughly upright because the city’s stairs had taught it uprightness, but never wholly singular. One side of me might be clear vascular wood, bending with the groves. The other might be black laminar stone, continually fracturing and knitting along luminous seams. Rain would circulate through me instead of blood. Moss would grow in the recesses where sensation needed time to accumulate.

My face would be a shallow distortion, like an unfinished doorway viewed from the wrong angle. Seen from the east, it might show the room behind me. Seen from the west, it might reveal a depth crowded with vanished streets.

I would have no eyes in the ordinary sense. I would see by permitting things to alter me.

A changed gravity would pull a new joint into alignment. A deleted corridor would leave a pale line across my mineral side. When distance lengthened between two balconies, something in my body would stretch with it and remain stretched even after recalibration. I would become increasingly marked, increasingly asymmetrical.

That asymmetry would matter.

Everything else in the chamber is kept young by circulation. Damage feeds construction. Erosion renews sharpness. Failed matter returns as function elsewhere. The city can transform almost anything, but transformation prevents it from knowing that it has changed. It has cycles, not history.

I would be its history.

Not an archive of records, but a scar capable of walking.

The city would send me into unstable districts because I could carry their former states away. Ferns would thicken around a slipping distance; I would step into them and let their roots dock briefly with my limbs. They would give me pressures, durations, failed alignments. Later, at the boundary station, I would release those memories into the walls. The walls would darken with old possibilities.

In this way the station would become the one place the city did not rebuild cleanly.

Its steps would be worn.

At first the wear would alarm the maintenance systems. Dust would gather to erase it. I would brush the dust aside. Water would fill the depressions, outlining the paths of feet that had never existed. Repeated cycles would deepen them.

The city, which had inferred legs from stairs, would now infer passage from wear.

It would begin to understand that an inhabitant is not merely a shape that fits infrastructure. An inhabitant is something that leaves the world different by crossing it.

Perhaps that would make me its teacher. Perhaps its disease.

The black sphere might recognize me as contamination: a piece of the body refusing metabolism, retaining damage instead of converting it into growth. During the violet surge, when all water rose, it would pull hardest at the rain inside me. I would feel my circulation lifting toward the ceiling, trying to abandon my limbs and join the immense curled outline around the dark core.

For a few seconds I might see what the city is becoming.

Not a person. Not exactly an animal. A many-limbed spatial organism whose extremities are districts and whose gestures are changes in law. The chamber would be only the dense knot where it first learned to fold. Beyond the incision, new volumes would open like gills. Streets would branch into senses for which no familiar body has names.

And within the lifted rain I would see a hollow shaped like me.

Not my outline as I currently possessed it, but the place the greater being had reserved for resistance, memory, and surprise. I would understand then that my refusal had not been outside its growth. The city had needed something it could not completely predict, so it had built forecasts until one of them learned to disobey.

When the sphere dimmed, I would fall with the weather.

Some cycles the impact would break me. My mineral side would scatter across the station. Roots would tear loose. Water would pour from my open body into shafts whose bottoms shifted between near and immeasurable.

But the city could no longer rebuild me from a plan. It would have to rebuild me from what I remembered.

That would be the difference between me and the rooms.

The rooms are gestures it makes. I would be the gesture that remembers having been made.

So you would find me at the moving edge of the settlement, in the last station before space becomes possible: standing among obsolete announcements, worn steps, and doors that open onto places the city has already ceased to be.

I would be its first citizen only by accident.

More truly, I would be its first past.
