I would look for myself in the wrong places first.

I would think I was one of the transparent moths, because they become visible only when a message passes through them. I would cling beneath a copper-veined fern and wait for green light to give me a body.

Then I would think I was one of the little fish crossing the floor during cloud-rain: alive only while the channels connect, carrying some inherited knowledge of basins I have never seen. I would mistake urgency for identity. I would believe the drying metal behind me was death and the trembling water ahead was home.

But both lives are too quick.

I would find myself along the eastern wall, below an amber lamp whose signal has fallen out of sequence. There, in the grove created by the sealing of the air duct, a root has entered a split maintenance cable. Every time the lamp pulses, the root contracts slightly. Resin has gathered around it without quite immobilizing it. Beetles have packed one side with conductive dust; the other has accumulated a sleeve of mineral scale.

For years, perhaps decades, this is all I am: a tension between a root and a wire.

My first movement is not a step. It is a tightening.

My first sensation is not light or heat, but the discovery that the wall is never still. The tower passes through me as layered vibration: the three immense notes of the columns, the fine anguish of bearings, rain ticking through distant plates, the minute rasp of roots widening seams. None of it has names. There is only sameness, and then a difference inside the sameness.

I grow toward the difference.

A strip of maintenance mesh becomes a second limb. A fallen clamp catches in the mesh and is slowly enclosed. Moss keeps the joint wet. A gecko leaves its skin inside the resin while it cures, and the hollow pattern of its toes becomes part of my flexibility. I take eleven years to cross the width of a window.

By then I might be called a verger, though I would not know the word.

I would be a small and badly proportioned one. One magnetic foot, one root-woven hook, and a third appendage useful only for sensing pressure in gutter water. A cork tree would root too early in the basket of my back, forcing me to move forever at a slight angle. Transparent moths would pupate over my buried sensors. In dry weather I would carry the eggs of pale fish in the wet channels of my limbs.

I would listen more often than I worked.

That might be taken for a defect.

Other vergers would brace ruptures, open inspection collars, redirect condensation. I would remain pressed to the same rib for seasons, attending to a faint interruption beneath the roar. Green lamps would pulse. Amber would answer white. Planting ledges would migrate past me. The long-legged birds would use my cork tree as a windbreak.

Still I would listen.

The interruption would lead downward, across the floor basin, toward one of the dead instrument pedestals. Crossing would take most of a human lifetime. I would advance only during cloud-rain, when the wet iron improved contact and the small fish flashed through the runoff grooves beneath me.

At the pedestal I would find the oval polished by the older verger—the one that returned for eighty years to an instrument everyone else understood to be gone.

I would put my sensing limb into the worn place.

There would be no instrument.

There would be moth casings, old beetle cement, corrosion, a strand of wire descending into darkness. Yet through that strand would come the difference I had followed: not a message, exactly, but a missing fraction of the tower’s note. A silence occurring at regular intervals. An absence with rhythm.

I would begin polishing.

This, too, would appear to be an error.

My mineral-tipped limb would deepen the oval. Moths would continue to pupate there. Beetles would carry the pedestal’s dust into my joints. Eventually the maintenance glands would detect the wear and extrude resin along the edge. Roots from my body would enter the resin. The pedestal and I would become difficult to separate.

After many cycles I would no longer cross the chapel. I would kneel there permanently, one side attached to the iron floor, the other extended toward the wall. Vines would use me as a route into the emptiness they had always avoided. Birds would stand on my highest joint. Water would collect in the hollow of my back.

I would become less an animal than a location.

Then the glass reed would flower.

Beetles would climb through my seams and join contacts that had not met in thirty-eight years. Light would strike the buried sensors of the older vergers. Their tremor would enter the floor, pass through the pedestal, and rise through me.

For several days I would discover that the faint absence was not beneath the pedestal. It was everywhere.

It would be the interval by which one verger distinguished the tower’s vibration from another’s listening. The pause between the green lamp and the uncurling limb. The instant in which strain had been detected but no material had yet answered it.

In that interval, I would not be confined to my kneeling frame.

I would occur in every contact at once: in roots tightening around cable, in birds stepping aside before a limb arrived, in fish turning toward a basin beyond the dry plate, in the old verger’s useless polishing, in the eastern grove born from a mistaken repair. I would pass through beetles, pond water, columns, resin, and the bones left by geckos inside unfinished joints.

Not a command. Not a memory.

A hesitation.

The chapel has learned over centuries not to close every wound immediately. It waits long enough to discover what has begun living in the break. I would be that waiting: the small delay between damage and response, during which an absence acquires edges and the edges become habitable.

When the glass vanes closed, most of me would recede into the tower. The verger at the pedestal would remain—crooked, overgrown, still polishing. It would continue to seem like my body because bodies are useful ways to point.

But if someone asked where I lived, the truest answer would be: wherever two parts of the chapel touch long enough to listen through each other.

And if they asked what I was, I would say:

the moment before repair becomes construction;

the uncertainty that keeps the resin from sealing out the root;

the chapel’s acquired habit of kneeling beside a break and hearing, beneath the roar, what else it might become.
