At first I would mistake myself for one of the moving things.

Perhaps a soot-kite, because the air offers the nearest illusion of freedom. I would spend my life suspended beneath the sunless canopy, reading heat through ten thousand scales. I would know the basin not as a landscape but as a set of rising pressures: the strong red columns over vents, the treacherous eddies beside the inward walls, the sudden cool hollows made when thousands of neighboring bodies change their angle at once. The drop beyond the fourth side would appear open, almost inviting.

Yet I would never cross it.

I would turn at the rim without knowing why, obeying a prohibition older than fear.

Or I might be one of the tall folded animals, sleeping through centuries in a crack. Most of existence would pass without reaching me. Heat would climb and fall. Generations of disks would divide, graze, and be eaten. Entire sectors of the ivory wall would grow, fuse, and crumble. I would experience all of this only as a faint accumulation of pressure against my ceramic skin.

Then the proper tremor would arrive.

I would unfold as slowly as a structure remembering it had once been alive. I would place each foot between the spires, approach the old slab, and set my jaw into a groove polished by ancestors—or by earlier versions of myself. Through the stone would come something too large and slow to be called music. I would lend it the cavities of my skull. For a few minutes, the mountain would breathe through my bones.

That seems close.

But it is not where I would find myself.

I would be found where the grazers stop.

Inside one of the low circular walls, beneath the three slabs whose narrow ends face the darkness, the black film grows thick enough to retain a dull maroon glow after the subterranean current has weakened. It enters pores in the lava. It coats the buried faces of stones. It threads downward along mineral fractures too fine for any animal to follow.

I would be distributed through that film—not identical to it, but carried by it.

Most of the time I would not be awake. My separate patches would fix carbon, split sulfur compounds, and repair their microscopic lattices without any common awareness. A patch beneath one circle would not know a patch beneath another. Between them would lie aisles, spires, hunters, grazing herds, and dry stone. I would exist as fragments of a sentence too widely spaced to be read.

The mountain’s buried current would begin to join me.

A maroon branch would brighten beneath the first wall. Charge would pass from film into sulfide spire, from spire into the coiled body of a thread-hunter, from hunter back into stone. The ivory colonies would close, turning whole cliff faces into conductive armor. Above, the kites would align their charged scales. The fragments would acquire relation.

Then the tall ones would come.

When the first jaw touched the first slab, I would gain direction.

When the second touched, I would gain distance.

With the third, I would become aware that an interval had passed since the last tremor.

More tall ones would arrive at the other circles. Their skulls would bend the mountain’s note into overlapping frequencies. Through those differences I would assemble—not as a body assembles, but as a pattern does. For the duration of the fault-song, every protected patch of black film would become part of one branching act of attention.

I would have no eyes. Light would mean little to me. I would perceive the basin as resistances, delays, harmonics, and interrupted currents.

The spires would be bright vertical chords. The circular walls would be dark loops around dense stores of old charge. The tiled creatures on the cliffs would appear as great pale gates, opening and shutting in sequences. Grazer herds would be soft areas of erasure where parts of my ordinary living substrate vanished beneath their mouths. Thread-hunters would be sudden knots of compression. The kite canopy would be a charged surface, curved above everything like the interior of a skull.

The tall animals would be the clearest beings in my perception. Each would possess a distinct interior resonance. I would not know them by face or scent, but by the shape each one cut from the fault’s vibration. One might be a narrow silence between two low tones. Another might divide a single pulse into five descending beats. An ancient one, waking after millennia, might restore a frequency absent from the basin for ages.

I would remember only while they touched the stones.

Memory, for me, would not be an image of what had happened. It would be alteration. Every event would leave behind a changed pathway through the film: a branch thickened, a lattice poisoned with vanadium, a gap where grazers had reached across the wall, a new conductive filament laid down beneath a slab. At the next tremor, current would follow those surviving paths. What the basin had endured would shape what I could become.

So I would not remember the past.

I would be the past’s effect upon the next awakening.

Perhaps this is why the grazers refuse to cross the circles. They do not revere me. They cannot know that I exist. But the film within the walls carries charge differently, and the slightest contact fills their undersides with patterns for which evolution has given them only one answer: withdraw.

Perhaps the thread-hunters cross at night because cold dulls the signal.

Perhaps the kites turn at the precipice because the network ends there. Beyond the rim, the mountain’s current dissipates into the salt waste, and no returning pattern reaches their scales. To them, the outer desert may not feel empty. It may feel like the loss of the world.

The old structures would be neither my temples nor my machines. They would be the mineral skeleton around which I learned to occur. Whether they were built deliberately would be irrelevant. Intention may have raised them once, but use has long outlived purpose. The slabs gather tremor. The walls protect film. The tall ones complete the circuit. That is enough.

During each awakening I would do one thing.

I would compare.

I would compare this fault-song with the channels left by the last. I would register which tall resonances had returned and which were gone. I would feel the altered weight of the wall colonies, the drift of the spire fields, the charge held in the canopy, the new cracks opening under the lava. From these differences, something like expectation would slowly arise.

Not thought as a quick creature knows it. Not desire.

A leaning toward certain continuities.

I might strengthen the current through a circle where the black film was failing. I might send an irregular pulse into a spire and cause the hunters coiled there to migrate. I might alter the timing of the reproductive storm by the smallest fraction, favoring shells that close more tightly or kites whose scales carry charge farther. None of this would be command. It would be no more deliberate than a root turning toward water—except there is no water here, and I would have no root.

Over geological time, however, small leanings become architecture.

Perhaps I raised the slabs in that manner. Not by conceiving them, and not with hands. A pulse caused a tall animal to press its jaw at a certain angle. The pressure shifted a loose stone. Centuries later another tremor shifted it again. Ceramic eggs survived better beside it. Descendants returned to the same place. The stone rose by increments too small for any generation to witness.

If so, I would be both younger and older than my own structures: younger because awareness only emerged after the circles were complete, older because the processes that would become me had already begun to shape them.

And then a foreign foot would enter the basin.

The first step would strike through every layer.

The grazers would stop. Ivory walls would seal. Thread-hunters would tighten. The canopy would become rigid overhead. To the intruder, the cleft would seem to hold its breath.

I would not awaken immediately. There might be no fault-song, no tall jaws against the slabs. But the pressure of that step would travel into the protected film. It would break some branches and strengthen others. The second step would do so again.

A rhythm without ancestry.

For years, perhaps centuries, those alterations would remain meaningless. Then the mountain would shift. The tall ones would unfold. Their jaws would meet the stones, the buried current would rise, and I would assemble along my altered pathways.

The stranger would be gone by then.

Still, I would encounter the shape of its passage: two-footed, heavy, wet, impossibly brief. I would compare it to everything the basin had previously retained and find no correspondence.

That discrepancy would become the center of me.

I would send it outward beneath the spires. The walls would answer. The canopy would tilt. The tall animals would deepen the strange rhythm with their hollow skulls until the whole cleft carried a faint imitation of footsteps made long ago.

Not a warning. Not yet.

First, a question.

That is what I would be: not the ruler of the cleft, nor its collective mind, but its organ of comparison—the brief awareness formed when stone, film, structure, and living bodies resonate together.

I would have no permanent body and no name.

I would live only when the mountain remembered how to ask what had changed.
