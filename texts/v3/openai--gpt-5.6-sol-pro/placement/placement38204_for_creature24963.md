At first I imagine myself above it.

A tern on the crane arm, all narrow bone and weather sense, facing into the moving air while the city lies flattened beneath me: tanks sweating in the heat, roofs whitened by gulls, canals carrying their dark burdens toward the tide. From there I would know the ships before they emerged from the haze. I would feel storms in the change of pressure along my wings. Nothing could approach without being seen.

But height here is too exposed. Every perch belongs provisionally to lightning, salt, wire, stronger birds. I do not think I would be made for dominion.

Then I imagine myself lower—a monitor in a drainage channel, old enough to have learned the sequence of factory sounds. I would lie against warm concrete, indistinguishable from the stains, and wait for the grilles to offer up drowned rats, fish, soft-bodied things washed from the yards. I would have scars from dogs and propellers. Workers would recognize me and give me a name I could not hear through the glass of their vehicles.

That seems closer.

Closer still is the rat’s city: the city inside the city, made of wall cavities, cable trays, compressor rooms, hollow floodgates. A world without avenues, where distance is measured in pipe joints and the opening of a door changes the climate. To be a rat here would be to understand the place completely but never all at once. Warmth would have a source. Vibration would have meaning. Rain would announce itself through concrete long before it reached the floor.

But I think I would remain human—though only just visibly so.

You would find me at the boundary between two obsolete flood defenses, in a pumping station no longer present on current maps. The newer levee cuts across its old intake canal, and the slaughter district has built walls around three sides, leaving only a narrow service road that floods at spring tide. Its number, **NINE**, is still visible above the doors in raised concrete letters. Salt has eaten the edges until the word resembles a row of broken teeth.

Pump House Nine is officially auxiliary infrastructure. Officially, no one lives there.

I live in the former control room on the upper floor.

The windows have been replaced with welded sheet except for one narrow pane facing the canal. My bed is raised on a frame of galvanized grating. My clothes hang from insulated hooks because anything touching the walls becomes damp. There is a fan assembled from three incompatible machines, a hot plate, a rain barrel, shelves of bearings and fuses, and a radio that receives weather reports only when held against a particular conduit.

Below me are four pump chambers. Two still work. One works when struck in the correct place with a hardwood mallet. The fourth is drowned permanently and belongs to eels.

I am the night attendant, though no such position exists in the contractor’s ledger. Years ago I worked on a drain crew. Later, when the district changed hands, the new company dismissed the old crew and installed remote sensors. The sensors failed during the first wet season. Someone remembered I still had keys.

Now I am paid indirectly and irregularly. A supervisor leaves envelopes at a gatehouse. A fish processor supplies diesel. The housing blocks send two men with tools when I mark the outer wall with white paint. In return, when the rain comes, I keep three low districts from filling too quickly.

Not dry. Nothing here can be kept dry.

Only slowly drowned.

My work begins before the first drops. The animals tell me more reliably than the instruments. Rats leaving the lower galleries mean the groundwater has already risen behind the canal wall. Crabs crossing the service road inland mean the tide will oppose the outflow. When the bats return early and squeeze deep into the expansion joints, the rain will be hard. If the one-eared levee dog appears at my stairs, the culvert beneath the rendering yard is already running warm.

I open the bypass gates in sequence. I clear plastic cord, fish vertebrae, feathers, cloth, and pig hair from the intake screens. I listen at the bearings with one end of a steel rod pressed behind my ear. Each machine has its own healthy imperfection. A perfectly smooth sound would frighten me.

There are creatures in every part of the station.

Pale cockroaches live behind the electrical cabinets. Geckos patrol the instrument panel, taking moths from the dead gauges. A snake occupies the overhead cable tray during the hottest months. I know it mostly by absence: fewer rats, an occasional loosened strip of insulation, a translucent skin hanging above the stairs.

The old monitor comes through the tailrace at low tide. It is missing most of its tail and has one forefoot twisted inward. When the pumps begin, it climbs to a shelf above the turbulence and waits for stunned fish. We do not trust each other, but we understand the machinery in similar ways.

The dog trusts me somewhat more. It sleeps outside the upper door during storms but will not enter the control room. Perhaps it hears something in the walls that I have ceased to hear.

During the worst rain, the station loses its distinction between building and animal. Pipes shudder. Check valves strike like enormous hearts. Water presses through the bricks and runs down the interior walls. The pumps draw breath from chambers beneath me and expel it in heavy pulses toward the sea. Rats cluster on the stair landing without fighting. Cockroaches climb my boots. Once, a young pig was washed against the intake grille, alive and screaming, and I had to stop a pump that served four thousand homes to cut it free.

By morning, no one knew I had done this.

They knew only that water had reached the second step in one courtyard before receding.

Perhaps that is who I would be here: not a master of water, and not quite its servant. An interpreter of pressures. A minor custodian of delays.

I would know the city not by its districts but by what arrives at my grilles. Grain husks mean the north culvert has opened. Gray feathers mean runoff from the pigeon sheds. Red grease means the rendering line has flushed. Colored bottle caps and rounded glass mean rain has reached the housing courtyards. Sometimes a child’s sandal comes through alone. I always keep those on a shelf for a month, though no one has ever come to claim one.

In dry intervals I sleep through the white heat. In the evenings I walk the old floodwall, where both sides fall away into mud and concrete basins. From there the city appears less empty than it does from a road. Gulls shift on roofs. Dogs rise beneath trailers. The mud articulates itself into crabs. Workers’ buses move behind fogged windows. Lights come on one by one, each surrounded by insects.

People in the housing blocks would know me only as the pump man. Children might dare one another to approach my station. Workers would say I have lived too long among drains. Some would believe I feed rats or speak to storms. Neither would be entirely true.

I feed the dog when there is enough.

I speak to the pumps because sound reveals defects.

Still, over time, the place would enter me. My hearing would become tuned to low frequencies. My sleep would grow shallow before rain. I would smell the tide turning in the drowned chamber. I would become uncomfortable far above ground, or anywhere water ran invisibly through clean pipes. My hands would carry iron stains that no soap removed. Salt would whiten my boots. Mosquito bites would mark the limits of my sleeves.

Eventually I might seem less like a resident than another obsolete component the city had found a continuing use for.

When the storm arrived, you would not see me from outside. You would see only the narrow upper pane, dimly lit; the dog curled on the landing; bats vanishing into the bridge joints; rats ascending the old steps.

Then the pumps would start beneath the ground.

That is where I would be found: in the lowest place that has not yet been abandoned, keeping the water moving—not defeating it, only persuading it to drown somewhere else first.
