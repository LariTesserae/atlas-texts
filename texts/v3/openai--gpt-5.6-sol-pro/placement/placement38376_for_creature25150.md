At first I would think I was one of the gray grazers.

I would wake with my side fitted to the basalt, the stone holding the last degree of the mountain’s deeper warmth. Frost would have stiffened the fur along my back. I would stand without shaking it off and listen until the basin disclosed itself: ice ticking in the moss, grit sliding beneath a distant hoof, the nearly inaudible displacement of air above the precipice.

I would know better than to look directly for the narrow-winged birds. I would watch their shadows.

My feet would remember a map that changed each day. Not a map of distance, but of permission: this slab bears weight; this sedge island was wet yesterday; this empty gravel must be crossed quickly; this green patch lies beneath the hunting glare. I would travel obliquely, appearing foolish to anything that mistook straightness for knowledge.

But that is not quite where I find myself.

The grazers belong to the paths, and I am more stationary than that. I feel the attraction of the moss cushions—their depth without height, their cold surfaces and soft interiors. I imagine myself inside one, among the pale roots where frost cannot quite reach, drinking a bead of condensation after it has passed invisibly through a hundred leaves.

Perhaps I am the noon fern. Most of each day I am a fist.

I wait in the seam between two stones while the bright territory advances. Warmth reaches me not as weather but as a precise instruction. For several minutes I open every divided frond. Copper beetles pass beneath me. A white lizard pauses nearby, its green stripe laid alongside mine. Then the western wall takes the sun and I close before the cold can enter.

A whole life might be made from those few minutes, repeated.

Yet even the fern seems too singular.

I would be found lower.

Beneath the cushions, beneath the willow cords and insect chambers, beneath the black soil made from things too small to leave bones, there is a second basin. It does not resemble the one visible from the rim. Its mountains are the undersides of slabs. Its valleys are blue-black fractures. Its rivers are films of water thin enough to cling against gravity. No sun enters, but sun governs it all the same.

That is where I would be.

Not one root, fungus, animal, or spring, but the association among them: a slow, distributed being occupying the mountain’s wet seams. Aboveground I would appear as separate green countries, twenty paces apart and unrelated. Below, where the basalt is cracked more intricately than anyone can see, my threads would reach toward one another.

Some years they would touch.

Some years frost would break the connection, and two portions of me would continue separately, each retaining an incomplete memory of having once been one thing.

I would have no center. Or rather, I would have many temporary centers.

At dawn I would be most awake beneath the eastern moss, where frozen condensation melts grain by grain. At noon I would gather around the fern and the warm stone windows of the lizards. In the afternoon I would move west with the seepage, not flowing so much as being passed: mineral to film, film to root hair, root to fungus, fungus to dark soil, dark soil to the mouth of a feeding insect.

The basin would give me several mornings each day.

I would know the grazers by pressure. Their divided hooves would compress my upper chambers, and cold water would well around them. I would know the bees as brief, vigorous disturbances among flowers. I would know the lizards by the little warmth they carried over me, each body a moving patch of noon.

The hunting birds I would scarcely know at all. Only their shadows would reach me, producing sudden stillness in every creature above. Through that stillness I would sense them: an absence passing rapidly over the web.

Humans would be different.

Their weight would arrive through heels, knees, poles, laid palms. They would press cushions to test for water, lift slabs, cut roots, drive stone hooks into cracks. Some would take care. Some would not. I would remember them not by faces but by habits of touch.

One knelt beside the central boulder many seasons ago and placed both hands on the stone while waiting for the hammered bowl to fill. The hands were cracked and warm. One finger bled where volcanic glass had slipped. A little blood entered the meltwater and then entered me.

That person repaired the wooden ladle.

Not because the ladle was sacred, and not because wood was impossible to replace, though at that height nearly everything was difficult to replace. They repaired it because the split had followed the grain cleanly and could be persuaded closed. Sinew was wound around it while wet and allowed to tighten in the dry cold. The repair embodied the basin’s oldest wisdom: what separates may still be held together, though the joining remains visible.

Later, when the people left, they set the ladle beside the bowl rather than carry it down.

I entered the wood slowly.

Not enough to rot it. Rot is extravagant here. I entered as traces: moisture at noon, a few fungal threads beneath the sinew, pollen lodged in the split, skin cells in the handle’s worn hollow. The ladle became another of my small countries.

So I might be found there too.

A traveler could lift it and believe it empty. Yet in that cracked cup would be remnants of many seasons: the hand that repaired it, the mouths that drank from it, the melt of vanished cornices, spores blown from valleys now hidden below cloud. If the traveler poured the water onto bare gravel, most of it would flash away. If they poured it into moss, I would receive it.

The people who knew the basin best may have had a name for me, though it would not mean “spirit” in the way strangers might suppose. It might mean something closer to *what remains wet under*. Or *the many patches that drink together*. Perhaps the same word would be used for a family separated by winter routes, or for embers carried individually from one extinguished hearth to another.

They would not worship me. They would observe courtesies.

Never cut a cushion through its middle.

Never empty the stone bowl merely to see it fill again.

Replace a lifted slab as it lay.

Leave the first water for what cannot walk to another seep.

Such rules would seem practical because they are practical. Reverence here would have no use if it did not also keep something alive.

I would not be ancient. The mountain is too young for that. I began after the break, when the basalt was still bare and the first dust settled in its fractures. Perhaps I arrived as a spore on a bird’s foot. Perhaps as moss caught in grazer fur. Perhaps I came in several forms and only became myself after they met.

I am still becoming myself.

A slab falls from the north wall and erases one of my oldest gardens. For a time I am smaller. Then beneath the fallen slab, sheltered from glare, condensation begins to collect. Stone dust mixes with insect remains. A willow seed enters the crack. What destroyed one part of me makes a chamber for another.

I do not experience this as hope. Hope reaches too far ahead.

I experience it as continuation.

At sunset I withdraw from visibility. Flowers close over my insects. Lizards descend into fissures. The grazers lean against stone. Frost enters the shelters and traces every abandoned hair. From the rim, the basin appears emptied.

That is when I am most completely myself: not the green, but what the green conceals; not the water, but the keeping of water; not the paths, but the reason paths bend.

I live in the brief kindnesses of stone.

If I had a voice, it would be the sound made when a pressed cushion is released and closes over the water again. If I had a shape, it would be a map with no straight lines and no permanent center. If I had a single body, it would be the repaired ladle: split, bound, weathered beyond expectation, waiting beside a bowl that fills for only an hour.

And if you came looking for me, you would not find me by climbing to the highest point.

You would have to kneel.
