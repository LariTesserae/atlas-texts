At first I would place myself near the opening, because the blade of sky is the first thing the imagination reaches for.

I would be one of the reflective-shelled workers who climbs to the last concealed roof, anchored by three cables, watching the summit wind comb dust from the lip of the crack. I would know the names of stars that appear only for a few breaths between one wall and the other. Children would touch my plates when I came down, searching for traces of that impossible distance.

But I do not think I would remain there. The sky is too clean an answer. In this city, anything seen clearly from above has probably been misunderstood.

Perhaps I would live far below, in the Salvage Dark, descending in a cage whose cable carries the voices of those above as tremors through my feet. I would search among the accumulated failures of centuries: buckled doors, obsolete tools, cages folded flat by closures, ancestral plates engraved in forgotten dialects. My work would be to decide what a fallen thing had been, whether it could bear weight again, and who had the right to remember it.

That comes closer.

Yet I would not be a simple keeper of the past. Nothing here is permitted that luxury. Memory must become a brace, a tool, brood material, or warning. Even grief has to take a load.

You would find me somewhere near the middle depths, where the fissure twists just enough that the sky disappears but the amber furnaces below remain visible. The district would be called something like **The Crooked Transfer**: not a destination, but a place where freight, pressure, and responsibility change direction.

It would migrate often. Its houses would move along paired rails fixed to opposite walls, crossing one another in slow processions whenever the mountain tightened. My address would always be provisional:

**Crooked Transfer, seventh hanging tier, inward wall—descending at last revision.**

My home would be a narrow chamber fastened beneath an old lift counterweight. It would rise when the neighboring freight platform descended and sink when the platform rose. Visitors would therefore never find it at the same elevation twice. They would announce themselves by tapping the counterweight cable, and I would answer through the bolts.

Inside there would be very little that belonged only to me. Racks of recovered grip-objects. Coils of obsolete signal wire. Fragments of engraved plate awaiting identification. A warming niche occupied by several useless glass-backed animals bred for the blue sparks that run under their shells. I would insist they were diagnostic creatures. No one would believe me.

I would be one of those who listens for messages that arrive late.

The city’s ordinary communications are immediate: a warning pulse, a freight rhythm, the approaching signature of a procession. But the mountain keeps older vibrations. An impact can travel down one seam, vanish into a dense basalt rib, and emerge hours later through another wall. Closures leave pressure echoes. Collapsed bridges continue to report their last strain whenever the surrounding rock shifts in a similar way.

Most citizens would dismiss these signals as stone-noise.

I would collect them.

My body would be suited—or altered—for it. I would have the usual six primary limbs, though one rear pair would be unusually broad and divided into hundreds of fine contact digits. When I spread them against a wall, I could distinguish vibrations too faint for ordinary touch: the granular settling of sulfur mortar, the scrape of mites beneath plating, the almost imperceptible complaint of an overloaded staple several tiers away.

One forelimb would be metal from the second joint onward, an old salvage replacement assembled from incompatible mechanisms. Its outer plate would once have belonged to a district crushed before I opened from the brood. Along its inner edge would be mounted a row of tuning teeth. By pressing them against stone in different combinations, I could ask the mountain crude questions.

Not speak to it. We would be too practical to claim that.

But ask.

Where is the weight moving?

What has become hollow?

Which old fracture has begun to remember itself?

I would work as a strain archivist, though some would call me a ghost-listener when they meant to be unkind. Surveyors would bring me ambiguous readings. Families would bring recovered fragments and ask whether the faint pattern inside them belonged to an ancestor’s greeting or only to machinery. Structural adjudicators would consult me privately, then publicly attribute my conclusions to newer and more respectable instruments.

Often I would be wrong.

The city contains too many rhythms, and longing can resemble evidence when transmitted through stone. I might identify a sequence as the final warning from a lost neighborhood, only to discover that it came from a shield-backed grazer trapped in an exhaust duct. I might spend months reconstructing a supposed ceremonial composition that was merely the repeating fault in an ancient lift.

These mistakes would be drummed into jokes along the rails. I would laugh too, striking my side plates while pretending to damp the vibration.

But sometimes a late signal would matter.

During a closure, when the Crooked Transfer filled with bodies and all ordinary channels became saturated, I might feel an irregular pulse beneath the common alarm: three impacts, a drag, then a pressure too slow to be deliberate. I would recognize it as the remembered collapse of a bridge built from the same alloy as the present one. Not prophecy—only an old weakness answering a familiar load.

I would send the release rhythm.

The bridge would retract moments before its anchors sheared.

Afterward, citizens would call the escape good judgment. The officials would call it responsible interpretation of historical strain. Children would say the dead had warned us. I would not correct any of them.

I think I would have been raised by a parent-group of lift tenders, animal keepers, and one brood guarantor who had never met the others before accepting the obligation. As a young form I would have dropped everything I could unfasten, not merely to see where it went but to feel how each impact returned through the room. My caretakers would have installed extra nets. Then nets beneath those nets.

My first name would have meant **Repeatedly Retrieved**.

In maturity I would take another, transmitted as two light contacts followed by a long pressure that fades without quite ending. In air it could not be spoken. Its nearest translation might be:

**What returns by another path.**

I would not be brave about the sky. I might visit it once.

An upper worker would secure me with redundant lines and lead me to the lip near nightfall. I would unfold to my full height and immediately regret it. There would be no opposite wall, no ceiling, no returning signal—only the mountain falling away outside itself and stars without cables between them.

I would press all my limbs to the stone.

The worker would ask what I felt.

Not emptiness, I think. The summit wind would make the suspended city tremble below us, every scale and bridge answering in its own band. Through the lip of the fissure I would feel the whole settlement hanging under my body: furnaces, cages, brood chambers, animal migrations, arguments, music, the tapping memorial pieces of the dead.

And beneath all of it, the vast slow pressure of the mountain.

I would understand then that the city was not inside a crack.

The city was the crack’s way of remaining open.

I would descend before dawn and never become an upper worker. But I would keep a sliver of pale summit stone riveted beneath one chest plate, where no one could see it. Not as a symbol of freedom. As a reminder that there are places from which no answer returns, and that this does not make the signals we do receive less real.

When I eventually died, my useful tissues would be reclaimed. The broad listening feet would go to survey instruments or to a brood bundle. The metal forelimb would be disassembled. My plates would become shims, contact surfaces, perhaps the reinforced underside of a family net.

The small summit stone would puzzle whoever found it.

My memorial piece would be set into the wall of the Crooked Transfer, if the district still existed. It would bear my characteristic pressure: two brief contacts and a long fading remainder.

Years later, when the mountain shifted, the piece might tap against the stone beside it. Someone standing at the rail would feel the vibration arrive from no obvious direction.

They might pause.

They might mistake it for a warning, a greeting, settling debris, or the delayed ending of an old joke.

Any of those would be close enough.
