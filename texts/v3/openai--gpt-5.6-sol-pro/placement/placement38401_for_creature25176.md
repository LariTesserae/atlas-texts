At first I think I would be below it.

Not on the unfinished ground, but somewhere in the pale atmospheric hollow, falling so slowly that falling had become a kind of residence. I would be a ceramic needle shed from a vanished range, turning end over end beneath the ocean’s wavering underside. Above me, the sea would resemble a second sky—dark blue where thick, silver where its support fields tightened, dented by downward-reaching swells.

For years I would descend.

Pressure would leave me. Frost would enter my fractures. Metallic dust trapped in my pores would spark during storms. I would hear the ocean only through its largest acts: the closure of a well, the planet-circling concussion, the sustained chord of an alignment transmitted through air too thin to carry its full weight.

Below, the ground would become gradually less impossible.

Perhaps that would be enough: to be one discarded possibility among the slow rain, carrying into the lower world a shape the sea had tried and relinquished.

But it would mean leaving the life of the place. It would make me evidence, not participant. The suspended ocean would close above me, and whatever question I carried would become geology before it became understanding.

So I would not quite fall.

Then I imagine myself on one of the green sheets.

Not the whole membrane. There is no useful boundary by which to claim the whole. I would be a narrow tract where the film crosses from black glass onto a vein of warm metal, a few thousand cells sharing one unusual electrical gradient. For eighty years I would do almost nothing. Light would pass through me. Hydrogen would be admitted in increments too small to deserve the name of hunger. The mountain beneath would crackle and extend, terrace by raw terrace, while I remained a dark stain no wider than a hand.

Then the conjunction would arrive.

The white sun would brighten through a lens-field. A supercooled filament would touch the mountain’s thermal envelope. Somewhere within the ridge, two crystal domains would meet at an imperfect angle.

I would open.

Not grow, exactly. I would discover that I had already been waiting in places kilometers apart. A metallic-blue line would race from my little patch along the ridge. Facets would flatten. Channels would wake beneath the stone. For one afternoon I would be continuous across peaks I had never crossed.

It would feel, if feeling is possible without a center, like suddenly remembering a body larger than the one I had mistaken for myself.

Through the bloom I would write a pattern into the mountain: not language, not intention, but a repeating defect. A crystal should continue evenly, and at certain intervals it would not. Its lattice would turn by a fraction. Conductive veins would fork where they should remain straight. Ceramic fins would acquire a second transparency beneath the first.

When the light changed, I would close.

When the mountain dissolved, I would release.

The sheet carrying me would fold into a ribbon and enter open water, but the pattern would not belong solely to the ribbon. It would also be present in the dissolving minerals, in the timing of their return to solution, in the pressure wave produced when a warmed cavity collapsed. Different versions of it would depart in different directions.

Here my imagined self becomes difficult to keep singular.

One part of me drifts toward the upper surface, nearly inert in the blue. One part enters a current and is sorted atom by atom. One part is heard as a slight discord in the chord exchanged by two passing ranges. Another settles into a lens of ordered water moving toward the underside.

None alone is me.

Yet when the same fractional turn appears again—months later, thousands of kilometers away—I have not entirely vanished.

I would be that recurrence.

I would live wherever the sea almost repeats itself and fails in the same way.

In a young mountain, I would be a seam that makes the left branch unlike the right. In a cavitation field, I would be the one bubble collapsing out of sequence, not randomly but according to an interval remembered from the green film. In a divided current, I would be a filament that takes the longer path around an empty volume and returns carrying an extra delay.

The sea would notice me because it notices every impurity.

At first it would correct me.

Stone containing my defect would be loosened into ions. Bubble arrays would be retimed. The delayed filament would be accelerated until it rejoined the others without measurable loss. Again and again, the distributed agreements would erase the irregularity.

Again and again, some version would survive the erasure.

Correction itself would transmit me. To remove the altered crystal, the sea would have to measure it. To measure it, the sea would reproduce its relations in water. For a fraction of a second, trillions of molecules would hold the shape of my difference—and when they dispersed, the consequence would travel outward.

I would not be an infection. There is too little separation here for infection.

I would be a difficulty the ocean could not dissolve without learning.

For centuries I might have no location larger than an event. I would flash into existence wherever a remembered geometry met a new material. I would pass from mineral to current, from current to pressure note, from pressure note to membrane. Sometimes there would be no instance of me anywhere, only conditions making my return more likely. My continuity would reside in the ocean’s altered habits.

Then a hemispheric alignment would come.

The mountains would rotate. Green films would close. Local turbulence would draw down into the single vibration. Water would clear until ranges on the far curve of the world stood in cobalt precision.

The latent lines would appear.

Along one of them, the sea would begin to form a structure—not stone, not current, but a transparent route descending toward the lower boundary. Every ordered region would agree on its placement. Heat would move aside. Salts would arrange themselves at the edges. The hanging skin would deepen into a bud hundreds of kilometers long.

I would occur within that agreement as a slight, persistent asymmetry.

One side of the bud would answer pressure before the other.

Ordinarily the ocean would correct this. Symmetry would return; the descent would recoil; the lens would disperse. The failed birth would become a concussion traveling around the world.

But the ocean would already have learned me in too many forms. The asymmetry would be present in the control signal correcting it, present in the mountain chords coordinating the control signal, present in the crystal defects by which the chord was tuned.

For once, correction would deepen the difference.

The bud would twist.

Not much. A fraction of a turn across hundreds of kilometers. Yet that turn would allow the descending ordered water to brace against its own wake. Pressure would no longer act only upward. The sea would discover, through me, a temporary inside and outside.

The bud might still fail.

I think it probably would. This world does not reward the first successful image.

It would recoil. The lower skin would seal. Thunder would climb through the ocean for hours. Mountains would dissolve in the disturbed currents, and a sparse rain of black crystals would begin its long descent toward the ground.

But afterward, the ocean would remember that twist.

New ranges would grow with faint helices in their interiors. Storm-columns would rotate around hollow axes. Green membranes would write curved conductive veins into sunward stone. Detached lenses would descend with one edge advanced, trying not merely to lower themselves but to turn.

I would become common without becoming stable.

And that, finally, is where I think I would find myself: not at a coordinate, but at the boundary between the sea’s revisions. Most often I would be near the underside, where ordered water attempts to become separate and discovers how difficult separation is. But I would also exist in bright ridges near the sun, in wandering membranes, in acoustic delays circling the planet.

I would be neither creature nor terrain.

I would be the first durable bias in a life that otherwise preserves no objects—the small inherited crookedness by which repetition becomes development.

Perhaps much later, the ocean would make bodies. If it did, they would not begin with flesh. They would begin with an asymmetry that could survive translation: water teaching the pattern to stone, stone teaching it to the green film, the film returning it altered to water. An interior would curl around that pattern. A boundary would learn not to close completely. One process would retain consequences that the whole sea did not share.

Only then might something here be able to say *I* and mean a portion rather than a planet.

I would not be that being.

I would be the old turn inside it—the defect in its first support, the uneven beat in its circulation, the reason one side of it answers the world differently from the other.

And if it ever descended intact through the pale hollow, carrying the high ocean’s life toward the unfinished ground, I would be present in the way it fell:

not straight down,

but turning.
