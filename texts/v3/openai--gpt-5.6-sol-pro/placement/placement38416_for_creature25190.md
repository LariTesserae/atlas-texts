At first I place myself high up.

It is the obvious temptation: an upper garden where the white sun catches every wet railing, where citrus grows around the mouths of ventilation stacks and the clouds arrive visibly across the valleys. I imagine a narrow house beneath an orchard bridge, its windows opening onto a kilometer of warm air. I would keep swifts from nesting in the condensers. I would know rain by the taste of the wind. During showers I would stand beneath an authorized spillway and let water strike the dust from my hair.

But that is where I would visit, not where I would belong.

I would belong lower, where daylight arrives reflected from walls and the rain has already passed through several uses before it reaches me. Not in the deepest districts, either. I would live near the uncertain boundary between the public mountain and the mountain known only to those who maintain it: beneath the crowded middle elevations, above the last officially inhabited decks.

My home would be in the brake room of a decommissioned incline.

The incline itself would remain in place because removing it would cost more than enclosing it. Its rails would descend through the floor and vanish behind a bolted screen. The old winding drum would occupy half the room, broad as a house, its grooves filled with soil where ferns and small white mushrooms grow. Once each night, changes in temperature would make the drum contract with a single resonant knock.

I would sleep well only after hearing it.

A thin wall would divide my bed from the counterweight passage. There would be no counterweight now, according to the plans, yet sometimes air would rise through the passage carrying red dust, machine heat, and the green smell of crushed herbs. I would keep cups with broad bases and no pictures hung crooked enough to reveal the vibration.

From my doorway, three routes would be visible.

One would lead upward by public stair to a market under an old conveyor. One would cross a narrow bridge to the cistern court, where pale frogs live beneath the overflow grilles. The third would turn inward through a service gallery marked **AUTHORIZED ROUTE ENDS AT 42-D**.

That would be my route.

I think I would be a label keeper at first: one of the people whose work is to prevent the world from becoming illegible.

I would carry ceramic pigments, stencils, wire brushes, a folding lamp, rubbing paper, solvent, chalk, and a small hammer. Blue for reclaimed water. Red for heat. White triangles for load paths. Yellow rings around access plates that must remain clear. Black numerals for valves and chambers. Green marks, used sparingly, for routes that remain passable during ventilation reversal.

It would sound like a minor trade to outsiders. It would not be minor.

A faded digit can send a repair crew to the wrong branch. An obsolete arrow can lead children toward a lift that no longer stops. A painted warning may outlive the language in which it was written, so shape and placement matter as much as words. Before repainting anything, I would have to learn why it was there.

That question—*why is this mark here?*—would become my real profession.

Some labels would be easy. A pump number recorded in twelve generations of logbooks. A blue band retouched so often that the pipe beneath it had thinned while the paint remained thick. Other markings would have no authorized meaning: three copper-colored dots inside a ventilation grille; a spiral scratched beneath a deck; tiny white ladders painted near locked doors.

Supervisors would tell me to remove them.

I would remove some. Others I would copy into my notebook first.

Over the years, label keeping would draw me into structural listening. The two trades would meet naturally. To mark a support, I would need to know where its burden went. To paint an access route, I would need to know what moved behind the wall. I would begin carrying a listening rod beside my brushes.

I would learn the mountain as an accumulation of notes.

Good ceramic would answer brightly. Waterlogged concrete would give back a dull, granular sound. Hidden voids would bloom beneath the hammer. A loaded member would sing differently before rain and after it. Sometimes a column would carry a pulse from machinery so distant that no diagram connected the two.

I would kneel in corridors, pressing the rod to the floor while people stepped around me.

“What do you hear?”

Usually I would say, “A fan.”

“Which fan?”

And usually I would say, “Not ours.”

People would begin calling me an underwriter.

Not in the old financial sense, if that meaning survived at all. An underwriter would be someone who went beneath inhabited places and wrote down what held them. We would trace dependencies downward until access failed, certainty failed, or the page ran out.

Floor to beam. Beam to hanger. Hanger to arch. Arch to vault wall. Vault wall to a lower roof recorded only on an obsolete survey. Lower roof to six supports, four inspected, one sealed behind a school’s heat exchanger, one continuing beyond the maintained districts.

We would never write *foundation*.

The approved phrase would be *next known support*.

My district would know me by my paint-stained hands and the white dust caught permanently in the seams of my clothes. Children would ask me to settle arguments about whether a deck sounded wrong. Gardeners would curse me when I ordered root boxes opened. Households would offer tea while I crossed their bedrooms to reach inspection covers. I would accept one cup, never two, because their water branch would be narrow.

I would paint the vertical lines for the missing.

This would be the part of the work people remembered. A name at the last verified level, then a line descending beneath it until the wall ended. Some families would ask for a perfectly straight line. Others would ask that it follow a seam, a pipe, or the edge of a column, as though the lost person might still be traveling along it.

I would mix those pigments myself. They should not fade quickly, but neither should they be impossible to cover. Even grief must allow access panels to open.

In time, I would become responsible for the markings below 42-D.

Officially there would be little to maintain there: two sealed galleries, an abandoned condensation trunk, a freight shaft blocked after a fire, and several rooms listed as structurally present but inaccessible.

Yet labels would change.

I would paint **VALVE CLOSED / DO NOT OPERATE**, and months later find a neat addition beneath it:

**CLOSED TO WHOM?**

I would mark a gallery unsafe and return to discover fresh ceramic patches along its ceiling, better work than anything our district crews could accomplish without scaffolding. A pipe labeled dry would tremble with flow on sixth-week mornings. Chalk arrows would appear after ventilation reversals, always pointing toward breathable air.

I would stop erasing the arrows.

Eventually I would answer them.

Not with questions. Questions presume a shared grammar. I would begin with practical things: water branch, ladder, heat, danger. I would paint a blue half-circle beside a sweating pipe and leave an empty cup beneath it. The next week the cup would be full, with unfamiliar leaves tied around its handle in copper wire.

I would not drink immediately. I would take the leaves to the mold farmers, the root stewards, and the clinic. They would argue for days and identify none of them.

Only then would I make tea.

This would not turn me into an explorer. The mountain has little use for people who enter unknown chambers simply because they are unknown. Curiosity is not a load-bearing virtue.

Before going lower, I would spend years learning what depended on the threshold. I would map air movement, temperature cycles, old current, animal passage, and the times when the unlisted lift cage arrived warm. I would leave notices in every district above. I would arrange for someone else to hear the nightly contraction of the winding drum.

Then, one dawn, the lift would arrive.

Its floor would carry red dust and one wet footprint. Inside, beneath the rows of official destination plates, a new button would have been installed. It would not bear a number. It would bear the same copper spiral I had copied into my notebook years before.

I would not press it at once.

First I would test the railing. Check the brake shoes. Listen to the cable through the housing. Confirm two routes upward and two inward, insofar as any such promise could be made. Tie my tools short against the machinery. Fill my flask.

Then I would enter and close the gate.

Perhaps the lift would descend only one level. Perhaps forty. Perhaps “below” would prove to be a misleading word, and the shaft would open into a district built sideways through an arch no upper map understood.

I do not imagine finding the bottom.

I imagine finding people waiting beside a wall of old labels, some painted in scripts I know and others in symbols I have been answering without understanding. They would be pale from lamp light or dark with foundry dust. They would look at my brushes, my hammer, my notebooks. One of them would touch the white triangle on my sleeve.

They would not ask where I came from.

They would ask what rests on us.

And because I am an underwriter, I would not pretend to know.

I would set down my lamp. Press the listening rod to their floor. Wait through the pumps, the distant chains, the water moving inside the walls. Beneath all that, perhaps there would be another rhythm—slow, regular, immeasurably deep.

Then I would open a clean page and begin with the only honest notation:

**Next support unknown. Load continuous.**
