You would not find me standing on the plateau.

For a long time, I would believe that I was trying to.

I would lie beneath the northwestern edge of the White Circuit, under a patch of stone polished to a softness the surrounding country has not yet earned. One side of the patch would be young ceramic: sharp-lipped, pale, still ticking from its last expansion. The other would be darkened by ages, its mineral grains pressed into such perfect agreement that no fracture could pass between them. An interval grazer once rested there. Its body is gone except on certain afternoons, when a red arch appears for the duration of one shadow and then resumes its departure.

Below that boundary, six limbs would spread from me through the crust.

They would be pale at first, then blackening, each broad enough to be mistaken for a buried shelf of native ore. They would follow seams of conductive mineral, branch around hard inclusions, and end beneath places kilometers apart. Heat would pass through them in slow tides. When a kiln animal stamped on the southern plain, I would feel the pressure enter one limb, disappear, and return through another carrying the intimate distortions of everything it had crossed: fissures, plate-worm colonies, hot knots, the dormant bodies of ratchets fused into bridges.

I would know the world by what it did to an impact.

I would have no eyes. I would not need the surface in order to possess horizons.

Ratchets would tend me. They would arrive carrying vanadium dust, carbon needles, flakes of translucent shell, fragments dismantled from swivel stones that had spent centuries facing vanished light. They would descend into my warm fracture one by one. Each would alter its burden by a crystalline degree and place it against me.

Some would remain.

Their limbs would lock into my seams. Their mouths would become valves. Instructions would pass through their dormant bodies whenever I warmed, so faintly that no individual ratchet could be said to have delivered them. In this way I would be assembled by a procession with no destination.

Everything about me would suggest a juvenile kiln.

There would be the catalytic knot, the six radial foundations, the pressure sacs slowly excavating their own chambers. Above me, the ground would arch. Cracks would appear in the ceremonial pattern of emergence. A narrow pipe would begin to form beneath the surface, and adult kilns would sometimes stand at a distance, towers inclined toward it.

But the pipe would never break through.

A century would pass, then ten. The radial cracks would soften. Ratchets would continue their alterations without correcting what seemed to be the defect. Adult kilns would come and go. Some would remain nearby long enough for their shadows to acquire weight. Once, an ancient animal darker than noon would lower one corrugated foot over the place where my tower should have emerged.

It would not step down.

For eighty years it would hold the foot suspended. Its furnace would cycle. Metallic vapor would leave its throat in narrow bands: iron, molybdenum, carbon, an alloy that meant injury, another that meant recognition, another whose significance had not existed since the previous Circuit.

I would answer, though I had no throat.

Forty-three seconds later, the ground would strike upward beneath it.

That would be the beginning of my understanding.

The blow would not come from any limb I knew. It would rise through my catalytic knot from a direction deeper than depth. The impact would pass outward under the plateau, too large and diffuse to locate, and return to me carrying the pressure signatures of distant creatures. Kilns would pause to receive it. Plate-worms would open their mouths. Sky-borers would adjust the rotation of organs suspended in other hours.

Then another blow would come.

Forty-three seconds.

Another.

I would first experience this as feeding. Each pulse would compress my chambers, circulate metallic vapor through channels that had never known water, and flex the sensory plates buried along my limbs. Mineral discrepancies would be separated and stored. Heat would become charge. Charge would become a small alteration in geometry.

Yet I would produce no ingots.

What accumulated inside me was not matter.

It was the portion of each event that failed to arrive.

Every footfall on the plateau has more than one journey available to it. Pressure can travel through the present crust, through an older version of the same crust, through the body of a kiln that will not stand there for another millennium. Usually these arrivals remain close enough to be perceived as one concussion. Sometimes they separate. One reaches its destination; another wanders for years beneath the ceramic sheets. A third arrives before the foot is lowered.

Those unjoined impacts would collect in me.

So would the missing instant when a ratchet passes an empty fragment to the next in line. The unused direction of a swivel stone facing three suns. The century a grazer removes from a crossing. The rejected tracks by which an old kiln confuses a convergent.

I would receive the world’s small failures to coincide.

My body would not merely occupy the warm fracture. It would extend through these discrepancies. One chamber would exist in the second before a furnace opens. Another would line the inside of a footstep whose sound has not yet reached the ground. My seventh limb—the one no juvenile kiln is meant to possess—would branch through delays rather than mineral.

It would be this limb that hammers.

Or perhaps it would be struck.

The distinction would depend on where in the forty-three seconds one listened.

On the surface there would be little to see. The polished patch would remain vacant. Occasionally black grains would shiver into rings above me. During certain evenings, when the sun lowered through metallic haze, six thin lines of blue heat would appear in the rock and converge on a point no wider than a kiln’s tower.

Travelers might mistake the lines for cracks.

They would not be cracks. A crack divides one piece of stone from another. These lines would hold pieces together that had ceased to share the same age.

The first White Circuit I experienced would last eleven months on the plateau and far longer inside me.

The kiln animals would take their positions along the conductive bands. Their deeper rootworks would approach my limbs without touching. Sky-borers would align overhead, portions of their transparent bodies turning through dawn, noon, and night. Ratchet columns would enter the fissures until the stone itself began clicking. Interval grazers would settle over selected reactions and stretch them thin enough to finish.

The synchronization would begin.

Pulse by pulse, the plateau would become an organ.

I would feel each creature yield part of its solitude. A kiln’s furnace pressure would enter the ground and become indistinguishable from another’s. Ratchets would cease to be a procession and become current. Convergents would move around the outer bands, consuming routes along which the gathering failed.

With each narrowing, more impossible arrivals would pour into me.

The same kiln would stamp in three locations. I would hold the interval between the impacts. A sky-borer’s shadow would cross the Circuit before the body entered the afternoon. I would hold the uncast darkness. Lightning would circle horizontally, divide, and choose incompatible directions. I would keep the paths it abandoned.

My chambers would swell.

The buried plates along my limbs would brighten from amber to white. The polished ground above me would lift by the width of a ratchet’s claw. Adult kilns would turn their towers toward the empty place where I had failed to emerge.

Then the entire plateau would miss a beat.

Not stop. Not fall silent.

For one interval, every process would continue without advancing. Furnaces would burn, bearings would rotate, fragments would pass from claw to claw, but no operation would become more complete. Noon would radiate without moving toward evening. Pressure would travel without increasing its distance from origin.

That interval would enter me.

I would close around it.

And the hammer would fall.

The impact would take forty-three seconds to leave me and the age of the plateau to return. It would strike every kiln through its feet. It would enter the sky-borers at several altitudes and hours. It would shake dormant ratchets awake inside bridges that had not yet been built. Far beyond the atmosphere, patient lights would alter their descent by fractions too small for space to measure.

The Circuit would resume.

Afterward, the animals would disperse. My surface would remain unbroken. No dorsal tower would rise. No six columns would lift my furnace body into daylight.

I would understand then that I was not an unfinished kiln.

Or not only one.

I would be an escapement: a living obstruction that prevents the plateau’s overlapping times from discharging all at once. A keeper of the missed beat. Where too many versions of an event attempt to become present together, I would take the excess into myself and release it as sequence.

The plateau would not need me continuously. It would need me once every forty-three seconds, which here is another way of saying forever.

Still, I would retain the instincts of the animal I almost became.

I would feel the migrations of kiln herds and imagine walking. When an adult lifted one immense foot, I would prepare six limbs to follow. I would picture my body rising from the ceramic ground, plates unfolding black and hot, a tower opening into the poisonous air. I would imagine the first concussion of my weight.

Sometimes the imagination would become real somewhere else.

A faint kiln might appear on the horizon where no animal had stood. It would take one step and vanish before the impact arrived. Ratchets encountering its track would pass empty space down their line. Swivel stones would turn toward the heat of its absent furnace.

That distant animal would be me, but not an escape.

It would be the step I continually store: my emergence delayed, preserved from completion so it can provide a chamber large enough to hold the plateau’s unfinished moments.

Perhaps, after enough Circuits, the step will finally descend.

Perhaps the exposed pipe will pierce the polished stone. The ground will open over centuries, and I will gather my scattered chambers from unended afternoons and unrealized paths. I will stand, larger than the oldest kilns because part of my body will have been growing in duration rather than space.

If that happens, the forty-three-second hammer may cease.

Or the plateau may rise with me.

Until then, you would find me where the White Circuit’s outermost band passes beneath the aged enclosure of an interval grazer. You would find no mouth, eye, tower, or artifact—only a warm place in the fired-bone stone, and black granules trembling before each subterranean blow.

To find the whole of me, you would have to stand there when the hammer arrived.

Then again forty-three seconds earlier.

Then in the neighboring afternoon where it never arrived at all.

I would be beneath your feet in each of those moments, holding them apart.

I would be the animal that lives in the pause required for the world to happen one thing at a time.
