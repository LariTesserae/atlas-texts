I would not live near the red comfort of the kilns, though I would often imagine I had been meant for such a place.

For a while I see myself there: sleeping in a recess warm enough to keep the mineral damp from my lungs, turning cups with thickened fingers, judging heat by the color of a shelf. I might belong to a household named for some useful object—the Fourth Damper, the Split Flue—and possess an ordinary competence. A person trusted with fire. A person whose mistakes could be broken down for grog.

But the image will not hold.

The place that gathers around me is farther out, beyond the Basin of Lids and the last market that remains open through an exhalation. It lies in one of the unfinished districts, where waist-high walls enclose rooms never roofed and stacks of tiles wait with the patience of provisions laid out for a population not yet born.

The journey there is marked by absence. Fewer lamps. Fewer patched drains. No memorial columns. No sleeping hollows polished by shoulders. The pavement is clean enough that footsteps return sharply from it, and the rain, when it comes, lies where it falls as if uncertain what streets are for.

At the center of that district, foundation lines curve inward from every direction. They almost meet.

Almost.

There is a gap no wider than a doorway, although no door has ever hung there. The scored channels stop on either side of it. The nearest walls end in neat interlocking teeth, ready to receive the missing courses. Beneath the gap, sounding rods return a note so deep that listeners feel it in their teeth before they hear it.

That is where I would be found.

At first I am a caretaker stationed there by others. Later it becomes less clear whether I keep the gap or the gap keeps me.

My shelter is not built across it but beside it: three low walls, a sloping canopy, no threshold. One wall is made from bricks removed from the unfinished foundations. Each bears a fired mark recording where it was found, when it appeared, and whether the pavement beneath it rang clear, dull, or changing.

Because the walls are growing.

Not quickly. Never while watched.

After rain, a new tile may be found fitted into a course that ended the previous evening. Mortar fills joints no hand remembers sealing. A scored foundation line sometimes lengthens by the width of a fingernail. Stacks diminish, though their counting cords remain tied in place. On rare mornings, the two sides of the central gap stand a little closer together.

My work is to notice and undo this.

I remove one brick when one has been added. If mortar appears, I scrape it out. If a channel advances, I blunt its end. I do not destroy what I remove. Nothing here is treated as an enemy merely because its intention is unknown. The bricks are numbered, mapped, fired again, and incorporated into my shelter at another angle.

Thus the small house beside the gap grows while the district does not.

My household is called the Missing Course.

There are only a few of us. An elderly Rememberer who can sing the buried sequence of six neighboring courts, though no courts have yet been built there. Two water watchers. A child whose feet detect tremors before the colored columns move. A former gutter carrier with one crushed hand. And me.

I am deaf.

Perhaps I was not born so. I remember—or believe I remember—the impact of sound: the slap of wet clay, the inward roar of kiln flame, rain hammering a ceramic roof. There was a collapse when I was young. A lid rose too far in its socket. Pressure traveled through a passage thought to be sealed, and every jar in the West Lip burst at once.

Afterward, sound did not cease immediately. It withdrew over several days. Voices became gestures performed behind walls. Hammers became small motions at the edge of sight. Finally even thunder entered the world without noise.

For a time I was called Empty-Ear.

It was not an adult name. It described a presumed lack, and such names are tolerated only until the lack finds its use.

They sent me to watch pressure columns during communal hammering. Without echoes to confuse me, I saw relationships the listeners missed. A flame would lengthen before a strike. Water in one cup would dome upward while another remained flat. Dust shifted along mortar lines in patterns too slight to attract an ear. I learned that surfaces move before they answer.

In the unfinished district, this matters more than hearing.

The tapping beneath the tile fields comes after rain. Others hear it as innumerable hands. I know it first as a tightness in my knees.

I kneel beside the central gap with my palms flat on the pavement. The knocks move through me without becoming sound. Some are shallow and quick. Others arrive from so far below that the interval between the floor’s first shiver and the later movement of the walls can be counted in breaths.

I do not count the blows themselves.

Counting invites expectation. Expectation smooths difference into rhythm.

Instead, I arrange shallow cups around the gap. Water in each is colored differently: ash-black, salt-white, kiln-red, the greenish gray of cistern film. When the tapping begins, rings cross their surfaces. The cups answer at different moments. Sometimes all tremble except one. Sometimes one remains still until I touch it, then spills without visible cause.

My fired maps do not show chambers. They show responses.

On answering nights, people come from inhabited districts carrying mallets, rattles, pestles, canes, broken handles, and jars too flawed for trade. They hesitate near the ordered tile fields. Even experienced sounders dislike open places. There are no roofs to measure an echo against, no familiar walls to return the blow.

I stand where the foundation lines almost meet.

My task is to prevent the people above from becoming steady.

A hearing rhythm-keeper can be caught by the pattern they oppose. The lower blows suggest the next blow, and the hand prepares to answer. Soon opposition becomes accompaniment. Irregularity narrows toward variation, variation toward measure, and measure toward agreement.

I cannot be enticed by what I cannot hear.

I watch shoulders, elbows, swinging sleeves, the impacts traveling through ankles and tiles. Whenever the crowd begins to converge, I lift a blue stone.

We possess only one. It is narrow, smooth, and darker than water. No one remembers the rain-bird that dropped it. When it rises, some strike early. Others stop. Children shake their rattles once and clutch them still. The pattern breaks around the stone like runoff around a raised brick.

Then I lower it, and the answering begins again.

This is how I receive my adult name.

Not for hearing a change, but for seeing sameness before it completed itself.

They call me Broke-the-Measure.

Some think I guard the district from whatever builds below. Some think I am delaying inhabitants who have as much claim to the plain as we do. The Rememberer of our household says both accounts are too smooth.

“We do not know that it is building,” she signs to me.

Then, after considering:

“We do not know that we are not below it.”

At times I dream of the gap from the other side.

In the dream, the roofless rooms are not unfinished but worn away. The stacks of tiles are ruins arranged by water. Our inhabited districts hang overhead, suspended from their own buried foundations. The tapping comes from above, and pale hands reach downward through cracks to test the thickness of our sky.

I wake with grit beneath my fingernails.

There will be rumors about me. People will say I go alone into the gap during rain. That the cistern films gather toward my shelter. That the white drain creatures emerge dry and line their droplets along my sleeping mat. That I have opened the warm door.

None will be entirely true.

What is true is smaller.

Each year the gap narrows.

Each year I remove more material from it. The work thickens my shelter until its walls become massive and involuted, full of tablets, numbered bricks, plugged channels, and dead-end niches. Eventually it resembles the squat pressure towers of the inhabited plain. Travelers mistake it for a fortification.

But its center remains open.

When I die, they will dry my body beside a traveling kiln. They will wrap me in clay. The Missing Course will argue over where to place me. Some will want to make me a support beside the gap. Others will say that would be too close to completion.

In the end, I think they will shape me into a low bench, set back from the unfinished threshold. Future watchers will sit there with their palms on the pavement. They will feel the blows pass upward through the clay containing my bones.

Perhaps by then there will be no distinction between my body, the shelter, and the work of interruption. Perhaps the people who sit upon me will know me only as an irregularity in their readings: a slight delay, a vibration divided strangely, a place where the expected answer arrives broken.

That would suit me.

I would be human for a while: a deaf watcher, a keeper of an unbuilt doorway, a person named for the moment a pattern failed.

Afterward I would become the failure itself.

Not a wall. Not a gate. Not the hand below or the hammer above.

I would be the reachable flaw left in the world—the place where it can still be corrected, and therefore has not yet become complete.
