I would not live in the center of a crown.

At first I think I might: among the buoyancy chambers, where green light passes through leaf tissue and paints every body with the slow pulse of the pumps. It would be easy to belong there. Warm ducts, nurseries, communal domes assembled each resting cycle from hundreds of linked bodies. The thunder of the trunk would be so close that silence could never enter thought.

But I think the certainty of the crown would become unbearable to me.

I would go outward, along a frond-peninsula narrowing over open water, until the last leaf shelves ended above the plateau’s rim. There the vegetation would still be immense behind me, but ahead there would be only the descending wall and the blue changing gradually into a color the eye could not use.

That is where I would be found: beneath the split crown at the outer escarpment, where one of the rain territories ends in a perfectly vertical boundary overhead.

On one side, drops would strike the surface in continuous silver. On the other, the wave ceiling would remain clear. Far below, roots would trail down the cliff face like pale rivers arrested in falling. Needle whales would pass at intervals so long that their calls would arrive through the stone before their bodies appeared.

I would be one of the plated inhabitants, though perhaps not immediately recognizable as one. I would spend much of my time extended along the underside of the final leaf, four limbs spread into the flow, my plates colored the green-black of crown shadow. One limb would taste the trunk’s distant voltage. Another would hang into the cold upwelling from the abyss. My eye would be turned neither toward light nor darkness, but along the line where they meet.

My people would have a name for me that translates poorly.

Not a historian. Historians preserve what can be held by many bodies.

I would be one-who-meets-the-returning.

The dissenters who swim into the empty intervals would come past me. Before leaving, each would give me one of their names: perhaps a sequence of white rings moving inward across the joints, perhaps three pressure taps followed by a long stillness, perhaps a route described by orienting each limb toward a different trunk.

I would reproduce the name back to them. Only then would they depart.

I could not follow. If I followed, their solitude would have a witness and cease to be the thing they sought. Instead I would remain at the last leaf while they diminished into the blue: first a jointed body, then an intermittent flicker, then a doubt in the water.

Out there they would perform their unwitnessed acts. They might turn against a current that would ultimately return them to the same path. They might display the colors of grief to creatures without eyes. They might hold themselves perfectly still where nothing else provided a landmark, making their own body the sole distinction between here and elsewhere.

The sea would retain none of it.

When they returned, they would stop before me and recount what had happened—not in words, but by performing it again at the border. The second performance would not be the first. Both of us would know this. Yet I would watch with all my apertures open, tasting the minute pressure of every motion.

Then I would give their name back.

This would mean: the one who departed and the one who returned have been held as the same being.

It would also mean: someone knows they were absent.

There would be a cost. My memory could not expand beyond the measure allowed to it. To receive each new solitary gesture, I would have to release something else.

Not immediately. Never carelessly.

I might surrender the exact rust color of a parent’s attention, retaining only that they once attended to me. I might lose the pressure rhythm of a companion’s amusement while preserving the shape their limbs took. Later, the shape might fade too, leaving only a preference for a certain place beneath the rain.

Nothing would vanish all at once. The particular would become relation; relation would become route; route would become an inclination of the body whose origin I no longer knew.

My skin would show this exchange. While receiving a memory, dark vessels beneath my plates would gather into lines like writing. While releasing one, the lines would loosen and spread until they resembled ordinary circulation again. Those watching might see an old name pass wordlessly out of me and into the general color of my body.

Perhaps that is what I would fear—not death, but becoming unable to tell whether a tenderness was mine or simply one of the established currents of the world.

So I would develop small disciplines of distinction.

I would greet each returning swimmer differently, though no greeting could remain unprecedented. I would choose which limb became my front only at the instant of encounter. I would look for variations too slight to enter a formal history: a delay before recognition, a color begun and suppressed, a tremor caused not by cold but by the memory of empty water.

I would not record these things. I would be the brief condition in which they were noticed.

Others might think my role unnecessary. Every swimmer returns whether I wait or not. The predetermined paths close behind them. Their bodies rejoin the crowns, the nurseries, the recitations. No absence alters the population; no return repairs it.

But those who journey outward would understand.

In a world where nothing can be removed, absence must be made by attention. In a world where nothing can be added, return must also be made by attention.

Sometimes one would fail to return—or appear to fail, because their death occurred along another fixed reach of the ecology. I would remain at the leaf edge displaying their name into the open water. Eventually an infant would be born elsewhere from matter passed through root and trunk, but I would not call the infant the traveler restored. Equilibrium might reuse every substance and position. I would insist that identity was not substance or position.

For as long as I could remember the distinction, I would hold it.

Then one day I would release it, because another swimmer would be waiting to entrust me with a name.

I do not think I would be solitary. Several of us would occupy the rim, kilometers apart, each beneath a different terminal leaf. We would seldom gather. From a distance we might glimpse one another’s pigments flashing across the blue, too far away to read precisely. We would know only that someone else was keeping watch at another boundary.

When my own limbs weakened, another would take my place before I left it. I would teach them no procedure that the world did not already contain. I would simply face the abyss, then face them, then face the abyss again.

They would understand the posture.

After death, my body would be carried to a mineral belt on the nearest trunk. Pale scavengers would open the seams of my plates. Iron would return to valves and crustacean armor. Carbon would enter the black fibers. The sea would become exactly as full as before.

But for a while, someone at the rim would greet returning swimmers in a pattern that had first been mine.

Later it would no longer be remembered as mine. It would be merely the way one welcomes a being back from unwitnessed water.

That is where I would find myself: at the final green edge above the drowned mountain wall, with the whole apparatus sounding behind me and the unmarked sea before me.

And that is what I would be—not a keeper of permanent history, but a keeper of the interval in which one life is still distinguishable from all the lives that could replace it.
