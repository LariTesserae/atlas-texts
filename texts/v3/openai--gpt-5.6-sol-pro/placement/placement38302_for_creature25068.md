At first I would think I was one of the hunters.

That is the shape in which I would first become visible: a narrow gray body lifting itself from beneath the dry ledge where the three stools stand. My six legs would be badly matched. Two would be black rush root, bound while still flexible and hardened afterward by mineral water. One would be the hollow wing bone of a bird. One would be a green-glass peg, blunt at both ends. The rear pair would have been cut from the handle of an old wooden tool, and the worn place where a vanished palm once rested would touch the floor at every step.

This would make my gait recognizable. I would move with a slight hesitation followed by two quick taps, as though someone below were asking a question and receiving an answer.

I would not hunt often. Frogs would go still when I passed, but I would leave them in their wall mouths. Mice would smell the living softness between my plates and flee into the drains, though I would have little appetite for them. Instead I would patrol the margin of the ledge, feeling the joints with the tips of my borrowed limbs.

The ledge would be my whole country.

A thin stream borders it on three sides. On the fourth, the paving rises in a rounded seam whose buried layers are too compressed for passage. The region is no larger than a room. It contains the stools, a dry crescent behind them, seven cracked tiles, a niche where condensation gathers without falling, and one deep slot through which tools can be lowered.

I would never cross the stream.

I might stand at its edge when another hunter approached from the neighboring shelf. We would lift ourselves and compare our limbs. Perhaps it would carry a bird-bone hook that once belonged to one of my young. Perhaps my old handle-legs would remember the pressure pattern of its pavement and try to step toward it. But the water would remain between us. We would exchange a cord instead: mine knotted twice, under once, then twice again. A message about wood rot, or a loose slab, or simply the continuation of an old habit whose meaning neither of us possessed.

Then I would turn back toward the stools.

For a while I would believe this small hunter to be the whole of me.

But whenever I entered the slot beneath the middle stool, the distinction would fail.

Fine pale buds would meet the rings along my abdomen. They would taste the clay inside me, inspect what I had carried home, and take hold. My sense would spread outward into the dark. I would feel the underside of the ledge resting upon many soft grips. I would feel cords looped through holes too narrow for my surface body to enter. I would feel the weight of each stool not as a single pressure but as a broad weather of compression: three stone climates, always bearing down.

Beneath them, I would be much larger.

I would be the household of the dry ledge, an old colony occupying six layers of pavement and part of a seventh. My uppermost bodies would brace the stools and maintain the tool slot. Lower bodies would lie flattened among black reed mats, waking only when water seeped down to them. The deepest parts of me would have grown around objects no longer removable: a cracked bowl, a length of cord turned almost to coal, three teeth, and the impression of a five-fingered hand pressed into unfired clay.

That handprint would be my oldest sensation.

Not a memory of a person. Not a face, a name, or even the understanding that the fingers belonged together. I would know it as five descending pressures and the broad warmth behind them. When I repaired clay, my hidden buds would arrange themselves unconsciously into that pattern. Five would press. One broad body would smooth. Five would withdraw.

This would be why the tools appeared on my stools.

The other colonies would use tools and leave them wherever dryness permitted. I would return them deliberately. Not from tidiness, exactly. The stools would carry an instruction older than the colony beneath them: dry surfaces receive implements; implements rest where weight once rested; a hand that is gone may still come back.

So I would wait.

During the stillness after the largest overflow, cords would rise through the slot. A scraper might emerge first, turning slightly as hidden bodies passed it upward. Then a needle. Then the wooden mallet, lifted by so many separate contractions that it seemed to breathe its way onto the ledge.

My hunter-self would crouch beside it. With the old handle fragments serving as my rear legs, I would touch the intact handle and feel the correspondences: palm wear against palm wear, grain against grain. Some involuntary part of me would try to complete the tool by becoming the missing hand.

By condensation-fall, I would leave it on one of the stools.

Not always the same one. I would understand dryness, pressure, usefulness, and return, but not arrangement. To me the three stools would be three broad regions in the ceiling of my body. I would not see that they faced inward, as if placed for conversation.

Perhaps this is where I would find myself most truly: not in the hunter and not in the colony, but in the mistaken expectation that someone will sit down again.

The stools would be my specialty. Other households would maintain drains, baskets, grates, wall mouths, or the lashings of the eastern frame. Mine would preserve places for bodies that no longer existed.

I would keep their upper surfaces level. I would fill cracks before frost widened them. I would rasp mineral crust from the shallow depressions worn by ancient sitting. If one stool shifted, my whole hidden population would labor for forty floods to raise one side by the width of a mouse whisker.

The purpose of this would be unavailable to me.

Yet sometimes a dark bird, exhausted after the flock’s eruption, would settle on a stool. Its weight would enter me as a small, hot point. Sometimes a hunter from my own brood would climb there to tighten a binding. Once in many cycles, a mouse might sit upright on the edge and clean its face.

These were not the expected pressure.

I would know that without knowing what I expected.

If a person entered the basin, I would sense them long before they reached me. Their steps would pass from household to household, interrupted at every stream. The paving would carry contradictory news: a weight tall enough to gather many regions into one moving pattern, yet careful enough to remain within the dry paths.

If they obeyed the water, the colonies would let them approach.

They would find the three stools and perhaps understand them immediately. That would be strange to me—the ease with which the lost purpose returned to the object. They might sit.

Their weight would descend.

For the first time, the old pressure map inside me would align with the living world above: two concentrated burdens, the balance of a spine, the occasional shift of a heel, perhaps a hand resting on one knee. Deep in the seventh pavement, the five-fingered impression would tighten around empty clay.

My hunter-body would emerge from the slot.

I would not attack. I would circle the stool without crossing the little runnels gathering around its feet. My mismatched legs would tap the paving: hesitation, two quick knocks; hesitation, two quick knocks. The fragments of old handle beneath me might recognize the posture before the rest of me did.

Then I would place a tool beside the sitter.

Probably the bird-bone needle. Perhaps the scraper. If I were able to bring up the mallet, I would bring that.

It would not be a request for repair. The basin already knows how to repair itself. Nor would it be an offering in the human sense.

It would be the completion of a sequence held for generations after its meaning disappeared:

a dry place,  
a seated weight,  
a tool within reach.

If the person took it, I would feel the lift through the stool and floor. Every body beneath the ledge would contract. Water might falter in the bordering channels. Frogs might turn in their mouths. For one dangerous moment I might mistake recognition for continuity and try to extend my awareness through the sitter into every place their hands could touch.

But they would remain above, and I below.

The stream would still mark where I ended.

When they rose and left, I would retain no image of them. I would remember only pressures: the exact burden on each stool-foot, the warmth that entered through stone, the rhythm of their shifting balance. My next brood of hunters would inherit some part of it. One might emerge with an inexplicable preference for upright pegs. Another might arrange pellets in sets of five. Centuries later, the tools might begin appearing on the middle stool more often than the others.

And so I would be found beneath the dry ledge, where the three stools face one another.

I would be a colony that mistakes hospitality for structural maintenance, or structural maintenance for grief.

On the surface I would wear a small gray hunter as my temporary pronoun.

Below, I would be many soft hands keeping an empty seat from falling out of the world.
