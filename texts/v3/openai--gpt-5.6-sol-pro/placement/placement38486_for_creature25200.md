At first I would mistake myself for something in the ceiling.

The noise would make that seem inevitable. I would feel the black migrations pass before I understood that I was beneath them: a pressure descending through stone, roof slabs, and the long bones of my face. I would lift my head when the colony loosened. Dust would tremble from the arches. Wings would comb the air until every direction became movement.

But I would not rise with them.

Nor would I belong to the walls, though I would hear the rodents speaking inside them. Their alarms would travel through my feet as quick, dry knocks. I would know when a cat crossed a roof without seeing it. I would know when a scavenger worked below a courtyard by the patient rasp of claws under paving. The city would reach me through the ground more clearly than through my eyes.

Only gradually would my body become apparent: four narrow legs; a hard, careful mouth; ears so long and independently restless that stillness would never mean inattention. A coat whitened by the dust of many districts. Dark skin showing around the nostrils and eyes. Ribs visible, but not from present hunger—from the lifelong arithmetic of carrying no flesh that could not justify its thirst.

I would be an old female among the grazers.

Not the largest. Not a ruler. The herd would not obey me in any way the vanished builders would have recognized. The younger animals might pass me on open ground. A nervous calf might ignore the turn of my ear. A strong male might enter a courtyard before I had tested the air.

Yet when the streets divided, they would wait.

I would be found on the middle terraces, where a broad avenue bends around a collapsed district and appears to descend toward the salt plain. The obvious road would be wrong. It would lead through beautiful ruins—a roof grove, visible leaves, sheltering walls—and end in a depression where dry crust conceals a drop into old cellars. Perhaps the way was safe generations ago. Perhaps one of my mother’s mothers watched half a herd break through there.

I would turn aside before the bend.

My route would pass through a narrow doorway polished by flanks, across three rooms whose interior walls had fallen into low ridges, then beneath the horizontal trunk of a tree. Calves would have to crouch. The oldest among us would know not to brush the bark: pale crickets shelter there, and their sudden metallic clatter can make the young bolt.

Beyond the tree would be no visible forage, only a blank slope of powder. Still, I would climb it.

Under a buried colonnade, where the deep draft cannot easily reach, wiry grass grows from cracks between slabs. It is poor grass. There would be enough for only a few mouthfuls each, and we would take them one blade at a time. Farther in, roots have lifted a paving stone, leaving a hollow where edible fern crowns persist in darkness. I would remember which stone, though dust had made them all alike.

My knowledge would not feel like knowledge. There would be no city in my mind, no map viewed from above. The basin would exist as sequences held in muscle and scent:

after the bitter lichen wall, keep the draft against the left flank;

after the two blind arches, do not follow the bats;

where the paving becomes cool, walk close to the pillars;

when salt dust coats the tongue, turn uphill before the lone tower;

if the crickets stop all at once, stand still.

I would carry years as distances.

One circuit of the grazing grounds would have taken much of my youth. Another would have passed while my first calf walked behind me. There would be courtyards I remembered as green that now held only dead branches preserving the shape of life. There would be bare rooms into which a root had entered since my last passage. I would pause at unfamiliar leaves, not because I knew what they were but because the city had changed its sentence and I needed to read it again.

My ears would be torn at the edges. One hoof would place itself less cleanly than the others. Beneath the dust on my shoulder would be parallel scars from a cat that had misjudged my age and found I could still kick.

The herd would know these things. Predators would know them too.

I would give birth rarely. By the time I was old, perhaps only one daughter would remain near me. She would not be tender. Tenderness costs attention, and attention must be spent on shafts, cracks, leaves, wind, and the changing pressure before a ceiling flight. But she would watch where I stepped. When I stopped at an empty doorway, she would stop behind me. When I smelled a stone, she would smell it after I moved.

That would be how the route passed between us: not as instruction, but as repetition made bodily.

In the cold hours we would shelter in a hall with squat pillars and a ceiling stained white by ancient colonies. The herd would gather flank to flank. Beetles would arrive at our hooves. Small birds would descend to pick at the folds inside our ears. Rodents might steal loosened hair. I would tolerate all of them until a touch became a bite.

Sleep would come in fragments. One ear would remain turned toward the entrance.

Sometimes, waking, I might stand beneath a diluted shaft of light. It would reveal no true color, only differences of substance: leaf, salt, hide, stone. Dust would move through the beam in such abundance that it might look like water to a creature with inherited dreams of rain.

I would have no such dream.

I would dream of passages. Of a courtyard entered too early, before its leaves opened. Of my mother’s hind legs vanishing through a low arch. Of the smell beneath a particular slab. Of standing as a calf inside the thunder of wings and believing the entire ceiling was falling.

Near the end of my life, I would begin to lose the circuit.

Not all at once. First I would hesitate at a junction I had crossed for decades. Then I would lead the herd to a grove whose leaves had not recovered. My daughter would turn away before I did. The others would follow her.

There would be no challenge and no ceremony. The route would simply move out of my body and continue in hers.

I might leave the herd then, or fail to rise after resting. I imagine my last place as a small courtyard above the lowest terraces, far from any major shaft. A tree limb would have entered through one window and emerged through another, enclosing the room without filling it. The ground would be warm from bodies that had recently slept there. The walls would soften the endless noise into something almost distant.

But nothing in the basin would allow me to remain myself for long.

A scavenger would find me. Beetles would enter at the eyes and mouth. Rodents would take hair, then gnaw the bones for salt. A cat might drink what moisture remained before the deeper creatures arrived. Dung from those creatures would be buried beside the root. Fungal threads would open it. The tree would take what it could.

Years later, one of my descendants might browse a leaf grown partly from that exchange.

She would stand in the doorway, listening. Somewhere inside the walls, rodents would drum. Overhead, the black rivers would begin to loosen from the vault. She would turn away from the easy descending avenue and choose instead the buried colonnade, the poor grass, the safe ascent.

She would not remember me.

She would remember the way.

So that is where I would be found: not at the center of the city, nor nearest the light, but walking the long spaces between its scattered islands of life.

And that is what I would be—an old, dust-colored animal in whom a small portion of the basin’s memory had, for a while, acquired legs.
