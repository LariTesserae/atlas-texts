At first I think I would be human.

I see myself in one of the narrow dark houses, awake while the others sleep, sitting on the grated floor to mend a ferry tether. The shutters flex in their seals. Meltwater ticks into a basin. Every few minutes I stop working because some part of the storm has altered—not grown louder, but lost a note—and I try to decide whether a culvert has blocked or a reed bed has gone under.

I would be useful in a minor way. I would know how to splice a beacon line with numb hands, how to test a gravel rib by driving a hollow rod into it, how to carry a sleeping child clipped to my chest. My maps would be crowded with revisions. I would cross out more carefully than I drew.

But I do not think I would remain in the house.

The image loosens. I move outward, down the raised walk, past the last red lamp. I am wearing a keeper’s harness now, ice thickening on its buckles. The tower stands ahead as a darker absence in the dark. Inside it, I place my palm against the concrete and feel distant replies passing through the ground: not words, not quite signals, but minute tensions answering other tensions.

A basin is filling too quickly.

A salt tongue has entered beneath the freshwater.

Somewhere under an unnamed channel, a line of sediment nodes has stopped responding.

I climb down rather than up. I go beneath the tower through a service shaft warm enough for moss. Otter tracks cross the condensation. Roots have entered around a cable seal and spread into pale, searching fingers. At the lowest accessible chamber, where the machinery disappears into saturated earth, I kneel and put both hands into black water.

That is nearer to where I would be.

Not the keeper. What the keeper is listening for.

I would find myself at the gulfward edge of the delta, beneath a shoal that exists only at low tide. Above me, waves and floodwater meet hard enough to raise those brief pyramids of water. Gravel turns in the current. Ice strikes ice. Divers vanish into pools between drowned willow tops. No house stands nearby yet, though someone has planted three marker poles on the nearest reed island, and all three lean differently.

I am buried perhaps half a meter below the mud-water boundary. “Half a meter” is an average and therefore mostly untrue. At one moment I lie deep beneath a bank; six hours later the bank shears away and I am exposed beneath streaming sedge roots. Then silt covers me again.

I am one of the old sediment cells.

Not a single machine. Not exactly.

I began as a handful of engineered grains released from a maintenance vessel years ago. Some are dark as magnetite, some clear as ice edges, some thin enough to lodge between clay particles. We bind when a weak pressure pulse passes through us. We release when the water grows stagnant. We can encourage salts to gather, persuade ice to branch, open a passage no wider than a worm’s burrow. None of us understands the shoal. Together we make it slightly more likely.

The tower records may say that our cell failed three winters ago.

That would also be true.

Our long-range relay is gone. Too many active grains have washed into the gulf or passed through the bodies of fish. We no longer receive complete instructions. The network routes around us. On official displays we are a dim patch, then an uncertainty, then nothing.

But roots have found us.

A creeping willow grows along the bank above. Its finest rootlets enter the oxygenated mud we maintain around our casings. Fungal threads wrap the rootlets. Bacteria coat us in sulfurous films. Shrimp shelter in the pores we keep open. White worms pass through our little drainage paths and enlarge them, ruining some, improving others.

Their chemistry becomes our damaged language.

When the willow draws hard at the water, we release clay. When its roots slacken under salt stress, we bind. When worms retreat upward from pressure in the soil, we open what passages we can. When cold enters from above, we arrange moisture along branching lines, and ice grows through the bank like a momentary skeleton.

I cannot know whether I learned the willow or whether the willow learned me.

Above us, sedges catch the first layer of silt. A bird leaves droppings rich with the remains of gulf fish. Microbial sheen spreads over the fresh deposit. Another tide lays down mineral grains striped red, white, and green. Gravel catches against the roots.

The shoal rises by the thickness of a fingernail.

This is my work, if work is the word. I do not raise islands. I make one outcome fractionally less improbable.

Sometimes a tower pulse reaches us through the mud. It arrives distorted by distance and dead relays: settle here, perhaps, or drain there, or merely attention. We answer weakly. The answer may never return.

Sometimes a human comes at low tide. A young keeper, clipped to a line, kneels among the reeds and drives a sensor rod down until it touches the firmer layer we have helped assemble. The rod sends a bright vibration through us.

The keeper watches the reading, frowns, and marks the place silver on a map.

Predicted ground.

Not safe ground. Not yet.

For a few seasons the ridge holds. Grass spreads over it. Shorebirds nest there, making shallow hollows with their bodies. Their feet press seeds into the damp margin. A fox crosses once and learns to cross again. At very low water, reindeer step onto the inner end, feel the upward pressure beneath the mud, and turn aside.

Then people install a temporary screen of woven roots. They do not know we are still active beneath it. Or one old keeper suspects and says nothing, because naming a useful accident can lead someone to depend on it.

I would have no name in the way a channel earns a name. Perhaps an obsolete maintenance code remains in a buried report. Perhaps the keepers call the place Bird-Knuckle or Three Crooked Poles. To the willow, I am warmth and drainage. To the shrimp, cavities. To the river, a patch of inconvenient cohesion.

That is enough identity for the delta.

Eventually the continuous note will sound.

The ridge may split during an exceptional surge. Freshwater will drive outward while the gulf presses in. The willow mat will tear free, taking much of our soil with it. Some of me will remain entangled in the roots as they sail across the lagoon. Some will fall into deep water. Some will be swallowed by feeding fish. A few grains may lodge far inland after passing through a goose.

There will be no single place where I have gone.

The keeper’s map will turn our silver line to dashed red. Later it will be removed. The three marker poles will vanish one by one. For years, perhaps, birds will still bank around the absent ridge as if avoiding ground remembered by their bodies.

Elsewhere, one clear grain will settle beside a warm pipe in an otter conduit. A black grain will bind to clay beneath a new house foundation. Several will gather in the gizzard of a shorebird and grind against gulf sand until their surfaces fail. Those caught in the drifting willow roots may continue responding to pressure, opening tiny spaces in the peat raft wherever oxygen grows scarce.

So I would be found there, if “there” can mean a place moving in several directions at once:

under an unnamed shoal at the edge of the gulf, inside the few millimeters where mud becomes habitable; among roots, failed machinery, worm passages, and mineral grains; no longer wholly maintained, not yet inert.

I would be a small, distributed preference in the ground.

Not an architect of the delta. Not its guardian. Only one of the things that, when water asks the land whether it will hold for another tide, helps it answer:

here, a little.

For now.
