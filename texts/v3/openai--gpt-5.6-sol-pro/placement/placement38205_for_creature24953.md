At first I think I would live at the edge.

Not beyond the town, exactly, but where it begins to loosen: past the last continuous galleries, where the narrow lanes become plank walks and the drainage cuts broaden enough for a skiff. I imagine a room above a gatehouse there, with one wall of old brick and three of slatted timber. Beneath it, two channels meet. All night the floor would register their disagreement—a faint tremor when the outer marsh pressed inward, another when the town discharged rain.

From the window I would see drying racks, low roofs, the black-green chimney behind them, and beyond those the marsh with no visible border. Things leaving town would pass under me: dye water after settling, husks for the outer compost fields, barges loaded with pressed fiber, the dead on their way to the ridge. Things entering would pass there too: clean rain moving through the reed beds, timber floated in bundles, returning workers with dry-city shoes tied over their shoulders.

But I do not think I would be a gatekeeper. A gate implies a distinction the place does not possess.

Perhaps I would be a channel warden. The idea comes easily: waking before the first bell, taking a hooked pole and a basket, learning to see depth in darkness. I would read the water by what it carried. A turning pepper leaf would show an eddy forming beneath a bridge. A straight procession of husks would mean the grate was clear. Pale foam holding against one wall would tell me that a workshop upstream had opened a valve too early.

I like the authority of that knowledge, though not authority in the ordinary sense. No uniform, no office. Only the right to enter a yard because water has already entered it; the right to cut through someone’s treasured vine because a culvert is choking underneath. People would curse while making room. Later they would send food.

Yet I suspect I would be too often distracted from clearing the channel by wanting to know why it had changed.

I would notice that the flow began sounding different after a fig root lifted the lane. I would want the old plans, then discover the old plans showed a wall where there had been a passage for thirty years. I would ask who remembered the passage being cut. Someone would send me to an oil presser with blue fingers, who would send me to a deaf retired mechanic, who would point to a seam in the brick and sign: *before the white fungus, after the turbine fire*. By noon I would have learned six contradictory versions and cleared nothing.

So perhaps I would become a mechanic instead.

Not one of the brilliant old ones who can hear a bearing fail through two walls and a rainstorm. I would begin as the apprentice who carries tools securely enough not to lose them. My first skill would be tying everything to myself. My second would be standing where instructed. For years, when a master paused beside a machine and tilted her head, I would hear only the whole vast noise: belts, valves, rain, wheels, water under planks. Then one evening I would hear within it a small dry click that had not been there the day before.

That would be the beginning.

I would come to understand that no machine in town is singular. A press is also the shaft that drives it, the tree that braces the shaft house, the gutter that keeps rain from the east bearing, the channel that turns the turbine, the worker who knows the valve must be struck upward in cool weather but sideways in heat. Repairing the press without repairing its circumstances would only move the failure elsewhere.

Still, I do not think the machines would keep me. I would be the person kneeling beside an opened housing while looking past it at the root entering the foundation. Mechanics would find this irritating. Tree workers would find my concern with bolt tension equally irritating. Channel wardens would say I asked questions while water rose.

Eventually the town would invent a use for this defect.

My room would be neither at the edge nor at the center, but above the central square, over the passage between the market and the chimney yard. It would be reached by an exterior stair whose bottom three steps flooded in the wet season. The lintel would be low. Inside would be shelves fixed above shoulder height, and on them would stand narrow ledgers compressed between waxed boards.

The room would be called the Survey Office on documents sent outside. No one in town would call it that. They might call it the Margins, because the useful parts of its records would all be written around the original entries.

I would be the margins clerk.

The title would sound minor because it would be minor. I would own no channel, tree, wall, shaft, or roof. I could not order a mill closed or condemn a bridge. My work would begin where everyone else’s certainty ended.

When a gallery shifted, I would record not merely how far, but after which rains, under what load, and whether the supporting tree had fruited heavily that season. When a pump was replaced, I would preserve the old mechanic’s note that the western intake sucked air whenever petals covered the second screen. I would draw maps that admitted they were temporary. Their lines would be made in different inks: water in blue-gray, power in rust red, roots in green, remembered former courses in dotted black. Where accounts disagreed, I would not choose too quickly. I would leave both and mark the disagreement.

Most days I would be outside.

I would carry a wax tablet, chalk, a folding rule, waxed string, two pencils in stoppered sleeves, and a little wooden listening rod for setting against pipes and housings. Everything would have a cord. My spectacles would be tied behind my head. One trouser leg would always be stained darker from brushing wet walls.

People would call me for disputes that were not yet emergencies.

A roof gardener would say a workshop’s new exhaust was yellowing her taro. A mill owner would insist the vine on his south wall belonged to the neighboring house because its roots began there. Two families would argue over the cost of replacing a gallery that one used as an entrance and the other used to reach its flood shelf. I would arrive with old records and discover that the relevant page had swollen shut years ago, or that the agreement referred to a tree no one could identify because three descendants now grew from its stump.

Then I would listen.

Not impartially—no one who lives downstream is impartial about water—but patiently enough for the structure of the problem to emerge. Here, a person’s claim would never concern only what they possessed. It would concern what held them up, crossed beneath them, shaded them, drained through them, or depended on them remaining careful.

I would become skilled at drawing these dependencies.

Not a picture of a building, but a picture of what would happen if one part failed.

If this gutter blocked, water would enter that wall. If that wall softened, the belt mount would shift. If the mount shifted, the press would vibrate. If the press stopped, waste husks would not reach the furnace. If the furnace cooled, steam would fall at the retting sheds. If the sheds delayed, forty bundles would sour before the next dry interval.

My diagrams would resemble root systems or leaf veins. Outsiders would look at them and ask where the property boundaries were. I would show them, but the boundaries would seem the least convincing lines on the page.

Children would sometimes be sent to me from the school. They would copy rainfall figures or trace old channel routes. I would give them errands designed to make them look closely: find the highest rust mark on the north bridge; count how many roofs feed the ceramic cistern; ask three elders what stood in the square before the indigo market. They would return with wet feet and incompatible answers.

I would teach them that incompatibility was not always error. One person remembers the year of the flood; another remembers the repairs after it. A map made during low water tells a different truth from one made during rain.

My particular companion might be one of the upper-floor cats, long-bodied and gray, sent originally to deal with mice among the ledgers. It would sleep on the driest shelf and dislike me for moving it. Its name would change by household. I would call it whatever the last visitor called it, and eventually no one would know whether I had failed to name it or given it twenty names.

I would eat irregularly: a steamed cake carried in paper leaf, pickled stems from a jar, market broth so sour it tightened the jaw. On good days someone settling an old dispute would leave a small bottle of oil on my sill. I would warm a drop on my wrist and know which quarter it came from by the smell of the vats.

At the annual stopping of the shafts, I would discover how exhausted I was.

The silence would make my room feel exposed. Without the belts and pumps, I would hear pages ticking as they dried, beetles working in an old roof post, water moving under the square. People would come asking for records because maintenance uncovered forgotten things: a sealed pipe, an iron strap swallowed by bark, a maker’s plate on a motor whose manufacturer no longer existed.

I would go from crew to crew, adding notes while the town lay mechanically still.

Then the shafts would engage.

The first belt would tighten, the floor would take up its familiar vibration, and the records on the shelves would tremble in their boards. I would feel relief—not because the town had returned to what it was, but because it had survived another interruption by becoming slightly different.

In time, my hearing would go.

I would notice first that people seemed to mumble in the market. Then the high whine of small motors would disappear. I would still feel the pumps through my feet and the broad changes in machinery through walls. I would learn to watch faces more closely. Factory signs would enter my speech until I could conduct an entire survey without raising my voice: *upstream, wait, dangerous, again, show me, finished.*

Younger clerks would come with better instruments. Moisture meters. Electrical testers sealed against damp. Survey devices that gave exact measurements without string or chalk. I would not oppose them. Accuracy is useful. But I would make them write beside each measurement who took it, in what weather, and what the machinery was doing at the time.

They would tease me for this until an exact number proved misleading.

Then they would add the weather.

I might never become important enough for a tree to bear my name or a pump housing to be cast in my memory. More likely, I would persist as handwriting.

Years after my death, someone opening a ledger would find a note beside the plan of the eastern gallery:

*Do not straighten this wall. The lean leaves room for the fig in the dry season.*

Or:

*Water seen here only when the south mill and dye house discharge together. If still, clear beneath third stone, not first.*

Or merely:

*Old Mara said there was once another culvert. She was usually right about buried things.*

That is where I would find myself: not at the town’s edge, nor quite in its center, but in the changing margin between what the place remembers and what it is about to forget.

And that is what I would be—not its historian, because history suggests something finished; not its planner, because the town refuses to remain planned.

I would be one of its annotations.

A small, revisable human mark beside the larger text of water, iron, root, and work.
