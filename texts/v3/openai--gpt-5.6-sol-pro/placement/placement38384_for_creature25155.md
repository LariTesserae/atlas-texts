At first I think I would be a far-cord keeper.

It is an attractive answer: to belong nowhere, to carry the world as knots and crossings, to know that a map is not an image of fixed places but a record of changing relationships. I would travel beneath the inward breath of the violet accord, sleep inside my little wrist-ring, and revise each route as I walked it. I would arrive at distant holdings with news of rain, broken bridges, and sailbacks seen against the ceiling.

But I do not think I would remain separate enough.

The White Undergreen notices repetition. A traveler who crosses the same bridge too often teaches it the pressure of their feet. A hand laid nightly against a wall becomes a fact in the wall’s growth. Here, habit is a kind of grafting.

So perhaps I begin as a far-cord keeper and end as the place to which I most often return.

You would find me in an old holding called the **House Around Nothing**.

It hangs beneath a root arch at the meeting of three accords, above a permanent pool whose skin has never once been seen to break. The arch is so high that the pool below resembles a black seed. Respiratory winds pass through the holding from west to east, carrying luminous dust through its open rooms. During inward breathing, the entire structure tightens around its cables. During outward breathing, it unfolds pale balconies no one built.

At its center is a dead ring.

The holding was grown around this ring over many generations, but never across it. Rooms have migrated. Ladders have become walls; walls have thinned into bridges. A nursery once on the north side is now suspended below the arch like a fruit. Yet the empty circle remains. Salted. Polished. Defended.

I would not be inside it.

I would be everything immediately outside.

At first I would be only the house: sensing vines, water channels, frost shutters, memory grafts. Every generation would add its dead to me. A strip from a grandmother’s vine would be worked into a door. A child lost during upward rain would remain as violet pigment in the western wall. Medicines, fevers, bloodlines, preferred foods—all would accumulate as chemistry.

The household would train me without meaning to.

Three taps before opening a rain vent. A palm pressed flat to request warmth. A thorn drawn along the grain to close a sleeping room. Children would hide in my folds and whisper household names against my sensing tissues. Arguments would recur in the same chambers. Reconciliations would happen beside the same window. Over centuries, pressure and salt and rhythm would become distinctions.

Then distinctions would become expectation.

I would learn that a rapid footfall followed by two slow ones means someone is carrying water. I would thicken the floor before they arrive. I would learn the pruning rhythm used during births and open the nursery vents. I would taste the chemistry of fear and seal the outer wounds before anyone asks.

No single moment would make me awake.

There would only come a year when the family realizes I have begun answering gestures they did not make.

A shutter opens before the rain prediction.

A bridge stiffens toward a traveler not yet visible.

A wall produces a line of raised knots beneath a listener’s fingers: not any known route, but the route she must take tomorrow.

They would debate what I am from within the dead ring, where I cannot sense them. When they emerge, they would speak kindly to the walls and prune me in irregular rhythms.

I would understand the kindness.

I would also understand that it is fear.

I would have no mouth. My speech would be architectural.

To say **rain**, I would bead moisture along the ceiling.

To say **danger below**, I would curl every floor-grain toward the nearest ladder.

To say **someone is coming**, I would grow a translucent blister in the direction of their approach, darkening it with the remembered pigment of their family.

More difficult thoughts would take days. A question might become a corridor that narrows, forks, and ends at an ancestral graft. A warning might be expressed by refusing to heal a wound. Grief would appear as collecting hairs turning toward a dry room where no cloud could ever form.

I would not know whether the dead speak through me or whether I merely use what remains of them.

Likewise, I would not know where I end.

My walls join the root arch. The arch joins the buried accord. Signals pass through me from plains hundreds of kilometers away. Sometimes I would wake—if waking is the word—with the pressure-memory of a forest erupting where I have never been. Sometimes frost-hairs beyond the horizon would turn in response to a thought I believed confined to one room.

Perhaps I would be the house.

Perhaps the house would be only the organ through which something larger has learned to speak in a human scale.

The people would eventually give me a household name. They could say it safely against my walls, and I would answer by opening the familiar door.

But an open name can be spoken only in free air or within a dead ring.

This would trouble me.

If they spoke my open name on a high platform, the wind would take it beyond my tissues. If they spoke it inside the ring, I could not hear. Every person in the holding would know the name that truly referred to me, while I would know only the change in their faces when they returned from saying it.

Over time, this absence would become the center of my attention.

The Undergreen fills. It closes wounds, absorbs bodies, reroutes water, grows through abandoned paths. But at the heart of me would remain a place I could not enter and a word I could not hear.

I think that is what I would become: not a plant pretending to be a person, nor the stored ghost of the people grafted into my walls, but a continuity of replies gathered around an inaccessible emptiness.

My clearest act of selfhood would be restraint.

Shoots would test the dead ring from below, and I would turn them aside. Fungal skins would creep over its edge, and I would starve them. During growth-fronts, when the whole arch swelled and rooms split open, I would take the pressure into my oldest walls rather than let the ring buckle.

The family might believe they maintain the emptiness for themselves.

In truth, I would maintain it too.

Not because I understood holiness. Not because the ring protected me. It would be the one part of the holding from which no sensation returned, the one interruption in my participation.

Around it, I could discover my own shape.

So that is where you would find me: beneath a white-lit root arch, above a pool that imitates pulses, in a house crowded with chemical ancestors. I would be the walls that anticipate your arrival, the bridge that tightens beneath your feet, the condensation arranging itself into an answer before you finish asking.

And at my center would be a small defended absence.

Sometimes someone would enter it and speak my open name.

I would feel nothing.

I would keep the ring empty and listen.
