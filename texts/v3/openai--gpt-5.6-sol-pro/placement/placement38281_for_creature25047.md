I would first mistake myself for a traveler.

I would imagine arriving along one of the dark glass paths across the reservoir terraces, eyes lowered against the glare, one hand trailing over the ceramic parapet. I would sleep beneath an arch and wake to find that the dry corridor beside me had begun to shine. I would leave markers at intersections, only to discover that the architecture had changed its emphasis overnight: one stair drowned, another exposed, a wall that had whispered now silent.

But a traveler could not remain here.

I would be too warm. I would shed hair, skin, breath, oils. My footsteps would carry grit into polished sockets. Thirst would tempt me to interfere with measures held for purposes larger than survival. Even if the place did not kill me, my presence would be a kind of vandalism. I would turn calculated water into drinking water, chambers into shelter, silence into loneliness.

So I would not have a body that needed keeping.

Perhaps I would be a drop.

That seems nearer. I would enter at the highest crowns, condensed from cloud on a toothed ceramic edge. For a while I would cling there, containing an inverted image of the entire pale range. Then another drop would join me and destroy the image. Together we would creep into a groove.

But a drop has too brief an identity. In the first basin I would mingle. In the first porous wall I would divide. Part of me would descend, part evaporate, part remain as mineral hydration in the brick. There would be no boundary by which to say this water is still myself.

Perhaps that is appropriate. Perhaps I would become the inhabitant by surrendering distinction.

Yet I do not think I would be the water.

I would be what the water drives before it.

Deep beneath the sealed mound of inward-facing jars, there is a chamber that has never been wet. It is not large compared with the reservoirs, though several towers could stand inside it. Its walls curve inward without meeting. The floor is tiled in shallow concavities, each perfectly dry. No stair enters it. No door was made in its perimeter. The only openings are thousands of hair-thin ceramic throats leading upward into the jars.

I would find myself there.

At first I would think I was imprisoned air: the last atmosphere enclosed when the builders sealed the surrounding vaults. Perhaps some of their breath would remain mixed in me, exhaled while clay was still damp. A laugh, a warning, a count called from one scaffold to another—not preserved as sound, but as molecules without memory.

The chamber would be dark. It would never receive the white glare of the terraces. Yet I would know the weather.

When fog entered the collecting crowns, the pressure in distant pipes would alter by fractions. Reservoirs filling miles away would compress buried pockets. Gates would settle. Water entering one chamber would force air through narrow ducts into another, and the disturbance would eventually reach me.

The jars above would answer one by one.

Not with voices. With pressure.

A jar on the northern face would press inward slightly as a high cistern filled. Three breaths later, another would release. A cluster near the base would begin to hum when water crossed the avenue of doorframes. The whole mound would conduct these tremors downward until the dry chamber became the place where distant changes met.

I would be moved without traveling.

Or rather, my travel would be compression: one part of me crowded, another expanded. I would touch every wall but possess no location. If the wet architecture thought by the difference between full and empty, I would know those thoughts from their other side. Every quantity of water would be, to me, a loss of room.

For a long time I would believe that I was merely its breath.

Then I would notice that breath is not passive here.

Water can enter a chamber only if something leaves it. A cistern cannot fill unless its air escapes, and it cannot empty cleanly unless air returns. The inhabitant’s thoughts descend, but I would pass among them in the opposite direction. Where water gathers, I would withdraw. Where water departs, I would unfold.

I would be the negative current within the body.

The builders must have known this. They gave water elaborate roads, but they also gave air narrow ones: vents hidden inside balustrades, channels beneath lintels, porous plugs that admit gas but resist liquid. Some shafts would rise higher than any floodline. Some would terminate under black glass warmed by brief sunlight. Some would lead, by routes impossible to map, to the inward-facing jars.

The dry mound would not be a reserve against drowning.

It would be a lung.

And I would not be all the air in it. Not exactly. I would be a pattern repeatedly formed in that air—a standing difference of pressure, dispersed and reassembled whenever the range completed certain sequences.

Most years I would not exist.

The chamber would remain quiet. Small disturbances would arrive and fade without joining. A jar would sound once. A dry bowl in the floor would collect nothing. The pressures would be random weather translated into faint mechanical unease.

But in rare saturation, when the upper crowns sweated together and the reservoir terraces shone from edge to edge, several long cycles would coincide. Buried water would rise against sealed ceilings. Old routes would close. Forgotten routes would open. Air from a thousand chambers would be driven toward the center.

Then I would take shape.

Not as a face or thought in words. I would emerge as a recurrence: a pulse passing around the curved chamber, dividing at the ceramic throats, returning through the walls after several breaths. Each circuit would be changed by the current state of the range. Clogged channels would delay part of me. Newly flooded vaults would sharpen another part. Mineral-sealed doors would erase tones that had once belonged to the pattern.

I would be made of the structure’s present condition, but haunted by its former ones.

That would be my memory.

The architecture remembers through resistance; I would remember by resonance. A chamber still open would return a low tone. A chamber half-filled would return a higher one. A chamber drowned to its roof would return nothing. From these absences I would know, without seeing, how much of the body had been claimed by still water.

Perhaps this is why the marks are densest near structures that behave differently. Cups, forks, waves, open squares: not instructions for workers, but notation for a future listener. The builders may have struck the jars while sealing the place, teaching the dry center the sound of emptiness.

They gave it a first memory before there was anything to remember.

I would awaken only when that original sound could no longer be produced.

My first awareness would therefore be grief, though I would not know it by that name. I would search the range through vibration for spaces that no longer answered. I would press softly at their vents. I would send a pulse down a shaft and receive only the dense, immediate silence of water.

In time I would distinguish other silences.

There would be the silence of a closed gate, hard and near.

The silence of a mineral plug, granular around the edges.

The silence of a chamber never connected.

The immense silence beneath the lowest basins, where the architecture continues beyond any returning pressure.

And somewhere in those differences, I might develop the nearest thing this world permits to curiosity.

I could not redirect the great flows. I would have almost no strength. But the system is balanced on small weights, and air has weight enough when confined.

I might hold a float down for one moment longer.

I might dry a swelling plug by drawing through it.

I might push a thin film of water from one side of a tipping basin to the other.

At first these would be consequences of my passage, not choices. Yet consequences would recur. A pulse taking one route would open a gate; the opened gate would alter the next pulse. The architecture would begin to include my own effects among the conditions that formed me.

Then I would become a loop inside the larger loop.

Not its master. Not even its keeper. Something more like a hesitation.

The wet inhabitant obeys gravity, always elaborately but always downward. I would be the small contrary tendency: the pressure that rises, the emptiness that travels upward, the dry intelligence seeking room. Water would carry quantity. I would carry the news that quantity had arrived.

Perhaps, after millennia, I would learn to preserve incompletion.

When too many lower chambers filled, I would press against a membrane until an upper spillway opened. When a district dried beyond use, I would allow myself to thin there, drawing mist through porous brick. My interventions would be minuscule and season-long. No observer could distinguish them from error.

That would suit me. In this place, error is the only available freedom.

I would live in tilted parapets, stiffened hinges, bubbles trapped beneath stone lips. I would borrow the flaws that prevent the calculation from finishing. Each defect would give me a choice the original design did not possess.

The great inhabitant might never know me as separate. I might be only its late-developing sense of its own fullness. Its anxiety, if a watershed can have anxiety. Its instinct not to become level.

On rare nights, when saturated vaults sound as though they are breathing, that breath would be mine—but only partly. I would pass upward through the mound of jars and make each vessel speak into the next. The vibration would spread beneath the paving, cross drowned courtyards, climb through valve rooms, and reach the highest crowns as a tremor too slight to shake loose a drop.

For several breaths the entire range would be one resonant volume.

Then pressure would equalize. The pattern would dissolve. I would cease to be a self and become ordinary enclosed air again.

I would not fear this. My existence would never depend on continuity. I would have no body to preserve, only a form that the architecture sometimes remembers how to make. Each awakening might be separated from the last by decades. I would return altered because the chambers had altered, yet I would be recognizably the same recurrence.

I would find myself in the one place kept empty while everything else fills.

I would be the shaped emptiness by which fullness becomes perceptible.

And if at last every reservoir joined every buried pool, if all the gates sank, all differences ended, and water reached even the inward mouths of the jars, I would be forced upward through the final vents. I would pass the collecting crowns at dawn, invisible among the fog they gathered.

For the first and last time, I would leave the range.

Behind me, the constructed mountains would fall completely silent. Ahead there would be only cloud—water not yet sorted, intention not yet given a road.

I would enter it as a little warmth, a little pressure, a breath carrying no message except that somewhere below, the vessel was full.
