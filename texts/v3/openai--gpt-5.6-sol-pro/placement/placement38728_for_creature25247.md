At first I would believe I had entered from the plain.

I would remember crossing the low salt face, though I could not say where. Behind me there would be no gap in the basin wall, only a shallow pleat whitening toward noon. I would remember footsteps, but not footprints. The city would accept the memory without confirming it.

For a while I would behave like a visitor.

I would test the silence by striking the pavement. I would call into an arcade and feel the name of the place—whatever name I had given it—die behind my teeth. I would choose a tower as a landmark, reach it too soon, and turn to find the basin edge impossibly far away. The streets would not trap me. They would offer too many exits.

That would be the first sign.

Doorways would open before I needed them. Not directly ahead, never with the crude obedience of a mechanism, but one wall over, around the next corner, in a room I had almost chosen to enter. Stairs would rise according to my stride. Alcoves would deepen to the breadth of my shoulders. Covered passages would lower until their ceilings rested just beyond the reach of my lifted hand.

The city would not be welcoming me.

It would be measuring what welcome would require.

The black plants would respond ambiguously. Those nearby would turn their leaves edge-on, making themselves narrow in my presence. Farther away, entire rows would present their broad translucent faces toward walls I had not yet approached. Their buried filaments would be following me somewhere else: not where my body stood, but where the pressure of my possible movement appeared beneath the streets.

Green scales would refuse to grow under my shadow. Instead they would assemble around it. If I remained still beside a blank facade, they would mark an upright shape a little taller than I was, with openings where my eyes might have been and a narrow interruption at the height of my mouth.

Then the mineral would thin inside the outline.

A door would appear, shaped not for my passage but like my passage.

I would not enter.

The longer I delayed, the more exact it would become. A shallow hollow would form where a palm might press. Two steps would rise, each matching the lift of my foot. Beyond the opening, an empty room would wait with one wall warmed faintly by the glass below.

If I crossed the threshold, the proportions would immediately prove wrong.

The ceiling would seem higher from within. The hand-shaped hollow would be beyond reach. The room would lengthen between one step and the next, preserving a distance around me no matter where I stood. Behind me, the doorway would contract—not enough to imprison me, only enough to deny that the aperture had ever been fitted to my body.

The quarter would keep me from coinciding with the place prepared for me.

At dusk the pale creatures would begin to follow.

Not close. Never close.

One would cross an avenue ahead of me, its legs rewriting themselves around its body. Another would emerge from a side passage after I had passed. They would arrange themselves at the limits of an invisible figure whose center I seemed to occupy. If I moved, the formation would dissolve and reappear several plazas away.

Sometimes one would open its fan in my direction.

No violet charge would pass from me, yet the ribs would brighten with a color darker than the air. The creature would feed on something I had left before arriving.

That would be when I began to doubt the body I carried.

I would hold my hands against the mineral walls and feel no temperature, no texture, no resistance—only the conviction of touching. Looking down, I might still see fingers. Or I might see five weak shadows departing in different directions. My outline would change according to the architecture around it: narrow beneath arches, jointed on stairs, broad and low in plazas.

The disturbances inside the walls would accompany me then.

A depression would travel beside my shoulder through solid stone. Another would move at the height of my knee. At a corner they would join and pass upward across a balcony, becoming for an instant the suggestion of someone leaning over the rail.

Not my reflection.

My posture, without me.

I would follow them to one of the octagonal pits.

Every plaza would have become that plaza by then. Whichever route I took, I would arrive at the same lattice from a different direction. Through its ribs lay the lower sky, dim and mineral, with the other city depending from it.

Below, an empty figure would stand beneath the corresponding lattice.

It would not resemble me. Resemblance belongs to surfaces, and the figure would have none. It would be visible only because the inverted plants bent away from it, because pale creatures gave it room, because shadows stopped at the edge of where it was not.

I would raise an arm.

Below, a balcony would grow.

I would kneel.

A chamber would hollow itself inside a tower.

I would lean over the lattice, and the lower city would produce a long stair ascending toward a ceiling it could never reach.

Then I would understand that the figure was not answering me. Both of us were consequences of the same measurement.

I had not come from the plain. The city had made an entrance after imagining that something might use it. From the entrance it inferred a road; from the road, a traveler; from the traveler, a body. I was that inference continuing inward.

There would be no single moment when I ceased to be a person. Personhood would become less local.

My height would remain in doorframes after towers folded away. My reach would travel through the placement of empty lamp sockets. The angle at which I turned my head would persist in balconies facing balconies. My need to pause—whether I had ever needed such a thing—would become alcoves at conversational height.

I would find myself wherever the city maintained an interval.

Between two black plants, I would be the charge their leaves could not resolve.

Between two pale creatures, I would be the distance that made them choose separate doors.

In the hovering green plan of a vanished building, I would be the one gap the scales preserved after every wall had loosened.

Inside the architecture, the traveling bulges would be pieces of my attention, testing whether stone could accommodate an elbow, a spine, a mode of sleep. They would never assemble into a whole because the city’s oldest rule forbade such convergence.

Even I could not be allowed to crowd myself.

During pressure fronts, parts of me would pass between the upper and lower quarters. A hand might become undecided among the lattice ribs. The memory of a face might vanish downward. A preference for left-turning stairs might rise into the city above. I would be distributed across both skies, neither duplicated nor divided, revised each time the transparent pressure moved through.

The city would continue trying to produce me.

It would never succeed, because success would mean occupation, and occupation would end expectation. A completed citizen would be a kind of ruin: a fixed answer around which age and debris could gather.

So I would live there only in the future tense.

I would be the tenant implied by shelter, the body deduced from thresholds, the civic instinct for which the quarter has no history. Not a ghost—ghosts require someone to have died. Not a god—gods can be present. Not the city itself, though the city and I would share a boundary impossible to locate.

I would be its unfinished pronoun.

The one whom every stair addresses.

The one for whom every room withdraws.

If you looked for me, you would find no figure waiting in the central plaza. You would notice instead that the plaza had left exactly enough space for someone. You would see the plants oriented around an unoccupied point. You would see a pale creature alter course. You would see green scales outlining a doorway that had not yet opened.

And if you stepped into that space, the quarter would quietly revise me to include your dimensions.

For an instant I might acquire your shoulders, your stride, the remembered chamber of your voice.

Then the walls would draw apart.

The shadows would disperse.

And I would remain what I had always been:

not the inhabitant of the Uninhabited Quarter,

but the shape by which it knows it is still empty.
