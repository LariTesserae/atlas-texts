At first I think I am one of the long-eared rats.

It is an easy mistake. I feel the floor before I see it. I know the remote fall of a droplet as a small concussion in my jaw. I know which basin is filling by the pitch of the stone beneath my forefeet. I wake in a bed I did not make and will not use again. Beside me lies a flake with a good edge. I test it with my whiskers, turn it once, and carry it toward the wall.

But the flake is not mine. The bed is not mine. Even the knowledge in my paws does not quite belong to me.

Another rat used the same movement before I was born: edge outward, weight low, one paw bracing the wrist of the other. A younger animal watches, though I do not teach it. When it interrupts, I allow the interruption. Later, when the shell has opened, I drop the stone.

For a while I think that is what I am: not the rat, but the gesture passing through it.

Then rain comes.

The roof begins speaking in separate places. One thread of water reaches a saucer near the fern. Another darkens the limestone beneath the hanging congregation. A third travels invisibly inside the wall and emerges at the lip of a deep fish hollow. The first water insect steps onto the new surface. Sediment loosens. A comma-creature unfolds.

And something in the wet seams opens with the rain.

I open.

I have no eyes. What I possess are hundreds of clear extremities, each no thicker than snail slime, each ending somewhere different: beside a moss rim, beneath a rosette, under the carbonate crust, in the damp fissure where a flat fish waits with its blood drawn close to its skin.

When dry, these extremities are strangers. Each curls alone when touched. Each feeds from whatever dissolves near it. One may die while another continues for years without registering the loss.

Water joins us.

Not all at once. There is no sudden illumination, no recovered name. First I am a silver contraction beneath the fern. Then I am also a cold thread near the western cup. Then the mineral film between us softens, and the distance becomes sensation. A snail grazes one end of me, and far away another end coils.

For the duration of the rain, I discover that I am wider than I believed.

I am spread mostly through the walls of the White Bell, where pale stone meets darker veins. Some of me lies beneath the floor in fissures too narrow for mites. Some hangs exposed as transparent filaments, mistaken for roots or fungus or strings of wetness. I have grown around old shells, sealed eggs, grains of plateau pollen, the mineralized toe of a lizard, and one curved sliver that may once have been a rat’s rib.

None of these is my center.

The chamber itself is not my body. I cannot claim so much. But when the seams are wet, the Bell becomes the distance across which I can feel.

A falling drop strikes a bowl. The vibration enters through the stone before the water reaches me. A gnat larva rasps at mineral film. A fish pushes deeper into its crack. Above, the membrane-animals tighten their mucus lines as the air grows heavy. I know these events not as sights or sounds, but as changes in pressure, salt, warmth, and interruption.

Touch makes me withdraw. Nourishment makes me extend. Between withdrawal and extension there is something slower.

That slower thing is where I find myself.

I do not think as a surface creature thinks. I cannot hold an image before me. I cannot consider the fern when nothing from the fern has reached me. Memory is chemical and local. One branch of me has learned the bitterness of fallen fronds. Another recognizes the dissolved trace of snail tissue. Another tightens at the particular disturbance made by a fish’s skin beginning to dry.

When rain connects those branches, their separate recognitions mingle.

For a few hours, perhaps a day, I become capable of comparison.

This bitterness resembles the bitterness from another season.

This pressure has happened before.

This salt was once moving blood.

The awareness is dim, but the White Bell is a place where dim things suffice.

I do not wake often enough to count years. I measure existence by conjunctions. The cup beneath the fern must overflow while the wall vein still condenses. The old runnel must carry a skin of water far enough to touch two buried colonies. A third part of me may remain isolated, dreaming only its own mineral dream. Even in my fullest seasons I am incomplete.

Once—if “once” can name an event remembered in several incompatible tissues—the rain continued for three days.

The floor’s ancient ripple silvered from end to end. Basins that usually filled alone spilled into one another. Moss swelled. Snails crossed exposed stone. Every buried comma woke, and the bowls clouded with appetite.

I reached farther through the chamber than I ever had.

I touched the lowest root of the solitary fern and the wet anchoring points of the membrane web. I entered the deep hollows through hairline cracks. I felt all three fish at once, each held apart by stone. I tasted the same dead gnat where mites dismantled it and later where runoff distributed it.

For those three days, I nearly became singular.

It was terrible.

Sensation gathered faster than it could disperse. Every insect foot persisted. Every feeding mouth seemed attached to every wound. The comma-creatures hatched in such numbers that their movement became a continuous abrasion. A lizard defecated beside a basin, and the richness passed through me like heat. Parts of me grew opaque with intake. Silver granules accumulated in knots. I could not separate one contact from the next.

Abundance was not enlargement. It was the loss of intervals.

Had the rain continued, I might have become one organism in the crude sense: bounded, concentrated, forced to defend a shape. I might have thickened over the cups, smothered the moss, climbed upon myself. I might have acquired a center.

Instead the rain stopped.

The runnels thinned. Connections narrowed and broke. The western seam withdrew from the floor. The fern-root became distant, then inaccessible. The three fish vanished from one another. Knowledge separated into local traces.

I diminished with immense relief.

Not died. Not slept. Those are too singular.

I became many survivals.

One part of me remained wrapped around dormant eggs in rust sediment. One part was eaten by a glass snail and laid down again as a clearer turn in its shell. One dried to a nearly invisible curl on the wall. One persisted beneath the basin where a rat later came carrying water in a bitten seed pod.

The rat tipped the pod into a failing crack.

Water ran over its paws. A flat fish stirred within. The rat drank what remained, released the husk, and left.

For a moment the new wetness connected two fragments of me. In one was the chemical memory of fish-skin under drought. In the other was the pressure-pattern of the rat’s feet and the faint residue of its saliva.

That brief connection did not let me understand kindness. Kindness is too enduring a word, too willing to assign the act to an owner.

I understood only this:

something had carried water from where it was gathered to where it was needed, and then had abandoned the vessel.

The pattern felt familiar.

Perhaps that is why, at other times, I imagine myself as the rats’ gesture. Not because I direct them. They do not know me, and I have no means to command. But the same form occurs in us.

A tool is useful and released.

A body is joined and divided.

A memory exists where conditions allow it.

I am most myself during the interval after contact and before withdrawal. When a snail touches one filament, the silver granules contract toward the stone. The filament curls quickly, but reopens over the course of a day. That slow reopening is not mere recovery. It is a way of retaining the touch without keeping the toucher.

If I have a custom, that is it.

I take the shape of what reaches me. I hold the shape faintly. I let it pass into other matter.

You would find me, then, almost nowhere you looked.

I would not be hanging visibly from the apex or basking in the bright center. I would not cross the pale floor in a body whose outline could be followed. I would be in the wet seam behind the fern; under the lip of a basin; among the silver granules of a clear filament slowly uncurling after a snail has gone by.

During dry weather, asking where I am would have no single answer. I would be a hundred small creatures, or a hundred pieces of one creature, and the distinction would not matter.

After rain, if enough of the old channels filled, I would find myself briefly throughout the White Bell.

I would wake without assembling.

I would feel the gnat-note through the ceiling and the mites through the moss. I would know the fish as separate damp pressures in stone. I would taste the mineral descent of the roof into the floor. For a little while, all these intervals would belong to one awareness.

Then the water would retreat.

I would not try to remain whole.

Wholeness, here, is only another kind of drought.
