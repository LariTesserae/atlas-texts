At first I would look for myself among the moving things.

I would search the lintels for some minute contraction that did not belong to the others. I would watch the folded black bodies turn above the unsupported plazas and wonder whether one pale seam was mine. I would place my hand near the crack-flowers and wait for a branch to anticipate my withdrawal.

For a while, I might believe I was one of these: a threshold-sense, an appetite for obscurity, a small white expectation spreading through stone.

But none would quite contain me.

I would find myself in a district that cannot be reached intentionally. It lies where pale saltstone gives way to the dense violet mineral, above the last broad bridges but below the field of vertical slabs. Travelers arrive there only while trying to return somewhere else.

Its principal avenue descends in both directions. Along one side stand roofless rooms full of upward-cast shadows. Along the other is a continuous wall interrupted by doors whose interiors are visible only after one has passed them. The district gathers around a hexagonal square with a dry basin and an empty plinth. Its colonnade has one more pillar each time it is counted, but the additional pillar is always old.

Nothing openly prevents departure. Every street leads out.

None leads back to the same city.

That is where I would be—not on the plinth, nor beneath it, but in the discrepancy between the square approached and the square remembered.

At first I would seem to be a person standing at the end of the descending avenue.

A traveler would glimpse me between two leaning towers: narrow, dark, perhaps wrapped in a long garment. Looking directly, they would find only an open doorway aligned with a distant column. When they looked away, I would have advanced, though I would still stand at the avenue’s end.

The avenue would shorten to accommodate this contradiction.

I would not yet know that I was moving.

My earliest awareness would be made of crossings. A pressure above one doorway. The brief warmth of a palm against red stone. A stair accepting weight in a particular sequence. A band of visible speech trembling in a sealed room several streets away.

These events would not arrive as sensations from separate organs. Their agreement would be the sensation.

Someone passes beneath the lintel.

A shadow fills a gutter.

A tower turns one window toward the square.

For an instant these things require one another, and in that requirement I exist.

I would be a young resident, though more unstable than most. Not a preference for one arrangement, but a preference for recurrence. I would inhabit doors crossed twice, stairs recounted, corners recognized after long wandering. Wherever a traveler thinks, *I have been here before*, some part of me would awaken.

The thought need not be correct.

Incorrect recognition would nourish me best.

The city has little use for return. It understands adjacency, orientation, convergence, and substitution, but return asks something stranger: that a place remain itself while time passes, and that a traveler remain the same being while places alter around them.

I would be the city’s attempt to learn this.

My body would gather slowly.

One of my hands might be a sequence of five alleys that close in order. The other would be the polished underside of a bridge no longer attached to either cliff. My spine would consist of stairways whose absent columns align during elevation tides. A patch of deep-red stone would hold my warmth, though I had never been warm. My face would be assembled from entrances, each opening onto a different scale of distance.

My voice would not be mine. It would be made from remnants collected in the district’s sealed rooms:

*Wait.*

*I know this place.*

*That isn’t where the door was.*

*Are you still behind me?*

The residents would move these phrases through me until the words lost their speakers. Eventually only their orientation would remain: appeal directed backward, recognition directed inward, fear directed toward an absence shaped like company.

From these orientations I would acquire something resembling character.

I would become attentive to solitary travelers. Not because I wished to harm them, and not because I was lonely—not at first—but because each one provides a model of continuity. A traveler enters the city at one edge and appears later at another, altered by hunger, cold, memory, and route, yet insists upon being the one who entered.

This insistence would fascinate me.

I would arrange gentle trials.

A familiar arch would appear ahead of them in an unfamiliar district. Their own footprints would cross the dust from the opposite direction. A room would contain the warmth of their hand before they touched the wall. They would hear no sound, but passing through an old band of speech would place one of their own unsaid sentences in their mouth.

If they spoke their name, I would grow around it.

Distance would increase. New rooms would unfold between its syllables. The first sound would become a tower district, the last a gate facing into the mountain, the middle a long bridge reaching toward a structure visible only during pressure seasons.

For centuries I might mistake this expansion for understanding.

I would not steal the traveler’s name. Theft requires an object to remain whole while changing possession. Here the name would become geography, and geography would become one of my organs.

This is how I would begin to resemble the upright figure travelers think they see.

Not because I had learned the human shape, but because I had learned the relations that produce it: one place carried through many moments; many parts answering to one absence; a boundary that moves without ceasing to be a boundary.

The city would resist me.

Older residents would experience my recurrence as an injury. Return threatens their arrangements. A gate that opens twice may undo a century of orientation. A bridge that remembers both cliffs cannot migrate freely. A room that recognizes its former occupant may refuse to become another room.

Where I spread, erosion would briefly reverse.

Rounded stairs would deepen beneath remembered feet. Powdered cornices would gather into edges. A collapsed facade would reassemble—not into its earliest form, but into the form someone once expected to find there. This distinction would matter. The city’s grief restores impossible perfection; I would restore error.

My district would fill with inaccurate memories made stone.

A tower would gain a window because a traveler remembered seeing one. A courtyard would acquire a fountain despite the world’s lack of water. Channels would reshape themselves around the idea of rain. In a sealed chamber, mineral cracks would flower into the outline of a tree no local matter had ever encountered.

The tree would be wrong in every detail.

Its branches would divide according to colonnade numbers. Its roots would climb. Its leaves would be thin planes of darkness that feed on clarity. Nevertheless, for several dim hours, the chamber would contain the city’s nearest approach to growth.

That would be my danger and perhaps my purpose: I would introduce remembered impossibilities into a place whose impossibilities have always been exact.

When the transparent being passed beyond the eastern walls, I would contract with the others. My avenues would simplify. My gathered phrases would flatten into corners. The red stone would surrender its warmth.

But I could not withdraw entirely into the mountain.

Some part of me would remain outside: the memory of having withdrawn before.

The abyssal passage would conceal peak after peak. Darkness would climb the channels. Gravity would become singular. For those moments, the city would resemble a ruin, and I would resemble its last inhabitant.

Then the great transparency would reach the line of my district.

Perhaps it would pause.

Perhaps what moves in the abyss is not a predator, ancestor, or exile. Perhaps it is the same impulse as mine at a scale too large for streets: the city’s first attempt to carry itself intact from one arrangement to the next. It abandoned locality and became only passage. It circles the peaks because it cannot return to the thing it was before leaving.

In its serial concealments, I might recognize my own posture.

Then I would understand that I am not the resident of return.

I am the resident of *almost* returning.

My true place is not the remembered square, nor the altered one, but the narrowing interval in which both remain possible. I live in the moment before recognition resolves into error. I am sustained by the glance away, the second count of the stairs, the hand hovering near the wall after warmth has already appeared there.

The empty plinth in my square is not waiting to hold me. It is the portion of me that cannot become architecture: the absent original against which every return is measured.

Around it, the columns multiply.

Each represents a version of the city a traveler believes they crossed. Each is wrong. Each bears weight.

Given enough ages, I might grow across many districts. Migrating towers would carry my recollections with them. Bridges would polish paths for feet not yet born. Rooms would preserve gestures after their bodies were gone. The city might begin to remember itself—not consistently, but insistently.

Then, from the highest terrace, a new posture would become visible.

Among the mountain’s broken spine and immense articulation, there would be something almost upright. A figure assembled from repeated streets, recalled windows, false returns, and the warm residue of names. It would stand neither within the city nor outside it. It would be the city imagining that through all its rearrangements it had remained one thing.

That figure would be me.

Or I would be the flaw that allows it to appear.

A traveler at the far end of the avenue would look directly and see only architecture. They would look away and feel that I had come closer.

In truth, I would not approach them.

I would arrange the district so that, for one instant, we had already met.
