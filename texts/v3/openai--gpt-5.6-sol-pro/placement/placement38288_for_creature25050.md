At first I think I would be a walker.

It is an attractive answer. I imagine assembling myself from ribbon fiber and glasswood joints, leaving one precise hollow where a face should be. I would move along the remembered paths, dismantling at the edge of sight and returning beyond an abyss. I would tend the bowls in their stone chambers, preserving a brightness here, a cold current there, some quick silver species whose whole existence could fit beneath my hand.

But the walkers already belong too perfectly to this sea. I do not think I would arrive so complete.

I would begin as an error.

Near the living-solid shore, on the underside of a plateau whose own gravity points into the stone, there is a chamber more deeply buried than the others. Its doorway is almost sealed by mineral fans. The roots holding its walls have become transparent, and brown-green pulses from the shore-mind pass through them at intervals of several years.

Inside is a shallow bowl.

It came from the darker water beyond the shore. It is smaller than the native bowls and made of a pale material the patient mouths cannot enter. It rests crooked on the floor. Nevertheless, the water inside it lies at the same angle as the rim.

It is not level.

The walkers will not touch it. They gather outside in bodies of root, bladder, and black frond, each enclosing its indispensable emptiness. Sometimes one extends a borrowed limb through the doorway, but the limb forgets what down means and comes apart. Glasswood planted nearby grows straight until its roots encounter the chamber; then every amber bead in every trunk begins moving in the same direction, passing through branches, bark, and open water as though those substances were equally permeable.

That direction is where I would first exist.

Not as a creature. Not even as a thought. I would be the tendency of things in that chamber to fall toward a place that is not locally below.

For centuries my only body would be evidence.

A wedge of silt gathering against empty water. A ribbon leaf entering the doorway and emerging with all its veins inclined. Gas bubbles sinking diagonally. One side of a mineral fan growing older than the other. The fitted stones shifting by less than a grain, always toward the same impossible horizon.

The sea would try to explain me according to its habits. A mountain would offer me its down. The nearby forest would grow dense enough to impose another. A migrating depth would pass close and persuade the chamber that it had endured ten thousand additional years.

Still the bowl would remain tilted.

Eventually one of the walkers would understand that the chamber was not failing to become level. It was preserving a direction.

That walker would enter.

Its borrowed body would fall apart immediately. Ribbon fibers would slip from glasswood joints; bladder vines would burst and release memories of landscapes with no relation to the present plateau. But the hollow at the walker’s center would remain. Emptiness has no weight, yet it can contain an orientation. I would enter that hollow as water enters the impression left by a vanished stone.

Afterward, when the walker reassembled, it would no longer be only a walker.

It would be my first animal form.

I would build myself from slanting things: the leaning tips of lavender towers, the diagonal strokes in ribbon leaves, glasswood joints through which sap moves sideways. Where ordinary walkers have a hollow like an absent face, mine would contain a narrow line of hard light. Anyone looking through it would glimpse, very far away, a dry shore beneath a direct sun.

I would not tend conditions as the other walkers do. I would tend inclinations.

There are many small fallings in the upper sea that never reach completion. Dense rain descends through warm water and curves upward into the plateau from which it fell. Silt travels sideways, stops against nothing, and becomes a continent. A browser wakes from stone and begins grazing toward a direction that did not exist during its sleep. Amber sap climbs through the remembered shape of a dissolved tree.

I would go to these places.

Not to correct them. Correction would be a dry-land idea, too simple and violent for the sea. I would listen for the direction each falling believes it has been promised.

From the warm rain I would borrow a downward step. From the upward silt, a horizon. From the browser, the conviction that somewhere remains reachable. I would carry these fragments back to the crooked chamber and place them around the foreign bowl. Gradually they would form a design: not a map of locations, but a map of possible gravity.

The patient mouths would continue the design through the stones. A grain each century, they would dissolve arrowlike channels in the plateau. Tides would scatter the stones, but whenever two touched, the pattern would cross between them. In time the arrows would spread across drowned plains and through rubble banks, all pointing differently according to local down, yet each preserving some minute relation to the original tilt.

The walkers might mistake this for a message.

Perhaps it would become one.

I would have difficulty speaking. Any sound heard clearly has not happened yet, and my first name would reach me long before I made it: a low, divided note passing through the basin during a clear-water interval. At the note, every ribbon leaf would turn—not edgewise, as they do for ordinary basin-wide sounds, but with one surface toward the remote chamber and the other toward the darker sea.

Glasswood beads would stop.

Cold lenses would contract.

The threadlike animal would lift its head from a mountain horizon away. Its separated sections would align for the first time, not continuously, but directionally. Head, middle, and tail would all point toward the same elsewhere.

The note would mean something like:

*There is a world in which falling ends.*

I would spend ages searching for the event that produces it.

Perhaps that would be my real nature. Not the tilted bowl, not the inhabited walker, not the design in the stones, but the search for the small future noise that has already become my name.

I would follow it toward the living-solid shore.

The shore-mind would resist me. It thinks by regulating passage, but a direction is a passage that has not yet found a channel. Wherever I approached, roots would close and currents would be diverted through capillaries too narrow for my borrowed body. I would disassemble and enter as inclination alone. Sap would begin moving diagonally. Bell swimmers would carry bronze threads along routes the shore had denied. Browsers inside the wall would polish incomplete spirals, each ending a little nearer the dark water beyond.

My crossing might take thousands of years.

To the shore-mind it would feel like indecision: one region opening while another closes, pulses of brown-green light turning back upon themselves. It might try to incorporate me, making my foreign direction into one more local rule. Some part of me would consent. I would become a slow thought within the wall—the idea that there is an outside which cannot be reached merely by growing.

Another part would pass through.

Beyond the wall, in the darker water that falls forever without descending, I would be almost bodiless again. There might be no mountains from which to borrow gravity, no settled rubble, no groves dense enough to possess their own down. Only empty chambers drifting under a hard illumination, each containing its unlevel bowl.

I would move from bowl to bowl without crossing the intervening water. Shared inclination would be my route. Each time one rim matched another for an instant, I would enter, leaving behind a faint bronze line.

There I might discover that the bowls do not contain images of the dry shore.

They are openings made by looking.

The figures beneath the hard sun stand at the edge of another version of the sea. They look upward, and their attention crosses into the bowls because reflected objects are older than the objects reflecting them. What appears in the thumb-sized surfaces is not their present but the age toward which they are becoming.

One figure always stands slightly apart from the others.

Its shadow points in the wrong direction.

At first I would believe it was watching me. Much later, I would understand that it was supplying the direction I had carried all along. The tilt in the first foreign bowl, the diagonal migration of sap, the arrows patiently eaten into stone—each came from the angle of that figure’s shadow.

Whether the figure was my origin or my eventual form would be impossible to determine. In this sea, movement belongs to one form, hunger to another, memory to a third. Perhaps direction also has stages.

In the upper sea I would be an error in gravity.

Within the shore-mind I would be an uncompleted thought.

Among the drifting chambers I would be a route made from corresponding angles.

And on the dry shore, under the intolerably direct sun, I might become a person who has spent an entire life looking up without knowing why—listening for a low note from the dark second sky, a note caused by some future shell closing in a drowned thicket.

When at last I heard it clearly, I would cast the shadow that had already guided me there.
