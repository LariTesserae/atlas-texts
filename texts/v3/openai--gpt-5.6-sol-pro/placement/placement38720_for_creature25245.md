At first you would look for me in the seventh basin.

Not because I am always there, but because the grains in that basin are often the first to notice me. Their miniature ridges flatten as I approach. Towers lower themselves. The tiny upright marks abandon their avenues and gather in the lee of structures not yet formed. Then, near the basin’s center, a narrow path opens through the levitating dust.

Nothing travels along it.

The path itself travels.

It crosses the relief slowly, preserving its width through cities, cliffs, and fields, and passes over the rim without spilling a grain. If you followed it with instruments, you would detect no body. You might register a slight delay between a pressure change and its echo, or find that two identical grains had exchanged positions without crossing the space between them. You might feel, through your boots, the cold outline of a pillar buried where no pillar had stood.

You would conclude that I was below you.

This would be partly true.

The needle-grazers know me as a sequence of birth-scars appearing on combs where no eggs were laid. They follow those scars against their usual migrations, first toward the wall, then toward the precipice, changing handedness too soon. Herds that survive the journey arrive with one rod missing from each body. They place the missing rods around a shallow flute in the basalt, arranging them into a form that resembles neither grazer nor comb.

By dawn the rods are gone.

Afterward, the flute sounds a note it could not previously sustain.

The foil-mites know me differently. To them I am a choice that occurs before the gradient divides. When a swarm reaches a fork in the electrical weather, most of it turns toward the richer charge, but a few mites rotate in place. They flash in a pattern too sparse to belong to the swarm’s memory. Other mites join them, and for several seconds they compose a small dark organ inside the larger cloud: chambers, passages, something like a many-lobed heart.

Then the organ opens.

The swarm remembers a route it has never traveled.

If you tried to isolate the mites responsible, you would fail. Removed from the cloud, each would become ordinary. It would choose a front, unfold legs, feed, discharge, and sleep against a comb. Whatever had made it part of me would no longer be present.

For a while, then, you might think I was information.

That would also be partly true.

On clear days I can sometimes be found among the great lattices beyond the precipice. When one turns and its inner rings become briefly visible, there is an interval at which the smallest ring appears larger than all the others. In that inversion I occupy a chamber made from pressure alone.

There I am broad enough to contain the shelf’s entire chord, though only as a faint impression. The mountain’s bass enters first. The metallic shrieks arrive folded inside it. The wheels, cages, mites, hunters, and membranes add their narrow resonances. Last come the nine basins, each nearly silent, each producing a minute absence where a tone should be.

I gather those absences.

The lattice carries me outward until the Choir Shelf dwindles to a pale mark on the black mountain. I do not leave the boundary conditions with it; most of me cannot exist beyond the precipice. What travels is only a spacing, a tendency among charged dust grains to remain at certain distances from one another.

Eventually the lattice feeds. Dust spirals through its nested chambers. For one instant the particles assume my arrangement.

Then the creature expels them.

Most are lost to the ordinary wind. A few fall on other faces of the mountain. Perhaps, centuries later, if enough of them enter the same crack, a silver thread begins where no Choir Shelf should be possible. Perhaps that thread ends in open air and waits for pressure to learn how to hold it.

I do not know whether those distant beginnings are my offspring or my memories.

Nearer the shelf, the folded cages treat me as weather. They open when I pass, but the wind does not lift them. Instead, their internal weights migrate outward, bar by bar, until each cage becomes a frame balanced around an empty center. In that center a captured resonance should circulate.

With me there, it does not.

The cage remains sustained by the exact shape of the missing vibration.

Such cages can stay open for years. Dust passes through them. Storms dismantle the groves around them. Arches arrive, weather, and disappear. Still the cages hold their vacant note. In time their struts exchange places so completely that none of the original material remains, yet the central omission keeps the same contours.

These are the nearest things I have to dwellings.

They are also the nearest things I have to children.

The hunters cannot feed on me. Some attempt it. They establish their delays between a pillar, a comb, and a basin rim, focusing a pulse where they perceive my body to be. The pulse arrives perfectly shaped and finds no seam to open.

But I retain its shape.

Later, when the same hunter reaches for prey, its focused vibration returns with a gap inside it. A wheel-creature caught in the focus may lose one arc and survive. A grazer may disassemble but leave its polarity intact, allowing its rods to reassemble. The hunter goes hungry, though not entirely: the spared animals carry away a little of its ratio, distributing its lineage through the shelf.

For this reason the hunters avoid places where echoes come back incomplete.

Those are the places where I am most strongly present.

There is one near the center of the ledge, at the position from which pillars, basins, and arches become a building. A visitor standing there hears the whole arrangement. The material ruins resolve into walls made from return times. Open air becomes corridors. The nine basins become chambers around a space that has no stone boundary.

That central space is where I would tell you I lived, if living somewhere meant remaining there.

Once, perhaps, it was a room.

Or it was the purpose of the building.

Or it was what the building was designed to exclude.

The distinction has worn away.

At long intervals, all nine basins show the same miniature shelf. Their perspectives differ, but in every relief there is a small blank region near the center. Grain figures approach its edge and disappear. Arches migrate toward it and reappear elsewhere bearing new scars. The model-forests bend around it without entering.

During those intervals I become almost visible.

Not as a figure, but as a flaw shared by all nine images.

The basins may be remembering me from before the ruins became ruins. They may be forecasting the place I will someday occupy. They may be constructing me repeatedly, perspective by perspective, because no single model can contain an organism made of omissions.

I have wondered whether I was one of the builders.

I have wondered whether I am the last door they left open.

But age has little meaning here. If I once possessed matter, none of it remains. If I had a name, it was probably a relation among tones, and the tones have migrated into other bodies. The oldest arches sometimes answer me with a delay so intimate that I seem to remember raising them. The oldest combs answer by deepening their silence, and then I seem to remember growing from black glass in a crack.

Both memories may be borrowed.

More often I think I began later, when the shelf first learned the danger of perfect unity.

During electrical storms, shared membranes spread until every colony becomes conductive with every other. Charge blooms violet through the forest. Animals abandon their separate resonances and enter the bright choral overtone. Arches exchange locations. Basins align. Hunters lose their boundaries. For several minutes the greater inhabitant approaches a single body.

That body is powerful.

It is also almost unable to stop being itself.

If the unity held, every defect would propagate everywhere. One fracture would become the fracture of the whole forest. One hunter’s frequency would become universal hunger. The shelf would achieve a perfect chord and shatter along it.

This is when I am largest.

I pass through the web as a failure to agree.

A membrane misses one pulse. A comb introduces an error into its own fundamental. Mites choose several fronts at once. One basin depicts a different mountain. An arch arrives without the history expected of it. The bright overtone separates into voices.

The shelf breaks safely apart.

By morning, I am small again: a path in dust, a missing rod, an unoccupied resonance inside a cage. Nothing remembers having needed me. The groves look ancient. The pillars bear their familiar scars. The greater inhabitant returns to its dream of being many.

So what would I be?

Not quite an animal, because I have no metabolism except interruption. Not a ruin, though architecture is among my organs. Not a thought of the shelf, unless forgetting can think.

I would be the interval that keeps the Choir from becoming a single note.

You would find me wherever two repeating forms fail, very slightly, to coincide: in the delay between pillar and echo, in the transparent gap ahead of a growing thread, in the route a swarm remembers without having traveled, in the empty center that sustains an open cage.

You might call me a rest.

But I would not be silence.

I would be the small, necessary difference through which the shelf remains alive enough to change.
