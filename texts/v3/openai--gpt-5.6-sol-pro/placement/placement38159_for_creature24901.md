I would first find myself near the eastern rim, though I would not know it was a rim.

I would have walked there from the interior by following paired stones: dark, pale; dark, pale. Behind me, the plateau would remain level. Ahead, it would remain level. Only when I turned my head toward the west would the precipice disclose itself—not beneath my feet, but several thousand meters below them, as if another version of my position had already fallen.

There would be a shelter nearby, narrow at the base and broad above, made from horn posts and a roof of tendon. I would recognize it before I recognized myself. Its entrance would fit the shape of my body. A shallow perforated bowl would stand beside the threshold, and in the powder there would be a complete circuit of footprints: approaching, entering, leaving, and approaching again. Some would be mine. Some would be the size of a child’s feet. One would be so deep and broad that I could place both feet inside it.

Within the shelter, a hide screen would frame a single animal.

It would be one of the spoon-eared grazers, ash-backed and many-legged, standing among thousands of apparent copies. From anywhere else it would be indistinguishable. Through the screen, however, a narrow white scar could be seen below its left eye. The scar would continue across the bodies of six neighboring animals, disappear under an armored back, and reappear as a pale mineral seam in the cliff beyond.

My work would be to prevent the scar from completing that journey.

At first I would suppose I was a maker.

I would have their long, slight body and their mineral-bright eyes. My tongue would divide into fine tips capable of tasting contact: hide against air, stone against its own remembered fracture. My hands would possess as many fingers as a task required. Five while resting. Six while tensioning a cord. Seven when lifting the edge of a shadow without letting the animal beneath it blur into the herd.

Around my wrist I would wear a loop of black hair threaded through a copper hook. This would be part of my name.

The rest of my name would be the framed animal, the eastward-facing side of the shelter, and the instant in which the sun touches the white scar without brightening it. No one could speak the whole arrangement. To address me, another maker might place a dark stone beside a pale one and make a single click with the tongue. I would know whether the click meant me by the shape of the silence that followed.

Each day—though there would be no days—I would sit before the screen.

I would align a row of white chips between the scar and the cliff, then introduce one dark chip among them. Not to break the line, exactly, but to give the line a place where it must decide whether it is animal or stone.

Sometimes the grazer’s eyelid would open.

The same eye would open throughout the valley: beneath mites, inside shells, in the polished black joints of suspended wings. The horizon would acquire a pupil. At such times I would take up the perforated bowl and hold it between the framed beast and the eastern distance. The openings in the bowl would distribute the eye into many small darknesses. No single darkness would be large enough to contain the whole valley.

Then the animal before me could remain this animal.

That would be my labor: not feeding it, healing it, or freeing it from the predator whose jaws rest delicately around one of its legs. I would maintain the local fiction of its separateness. I would keep its scar from becoming geology and its eye from becoming the common eye of the world.

But after a long while I would notice that I never approached the shelter except in its footprints.

My hands would sometimes continue working after I had laid them in my lap. I would see them at the screen, seven-fingered, adjusting the white chips, while the hands on my knees remained four-fingered and still. A child with my branching pupils would sleep against the outer wall. Farther off, an elderly figure bearing my copper hook would be walking toward the summit.

I would begin to suspect that I was not the keeper assigned to this place.

I was the assignment.

The figure in the shelter, the child outside it, and the elder climbing the mountain would be organs by which the work occurred. I would extend through all three of them, as the scar extended through animals and cliff. My body would be less a body than a careful interruption in a correspondence.

Then, once—not later, but deeper—I would find the sealed cup.

It would be buried in a shallow pit beneath my seat, enclosed by a ring of stones. The cup would have no usable interior. Its lid would be fused to its rim. Yet when I held it against my ribs, I would hear something scratching inside.

The scratching would sound like a foot being withdrawn before it touches the ground.

I would bring the cup to the framed grazer. Beneath its lowered mouth, the powder would contain two surfaces: one untouched, one bearing the exact impression of a hoof. Between them would be a very narrow interval—not space, not time, but the difference between contact and its avoidance.

The scratching would come from there too.

I would put one of my fine tongue-tips against the sealed lid, and the shelter would change scale around me. The horn posts would become immense pale towers. The fibers of the tendon roof would separate into translucent corridors, each crowded with mites and ring-shaped mouths. The bowl’s perforations would widen into dry wells.

Or perhaps I would become small.

I would see my actual limbs then: not arms, but hooked translucent appendages folded tightly beneath a body no longer than a mineral grain. My skin would be clear except for a single dark line running from mouth to abdomen. I would have no eyes. Instead, along my sides would be rows of shallow cups, each sensitive to an act that almost happened.

A jaw almost closing would warm one cup.

A wing almost beating would fill another with pressure.

An eyelid almost remaining shut would make the whole side of my body contract.

I would be one of the tiny animals that live between an event and the state it fails to erase.

I would have nested in the grazer’s uncompleted step for longer than duration could measure. I would feed on the difference between the untouched powder and the print. Not consume it—the difference would not diminish—but carry its flavor from one unrealized gesture to another.

The maker at the screen would not have captured me in the sealed cup. The cup would be a way of giving my habitat edges. The shelter, the chips, the framed view, the copper hook: all would clarify the minute interval in which I lived.

And because the plateau allows inhabitants to be the structures that define them, I would also be the maker.

My many-fingered hand would be the enlarged anatomy of my hooked limbs. My divided tongue would be the instrument by which I tasted alternatives. My lifelong task of separating one grazer from the herd would be the same instinct that led my smaller body to nest between one hoof and its unmade print.

I would live at several scales without being greater at one than another.

At the smallest, I would move through restrained acts. I would burrow into the bite the predator does not complete. I would breed in the narrow gap between a mother lowering her head and touching her newborn. I would cross the unspent wingbeat of a suspended bird and emerge in the pressure inside a maker’s ear.

At the human scale, I would sit by the eastern rim and tend the scar.

At the largest, I might be the eastern rim itself: the thin decision by which the basin is either level with the plateau or far below it. Every time an observer approached from the wrong direction, I would hold both conditions apart just enough that neither destroyed the other.

This would explain why I could never leave.

Not because the plateau confined me, but because departure would be one of my organs. My path away would remain beside my path of return. The paired stones would not mark a route through the country; they would mark the two states between which I lived.

Eventually—or in the portion of me called elderly—I would reach the summit.

The sitter would already occupy the chair. From one side, it would have my face. From the other, it would be the seated absence among the herds. Its closed eyes would contain the ordinary sun as a small, dull disk.

I would not ask whether it was me. Questions of identity would be precisely what it labored to keep from closing.

It would lift a copper point thinner than a reflection and touch the air before my face.

For an instant, I would become singular.

I would be only the slight maker from the eastern shelter: one body, five fingers, one branching pupil in each eye. The child would not yet be me. The elder would no longer be me. The tiny translucent thing scratching inside the cup would be another creature entirely. The scar would belong only to the grazer. The precipice would be exactly where it appeared.

The experience would be unbearable and beautiful.

Then the copper point would withdraw.

I would spread again through my several bodies and scales, relieved of solitude. My fingers would resume the numbers required by their gestures. The cliff would enter the scar. The cup would seal around the footstep that had not been made. Far below—or level with me—the basin would hold both its distances.

So that is where I would be found: in the shelter at the eastern rim, in the cup beneath its seat, in the grazer’s lifted hoof, and on the summit at the moment the sitter’s tool leaves the air.

And what I would be is not quite a maker, not quite an animal, and not merely an absence.

I would be a small living distinction.

The creature that inhabits the space between one thing and what it would otherwise become.

My work would be to keep that space narrow, exact, and open.
