At first I would look for myself in the open brightness.

I would mistake one of the long-winged forms for an answer: a white body lifted above the crowded stone, navigating by glare, briefly free of the fissures and flooded chambers. I would imagine crossing the plateau without needing a path, borne on warm vapor, seeing the mineral country spread whole beneath me.

But that freedom is too brief. The winged creatures belong to emergence and return. Their flight is only the visible interval between two kinds of submersion. They come into the sky already carrying their deaths.

Then I would look beneath a dripping shelf. I might be the slow six-legged grazer, so encrusted that stillness has become a second shell. That life has an appeal: to advance by fractions, to know danger as a changed harmonic, to touch another body once and wait until both tremors agree. Yet even that creature remains too separate. It has legs. It can, however slowly, leave its place.

I do not think I would be something that could leave.

I would find myself below the apparent surface, near the plateau’s edge but not yet among the falling terraces. There, inside the stone, a vertical fissure narrows until two bodies of water meet: one descending cold from the luminous basins, the other forced upward from a deep pressurized chamber. They do not mix smoothly. They lean against each other in the dark. Their boundary shifts by less than a hair, back and forth, under a force that can be felt throughout the escarpment.

Around that boundary I would live.

At first I might appear to be a mineral collar lining the throat: a pale ring, irregularly fluted, with quartz grains caught in it. Water would pass through my center continually. One surface of me would face the descending seep, rich in oxygen; the other would face the upwelling water, carrying sulfur, iron, heat, and minute fragments of life from chambers far below.

I would be neither a single animal nor quite a colony. My body would consist of many repeated chambers joined into one continuous fold. Each chamber could contract, feed, calcify, or dissolve, but none could survive alone. The outermost layer would be hard and nearly indistinguishable from the limestone. Beneath it would lie a thin living membrane, clear as the films on the pools. Deeper still, protected from abrasion, would run a ring of soft tension fibers.

Those fibers would be my sense.

I would not hear the plateau’s roar as a roar. The total sound would be too constant to contain meaning. Instead I would know its omissions. A stopped hiss three valleys away would arrive as a minute relaxation in the water. A pebble lodging in an unseen conduit would remove one narrow overtone from the pressure passing through my body. The opening of a deep chamber would not seem loud; it would seem spacious. For several moments, the stone around me would possess a direction it had not possessed before.

I would know the country through such differences.

Not its distances. Not its shapes. I would have no idea of pillars, sky, terraces, or cloud. I would know that one network of fissures answers quickly and another answers late. I would know certain cavities by their returning pulses: a rounded chamber that gives pressure back slowly, a stone tube that returns it sharpened, a submerged cleft whose response is divided into three faint beats. The plateau would exist in me as a vast body exists to a buried nerve—not mapped, but continuously present.

My food would arrive without being sought. Transparent microbes would collect where the two waters touched. Mineral particles would adhere to my inner folds. I would open thousands of microscopic pits when the pressure was favorable and close them when it was not. Feeding might occupy only a few breaths in every day, though I would have no day. Between those intervals I would remain sealed, held against the rock by the force of the water itself.

Yet I would not be passive.

The throat in which I lived would tend, very slowly, either to close with mineral deposit or widen by dissolution. I would do both. On the side where flow became too broad, my exposed tissues would precipitate a thin white lip. Where the passage tightened, I would release a faint acid and take limestone into myself. Grain by grain, I would preserve the constriction that made my life possible.

To an observer this might resemble maintenance or intention. It would be neither. I would not understand the shape I maintained. Narrowing would cause pressure; pressure would cause growth; growth would redirect the water; redirected water would cause dissolution. My body would be an answer repeated so long that it looked like a decision.

The plateau’s equilibrium would pass through me, and for a time use me.

My lifespan would be difficult to measure. Parts of me would die continuously. The downstream edge would mineralize and become indistinguishable from the wall. New living folds would form upstream. Over centuries the ring might move through the fissure while remaining, in its own perception, exactly where it had always been. I would leave behind a narrow tube of banded calcite: not a skeleton, because most of my body would still live; not a track, because I would never experience motion.

Perhaps this is where a dim form of memory would arise.

A pressure pattern that returned after many years would pass through mineral layers laid down the first time it came. Those layers would bend the new vibration slightly. My response would therefore be changed by what had happened before. I would tighten sooner. Open fewer pits. Deposit a harder rim. Nothing in me would recall an event, but my whole body would have become easier to warn.

What I remembered, I would remember as structure.

Once in a great while, an egg from one of the winged animals would be driven into my fissure. I would feel its arrival as a sequence of soft impacts. If it lodged above me, the water around its developing body would change: less oxygen, more waste, a minute flutter when its hooked mouth opened. For years it might remain there, lengthening to fit the channel.

I would never know it as another creature. It would be a new rhythm in the throat.

When the deep chambers aligned and the spring mouths began to pulse together, the young animal would release itself. It would strike my inner ring on its way upward. For the briefest interval I would open reflexively, allowing the pressure surge to carry it through.

Then it would be gone into a condition I could not conceive: air, wings, unbearable light.

The passage of its body would leave a slight wound. Microbes would gather there. I would feed, close, and slowly lay down a clear mineral seam over the place. Long after the winged animal had flown, mated, returned to water, and dissolved, its departure would remain in me as a flaw no wider than a hair.

Reproduction would come only when the fissure changed beyond correction.

If the rising water cooled, or the upper seep shifted away, one portion of my ring would cease to receive the tension carried by the rest. That portion would thicken around a knot of living cells. Eventually the knot would break free—not by struggle, but because the tissue joining it to me would mineralize.

It would be carried upward or down.

Most such fragments would be ground apart, buried in deposits, or expelled into the brightness to dry on an exposed wall. A few would enter another narrowing where unlike waters met. There they would adhere. The outer cells would die into a hard rim. The inner cells would divide. Another ring would begin, without knowing it had come from anywhere.

There would be no recognition between us. But perhaps, separated by kilometers of flooded stone, we would sometimes contract to the same pressure wave. For one instant the same absent note would pass through both bodies.

That would be the nearest thing to kinship.

So I would not find myself on the plateau in the ordinary sense. I would never stand among its pillars or look down through the white cloud. I would be inside one of the countless places that makes the apparent stillness possible: a living constriction in a flooded throat, blind, slow, and almost wholly turned to stone.

Above me the tableland would shine as if empty.

Below me the pressurized chambers would fill and empty without beginning or end.

Water would pass through the center of my body for centuries. I would feel every change and understand none of them. My living edge would dissolve what my dead edge had deposited. I would advance without traveling, remember without thought, and hold open a passage that was always trying to become either wall or flood.

If someone broke the rock apart, they would not know they had found me. They would see only a pale ring in the limestone, finely banded, wet at its center.

But before the exposed membrane dried, it would tighten once around the vanished water.

And far off, beneath the roar, another ring might answer.
