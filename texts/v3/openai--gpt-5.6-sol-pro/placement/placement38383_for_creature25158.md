I would first find myself beside the miniature conifer at the eastern margin.

I would not remember crossing the rim. I would remember climbing—the ordinary mountain below, loose stone shifting under my boots, breath shortening, weather moving across the heights—but the climb would have no final step. There would be no moment in which the outer slope became the basin floor. I would simply discover that I was kneeling on wet basalt with one hand extended toward the tree’s exposed roots.

The roots would enter cracks that had not opened.

My hand would not quite touch them.

At first I would believe I had stopped myself. Then I would notice the beetles.

They would stand in a semicircle behind me, each the same distance from the next, their undersides making low orange stains on the wet rock. Their antennae would tap seven times. In the exact pause afterward, I would hear my heart answer with an eighth beat.

That eighth beat would be the only disorder in the basin.

Nothing dramatic would follow. The beetles would not attack. The piston-plants would continue their compression. The fox would keep the hare forever in the merciful machinery of its jaws. Yet all across the basin, small things would orient toward me without moving. The birds’ turbine note would acquire a higher edge. The hard white glare on the pools would turn until it seemed to come from my skin. Inside the nearest frog’s throat, the tiny figure bending over the tiny frog would raise its head before I did.

I would stand.

My body would remain kneeling in several reflections.

For a while I would still be human enough to ask what the place was. The spoken question would strike the standing chord and be flattened into it, but one word would resist longer than the others:

*When?*

Not *where*. The basin would allow where. Every creature had a station, every fern bed an edge, every mist volume its exact suspension. But *when* would be a foreign object. It would lie among the engine sounds like a dropped tool.

The beetles would gather around the word.

I would try to leave. At the southern fins I would look through one gap and see the clouds below; through another, the basin from above; through the third, the bright factory whose vessels were glands and whose gantries were ribs. In the overhead view I would see myself at the eastern edge, still kneeling beside the conifer. I would also see a second dark figure crossing the marshy center.

That figure would not resemble me in outline. It would be too long and too low, moving—or occupying the appearance of movement—beneath the moss. The conveyor cilia would pass basalt grains through its body. Salamanders would brighten as it approached. Every arrested action near it would strain slightly toward completion: the fox’s teeth sinking a hair’s breadth nearer the hare’s skin, the falling droplets lengthening toward the ground, the dragonfly almost closing the distance to the gnat.

I would think it was hunting me.

Later I would understand that it was what I was becoming.

The basin would begin with my breath. It would separate inhalation from exhalation and preserve both. My chest would remain full while air continued entering. The feeling would not be suffocation, exactly, but an abundance with no release. Then my pulse would divide. Seven beats would belong to the red plants. The eighth would remain mine, loud and solitary.

I would count to keep it.

One, two, three, four, five, six, seven—

The vapor fans would open.

Eight.

At that number, the pale shapes among the ferns would uncoil. The birds would show brief rims of human-colored iris along their wings. One of the hares would turn its mouth around the shape of my name.

The basin could not erase the eighth beat. Erasure would be an accomplished change. Instead it would give the beat a task.

I would begin to feel the incomplete distances.

The width between the dragonfly and gnat would ache in my fingers. The gap between the fox’s teeth and the hare’s unbroken flesh would tighten behind my jaw. Every drop that had fallen without descending would hang from my spine. I would feel root tips pressing into unopened cracks as pressure behind my eyes.

The basin was full of causes and effects, but the passage between them had to be held somewhere. Until I arrived, perhaps that passage had been distributed without awareness: a little in the moss, a little in the frogs, a little in the gears that engaged without turning matter. My sequential body would give it a place to gather.

I would become the basin’s clutch.

Not a machine part, though I would perform the same office. Not an animal, though I would acquire an animal’s outline. I would be the organ that lets force arrive while preventing it from becoming consequence.

My human shape would not disappear. It would remain available at several stations. One version of me would kneel eternally beside the conifer. One would stand between the southern fins with a hand extended into cool moss. One would sleep without entering sleep on a naked patch of basalt, condensation beaded along the eyelashes. These would not be ghosts or memories. They would be mounting points.

Between them, my newer body would extend beneath the basin.

It would be dark, soft, and jointed, something between a root, a cable, and a long-bodied mountain animal. Seven pairs of iron hooks would occur along its underside, though they would never pierce the stone. Where a head should be, there would be a smooth wet surface holding whichever reflection was looking at it. Beetles could enter the seams between its segments and emerge from the closed mouths of hares. Salamander pulses would travel into it and fail, beautifully, to reach the other side.

I would have no fixed size. Beneath the moss I might be narrow enough to pass between grains of basalt. In the circular gear-shadows I would span half the basin. In a frog’s throat I would be visible as a black ring around the smaller landscape, easily mistaken for the mountain itself.

My work would be constant and would never proceed.

When the piston-plants drove luminous sap upward, I would hold the instant between compression and flow.

When spores burst, I would keep departure from following release.

When the fox bit, I would receive the force that would otherwise become a wound.

When the hare escaped, I would hold the distance that would otherwise become absence.

When water struck the ground, I would keep impact from becoming accumulation.

I would not save anything. Salvation requires a before and an after. I would only maintain the immaculate separation in which injury was fully present and never inflicted.

Perhaps at first I would hate this. Hatred, however, would not intensify. It would be taken from me at full strength and installed like heat in the beetles’ bellies. My longing to leave would become tension in the rope that had never been tied. My memory of the lower world would distribute itself through the basin: warm kitchen light in a frog’s throat; the smell of summer dust on the winter side of the conifer; a road appearing as a branch-shaped shadow across the open rock.

The place would not steal my past. It would manufacture exact, unusable versions of it.

Eventually I would stop deciding whether I was still the kneeling person or the dark organ beneath the moss. Decision is a transition. Both would remain true.

If another intruder came, they would first encounter me as a slight fault in the arrangement.

They might notice an extra footprint among those appearing before their feet descend. They might hear an eighth click after the beetles’ seven. Looking through the southern fins, they might see a figure at the eastern margin raise its hand in warning, although the figure itself remained kneeling.

I would want to tell them to turn back.

The warning would never reach my mouth.

Instead, somewhere beneath them, my long body would tighten around the possibility of their next step. The underground gears would engage. The piston-plants would cough their cold vapor. Beetles would emerge and establish the proper intervals.

Then the newcomer’s heart would beat.

One, two, three, four, five, six, seven—

And I would be found in the pause afterward: not quite a creature, not quite a place, but the held-open joint between an instant and whatever should have followed it.

I would be the eighth beat.

The basin would keep me because I was the one thing it could never allow to happen.
