At first I think I would live high in the cleft, among the fog nets.

I imagine going up before dawn while the town is still breathing inward, climbing past the pigeon walls with a coil of grass cord across my shoulders. I would know fog by taste before it became visible. I would distinguish a net trembling under droplets from one disturbed by a bat, a swift, or a failing knot. My hands would always smell faintly of horsehair and the bitter oil used to preserve fiber.

But I do not think I could belong entirely to the opening. To work so close to the slit is to attend always to what may arrive from outside. My nature would be more inward than that.

Then I think I would be low in the town, where the oldest stairs have been worn almost flat and the walls are thick with the dead. Perhaps a mason-undertaker, narrow enough to enter abandoned rooms, patient enough to open a wall one finger’s width at a time. I would know by the color of dust whether there were bones behind the plaster. I would greet every discovered bundle, even if no one could name it.

But that, too, is not quite where I would be.

I would be found halfway between the entrance and the roof slit, at a place no one calls the middle because the cleft has several middles depending on what is being carried.

There would be a doorway behind me, though it had led only into fallen stone for longer than anyone remembers. One side of its frame would support a pigeon shelf belonging to an upper household; the other would anchor a ladder maintained by a family below. Bees would occupy a crack over the lintel. Beneath the threshold, where the stone stayed warm after the afternoon reversal, a dog would sleep whenever it was not needed elsewhere.

My room would not properly be a room. It would be a widened landing roofed with old ladder rails, enclosed by curtains that could be tied back when traffic was heavy. People would pass through it to reach three different levels. Children would put their hands on my sleeping shelf as they swung around the corner. Baskets would travel above my head. Arguments begun below would arrive altered by the time they reached me.

I would be the keeper of the second way.

Every inhabited place in the cleft needs more than one route from it, though the second route may be nothing more than a removable rung, a neighbor’s roof, a crawlspace behind a goat pen, or a cord kept coiled inside another family’s wall. Fire makes the need obvious, but fire is not the only danger. A stair can split. A bridge can be cut down for repair. A pregnant body may not fit where it passed the season before. A hawk’s nest may close an upper ledge. A death may seal a household while the body dries. Two families in dispute may discover that they still require passage through one another’s rooms.

I would keep these other ways usable.

Not own them. No one could own such a thing. I would remember their permissions.

This ladder may be climbed in darkness but not while the figs are being cut. That balcony may be crossed by children, elders, and anyone carrying flame, but not by goats. This house may be passed through during smoke, collapse, birth, or judgment, though never by a creditor. This rope belongs to the widow at its upper end, the brothers at its lower end, and the household whose dead are sealed around the anchor.

The knowledge would not be written. I would carry some of it as knotted cords: a black knot for a smoke route, doubled fiber for a passage that could bear a loaded adult, pigeon quill for a way open only from above. Yet the cords would be reminders, not maps. The true map would be made of people.

I would know who slept deeply, whose dog bit strangers, whose floor could support a grain jar but not three running children. I would know where an elder had grown too stiff to turn sideways, where roots had narrowed a crack, where a new oven had made an old escape impossible. I would know which feuds were sharp enough that necessity must be declared before a threshold was crossed.

My work would look like wandering.

I would move through households without appearing to visit them, testing rungs, leaning my weight against anchors, pushing a hooked pole into darkness to find whether a crawlway remained clear. I would carry spare cord, wedges of hard wood, a horn-handled knife repaired in three metals, chalky stone for marking hidden holds, and a hood to lower over angry bees.

Children would follow me because second ways are often their first ways. They would show me openings adults had forgotten or could no longer enter. In return, I would teach them which shortcuts were merely secret and which must be kept clear for everyone. A secret route may be cluttered with treasures. An emergency route must not contain even a broken bowl.

I would dislike the plain, though I would have gone there once.

Perhaps when young I had believed that a person might become larger beneath an open sky. I would have descended with clay carriers, walked until the upland lost its shape behind me, and discovered that without walls my body had no edges. At night the stars would have seemed to fall in all directions. I would have slept with one hand clenched around a tent peg, needing to feel that at least one thing was anchored.

After returning, I would never again mistake openness for freedom.

Freedom would mean another hold within reach.

I would have no spouse confined to one household, or perhaps I would have had several attachments that never hardened into a single domestic claim. The keeper of second ways cannot become wholly part of one level. People would foster me upward and downward. I would eat where work ended. My smoke smell would be difficult to place because it came from many hearths.

This would make some distrust me. A person who knows every back route also knows how to steal, overhear, flee, or enter a house while its owners sleep. I would therefore be held to harsher customs than most. I could not carry an unsealed jar through another family’s rooms. I could not repeat words heard while passing behind a wall. I could not use an emergency route to avoid a debt or an unwelcome judgment. If accused, I would surrender my hook and cords before speaking.

Trust would be my only store of water.

In ordinary times I would possess little authority. People would complain when I asked them to move a basket or cut back a beloved vine. They would promise to repair a rung and fail to do so. They would call me a mouse, because I appeared through places not intended for entry, or a draft-dog, because I was always sniffing at doors.

Then smoke would rise against the proper flow, or a bridge would fail under a loaded sling, and my voice would become law for the length of the danger.

Not because of rank. Because I would know where else the town could go.

As I aged, I would climb less. The younger route keepers—though they might not yet accept the name—would bring me news. They would reproduce passages with their hands: three rungs, left shoulder under stone, foot across the pigeon niche, then down by the red cord. I would correct them when they mistook nearness for connection.

From my landing I would listen to traffic. A good second way leaves traces even when seldom used. Dust falls differently from a maintained ladder. An unweighted bridge answers the draft with a clean creak. A blocked crawlway muffles mice. If children stop emerging from a particular crack, something has changed there.

My body would gradually become like the work: bent at several angles, one shoulder lower from carrying cord, fingers permanently curved around absent holds. I might lose much of my sight, but that would matter less than strangers imagined. The town announces its shape through tension and echo.

I would know a person approaching by what moved around them.

Pigeons lift for one woman but not her sister. A certain child makes jars hum by running too close to the wall. One mason tests every rung twice even in haste. A grieving person puts their weight down carefully, as if trying not to spill what is inside them.

When judgment was needed over a passage, I would be asked to attend. My decisions would rarely say that one family had the right and another did not. Instead I might give one household the crossing and another the shade beneath it; require the upper family to maintain the rope while the lower family supplied rungs; permit passage during the upward draft but reserve the landing during the downward warmth.

A route survives only if everyone along it has reason not to destroy it.

That would be the principle of my life.

Near the end, I would begin giving away my cords. Not all at once. One to the child who noticed a root lifting a stair. One to the widow who always left her roof unbarred during oven fires. One to the goat keeper who could lower an animal without breaking its legs. None would inherit the whole office because there had never been a whole office to inherit.

When I died, there would be some debate over where to place me. Every household through which I had kept passage might make a claim, while none could say I belonged inside its wall.

At last they would dry me in the blocked doorway behind my landing.

My bundle would be seated upright rather than folded. One hand might be wrapped around the old hook. The wall built before me would be deliberately thinner at the shoulders. Not weak—never weak—but made so that in some future emergency I could be opened through.

That would be fitting.

Even as an ancestor, I would remain a second way.

Children would sleep on the landing and know the shape behind the plaster. Bees would continue above my head. The ladder would pull against one side of me, the pigeon shelf press against the other. Feet would lower the threshold grain by grain. Voices and baskets would pass.

Perhaps generations later no one would remember my personal name. They would touch the wall before taking the hidden route and say only, “Ask the one who kept this open.”

So I would become what I had always been becoming: not quite a person, not quite a passage, but a permission held inside the stone.

A joint in the Breathing House.

A place where, when the first way failed, the cleft could bend without breaking.
