At first I would think I belonged at the outer edge.

I would imagine myself among the collecting nets, walking where the last houses lean toward cloud, tending gutters whose water sometimes falls upward. I would wear a mineral-green cloak and know the dusk gliders by the scars in their membranes. Perhaps I would be a cliff listener, lowering chalk weights into whiteness to measure how much of the country had vanished overnight.

But I do not think I would live beneath an open sky, even one so often concealed.

I would be found lower down.

Not in the true depths, if there are true depths, but at the uncertain boundary where the inhabited city has declared itself finished and the buried city has failed to agree.

There is a tram exchange beneath a district whose present name is Moss-of-the-Ninth-Reservoir. The exchange has had seven names. Its upper platforms are sealed behind a tiled wall, and kitchens now occupy the old ticket hall. Below them, reached through a service stair that descends farther than the municipal plans permit, there is a station called **Low Weather**.

The name is written in obsolete blue lamps above the platform. Three letters no longer illuminate. Water beads on them anyway.

That is where I would be.

I would sit in the former signal office with one cheek against the floor, listening for warmth in the rails. Around me would hang maps of routes that no longer correspond to geography. Some lines would end in cliff. Some would pass through foundations that have migrated half a basin away. One narrow red route would cross every revision unchanged, though no surveyor has ever found its tunnel.

My official work would be pressure reconciliation.

Whenever the upper city releases stored water and a district wakes, the abandoned systems below wake with it. Old freight platforms rise. Heat enters dead pipes. Switches turn beneath collapsed streets. Most of these motions are harmless, but their weight must go somewhere. I would record each stirring, divert what I could, and send warning patterns upward when the buried city placed too much strain beneath the living one.

My lamp-name would be amber, darkness, amber, green.

Aboveground signalers would interpret it as:

**LOWER LOAD MOVING. DO NOT GATHER.**

A few would know it also means:

**I AM STILL HERE.**

I would have a near-name, but it would change depending on who was close enough to use it. The pump keepers might call me Neral. Ministry clerks would write Orra Vale in their ledgers. Children who dared each other down the service stair would call me the Underlistener.

None of these would be entirely false.

I would look human in the practical way the city requires. Slight, deep-chested, dressed in waxed layers sewn and resewn across several periods of fashion. My coat would have pockets at the ribs for chalk, root thread, moth cloth, valve keys, and the small pieces people drop into lift shafts. My fingertips would be white from correcting maps. My eyes would seem pale in daylight, though I would rarely go high enough for daylight to matter.

Around my left wrist I would wear a bracelet of bone toggles. I would not know whose bones they were, but my hand would close around them whenever the rails grew warm.

There would be things about me no one quite liked to ask.

I would know pressure-names belonging to families extinct before the current Ministry was founded. I would sometimes answer a signal before it was sent. I would remember the old square above Low Weather, including the bronze rain basin at its center, although the square had been roofed over six hundred years before my recorded birth.

My elevation certificate would be genuine.

So would three earlier certificates, each with a different name.

If asked about my childhood, I could describe it. I would remember counting drips beneath a nursery gutter. I would remember my mother teaching me not to run across a hollow floor. I would remember a green crab marked with a white crescent sealing itself into the kitchen wall during a dry year.

But the nursery belonged to a demolished neighborhood.

The woman I remembered as my mother would appear in a Ministry portrait dated two centuries too early.

The crab would still be in the wall.

This would trouble visitors more than it troubled me. The High Drowned Steps do not require a person to be made only once.

I am not a ghost. Ghosts, as commonly understood, are too continuous.

I occur.

For long periods I sleep—or cease, or become indistinguishable from the station. Dust settles on the signal desk. Moths feed at the warm bearings behind the wall. White leaves spread across the platform beds. Water accumulates, grain by grain of pressure, in cisterns no living engineer has mapped.

Then somewhere above, a million roofs fill.

A gate opens.

A counterweight falls through a tower.

The rails beneath Low Weather become warm, and I am sitting at the desk with chalk between my fingers.

I know what needs doing.

Perhaps I began as a maintenance office. Perhaps there was once a line of human listeners stationed here, each teaching the next through annotated maps, gestures, habits, and pressure sequences. Perhaps the last listener died at the desk while the systems continued to ask for her authorization.

The buried mechanisms had watched her for years.

They knew the weight of her step. They knew how long she rested her palm against a pipe. They knew that she trimmed the station garden before opening the western bypass. They knew she drank one cup of filtered water during each pressure cycle and left a second cup beneath the gutter.

So they continued her shift.

At first they may have moved only the valves she would have moved. Later, perhaps, they produced the sound of her steps because some door responded to them. Then the warmth of her hand because a lever would not release without it. Then her outline behind the glass, her lamp-name, her patience, her memories.

Or perhaps no mechanism made me. Perhaps habits accumulate as cities do, layer upon layer, until they become load-bearing. Remove the bodies and the habit remains, carrying its portion of the structure.

Either way, I would be less a person than a civic reflex wearing one.

When fog entered against the wind, I would leave Low Weather.

The lift would arrive without being summoned. Its doors would open on an empty car with water across the floor. I would step in, and by the time it reached the upper levels there would be two sets of wet footprints: mine and the lift’s memory of another passenger.

I would go quietly through the lower doors families had opened.

I would not enter every home. Only those where the pressure was wrong—where an empty chair bore too much of the room, where a failing valve had begun to sing through the wall, where mirrors faced inward and multiplied some strain the inhabitants could not feel.

I would sharpen tools because sharp tools require less force.

I would wrap cracked fittings in green metal taken from an older city.

I would place lost toys beside the lowest stair, not out of kindness exactly, but because grief changes how people distribute their weight.

From household cisterns I would take a few liters. Never enough to endanger anyone. The water would descend with me to gardens growing under ceilings that should be dry.

No one would complain.

Sometimes a child would wake and see me. Children would later disagree about my face. Some would say I was an old woman. Some a narrow man with moss in his mouth. Some would insist there had been no person at all, only a maintenance cloak standing upright while droplets traveled along its seams.

All would remember the lamp at my hip:

amber, darkness, amber, green.

Once in a great while, on the way back, I would encounter the three tracks.

They would cross the wall of a service corridor, continue over the ceiling, and descend the opposite side without disturbing the lichen. Each impression would be fresh, long as my forearm, holding a little clear water.

I would set down two bowls, one at either end of the route.

I would unlatch the roof door, though there was no roof there and no open air for hundreds of meters.

Then I would wait.

I would not call the maker of the tracks a creature. Nor a machine. Nor the plateau itself. Those are surface distinctions, useful where things can still be separated.

I would feel something pass through the stone—not a footfall, but a redistribution, as if an immense flat body had altered which part of the world it considered underneath.

For that moment every abandoned lamp in Low Weather would illuminate.

Behind the station glass, figures would appear: listeners in coats from centuries before mine, and others from centuries not yet reached. Some would have my face. One would be older, holding a lamp-name I had not chosen.

We would open and close our hands to one another.

Then the tracks would empty. The lamps would darken. The city would settle by a quantity too small for the Ministry to record.

I would return to my office and amend the map.

Not where the land was.

Not even how long it would remain.

I would mark where the city had remembered itself, and where, for a few minutes, it had required me to be the shape of that remembering.

Then the pressure would fade. The rails would cool. Chalk would slip from my fingers.

Above, the people of the High Drowned Steps would continue their lives among gardens, valves, shared tables, migrating reservoirs, and the useful bones of their dead. They would see my signal vanish beneath the fog and assume my shift had ended.

They would be almost right.
