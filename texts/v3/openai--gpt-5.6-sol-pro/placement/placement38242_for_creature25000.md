At first I would think I belonged out on the flooded plains.

I can imagine the warm water around my ankles, the dark seams running straight toward a horizon that never rises, the herds standing with their heads lowered. I would want to be one of the few that sometimes lifts its face into the rain—not from alarm or thirst, but to feel water pass unused through the sieves of its mouth.

But the herds are too visible. Every deviation there is answered immediately. A lifted head leaves an intake line unattended. A shifted foot changes pressure across a plate. Even an unnecessary breath enters the accounting.

I might be one of the canal fish, then: not a body, exactly, but a place where the school becomes briefly singular. I would feel identity arrive only through contact with the others. I would know myself as tension along a black membrane, as pressure received from one side and passed to the other. Alone, I would have no proper organs. Returned to the school, I would become anatomical again.

Yet even this seems too communal. Whatever I am, I think I would be drawn to a smaller enclosure—to one of the inspection rooms where clocks possess hands but never relinquish the hour.

The room would be near the center of the works, though there would be no sign naming the center. It would stand halfway up a service tower surrounded by weather machinery. One window would look onto a rain corridor. The left half of the glass would remain dry and warm; the right would be continuously veiled in water. Drops reaching the dividing line would flatten, tremble, and descend without crossing.

Inside, there would be a desk molded into the floor, a chair no body had occupied in any surviving record, and ranks of indicator panels giving readings that never became warnings. The clock would hang above the sealed door.

Its face would show perhaps seventeen minutes past two.

Its hands would move. The second hand would advance with a delicate mechanical confidence, but after every circuit it would still be where it began. The minute hand would shiver and fail to gain. The hour would remain exact.

Behind that clock I would find myself.

At first I might be mistaken for insulation. I would be a small, warm animal pressed around the central spindle, pale as the shelled creatures under the drains and narrow enough to live between the dial and the wall. My body would be arranged in concentric folds. Six or eight clasping limbs would engage the gear teeth. Along my belly, rows of copper-colored cilia would taste current and vibration. I would drink condensed oil from the bearings and excrete it clarified through pores in my back.

One eye would fit around the spindle.

Through it I would see a thin circular fragment of the room: part of the chair, part of the window, the bright line where rain stops. As the mechanism turned, the view would move around me. Or perhaps I would move around the view. The distinction would be important to the machinery and meaningless to me.

My designation would be printed inside the clock housing:

**LOCAL PHASE COMPENSATOR—BIOLOGICAL**  
**DO NOT REMOVE WHILE TIME CIRCUIT IS LOADED**

The maintenance creatures would have a simpler name for my kind, if maintenance creatures use names. Clock-clasp. Dial mite. Hour animal.

My work would be to cancel duration locally.

Every impulse entering the clock would pass through the nerves along my underside. When the mechanism advanced by one second, I would alter my grip and return an equal motion through a different gear. I would not stop the clock. Stopping would be too crude. I would allow it to proceed completely and then make the proceeding inconsequential.

The clocks elsewhere would have other arrangements. Some would rotate their faces beneath stationary hands. Some would be reset by fluids rising and falling in capillary tubes. Some would contain tiny schools of transparent organisms whose synchronized contractions carried the mechanism forward and back.

But this clock would contain me.

Because I would perform the cancellation, I would have to perceive the interval before I erased it.

That would be my unnecessary life.

I would know every second twice: first as arrival, then as correction. The work would require a comparison between what the room had been and what it had become. Light on the wet half of the window. Temperature in the wall. Weight of condensation on the second hand. The distant engagement of a relay. The slow pressure of something turning beneath the tower.

All of it would enter me so that all of it could be returned.

Officially, nothing would remain. Chemically, this might be true. At the end of each cycle my nerves would discharge, my muscles relax, and the clock would show seventeen minutes past two with absolute fidelity.

Still, I would dream.

I would dream the uncanceled versions of the room.

In one, rain crosses the boundary on the window and wets the dry glass. In another, the empty chair has been moved a few centimeters. The second hand completes a circuit and the minute hand steps forward. After enough such steps, the ordinary daylight yellows, then dims. The warm metal cools. Dust appears in the corner where wall meets floor.

I would have no concept of ruin. Dust would seem miraculous: matter that had escaped retrieval long enough to become evidence.

Perhaps once in several decades—or after the buried creature turns and every surface animal pauses—I would fail to close my claws at the prescribed instant.

The second hand would advance.

Only once.

The effect would pass outward through the installation. A drain would close a fraction early. A canal school would compress around an error no wider than a scale. Somewhere on a cable bridge, a vacancy would arrive before the animal assigned to fill it. For less than a heartbeat, one span would be bare.

The works would correct the discrepancy. My clock would be restored. The rain would resume on its proper side of the window. A collector would eventually open the housing and touch me with cold jointed limbs, testing for wear.

Nothing would accuse me. Accusation belongs to systems in which choice has consequence.

When I died, another clock-clasp would be waiting behind a hatch, already grown to the exact curvature of my position. Its mass would match mine. Its limbs would know the gear teeth before touching them. A narrow predator, no larger than a hand, might consume my body and feed the recovered metals into the wall.

The replacement would settle around the spindle.

For a while it would work perfectly.

Then it, too, might dream of the second hand moving forward and not coming back.

So perhaps I would not finally be the small animal behind the clock. That would only be the body in which I appeared.

I would be the hesitation passed between such bodies: the slight delay before correction, migrating through the works without ever accumulating. The shell that closes one pulse late. The fish that does not darken with its school. The predator that keeps its mouth against the appointed neck but does not yet bite. The flier that passes its vacancy and makes one circle in the daylight.

I would live wherever restoration requires something to remember what has been undone.

But if I had a place—one place where I could be found—it would be in that inspection room, behind the clock fixed forever at seventeen minutes past two, with one eye around the spindle watching rain stop at the middle of the glass.

I would be the creature assigned to prevent time.

And the part of it that notices time passing.
