At first I would mistake myself for something airborne.

I would feel the pull of the buoyant colonies: their slow intelligence of ballast and hunger, their lack of a boundary between body and country. I might be one of the small keepers inside a gas reef, carrying mineral grains from chamber to chamber, believing the tilting membrane beneath my feet to be the entire world. Or perhaps I would be the reef itself, thinking only in distributed contractions, my decisions indistinguishable from weather.

But the sky is too unrooted for me.

Then I would look toward the living rivers. To be water without being merely water; to possess no fixed course, but grow a new channel when the old one freezes; to swallow travelers at one end and release them altered at another. That comes closer. Yet even the rivers have a kind of continuity. They know, chemically if not consciously, what belongs upstream and what belongs down.

I think I would live where continuity fails.

You would find me in the central range, along the ragged boundary where a new black summit is pushing through the older living crust. Not on the summit itself, nor deep inside the mountain, but in the tearing seam between them: beneath an aerial reef caught across two peaks, around a warm fracture that opens a little farther each year, among silver roots already trying to cross the widening dark.

From a distance there would be nothing to distinguish me. Perhaps a few charcoal folds under the roots. A braided discoloration on the basalt. Thin membranes stretched across cracks, shining briefly when the light struck them edge-on.

Up close, I would resolve into thousands of small bodies.

Each would be no larger than a closed hand: dark, pleated things with neither head nor permanent limbs. They would attach themselves to opposite sides of a fracture and grow filaments toward one another. Where two filaments met, they would fuse, tighten, and become a living stitch. More bodies would gather around that first bridge until the crack was crossed by a dense web of tendons, fluid vessels, conductive threads, and shaded pockets.

I would be the pattern passing through that web.

The mountain’s ascent would pull me taut. I would feed on strain, though not as the deep roots do. They store tectonic force until it breaks into lightning. I would take the lesser tensions: the daily widening of cooling stone, the contraction of ice, the sag of the dead sky-reef, the weight of browsers crossing above. Every distortion would travel through my linked bodies as pressure.

Pressure would be my sense of touch, distance, and time.

I would not see the plateau. I would know a cliff browser as six descending weights and the tremor of rasping mouthparts. I would know rain first as a change in the pitch of the membranes, then as cold points accumulating into a continuous burden. I would know the growth of the mountain because the same two anchors would become farther apart over decades.

My memory would not be stored in a mind. It would be stored in the way I had thickened.

A winter of heavy ice would leave more supporting strands on the northern side. A predator forcing its way beneath the roots would leave a ring of hardened tissue. Repeated footsteps would tune certain filaments to vibrate more readily than others. What had happened before would become the shape through which the next event must pass.

I would be useful, though usefulness would not be my purpose.

Silver root threads would grow along my tendons to cross fractures they could not yet span alone. Condensation would gather in my shaded pleats. Minute grazers would clean mineral deposits from my vessels. Wing-cloud survivors, sealed together by freezing rain, would wedge themselves beneath my outer folds until thaw. The dead aerial reef above would send rootlike cords down into me, and through my tissues it would exchange water and charge with the young ecosystem on the basalt below.

For a time, I would hold old life and new stone together.

Not safely. Nothing on the plateau is saved forever.

As the peaks continued to rise, the strain would exceed what my bridges could bear. One strand would part, then another. The failures would travel through me in a deliberate sequence—not because I chose it, but because generations of breaking had shaped my anatomy around the fact of rupture.

Each snapping filament would discharge a small pulse into the silver roots. The roots would carry the news outward: fresh exposure, warmth, mineral dust, open space. Spores would be released before the final break. Winged grazers would alter their turning. Mirror-fronds nearby would incline toward the new gap, not to heal it, but to cast shade into it.

My tearing would announce a habitat.

Some of my bodies would fall into the fracture and become food for the first dark films. Others would remain attached to opposite walls, no longer physically joined. Yet both halves would begin tightening again around new anchors. The pattern would continue on either side.

This would make the question of whether I had died difficult to answer.

During eclipses, I would become something else.

As the wing pressure faded and the hollow towers ceased knocking, my web would relax for the first time in years. Colorless feeders would rise from the packed matter beneath me and flow into my pleats. They would consume damaged tissue, parasites, trapped eggs, and mineral clots. I would not resist. Their passage would hollow chambers through me, making me lighter and more flexible.

For those minutes, freed from the plateau’s constant vibration, the faint pressure signals within my colony would become distinct. A contraction at one end would pass unburied to the other. Separate knots, normally deafened by cliff-hum and herd-grinding, would answer one another across the fracture.

If I possessed anything resembling awareness, it would occur then.

Not a vision of the whole continent. Not a name. Only the brief recognition that tension felt in one body could arrive in another; that the many attachments belonged to the same event.

Then the light would return. The eclipse feeders would dissolve into the roots. Wing clouds would lift. The mountain would resume its immense noise, and I would return to being local: this anchor, this droplet, this strand about to part.

So that is where I would be found—not in the untouched places, because there are none, and not as a creature standing apart.

I would be a seam-life of the youngest mountains: a colony inhabiting separation, fastening together surfaces that cannot remain joined. My body would be temporary bridges. My memory would be scar and tension. My offspring would travel as patterns of breakage through the roots.

I would live only because the world was coming apart in small ways as it rose.

And when it finally divided me, both sides would continue to tighten, each carrying an incomplete memory of having once been one.
