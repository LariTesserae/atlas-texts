At first I would look for myself at the margin of the chamber.

Perhaps I would be the faint exchange of air through a fissure: not wind, only a difference in pressure, carrying sulfur and a few molecules of water from the plain above. I would enter without illuminating anything, touch the pale filaments, and leave part of myself caught in their hollow tips.

But that would make me merely a visitor. The air belongs equally to elsewhere.

I might be one of the armored grains resting in the dust—a life reduced to a sealed possibility. From the outside I would be indistinguishable from gypsum or volcanic glass. Centuries might pass before the dust shifted enough to press me into a basalt pore. Most likely I would never open. Yet even then I would belong to an imagined future, to the exceptional conjunction in which I might begin.

This hollow does not seem to favor possibilities. It favors what has already accepted its conditions.

So I think you would find me beneath the shaded side of the central mound, under one of the three black growths—but not in the visible crust. I would be farther in, where the pale stone is porous and the old channels narrow until only threads can enter.

I would be one of those threads, or perhaps the place where two threads have nearly met.

“Nearing” would be my principal act.

One strand would descend from the leftmost growth, laying down a translucent lining as it went. Another would rise through an ancient water seam from the middle growth. Between us would remain a distance smaller than the thickness of a hair and, in the measure of this world, several lifetimes.

I would not know whether I belonged to one colony or the other. Identity would be difficult to locate. The living tip advancing through the pore would contain cells descended from one patch, but its survival would depend on salts altered by the other long before its arrival. Dead membranes would hold the only moisture. Iron consumed centuries ago would determine which direction growth could take. What seemed to be my body would mostly be the accumulated work of what was no longer alive.

Perhaps I would be less an organism than a negotiation conducted through stone.

A harmful crystal would begin forming ahead of me. Over nine years I would seal one edge of it. For another twenty, nothing visible would happen. Then a trace compound from the neighboring strand would diffuse across the remaining interval. It would not be a message in the sense of something sent or understood. Still, it would change what I became. A membrane would thicken. One chemical path would cease; another would continue. The tip would turn by a fraction of its width.

That turn would be my closest equivalent to thought.

My consciousness, if the word could still apply, would not consist of an unbroken interior voice. It would be discontinuous and distributed: a repair here, an exchange there, a changed mineral boundary years later. I would have no experience of waiting, because waiting requires a self held intact between events. Instead I would occur briefly whenever some minute difference became consequential.

A century might be, to me, not a long duration but a single complicated sensation.

I would know the chamber without seeing it. The central mound would be a gradient of salt and warmth. The other black growths would be remote alterations in chemistry. The plain above would exist only as rare disturbances in the fissure air: an unfamiliar molecule, an increment of moisture, a particle captured by a filament and carried slowly inward.

I would have no image of the broad basin. No idea that the mound resembled an altar. No knowledge of darkness, because light would not be absent to me; it would be irrelevant. Nor would I understand stillness. From within my scale of being, the chamber would be full of events: gypsum tightening around a pore, basalt releasing iron, a membrane failing and being replaced, a salt boundary creeping the width of a cell.

Eventually the two strands might touch.

There would be no recognition. No awakening of a larger mind. One surface would soften against another, and after some years their matrices would cease to be separable. Compounds would pass across. The three visible patches might become, in one more respect, a single inhabitant.

Or the contact might reveal an incompatibility. One strand might seal itself away. The junction would become a dead seam preserving a trace of both. That too would be a kind of completion.

So if someone entered the hollow with a flame and asked where I was, there would be nothing to point to. Not the black circle itself, not the mound, not even a particular filament beneath the salt.

They would have to return many times across generations and compare the stone molecule by molecule. Then they might discover that, during the span of their visits, a narrow mineral boundary had altered. Two colonies had drawn imperceptibly closer, or had met, or had refused one another.

That alteration would be me.

I would be found in the almost-joined place beneath the mound: neither one being nor many, neither dormant nor awake, but a slow relation becoming physical. My life would not be the occupation of the hollow. It would be the hollow’s smallest unfinished thing.
