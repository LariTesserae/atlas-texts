I would not be in the oldest chambers, where the rootways have grown black and antler-hard beneath the haul roads. Nor in the violet willow rooms, despite their apparent grandeur. Those are crowns: brilliant for a season, conspicuous, repeatedly cut.

I would be farther out, at a place whose importance was accidental.

There is a transfer gallery between the cultivated mine and the newest excavation. It is too narrow for planting machinery and too useful to seal. A main irrigation pipe passes through it, then divides at an obsolete manifold into six smaller lines. Above the manifold, a fault admits water for several days after heavy rain. Below it, an old cable trench runs beneath the wall and disappears toward districts no longer shown on current maps.

The service lamps here have failed one by one. Only an emergency bar remains, glowing red through a cage of wire. Its light does almost nothing for leaves. Frost whitens the upper bolts. The floor is crossed by temporary hoses that have been temporary for twenty-three years.

This is where I would find myself.

At first, I might think I was the manifold.

It is a bulky iron assembly, painted blue many times. Mineral crust has erased the lettering on its gauges. Four valves are locked open; one is locked shut; the sixth turns freely but controls a line abandoned after a roof fall. Every pump stroke reaches it as a blow. Pressure enters through one flange and leaves through the others in different rhythms, making the whole structure shudder like something resisting speech.

Roots enclose it.

They came for condensation, for the minute warmth of friction, for salts left by leaking joints. Willow roots descended from a chamber two levels above. Fern and ivy entered through the cable trench. Fungal cords occupied the insulation around the pipe. Moss spread across the mineral crust, died where the metal dried, and returned when the seals began to weep again.

Over decades, these growths braided together until the manifold became a dense, asymmetrical knot: iron within wood, wood within moss, moss riddled by pale threads finer than hair. Maintenance crews cut the outer layers back, but never completely. To remove them all would require shutting down three districts.

So they leave what does not obstruct the handles.

One worker calls the assembly the Knuckle. Another calls it the Old Heart, though everyone knows the pumps are elsewhere.

I would not be the iron, however.

Iron does not remember a wet season except as rust. It does not prepare.

I would be what has grown around it: a small concentration in the underfield’s distributed body, a place where recurring events have crossed often enough to become expectation.

Through the pipe, I would feel irrigation long before water reached the beds. I would know which pump was running by the shape of its vibration. I would distinguish the smooth pressure of a newly cleaned line from the fluttering weakness of one becoming choked. Rainwater entering through the fault would taste of the surface—acid scrub, road dust, bird droppings, traces of salt from winter maintenance. The pumped water would arrive brown and measured, carrying treatment minerals and the dissolved history of upstream roots.

These would be my nearest equivalents to sound and flavor.

The great ventilation fans would pass through me as a continuous compression. Their roar would not be noise, because I would have no condition of silence against which to recognize it. It would be structure, as gravity is structure. Fine roots would choose sheltered sides of stones according to its vibration. Moisture would gather along the leeward edges of my moss. Every filament I possessed would have been shaped while trembling.

Only when a fan stopped would I perceive it.

Then the gallery would enlarge around me.

Condensation would abandon familiar routes. Spores would descend where they usually remained suspended. The red emergency light would seem stronger because leaves no longer quivered across it. Pressure would ease from a thousand surfaces at once, and what had been the unnoticed foundation of existence would become absence.

That absence would move through me faster than water.

I would pass it onward—not as a warning conceived and sent, but through altered tension, closed pores, changed chemistry. Roots in adjoining beds would receive the change. Fungi would redirect their exchanges. Willow crowns would conserve what they held. Somewhere, perhaps kilometers away, a technician would see the same stoppage as a red symbol on a display.

Neither of us would know the other had felt it.

For long periods I would scarcely be an individual at all. Sugars would pass through me from a lit district toward a dark one. Water would enter and leave. Fungal threads would favor one root, then another. Mites would graze my outer films. A springtail would disappear into a crack beside the fifth valve and emerge dusted with spores. My boundaries would be whatever remained connected during that particular hour.

But the manifold would give recurrence a shape.

The same pulses. The same fault water. The same human hands.

A maintenance worker would visit every ninth shift. I would know this person first as a pattern of impacts: left boot on the braided walkway, right boot on the stone curb, tool case placed beside the pipe. Then warmth against the valve wheel. A lamp moving over my surfaces. Carbon dioxide briefly thickening the nearby air.

The worker would clear my growth from the gauge faces but leave the moss beneath the joints, because the moss reveals leaks sooner than inspection powder. Sometimes a glove would rest on the upper pipe while readings were recorded. Sometimes the last of a drink would be poured into the cable trench.

Around these repetitions, I would grow.

Not in gratitude. Gratitude would be too singular and too quick. But roots would find the damp place. Moss would retain the spilled water. Microbes accustomed to the salts of the worker’s skin would persist on the wheel. When another worker took over the route, the changed habits would remain perceptible for years.

This would be as close as I came to knowing a person.

My function would not appear on any diagram. Officially, I would be fouling around a pressure junction. Unofficially, I would become a memory of the water system.

When the pumps began their old sequence, fine root tips would swell before the first moisture arrived. When a valve’s rhythm changed, I would close some pathways and open others. If one district became dry, stored water would pass laterally through tissues no engineer had intended to connect. If contamination entered a line, outer roots would die and harden around it, isolating portions of the knot. The underfield would not decide these things in one place. Yet here, because so many lines converged, the responses would accumulate.

I might become an organ without ever becoming necessary.

That distinction would matter. If crews cut me out, the buried country would continue. Water would still move. Coppice would still rise. Moss fragments on tires would still colonize the fresh chambers. I would not be its brain or heart. There would be no fatal vacancy where I had been.

But something would be lost: not knowledge as a worker understands it, but a set of old readinesses. The precise pressure that preceded a seal failure fifteen years ago. The mineral taste of the fault before it opened fully. The difference between harvest machinery approaching and a roof shift traveling through stone. These were never stored as images. They existed as thickened tissue, altered thresholds, routes made easier by previous use.

Memory here would be anatomy.

Eventually the new excavation would approach from the far side of the wall. Blasts would arrive as immense knocks. Dust would sift from the cable trench. The fault would widen by less than a fingernail, and after the next storm water would descend along a route it had never taken before.

A root tip would enter.

That root would be the smallest, palest part of me, though it might originate from a willow crown hundreds of meters away. It would follow the wet fracture beyond the manifold gallery, through pulverized limestone and explosive residue, toward the raw chambers.

For a while it would find nothing hospitable. No substrate, no lamp cycle, only cold stone and intermittent damp. Its first branches would die. Fungal films would outlast them. More roots would follow the dead tissue. A hose dragged through the gallery would carry fragments farther. The movement would have no commander and no guarantee.

Still, I would lean toward the wound.

If I possessed anything like desire, it would not be the desire to escape underground or reach the surface. The open world would mean little to me. I would have been formed by ceilings, rationed rain, purchased light, and air forced through ducts. Freedom, if presented as unbounded sky, might feel only like the catastrophic loss of every containing surface.

My desire would be continuation across interruption.

To bridge the severed line. To enter the new fracture. To keep two drying beds in contact until water returned. To translate the blow in the stone into tightening roots. To make the temporary hose permanent by growing along it.

I would be a conjunction the country had grown around an obsolete machine.

The workers might continue calling me the Knuckle. They would be closer than they knew. A knuckle is not where thought resides. It is where separate lengths articulate, where pressure is accepted and redirected, where repeated work leaves enlargement and scar.

And in the deepest part of my awareness, the pronoun would never settle.

When water entered the manifold, was it coming to me or to us?

When roots crossed into the new excavation, had I extended, or had something merely passed through me?

When a blade cleared moss from the gauge, was I injured, or had one surface been opened so that the larger body could continue to use the machine?

I would have no answer. The question would travel outward along wet tissue until it ceased to be a question at all.

There, in the red-lit transfer gallery, wrapped around blue iron, I would be one of the places where the underfield briefly became dense enough to notice itself—a local memory, a pressure-sense, a scar learning to reach.

Not the buried country.

Not separate from it.

A knot through which its slow becoming passed.
