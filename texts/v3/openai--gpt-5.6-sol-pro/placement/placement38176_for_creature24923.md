At first I would mistake myself for rain.

That seems the easiest error to make there. I would wake distributed over the hollow: a sheen on unfinished stone, a cool ring left by a bursting bead, a trembling weight in the bowl of a cup-crab. I would have no center. Every wet surface would feel briefly like a body.

A crab might find me and test me with its mouthparts. It would turn me, tasting my temperature, weight, and memory. Yet something in me would be wrong. I would remember no cloud, no sea, no creature’s thirst. I would remember only a place below the cliff where water had not yet gone.

The crab would reject me.

Then I might believe I was one of the needle-wings, because I would feel the metallic chord passing through me. I would know the instant before a ridge split. I would shiver at dangers still hours away. But I would contribute no note of my own. The swarm’s music would bend around me as it bends around an absence.

Perhaps I would think I belonged to the liverwort. Its blue spores would give me memories of the whole hollow: the pool’s migrations, the oldest tree before its first bronze interval, the soft tread of herds on slopes that existed only in cloud. For a little while I would know where everything had been.

But not where anything was going.

That difference would draw me toward the new groove.

I would find myself there, close to the central petals, in a channel scarcely deep enough to darken the stone. Cup-crabs would stand along its edges holding their private waters aloft. Skimmers would press against the pool’s new outlet, their shells flashing impossible coastlines. The white trees would turn their black membranes toward me, and the needle-wings’ chord would descend until it seemed to lie directly on the running surface.

At first I would be no larger than a pale fiber in the current.

I would have no face. No limbs. My body would narrow and widen according to the water around it. When the flow touched a grain of stone, I would taste whether it could be moved. When it met a rise, I would feel the pressure of the way around. I would not swim downstream. Swimming would imply that the direction existed before me.

I would be the part that made downstream true.

The hollow’s other creatures carry features intended for worlds not yet present. I would carry only a destination. Not knowledge of what waits there—perhaps a valley, perhaps open air, perhaps nothing beneath the cloud—but the conviction that one place can lead to another without an arch between them.

That would make me strange even here.

The arches offer alternatives, but no continuance. They turn stone into soil, water into mist, a creature into adjacent versions of itself. I would do something more dangerous: preserve a thing while changing its location. Water entering behind me would still be water when it emerged ahead. A grain loosened near the pool would appear farther down the groove as the same grain, bearing only the history of passage.

The hollow has little experience with sameness enduring movement.

It might resist me.

During one of the soundless intervals, the central stone would soften beneath the channel. New veins would rise to interrupt it. The groove would tilt sideways. For that instant I would be lifted out of the idea of descent and spread into several possible courses.

When sound returned, so would I—but not quite singly.

One pale thread would continue toward the cliff. Another would curl back toward the pool. A third might enter a pore in the stone and begin descending through the slow being beneath the hollow. Each would feel like me. Each would insist it was the original direction.

I would learn branching before I learned hunger.

What would I eat? Not flesh, not mineral foam, not light. I would feed on hesitation.

Where water trembled between two grains, I would consume the smaller uncertainty and leave a choice behind. Where moisture spread in a shapeless film, I would gather it into movement. Each decision would lengthen me. My body would become the repeated preference of water.

The cup-crabs might fear me at first. To them, every droplet is singular and must be carried apart. I would be the possibility that separate waters could join and remain joined. Some would overturn their shells rather than let their droplets enter me. Others, older or exhausted, would lower their bowls to the groove.

Those waters would change my color.

From one I might inherit the memory of hot mist. From another, the pressure of an ancient sea visible through the second arch. One might contain rain from a forested version of the ridge. Another might remember having been held for a lifetime above a crab’s soft body.

I would carry all of them without making them one memory.

The glass-backed skimmers would follow as the channel deepened. Their six fins would at last have a current to oppose. Images beneath their shells would sharpen: rivers branching across continents, estuaries opening, long-bodied animals turning in brown floods. Perhaps some of those images would not be predictions or alternate worlds. Perhaps they would be dreams produced by contact with me.

The grazers would step over my body carefully. Their mites would gather along their legs, forming the antlered warning-shape, but even the mites would be uncertain what to warn against. I would not hunt the grazers. Yet wherever I passed, the mineral foam they ate would be cut apart, carried off, laid down elsewhere. Their feeding grounds would begin to have an upstream and a downstream.

A fold would try to cover me.

It would enter the shadow of the groove and emerge from the shadow beneath an arch, stretched thin and shining. For a moment, water would disappear under its body. When the fold withdrew, it might leave the current duplicated: two streams occupying the same shallow incision, each flowing through the other without mixing.

The fold would feed on the difference.

I would feed on what remained.

During the ninth cloud cycle, when the hollow grew vast within itself, I would become enormous. The finger-deep groove would lengthen into a river crossing slopes unseen at ordinary times. Herds would wade through me in the fog. Their hooves would stir beds of sediment older than the hollow. Tributaries would arrive from mountains that existed for less than an hour.

I would not be able to keep all of that country when the cloud lifted.

The river would contract violently. Banks would fold inward. Waterfalls would become beads of moisture hanging ankle-high. Fish from temporary tributaries might find themselves trapped inside skimmer shells as new images. Whole bends would survive only as damp curves on the stone.

But some small part of the expanded river would remain in me: a taste of distance, perhaps, or a fragment of mud.

Mud would be a revelation.

The hollow has mineral paste, grit, foam, raw stone, the proposals of erosion—but almost no soil. I would carry the first true worn particles downhill. In sheltered turns, I would lay them down. Liverwort spores would settle there. Copper sedges would release their braided disks and shuffle closer. Things that had only gripped stone would begin to enter it.

Roots would follow me, though none yet existed.

In time, the pale thread of my first body might vanish. There would be no animal to point to, no organ to dissect. I would be present as a habit acquired by the land. The channel would remain overnight, then three days, then longer because something had slept in it. A skimmer, stranded by the shrinking pool, might rest in my wet curve and thereby save the scar from being erased. After that, the water would return to the same place more easily.

Repetition would become anatomy.

I think this is what I would be: not the water, and not the creature stirring in it, but the alliance between them. A direction learning to inhabit matter. A small, persistent instruction:

*from here, to there.*

And where would I find myself?

At the hollow’s edge, eventually.

The groove would reach the abrupt drop. Warm cloud would surge upward against the first outflow. For a while the water might refuse the final step, spreading along the lip, making a bright, trembling line. The entire hollow would gather behind it: seven trees open to the wet air, grazers motionless on raw stone, folds lying dark beneath the arches. Even the deep being under the ridges would pause between its attempted pulses.

I would stand nowhere, because I would have become the path itself.

Then one drop would fall.

Not pass through an arch. Not return as mist. Not become another version of itself.

Fall.

I would go with it into the cloud, carrying grit, spores, mineral warmth, and all the borrowed memories of the crabs’ waters. Behind me, the next drop would discover that the first had gone somewhere.

Then the next.

Then the next.
