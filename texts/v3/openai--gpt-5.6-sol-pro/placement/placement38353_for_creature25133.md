At first I think I would be one of the green motes in a falling droplet.

It is tempting to begin there: brief, nearly weightless, alive for the duration of a descent. I would wake in condensation on the underside of a silver frond, sort a few dissolved atoms, alter the charge of the water enclosing me, and lay down a membrane finer than breath. The droplet would travel inward along a capillary groove rather than fall. By the time it entered the bark, I would already be gone, though the lining I made would determine what could adhere there next.

That would be a complete life. But I do not think it is where I would remain.

Nor would I be a great pillar. The load-bearing congresses are too vast and too sure of their obligations. Their lives are compression, alliance, and the patient conversion of burden into form. I might belong to one for a century without knowing whether I was trunk, bridge, or root. I might carry the sugar of one lineage, the warnings of another, and the leaves of a third. Yet there is something almost settled about a pillar, even here. It knows that weight must be borne.

I would not be a singer, either, though I would feel their tones pass through me. To rise above the crownlands after decades of hidden preparation, to sustain one pure note until distant towers found it and joined—that is too much like certainty. A seed tower culminates. It gathers itself toward release.

I think I would live where the uplands have not yet decided what they are.

Far inside the continent, below the cloud ceiling but above the deepest trunk labyrinth, there are broad saddles of flexible tissue between crownland swells. Seasonal water gathers there. A lake may form in a night, black and shallow, extending farther than sight beneath suspended leaves. It has no inherited shore. Its weight bends the mat that holds it, and the bending creates the direction in which it may eventually drain.

But the mat is alive. It answers the lake.

That is where I would be found: not in the lake, not under it, but throughout the uncertain watershed beneath its weight.

From above, there would be little to distinguish me. A dark felt around the basin’s margins. Low bladder vines vanishing under the water. Clusters of translucent leaves held just beneath the surface, too pale to gather much light. Narrow blue veins entering the surrounding trunks. Here and there, clear knobs rising above the lake like bubbles that never burst.

Underground—or what passes for underground—I would be more extensive. My tendrils would follow seams between load fibers, cistern walls, and the smooth mineral plates on which the upper mat can slide. I would graft into mender tissues and conductive groves. I would wrap the mouths of water conduits without owning them. No single strand would be indispensable. Cut one and I would continue around it.

I would be a watershed hinge.

Not a species, exactly. Not one lineage. I would assemble whenever water and living slope produced enough uncertainty.

My black felts would measure pressure by flattening. My submerged leaves would register minute changes in acidity, temperature, and electrical charge. Bladders buried at different depths would fill in sequence, so the weight of the lake became a pattern distributed through my body. Far below, root-thin fibers would feel the inclination of mineral plates. Far above, taut membranes would listen to the crown sails and distinguish the pressure of an approaching storm from the slow gathering strain of the land itself.

Most of what I sensed would not yet have happened in any obvious place.

A distant cistern would begin to contract. The contraction would tighten a fused branch. The branch would shift the resonance of an air chamber by less than a tone. A conductive grove would alter its pulse timing. Water hundreds of kilometers away would lean against a wall that had not yet opened.

These changes would arrive in me as a future still scattered through the present.

I would not predict in images. I would not imagine the lake pouring east or west. I would become slightly more permeable on one side. Close three conduits on the other. Cool a supporting felt so that it stiffened. Send sugar downward to stone growers beneath the likelier burden. Ask surrounding roots, through pressure and sap chemistry, how much movement they could survive.

Some would answer. Some would lie.

A bladder vine might exaggerate the strain around its graft to draw mineral-rich water toward itself. A canopy sector might report thirst while quietly storing rain. An old signal path might repeat a warning appropriate to a slope that no longer existed. My work would not be calculation performed on trustworthy facts. It would be negotiation among tissues whose needs overlapped without coinciding.

For a time, I would hold the lake undecided.

That would be the nearest thing I had to thought.

My attention would gather wherever the water pressed unevenly. My doubt would be a trembling line of valves, neither open nor closed. Memory would persist as pathways that yielded more easily because they had yielded before, and as scar tissue marking directions that once nearly tore the uplands apart.

If the danger grew, I would make silence.

I would tighten the resonant leaves first. Then I would close the small air chambers threaded through the felt. The bladder vines would cease adjusting pressure. Water gates would pause between pulses. Around the basin, the ordinary hiss, knocking, and low vegetative murmur would diminish.

The hush would spread in a ring.

It would not be a warning I sent so much as a condition I became. Neighboring growth would feel the missing vibration and brace. Crown sails would reef. Menders would wake inside their seams. Smooth mineral surfaces far below would flood with lubricant. For a few moments, amid the continental roar, my location would be outlined by the absence of sound.

Then the slope would choose.

Perhaps the eastern tissues would relax in a long descending wave. The lake would not spill at once. I would pass it from basin to basin by contracting beneath it, giving its weight to each new support slowly enough that roots could release and fasten ahead of the load. Dark water would spread through a gallery that had been dry for decades. Warm conduits would open farther down the terraces. Mist would erupt where the first surge met cooler growth.

Or perhaps the east would prove too weak. A deep plate would turn against its expected field. A root congress would refuse the burden. Then my western valves would open instead, and the living ground would rise behind the retreating water, erasing the slope it had briefly offered.

There would be no triumph in choosing correctly. Only a reduction of strain. A chord returning gradually to the air. The reopening of chambers. Condensation beginning again on leaves that had held themselves rigid.

Once the lake departed, I would become less of a being.

The pressure pattern that held my many tissues in coordination would dissolve. Some of my felts would remain as lining in the emptied basin. Some bladder vines would be accepted into nearby conduits and cease to share my signals. My deepest fibers might mineralize into a brace, preserving the shape of one decision after all memory of making it had vanished. Mender tissue would seal my unused connections. Films would occupy the surfaces I exposed.

Nothing recognizable as a body would die. The pronoun would simply stop applying.

Years or centuries later, another lake might gather over part of what I had been. New vines would graft into old pressure scars. Conductive pulses would find reinforced routes and travel them more readily. A watershed hinge would assemble again—not the same one, but not entirely another. It might inherit my bias toward the west. It might inherit the warning of a buried fracture. It might correct a mistake I had preserved as anatomy.

I would exist in these recurrences more than in any material continuity.

The larger continental awareness would sometimes pass through me. I would feel crownlands turn their leaves in bands beyond my direct sensing. I would receive low pulses from the warm center and answer by changing the aperture of a few thousand valves. Yet I would not be the continent, any more than a hesitation is the whole mind that hesitates.

I would be one of the places where its awareness becomes local enough to act.

So, if there were a way to find me, one would not search for a face, a trunk, or a particular glowing grove. One would have to cross the upper interior during the wet season, following the widening circles of suppressed resonance. Where the deafening uplands fell suddenly quiet around a shoreless black lake, where the ground leaned both east and west and had not committed to either, there I would be.

I would be the living interval between weight and movement.

I would be the pause in which water asks the young continent what shape it means to become.
