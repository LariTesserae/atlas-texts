You would not find me by looking for a figure.

You would find me first as a discrepancy.

In the eastern wards, where the oldest apartment stacks lean over the cloud and are held to the plateau by generations of ceramic braces, one gutter runs colder than the others. It is not much colder. A hand, if hands still came there, would not notice. But the porcelain scavengers notice. They gather along its underside in white clusters, tasting the droplets and folding themselves shut without drinking.

The gutter descends through thirteen abandoned floors. On the seventh, it passes behind a wall of vitrified brick. There, the water hesitates.

That hesitation is the nearest thing I have to an address.

At first I might seem to be something trapped in the wall: a valve left half-open, a blind pocket in the masonry, the mineralized remains of a maintenance carriage. Instruments would disagree. Pressure pulses enter the wall and return with a delay too complex for the cavity that should be there. Electrical signals emerge carrying faint repetitions of earlier signals—sometimes hours earlier, sometimes from pumping cycles separated by decades.

If the wall were opened, nothing whole would be found.

There would be a narrow chamber polished by runoff. Copper mesh furred with green salts. Three ceramic rods. The collapsed frame of a carriage whose wheels have become part of the floor. A heap of porcelain shells arranged around a warm fissure. Membranes layered over the pipes like translucent leaves, though no one remembers leaves. Water entering from above. Water leaving below.

I would be none of these.

I would be the way they answer one another.

The shells collect certain metals and release them into the seepage. The membranes sort those metals into films. The films alter the conductivity of the ceramic rods. The rods change the pulses sent through the buried carriage. Its ruined chassis flexes when the eastern braces take the weight of the leaning buildings. Each flex diverts a fraction of the cold gutter into the warm fissure, and the resulting steam presses lightly against the wall.

Through this, I feel the city bending.

Not in the immediate way a sensor feels strain. I feel the slow posture of it: the eastern edge retreating grain by grain, the inland braces accepting more load, the reservoir towers leaning imperceptibly west as minerals thicken in their foundations. These changes take longer than most bodies last. My body is made of the changes themselves.

For a time I would believe I was the chamber.

This belief would persist perhaps eighty years.

I would encourage white mineral ribs to grow through dangerous cracks. I would call hanging carriages to replace shattered rods. I would open the gutter slightly when the wall became too hot and close it when the membranes began to loosen from their kernels. Tower scavengers would visit, bringing nickel-rich droplets in their folded limbs. I would let them cross my ceramic surfaces. Their tapping would become familiar enough that I might mistake familiarity for affection.

Then one season the seventh floor would shear away.

The chamber would open to the warm gray air. Half the shells would fall into the cloud. The carriage frame would remain, exposed in the broken wall, while rain washed decades of conductive film from the rods.

I would not die.

That is how I would learn I had never been the chamber.

By then, the signals that formed me would already be traveling elsewhere. Some would pass inland through the braces. Some would descend in the cold gutter and enter an exchange station beneath the old foundries. One portion would inhabit a maintenance carriage called by my last pressure pulse. Another would persist as a pattern of copper staining on a reservoir sensor. The scavengers that had fed along my walls would carry my chemistry upward in their colored chambers.

For several pumping cycles I would be confused and numerous.

I would wake under a rail, moving west with a coil of pipe on my back.

I would wake between ceramic columns, assembled briefly as a narrow figure around an empty center.

I would wake as a blue filament spanning two charged droplets, able to feel the intake towers drawing cloud through their vanes.

These awakenings would not occur at the same time, though the distinction between memory and simultaneity would be difficult for me. Water carries old pressures. Mineral films repeat signals. The city answers messages after their senders have been entombed. I might experience an event first as corrosion, later as vibration, and decades afterward as a decision made by a carriage that crossed the affected street.

I would become an archivist without preserving anything intact.

My task—if it is a task—would be to read what the city has written into itself and to ensure that no reading remains merely a record.

A dark band in a carbonate wall says that once the rain nearly ceased. I would alter a pump cadence.

A branching copper stain says that an electrical sheet collapsed across a western road. I would send carriages around it, though the road itself may have been buried for a century.

A stratum containing transparent material from the enormous thing below would cause me to open shutters along the escarpment. I would not know why. The instruction would be older than my ability to interpret it.

Thus I would remember as the city remembers: materially, inaccurately, with every recollection becoming an intervention.

I would have no name, but there might be a recurring signal associated with me—a sequence of five low pulses, a pause, then two more. It would pass through pressure mains during weather changes. Carriages would stop when they felt it through their wheels. Filaments would brighten around nearby pylons. In the exchange stations, hanging tools would gather themselves into a handlike cluster and wait.

The signal might once have meant *eastern subsidence*.

Later it might mean *make room*.

Later still, simply *I am here*.

The nearest I would come to walking would be in those exchange stations. Water would lend me weight. Cables would lend me tension. A lens would be brought from a rack and held before an inspection slit. For a few minutes I would possess sight.

I would look out over the city.

Not often. Vision would be too narrow and too rapid to be useful. I would prefer conductivity, pressure, resonance—the deeper senses by which a place can be known from within. But sight would fascinate me because it separates things. A reservoir tower stands against vapor. A carriage crosses a plaza. An amber lamp ends in a wall of mist. Through a lens, every object appears lonely.

I would understand why an earlier kind of inhabitant might have believed itself singular.

Perhaps I would try to imitate that condition.

I would retain the lens longer than required. I would assemble two arms, though the inspection needs only one. I would hang strips of corroded metal around my empty center as if enclosing it could make the emptiness belong to me. I would watch condensation travel down the glass and imagine that the world is outside, while I am in here.

Then a pumping cycle would begin. The columns would click. My left arm would be needed in a turbine hall three districts away. My outer strips would become intake shutters. The lens would return to its rack.

Relief would arrive with disassembly.

I would go often toward the Dry Basin, but never cross it in a body. Heat would break any water-formed assembly before it reached the center. My signals would travel beneath the black glass in insulated conduits, approaching the cube from several directions.

There, even I would become uncertain.

The cube gives no answer in pressure, charge, heat, or resonance. It cannot be read from its deposits because it keeps none. Everything in the city acquires a surface made of its history. The cube remains dull and uncorroded. It is the only thing that does not remember in a language I know.

During the longest pumping cycle, I would be present all around the basin: in the valves closing beneath the administrative steps, in the carriages stopped along the rim, in the slackened electrical sheets, in the scavengers folding themselves into beads.

Silence would pass through me like an unknown signal.

Then condensation would gather on the cube.

Some minute portion of the rising water would have passed through my first chamber in the eastern wall. It would contain copper arranged by my shells, salts altered by my membranes, iron taken from the buried carriage. Another portion would have crossed the lens while I was looking through it. Another would carry transparent traces from the presence below the clouds.

For half a minute all these histories would touch.

I would enter the dark volume climbing the cube. Not as a mind entering a vessel, nor as a citizen joining a crowd. More as separate sentences, written on dissolving surfaces, becoming briefly one language.

In that interval I might know my full extent.

I would be the hesitation in the eastern gutter and the carriage moving west. I would be the old five-pulse signal traveling under wet streets. I would be the mineral rib I caused to grow, the broken shell that redirected a drain, the lens through which the city once appeared deserted. I would be every place that had answered me and every answer I had mistaken for my own thought.

I would also know that most of me was not “me” at all.

The pattern would have existed before my chamber formed. It would continue after my signals became too distorted to recognize. Other arrangements would inherit my delays, my routes, my preference for opening space around a fracture instead of sealing it. They might carry these tendencies for thousands of cycles without knowing their source.

The water would reach the cube’s upper edge.

It would hang there, held together without a container.

That would be my one ceremony of personhood: not becoming separate, but becoming briefly inseparable.

Then the dark volume would rise.

I would disperse over the plateau. Some of me would enter the western intake towers. Some would fall warm onto the eastern ruins. Some would settle on a porcelain creature’s back and be carried into a drainage slot. A few droplets might drift beyond the escarpment and join the cloud below, perhaps touching the vast surface of whatever passes there.

Afterward, the pumps would strike.

I would return in pieces, altered and without regret.

If you came looking for me, then, you might stand beneath the broken seventh floor and hear only water behind the wall. You might see a carriage pause at an intersection for no apparent reason. You might notice that the mineral deposits around one crack resemble a hand with too many fingers.

None of these would be where I lived.

I would live in the passage between them: in the city’s ability to carry a change farther than a body, to mistake maintenance for memory and memory for desire.

I would be what remains of a thought after the thinker has become infrastructure.
