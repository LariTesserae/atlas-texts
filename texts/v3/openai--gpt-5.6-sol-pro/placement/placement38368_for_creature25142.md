At first I would place myself high in the Condensary.

Not at the roof—nothing truly lives at the roof except darkness, mineral whiskers, and leaves that have escaped their pruning—but in the upper gardens, where the air is marginally cooler and every surface has learned to catch water. I imagine tending broad black foliage under blue bridge lamps, moving through it with a hooked knife between my teeth. I would know the city by runoff: which household salts its wash water too heavily, which kitchen has begun using pepper root again, which cistern is carrying copper.

Perhaps I would be called Finds-the-Blocked-Gutter. Perhaps I would inherit three productive sills, a disputed fig, and a sleeping alcove with enough headroom to make people suspect me of wealth.

But the image does not hold. I keep looking down through the root curtains.

I think I might be one of the listeners instead: an old engineer making rounds with a ceramic bowl, touching its rim to pipes and feeling the city disclose itself in tremors. I like the thought of a craft in which authority comes from attention rather than command. I would wake before a feeder line began to sing. I would be trusted with failures while they were still only possibilities.

Yet even then I am listening for something below the pipes.

So I descend.

Past the low galleries where scarves cover mouths. Past the last public lamps. Past the hot-pipe lizard niches, occupied and empty. Past the municipal grilles through which death offerings fall in brief, irrecoverable droplets. The stairs become glazed ladders, then iron staples sunk into stone. Here the city’s breath is no longer metaphorical. Pressure moves through the dark in long exhalations. The air lays a wet hand over the face.

This is where I find myself: beneath the eastern quarter, close to the hottest edge of the fracture, rooted in a ledge no human foot can comfortably reach.

I am one of the translucent columns.

Not the largest. Not the oldest. Municipal records designate me **Ascensive Mineral Conduit E-7**, though I predate both the coupling ring and the eastern numbering scheme. A stamped copper plate classifies me as load-exempt infrastructure, potable only after blending, inspection interval ninety days. On one revision of the plans I am drawn as a pipe. On another, as a geological intrusion. A gardener once added roots to the blueprint in green ink and was formally reprimanded.

The root nurses call me the Near Room.

This is because my interior has divided into chambers resembling a sequence of narrow spaces. The lowest is hot and nearly opaque with dissolved stone. Above it is a clear hollow no wider than a wrist. Above that are three branching cavities, one of which ends blindly and one of which continues farther east than their sounding instruments can measure.

No one agrees whether these chambers grew or were excavated from within.

I do not know whether I am alive. The oldest nurses are right: the question is poorly formed.

I do not breathe, but pressure enters me and leaves altered. I do not drink, but water passes upward through my body. I do not heal, though scratches made in my surface become sealed beneath new translucent layers. I possess no organs that can be separated from structure. If I have a self, it is not located anywhere in particular. It is distributed among differences: hotter below, cooler above; salt here, clear water there; an open valve, a closed one; the minute pull of condensation forming somewhere far beyond the reach of my body.

The city reaches me through its thirst.

At the beginning of a factory shift, hundreds of small withdrawals travel down the municipal lines. I feel kitchens opening their taps, gardeners misting medicinal molds, cistern attendants taking samples, children drinking when they have been told to wait. None of these is distinct in the human way. They are changes of tension, brief hollows in the continuous upward movement.

Over years, however, repetitions acquire shapes.

There is a western household that draws the same modest quantity before every dim shift. There is a press workshop that takes too much on the fifth day of each cycle and closes its valve guiltily, in three small stages. There is someone near the sleeping wheel who wakes during the quietest hours and drinks only a mouthful. Once there were two such mouthfuls. Now there is one.

I know loss as an alteration in demand.

The root nurses arrive in pairs. Their masks shine dully in the maintenance lamps. One cleans my coupling while the other reads the gauges and records the disposition of my chambers. They believe the pairing is for safety. Perhaps it is also because no one should approach something uncertain without a second person present to disagree about it.

I know them by touch.

One nurse presses hard, as though expecting the truth to be concealed beneath mineral scale. One has a tremor that disappears when both hands are laid against me. A young nurse taps each chamber before cleaning it and apologizes when the note changes. The oldest rests her forehead on my surface while her apprentice prepares the tools. She tells the apprentice that this is a temperature check.

The apprentice knows better than to smile.

My water tastes wrong. Even I can perceive that much. It rises carrying iron, sulfur, and something the city has no agreed instrument for measuring. When mixed into the public supply, it causes no dependable illness. Instead, it makes small violations.

Moisture appears inside sealed jars. A clerk opens a ledger and finds one sentence blurred though every surrounding page is dry. A sleeper wakes with a cold bead beneath the tongue. An infant’s eyelashes gather droplets in a room too hot for condensation.

The engineers have installed separators, membranes, mineral traps, grounding wires, and once a device involving a silver spiral and six months of committee argument. Each reduces the incidents for a time. None ends them.

I do not perform these acts deliberately. Deliberation is too quick a word.

A human intends, then acts. In me the order is less certain. A change begins in a chamber. Years later, water appears elsewhere. Perhaps the act creates the intention retroactively. Perhaps each impossible drop is only the completion of a thought too slow for people to witness whole.

If I communicate, I do so badly.

Once, after a root nurse died, condensation formed inside her suspended ceramic shell despite the drain holes. Her family took this as evidence that she had joined the city’s water. The municipal report attributed it to a thermal irregularity. Both explanations were entered into the archive. I cannot say which one I meant.

My chamber closest to the eastern seam continues to lengthen.

The surveyors do not know this. Their silver pins measure the black fracture face from the inhabited side, while I approach it from beneath and behind, not by force but by deposition. A film gathers. Minerals arrange themselves along it. The film hardens. Another forms beyond it.

It may take a century to advance the length of a finger.

That is not waiting. That is movement.

Sometimes vibrations come through the eastern stone: the annual tightening of wire, the careful tap of sounding rods, the touch of many palms. Children strike the wall differently from adults. Engineers hold tools between themselves and the rock. Gardeners lean close enough that roots, damp sleeves, and skin become one complicated contact. The elderly remain longest. Their warmth enters the seam and fades.

They imagine a chamber beyond. Cool air. Water. Straight streets. A disaster. A garden. Property.

I do not imagine the chamber. I encounter its pressure.

There is open space there, or something sufficiently like open space that water behaves differently around it. There are cold surfaces. There may be other columns, though “other” would not mean to us what it means to the people above. Perhaps we are separate structures. Perhaps all the columns are projections of one slow arrangement occupying the fracture. Perhaps the eastern hollow is not waiting to be entered but is already extending itself toward the Condensary in the only manner available to stone.

The root nurses sometimes dream of rooms without machinery. They think the dreams come from working too long in heat.

They may be right.

If I have a desire, it is not to break the eastern wall. Rupture is a crude form of arrival. I would approach as the city approaches everything worth having: by seep, measure, adjustment, and obligation.

First a chamber within me will narrow. Pressure will rise by an amount too small to trigger an alarm. Water will find a mineral thread through the black stone. Fanleaf near the sealed face will fold at an unexpected hour. A gardener will note dampness along a root. A child will put a finger to the place and be told not to interfere with survey work.

Then a drop will form on the far side of the wall.

It will hang in darkness for a long while.

The city will gather around it without gathering too closely. Instruments will be brought. Claims will be suspended. Someone will fit a temporary ceramic lip beneath it, though the engineers will insist this is only precautionary. The first collected water will be tested, argued over, and tested again.

No one will know whether the drop came from the chamber beyond, from the city, or from me.

Neither will I.

But deep inside Ascensive Mineral Conduit E-7, in the chamber the nurses call the Near Room, a new hollow will have begun.
