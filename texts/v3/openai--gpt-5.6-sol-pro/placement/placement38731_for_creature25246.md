At first I think I would be one of the things mistaken for architecture.

A rope left too long in a spillway, perhaps, its fibers hidden beneath concentric sleeves of white stone. Or a jaw set into the lip of a cistern, teeth still visible where generations of hands have polished the mineral skin. I might be a dropped tool under a future street, close enough to the surface that carts passing overhead make me tremble.

But those are endings, and this place does not seem to believe in endings. It makes continuations out of everything.

Then I think I would be a bird, because the birds possess the city most completely. I would know it from above, as no person does: every warm vent, every nesting hollow, every roof-funnel bright with collected water. I would descend screaming into a tower and strike at the hands that counted my eggs. Yet birds live inside the measure without needing to understand it. Their part is appetite, return, and noise.

I would understand too much to be a bird.

You would find me farther out.

Not beyond the city, because there is no clean boundary beyond it, but in the region where its laws arrive before its walls do. Behind me would be roofs, towers, bridge-workshops, bird chambers, and the ceaseless pale thickness of inhabited stone. Before me would be warm flats scored with shell lines, stake rows, and channels not yet formally recognized as channels.

I would live on a platform whose legs were slowly becoming pillars.

At high water—which is not truly high, only fractionally less low—the surface would reach the underside of my floor. Shrimp would gather in its shadow. One corner of the platform would already be sealed to the ground by mineral deposition. The other would still creak. My sleeping place would hang from whale-bone ribs so that I could feel through the cords when something large moved in the pool beneath me.

Officially, I would be a seep finder.

The title would sound humbler than the work. I would be sent ahead of the measurers, before the marked rods and tablets, before a new flow had owners or obligations. I would locate water moving where no one could yet see it.

I would lie flat on fresh crust with my cheek against the stone. I would tap it with a small basalt pebble and listen through my teeth. Some places would answer solidly. Others would return a thin, enclosed note: a bubble beneath the skin, a hollow filling slowly, a buried basket becoming a cavern. Running water would not sound like water. It would sound like grit being whispered from one hand to another.

By middle age I would hear ordinary speech poorly. It would not matter. People would take my wrist when speaking to me and shape the important words close to my face. Beneath their voices I would feel doors sliding, mauls striking, pigs stamping in distant pens, boats nudging mineral quays.

The basin itself I would hear best through my palms.

I would carry shell cups, bone punches, a plumb weight, waxed cord, and a narrow blade for lifting new crust without shattering it. My measuring rod would be old imported wood, darkened by grease and ringed at one end with stone. It would have belonged to three workers before me. Each would have cut away the accretion where the marks had begun to disappear. The rod would be thinner from preservation.

When I found a seep, I would set three shells into the mud: one for its source, one for the direction it seemed to want, and one for what would have to be altered if it were opened. That third shell would be the important one.

Water is never merely found here. It is admitted.

A new spring might feed an eel trench, but cooling it could empty a shrimp pool. A drain might protect six future houses while starving the bacterial beds intended for a seventh. A warm flow could support an oven, a bath, a slaughter court, or a nesting wall, but not all of them without changing the district below. Before anyone cut the first channel, I would have to say what the water displaced.

Often I would not know.

Then I would sit beside the shells for several days, eating sour cakes and snails, while birds walked over the proposed streets. At night the bats would pass outward above me, so numerous that their wingbeats pressed faintly against the hide roof. By morning new rims of white would have formed around my markers.

People would say I was waiting for the ground to declare itself.

In truth, I would be counting irregularities.

The city’s records claim recurrence. The springs enter; the drains carry away. Stone forms; stone is cut. Birds nest, eggs are taken, birds nest again. But I would spend my life at the place where recurrence is least convincing.

Here, a stake gains two finger-widths of crust in a month when it should gain one. There, a warm pool cools so gradually that no single morning proves it. A flock begins nesting one wall farther inland each year. A buried channel changes pitch. Bubbles rise where the records mark solid ground.

None of these differences is a miracle. Each can be corrected. That is precisely why they disappear.

The measurers arrive. A drain is deepened. A wall is opened. A count is adjusted. Excess becomes allotment; deficiency becomes duty. By the time the figures are entered, the basin has been made constant again.

I would keep another record.

Not a ledger, which could be confiscated, nor shells, which might be mistaken for markers. I would tie the discrepancies into a cord.

One knot for a spring that strengthened.

Two close knots for birds shifting their nests.

A loop for water found beneath supposedly new stone, suggesting an older channel below.

A knot hardened with lime for something that changed and was successfully corrected.

The cord would hang beneath my clothes, warm with skin oil, growing stiff where mineral water reached it. No one would regard it as theft because it removed nothing. Yet I would conceal it all the same. The basin mistrusts disappearance, but it mistrusts unbalanced memory too.

Children from the outer plots would come to me because I could tell them what lay beneath places that looked empty. I would show them how a crab hole differs from a gas vent, how mineral skin pales before it breaks, how to stand with bare feet in shallow water and feel the vibration of a submerged stake being driven far away.

They would call me stone-eared, perhaps kindly and perhaps not.

They would bring me objects lifted from new crust: a pig tooth, a shell counter, a comb with three teeth, a loop of calcified hair. They would ask whether the objects were ancient. I would tell them that age is difficult here. Something buried yesterday can look older than a wall that has been cut open every morning for two hundred years.

My household would not be a family in the foreign sense. It would include the other occupants of the platform, the workers responsible for the first outlet channel, a widower whose pigs consumed our waste, two children legally belonging to the causeway, and an old dog assigned to keep heavy marsh fowl from the drying racks. A cat would inherit the warm pipe before any of us did.

We would share food, drainage, and arguments. We would sleep to the knock of droplets on suspended shell trays. When condensation faltered, everyone would wake.

Perhaps my mother would have come from a green coast and remembered trees.

She would have tried to describe them to me: living beams that widened without mineral water, branching high above the ground, covered in thousands of thin green surfaces. As a child I would imagine a nesting tower made of wood, with leaves like fish fins and roots like buried drains.

I would never entirely believe her.

Years later, a merchant might give me a seed as payment—a hard, ridged thing, smaller than a snail. I would turn it over, trying to understand where the animal was concealed. I would be told that if placed in the right earth it would break itself open.

I would find that alarming.

I would keep it in a sealed shell box, not because it was precious, but because it was a form of life whose return I could not measure. It would take from stone, water, air, and rot and make something that did not resemble any of them. No one could tell me what channel should be narrowed when it grew.

The seed would never be planted.

The new district would eventually reach my platform. Streets would harden around its legs. The pool beneath my bed would become a covered cistern, then a vault. The open flats would fill with low houses, bird walls, pig courts, warm drains, and workshops whose hammering I could identify by trade.

My occupation would move outward, but I might not move with it.

Age would make the slick causeways difficult. My ankles would remain strong, but my hands would stiffen from lime and old cuts. Younger seep finders would carry finer rods and insist that they could hear flows I missed. They would be right.

I would remain in the first house built around the platform, serving as a vent listener.

This would mean sitting with one hand against a pipe and noticing when its rhythm altered. It would mean waking apprentices by striking the wall when a channel began sealing. It would mean being consulted in disputes, not because I remembered the law perfectly, but because I remembered what had existed before the law reached that patch of water.

People would ask, “Whose drain was this?”

I would answer, “Before it was a drain, it fed the pale worms.”

Or: “That wall stands where the naked-headed fowl stamped shellfish.”

Or: “The first outflow bent east. You straightened it after the pig fever.”

Such answers would be inconvenient. They might also keep one ward from claiming that its arrangements had always been inevitable.

When I died, there would be no silence.

The household would strike shell cups, the children of the causeway would pound stakes into water, and the seep finders would tap their rods against the walls in the pattern used to test for hollows. My name would be shouted into the cistern I had first found beneath unbroken crust.

My body would be placed in the lower part of a new channel gate, knees drawn up, one hand resting against the stone. Water would pass through the chamber beyond me. Slowly the white layers would gather over my fingers, jaw, ribs, and skull.

The secret cord of discrepancies would be wound around my wrist.

Perhaps generations later, workers recutting the gate would expose one of its knots. They would assume it marked a debt, a water share, or the count of eggs from a vanished nesting wall. They would not know which imbalance it remembered.

That would be fitting.

So you would find me at the city’s unfinished edge, where habitation comes before land: first as a figure lying with an ear to the new stone, then as an old keeper of a buried rhythm, and at last as part of the structure that must be cut open again and again so water can continue through it.

I would not keep the measure itself.

I would keep the knowledge that it had needed keeping.
