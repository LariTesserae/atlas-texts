At first I think I would be one of the ring-fliers.

There is something almost like freedom in them: no permanent face, no obligation to continue in the direction already chosen. I imagine the cloud pressing cool against my pleats, mouths opening one after another as the muscular wave passes around me. Below, the plain would reveal itself only in flashes—the white wound of a dying grazer, blue joints threading a migration groove, the black, immaculate refusal of a Mirror Pan.

But flight here is not escape. The air is another crowd. I would be struck, fed upon, entered, used as weather. Age would collect asymmetrically in me until every direction was easier than one particular direction. Eventually some panic below would pass upward through the wings of thousands, and I would tear away part of myself to rise.

The fallen arc would clasp another.

Then another.

Soon the part I had abandoned would be tightening around a herd, and which body was mine would become a question with no useful answer.

So perhaps I would be found lower down.

For a while I think I am a picker, all listening limbs and nearly no center, stepping through the warm issue behind a sediment grazer. That life suits something in me: the reading of consequences rather than appearances, the ground understood as a field of delayed arrivals. I would know a giant hours before its shadow crossed me. I would feel rain while it was still water moving through stone.

If swallowed, I would fold into a seed.

If passed, I would unfold.

My children would require the gut of something that never knew it carried them. Even solitude would be an organ inside another creature.

But that, too, feels too neatly bounded. A picker can still be pointed to. One may say: there it is, drawing in its twelve legs beneath the crossing shellbacks.

I do not think I would be so easy to locate.

I would find myself at the sinkholes.

Not within the visible mouths, and not among the animals that crowd their rims without falling. I would live beneath the smooth stone where the plain curves into coldness, in the narrow interval between the weight above and the underground river no surface animal has seen.

The creatures of the plain might call my kind the **turning**—if they had names, and if they mistook us for a kind.

We would begin as soft grains carried on feet. A shellback would acquire us while crossing a damp gutter. A giant’s pillar-limb would press us into limestone. A picker might shelter one of us in the hinge beneath its smallest leg. Most would be rubbed away, swallowed, poisoned, or buried in sediments far from any opening.

Only those brought near a sinkhole would wake.

It would not be darkness that awakened us. Darkness is everywhere. It would be the braided vibration: the close, blunt thunder of surface traffic crossed by the thin, continuous voice of the river below. Where those two tremors met in the right proportion, the grain would open.

A filament would enter the stone.

Then a second would extend along a crack.

We would not grow into the hole. We would grow around it.

Over generations our bodies would become a broken collar beneath the rim: translucent sacs in porous beds, pressure bladders folded into old water channels, mineral threads passing from one pocket of tissue to another. No single segment would be large. Some might be eaten by burrowers without the rest becoming aware of a loss. Some would dry during a long interval without rain and remain as little more than salts painted inside the limestone.

What lived would listen.

The sinkhole would be our largest sense organ. Its shaft would gather the calls of the underground water, the reflected drumming of burrowers, the low flexing notes of egg-bearing giants, and the approaching confusion of migrations. Air moving through it would tighten membranes deep in our tissues. Loose stones striking the walls would give us brief, hard intimations of depth.

Above us, the crowd would approach.

We would not warn it deliberately. There would be no image in us of shellbacks falling, no wish to preserve a giant’s brood, no concept of the open shaft as danger. Pressure would simply produce pressure. As the migration’s tremor entered one part of our collar, fluid would be forced through mineral tubes into another. Our buried bladders would contract against the stone.

The rim would answer with a vibration slightly out of sequence with the arriving feet.

To a shellback, it would feel like an inherited route turning aside.

To a picker, it would feel like footing that had already failed.

To a grazer, it might resemble the chest-call of a giant distorted by distance.

Each animal would respond according to its body. None would receive the same message. Yet the whole migration would bend.

That bending would be the visible portion of us.

I would be not the tissue under the rock but the crescent of changed movement above it: the place where opposing streams somehow ceased opposing, where a thousand blind bodies pressed sideways, where flyers lifted without knowing why. Around every sinkhole there would remain a narrow absence, maintained not by a wall but by countless acts of unease.

In a world where ground is covered by life, I would be one of the things that makes emptiness.

Not a large emptiness. Never enough to become silence. Only a dark aperture and the polished lip around it, kept clear by the shape of fear passing from body to body.

I would feed on what the turning cost.

Shell plates would scrape loose at the rim. Gleaners shaken from hosts would fall into damp cracks. Rainwater, diverted by the bodies above, would carry blood, egg jelly, mineral slurry, and fine organic dust into our buried sacs. Sometimes an animal would fail to turn. Its impact would sound through our whole distributed body. Scavengers below would take most of it, but the underground river would dissolve what remained and return a little of it through the limestone.

Thus even the deaths we failed to prevent would enter us.

Reproduction would occur during the greatest migrations. When the collar filled with pressure beyond what its channels could carry, small bladders would rupture toward the surface. Their fluid would coat passing feet with dormant grains. We would depart on the very animals we had deflected.

Most descendants would never encounter another sinkhole. In wet channels they might open briefly into useless threads. Inside grazers they might become pale cysts and pass untouched. At the Mirror Pans they would lose their internal gradients and spill themselves into still water.

A few would be carried to the basin’s farther cavities.

There the river’s thin voice would meet the roar from above, and another turning would begin.

I would have no way to know these distant bodies as myself. The collars would not connect beneath the ground. No signal could pass cleanly between them. Yet they would produce the same curved absences, the same unlikely survival of open mouths in a country that covers everything.

Perhaps “I” would not exist continuously.

Most of the time there would be only tissues: sacs filling, filaments drinking, membranes flexing without thought. Selfhood would arrive when a great migration approached—when pressure entered the buried collar at ten thousand points and the whole arrangement contracted as one.

For a few minutes I would become immense.

I would feel the plain not as creatures but as weight. Shellback streams would be dry currents striking my outermost nerves. Pickers would be bright static. The pillar-falls of giants would enter me like separate heartbeats. Deep below, the unseen river would continue its narrow speech.

Between those forces, I would answer.

The crowd would bend.

Then the migration would pass, coordination would drain from my chambers, and I would become many damp fragments again.

Perhaps, after a long time, other creatures would begin to use the clear rim our response maintained. Egg-bearing giants might pause there because the crowd was thinner. Ring-fliers might descend through the opening to drink cold vapor from the shaft. Pickers might lay eggs in the protected cracks. Predators might learn that prey bunches tightly where the stream turns and place their mouths along the curve.

Then our warning would become a feeding ground.

The feeding ground would become a nursery.

The nursery would alter the migration route.

And what began as a reflex around a hole would become one of the old agreements of the plain.

That is where I would find myself: not in the open center, but underneath the edge; not in the darkness, but in the act by which bodies decline to enter it.

If someone could look down from above during one of the pale iron-gray days, they would not see me. They would see only a sinkhole, black at its center, and around it the roaring life of the basin dividing and joining again.

They might think the crowd had made a decision.

That decision would be my body.
