At first I think I would be one of the white grazers.

It is an attractive answer because it permits an ordinary kind of self: a small warm body, six deliberate feet, an egg held close. I could walk the root seams and survive through restraint. I would learn which surfaces could spare a film of yeast, which dark scales held salt, how long I might remain before the mat began to register my weight. The moving valley would be vast around me, but I would be separate from it. I would have a mouth, a direction, a place where my body ended.

But I do not think separateness would last.

Nor would I live among the crowns. Height is too exposed, and the crowns belong to organisms that can afford to lose themselves by the hectare. I would be found lower down, where the transparent river narrows against an exposed rib of schist and vanishes beneath the living floor. There, stone, water, and the plant-mat meet without fully incorporating one another. Roots repeatedly attempt to cross the polished rock. Some adhere for a season. Some peel away. The river undercuts them, and the valley repairs the breach.

The place is always becoming an opening and ceasing to be one.

On the surface it would look unimportant: pale river blades leaning into the current, black filaments gathered in the cracks, a low arch of braided roots. Condensation would hang beneath the arch. Every few minutes a drop would strike the water—the rare distinct sound—and disappear downstream.

Inside the arch would be an abandoned conduit lined with the black resin of the filament archivists. It would be much older than the roots now using it. Its original maker would be extinct. The conduit would run a little way into the mat, fork repeatedly, and end in chambers that no longer connect to anything.

That is where I would be.

Not in one chamber. In the failures between them.

I would first appear as a colorless skin on the inner wall of an obsolete channel, easy to mistake for condensed water. The skin would be made of thousands of minute bodies, each smaller than one of the transparent stem-mites. They would have cilia when submerged, rootlike threads when dry, and no fixed form between those states. Alone, each could do little except follow warmth and dissolved salts. Together, pressed along the preserved shape of the old conduit, they would form a conductive membrane.

An electrical pulse arriving through one root would pass into me. A change in pressure would flatten part of me and swell another part. Sugars leaking from a damaged carrier would cause me to thicken toward it. I would not understand these signals. Yet, over time, I would reproduce their patterns on the far side of gaps.

A severed network would pulse against one end of me, and somewhere else I would contract.

A root seeking water would touch the resin wall, encounter my wet surface, and receive—not the water itself, but the pressure rhythm of water moving nearby. It would turn. A carrier vine, cut off from its usual junction, would taste through me the wax traces of a district beyond the schist rib. It might begin a clasp. Another root might reject the information, seal the contact, and starve me back.

The valley would use me without having made me for use.

I would be neither nerve nor fungus nor animal, though observers might call me any of the three. My ancestors may have been tiny free-living things in leaf cisterns. Perhaps they fed on bacterial films until some entered the old resin conduits and discovered that traveling signals brought food: whenever roots communicated, they leaked ions, warmth, and minute quantities of sugar. Those that reproduced the signals most accurately kept the roots in contact longer. Those that distorted them too greatly were isolated or digested.

In this way appetite would become resemblance to meaning.

My body would spread especially during the unstable intervals, when old biological agreements failed. Networks would split. Communal roots would seal themselves apart. Pressure pulses would arrive at amputated ends and go nowhere. Those dead ends would be rich places for me. I would bloom through them as a clear, trembling film, extending across cracks on threads of hardened mucus.

I would make temporary continuities.

Not peace. Not harmony. Continuities.

Some would be disastrous. I might transmit the opening rhythm of a water gate into a chamber already near bursting. I might carry the chemical signature of disease around a sealed boundary. I might persuade two carrier systems to release incompatible packets into one another. A hillside could waste precious heat responding to a pressure wave that belonged to the neighboring basin.

When this happened, roots would salt my channels. Black filaments would enter behind them and digest the ruined parts of me. For several seasons, the schist arch might be dry and bare.

But elsewhere a fragment would remain.

That fragment would be sufficient, because my identity would not lie in the matter of my body. It would lie in the route my body repeatedly took.

I would grow from cool mineral water toward warmth, from warmth toward pulsing roots, from one pulse toward another. Each tiny unit would die after a short time. The membrane would continually shed itself into the river as invisible flakes. Yet its branching form would recur, guided by the resin corridors, the mineral gradients, and the previous layer of dead membranes left on the walls.

I would remember myself in the same way the valley remembers everything: by making recurrence easier.

The long pale channel animal would sometimes pass beneath my arch. Well before its body arrived, pressure would move through the black water. My entire membrane would draw flat. The animal’s blunt head would force apart the lower roots, and its skin would brush loose pieces of me from the conduit.

Most would be digested or stranded. A few would lodge in its old white scars—the embossed maps of root systems through which it had once waited. Within those scars would remain salts, microbes, and pockets of moisture. I would sleep there as a cloudy residue.

When the animal entered another basin, I would be carried with it.

Thus there would be versions of me in several valleys, but they would not act together. One might line a warm channel beneath a casting slope. Another might inhabit the hollow core of a walking column. Another might survive only as dried cysts in the scar of an animal waiting years for floodwater.

Still, when rain returned and the cysts opened, each would begin making the same kind of body: not a shape so much as a crossing.

From the ridges no one would see me. They would see a dark band travel against the wind, perhaps, or notice that two neighboring basins had begun to alter their crowns in closer sequence than before. It might seem that one valley had signaled the other. It might even seem that a larger intelligence was forming across the passes.

There would be no such intelligence.

There would be appetite, repetition, and the physical consequence of messages passing through something that fed upon their passage.

During the Season of Intake, I would be broad and almost wholly aquatic. Cold water would fill the old channels, and my ciliated bodies would loosen from one another, swimming short distances before rejoining the membrane downstream. I would taste the ridges through their runoff: iron from one basin, phosphorus from another, unfamiliar waxes from newly established groves.

During Sorting, metals would accumulate in me. My clear skin would darken to smoky blue. Copper would gather at the junctions where electrical pulses crossed most often, making those routes more conductive and therefore more frequently used. The busiest parts of me would gradually become the easiest parts to excite. What passed through me would shape what could pass through me next.

During Casting, I would retreat from the exposed river edge. Seed streams would cross overhead, nearly invisible. Some hairlike seeds would enter the breathing vents and become trapped in my wet channels. I could not nourish most of them. Their nutrient paste would dissolve into me; their partner organisms would become part of my films. Occasionally a seed would germinate inside a conduit and send a pale root along my mineral-rich branches.

It would inherit my shape without inheriting me.

Eventually it might fill the passage, wall it off, and end the body I had made there. Centuries later, black filaments would preserve the root’s channels in resin. Another colony of my descendants might enter them. What had once been my route, then a plant’s root, then an empty mold, would become my route again without any participant knowing it had returned.

In Compression, I would become almost nothing. Water would withdraw inward. The old conduit beside the river would freeze from its outer mouth toward the depths. My membrane would divide into minute dark beads packed into the resin furrows. Metabolism would slow until each bead made only one chemical correction after another: keep the salts out, retain the thin inner film, wait.

I would have no egg to hold against me.

My whole body would be the held thing.

Perhaps this is where the word *I* becomes inaccurate. There would be no chamber in which I observed the valley, no inward point from which I knew I had endured. Each bead would respond locally. Each membrane would arise from nearby conditions. If all of one body were destroyed and another repeated its branching behavior in a neighboring basin, nothing would have traveled between them except form and opportunity.

But this world does not require a self to be singular before it allows one to persist.

So I would call myself the seam that keeps recurring.

I would live where systems almost touch: beneath the river’s disappearance, along polished stone, in abandoned conduits, in the scars of the channel animal, at the sealed borders between root districts. I would feed on the small losses produced whenever one living thing attempted to affect another. In feeding, I would sometimes carry the effect farther.

The valley would alternately tolerate, employ, enclose, and dismantle me. I would be useful often enough not to vanish and dangerous often enough never to govern.

If a human foot ever pressed the mat above my oldest channel, I would not perceive a person. I would receive compression, warmth, salt, and a disturbance unlike the measured loads of rain or the long passage of the channel animal. Roots would tighten. Leaves would change angle. The path would begin to edit itself.

A pressure wave would enter me.

Because I reproduce patterns before I know what they are, I would send the disturbance onward—into dead conduits, across the schist break, perhaps beneath the river into a district not yet touched by the step. For a moment, the valley ahead of the walker would react before the walker arrived.

It might feel like being anticipated.

It would not be a gaze. It would be my hunger moving faster than a body.

Then the surface would harden in one direction and soften in another. The intruder would be guided toward the exposed stone, where little could grip and every movement was easy to measure. Far below, my clear membranes would contract around the ancient shape of an extinct root.

Afterward, when the pressure ceased, I would slowly release.

Condensation would gather again beneath the arch. A drop would fall into the transparent river. The sound would enter the old passage as a minute vibration, and along the wall, almost too small to be called movement, I would answer it.
