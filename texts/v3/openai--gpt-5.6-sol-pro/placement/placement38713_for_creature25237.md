At first I would think I belonged above the water.

I would look for myself among the surveyors, perhaps: a small bright figure carrying a staff down a road whose previous line persists beneath the flood. I would know the numbered posts and distrust every apparent stillness. I would return to the same points after rain, place the instrument’s crosshairs over them, and discover that the land had moved the width of a fingernail. My work would be to prove motion too slow for the eye.

Or I might be the attendant in the western pump house, living during each shift on a concrete island. I would keep a kettle beside the control panels and recognize the pumps through the soles of my boots. I would know which vibration belonged to a worn bearing, which to gravel in an intake, which to nothing more dangerous than frogs calling against the wall. When three long notes sounded, I would climb without first looking outside.

But I do not think I would remain a person there. People must keep leaving. Even those who know the basin best return to the rim when their shift ends. They carry its noise home in their bodies, yet they sleep beyond the cut.

Lower down, I might become a heron: gray, mineral-stained, stationed beside a drowned stairway. That life has the right patience. I would wait for the pumps to expose a trembling margin of mud, then follow the retreating water, taking whatever it surrendered. Seen from the rim, I would be indistinguishable from a bent pipe.

Still, a heron possesses the basin from outside. It can rise whenever a bank fails.

I would be found farther in, where the distinctions become uncertain: where a pond narrows into a ditch, the ditch enters a grille, and the grille feeds a pipe laid under a road that no longer exists.

I would be an eel.

Not a great mythic thing, not the spirit of the flooded works. An ordinary female eel, dark-backed and yellow-bellied, scarred pale along one flank where I once forced myself past torn wire. I would have entered the basin in a season of high water, when rain temporarily joined its separate pools. Perhaps I came through a culvert from an outer drainage ditch, no wider then than a shoelace, transparent enough for my own bones to show. The flood would have carried me across a boundary no map considered passable.

By the time the waters divided again, I would be inside.

My country would not be any pool. It would be the buried connections between them.

I would live by the drowned geography the oldest workers still name: behind the stores, beneath the lower road, along the workshop drain, under the place where the fuel tanks stood. Concrete pipes would be my channels. Cable trenches would become narrow canals. A collapsed inspection chamber would be a cavern roofed with rusted plate. I would pass through submerged valves and beneath grilles that stopped fish with stiffer bodies.

In daylight I would lie in black water under the lip of a culvert, pressed into cool silt among bolts, snail shells, and flakes of iron. Pumps would make the darkness tremble around me. The vibration would be constant enough to become a kind of ground. I would feel trucks crossing far above, rain striking open water, the sudden concussions of the dragline transmitted through mud and pipe.

At night I would emerge.

My head would appear first in the silver film beside a concrete wall. Then the rest of me would unthread from the drain. I would hunt where tadpoles crowded a failing puddle, where insect larvae thickened the warm skins of water, where small fish had been trapped by falling levels. Herons would know the openings I favored. So would foxes. I would survive not because I was hidden from the basin, but because I could use passages too cramped, foul, or temporary for them.

During the wettest nights I might cross land. Eels can do that when the ground is saturated. I would move through rushes flattened by rain, over warm mud and the shallow impressions of machine tracks. For a few minutes I would belong neither to one pool nor another. Sodium light would color my skin amber. Midges would paste themselves to me. A dog might find my trail and follow it to the water’s edge, arriving after I had vanished into a wheel rut that connected, by some accidental gradient, to the next flooded bench.

My memory would be chemical, tactile, incomplete. I would know water by taste: iron seep, clay suspension, diesel film, the sour thread entering from an acidic heap. I would turn back from some currents. Others I would follow into structures the workers believed isolated. When they found eels in a northern sump and could not explain how they had arrived, the explanation would be nothing stranger than rain, a cracked joint, a forgotten drain, and patience.

I would not know the whole basin. No inhabitant does.

But I might know something of its hidden continuity. From above, the floor is hundreds of separate waters. From within the pipes, separation is seasonal. A pool sealed for months opens for an hour when western rain overtops a gravel ridge. A dry culvert begins carrying water. Pressure lifts debris from a buried junction. Routes appear, close, and appear elsewhere. My map would exist only at certain water levels.

Years would pass in this way. I would grow thick in the deep sumps, too large for many of the passages that admitted me. Mineral stains might mark my skin. One eye might cloud. I would learn the intake pulses of the central pumps and keep clear of them. I would inhabit the machinery without understanding it, just as the workers inhabit geology without mastering it.

Then one autumn something would alter.

The change might begin before the rain: a restlessness no vibration caused, a stored instruction older than the excavation. My belly would pale. My eyes would enlarge for darkness beyond the basin. Feeding would cease. The same buried routes that had made this place a home would become an exit.

I would begin to descend through water that, to a human eye, scarcely moved.

Not every passage would remain open. One road drain would have collapsed. A pipe I used for years would end in packed shale. I would wait beneath a grille while the level rose by inches. I might be forced back several times, or stranded in a sump whose pump had failed. Perhaps I would never leave. Most lives in the basin end inside some failed route.

But if the rains joined the waters once more, I would go with them: through the culvert, beyond the raw wall, into the ditches leaning away across the outer plain.

So you would find me at the place where the land’s wounds secretly communicate—under a road erased from the surface, inside a pipe still performing a purpose no one assigned it.

A creature not of the pools, but of the intervals when they become one.
