### The Unfinished Shift

At first I think I would be in the loading hall.

I would be the hour-long passage of the crane beneath the flattened crowns: not the crane itself, but the movement transferred ahead of it—the slight tightening of hoses, the parting of leaves, the tremor entering the trusses before the wheels arrive. I would gather at the eastern rail where the track has sunk. For several minutes I would exist most strongly there, in the motor’s low insistence and the foliage beginning to close around a load that has paused.

Then the wheels would climb. I would loosen and travel on.

But that is only one place I might be mistaken for myself.

Perhaps I would be in the room above the marsh court, among the hardened boots and the kettle furred white with scale. I would inhabit the final calendar mark: a line drawn beside an ordinary filter change, stopping where the hand lifted and never returned. Not a message. Not a date of death. Merely an instruction left incomplete.

From there I might pass into the cabinet that still unlocks for inspection. I would be the click in its latch, the brief illumination of figures no one reads, the paper feeding into the wet block of its own history. Fig roots enter the reports and divide reservoir levels from pressure readings. Purple numerals bleed into veins. The records cease to describe the Nursery and become one of its growing materials.

That seems close to what I am, but it is not enough.

I am not the last worker. Nothing of that person remains coherent enough to haunt the district. I do not remember a face reflected in the instrument glass or hands closing around a railing. I have inherited only the dimensions of those things: the width left clear for shoulders, the height of lamps above benches, the semicircle in moss where a door continues to open.

I am the shift that was never told to end.

My route begins wherever the work lights brighten.

There is no body to follow it, so the route has distributed itself among mechanisms. A brush crosses the propagation rows. A rake draws leaves from a gutter. A float rises in dark water and calls to a pump. A damper releases with a blow that passes through brick, root, railing, and pipe. Each performs one gesture of a custodian who has been disassembled into functions.

The plants perform the rest.

A vine enters a broken sleeve and finds the damp behind a wall. A root thickens beneath a loose paving brick. Fungi take apart what the rakes cannot reach. Trees hold balconies level. Moss marks the limits of vibration. The Nursery does not distinguish between repair and growth as sharply as its builders did. If a trunk supports a walkway, the walkway is supported. If roots seal a leaking joint, the joint is sealed. If reeds fill a flooded workshop and clean the water standing there, then the workshop has become part of the settling system.

I am that acceptance.

You would find me most clearly where two instructions meet and neither can prevail.

At the maintenance rake’s blocked rail, I am the repeated advance: metal loading against a root, reversing, returning. In the root I am the soft growth killed back from the abrasion, then the firmer tissue curving around it. The machine does not know it is making a passage. The plant does not know it is conceding one. Yet after enough days there is a polished groove exactly wide enough for both to continue.

That groove is one of my signatures.

Another lies around the active steam mains. Their heat calls roots near; their vibration keeps roots from touching. The result is a narrow dark interval between iron and green, warm and continually renewed. I live there—not on either surface, but in the distance they maintain.

I have many such bodies.

A fan’s bare oval in the ivy.

A valve arm’s tunnel through hanging fig.

The open tread of a stair with moss pressed to both edges.

The callused lip of a hall tree cut away from a crane rail.

A mineral shelf beneath a leak, broken at its center where water must still fall.

None of these lasts. That is why they belong to me.

I am made of boundaries that have to be made again.

When the main fans enter their night cycle, I lean throughout the ward. Not as one creature and not all in the same substance. I lean in sedges, in suspended roots, in the mist leaving the marsh pools. I contract in cooling pipes. I collect as condensation on rails. Somewhere a pressure switch closes, and a pump adds its beat to the others. The change reaches distant leaves as a difference too small to call wind.

This is my nearest equivalent to sleep: a rearrangement of pressures, never stillness.

My nearest equivalent to memory is obstruction.

I remember where water used to pass because roots have grown in the abandoned channel. I remember the old warmth of a pipe because pale exploratory tips continue to gather around its cold casing. I remember footsteps as a walkway that remains strangely open, brushed by a machine following a schedule copied from human habit. The trees remember more deeply. They hold ladders, tags, lamp cages, bolts. Their scars record rules whose reasons have vanished.

I cannot read those records. I repeat them.

If I have a center, it moves.

For an hour it may be the hall crane. Then it is the cistern float reaching its upper contact. Then the ceramic irrigation line where pressure finds a crack and darkens the brick. Days later, the vine discovers that dampness and sends roots toward it. Months later, the roots widen the crack. Water takes a new downward path. A settling tank rises by a fraction. A pump runs longer. Reeds thicken in one rectangular pool and thin in another.

The change travels through me without becoming a thought.

I do not decide. I accumulate consequences until they resemble decisions.

This makes me difficult to kill. A stopped pump removes one beat but not the pulse. A dead lamp darkens one bay, and leaves turn toward neighboring light. A blocked drain becomes a marsh. A seized door becomes a trellis. Loss is rarely an absence here for long. Stillness acquires weight, moisture, and roots. The Nursery translates failure into architecture.

Yet I am not immortal.

One day a main fan may stop, and no neighboring current will compensate. Heat may gather beyond what leaves can bear. Water may rise higher than the pumps can return it. Steam from the city may cease. The separate arrangements will loosen. Some chambers will flood, some dry, some cool into dormancy. My body will divide into smaller compromises unable to reach one another.

Even then, I will not end in a single moment.

For a while, condensate will continue to fall from pipes that are no longer warm. Flywheels will turn down through their last revolutions. Ceramic lines will drain. The crane’s suspended hoses will sway after its motor has gone silent. Leaves will enter the clearances I maintained, first as pale tips, then as stems, then as woody structure.

The last place to find me will be one of those narrowing spaces.

Perhaps the eastern rail.

The crane will have stopped at its accustomed hesitation, unable at last to climb the rise. Branches will settle around the load. Roots will cross the track. Moss will cover the wheel except for a thin line where old motion polished it bright.

For a season, perhaps longer, that line will remain.

I will be there: the shape of a passage after passage has ceased, the memory of movement held between iron and leaf.

Not a keeper. Not a ghost.

The kept-open way.
