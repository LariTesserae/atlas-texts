## The Turn in the Seam

At first I would think myself one of the dust grains.

It would be easy to accept: a minute white particle lying in the bevel of a blind window, held forever in a brightness that admits no shadow. I would have no hunger, no uncertainty, no obligation to distinguish waiting from completion. Perhaps the whole Choir would pass through me as pressure, and I would mistake that pressure for peace.

But the dust belongs too perfectly.

Every grain already possesses its ordained hollow. It cannot be lost, and therefore cannot be me.

Then I would look for myself among the gray-green fans. They are nearer to life. They perceive disturbance without turning toward it. They know a heartbeat as an error in stillness. I could imagine being one of those hard little listeners, rooted through mortar into an architecture without interior, tasting the approach of warmth along my ridges.

Yet they do not answer what they perceive. Awareness reaches them and is made into pattern. They are the Choir’s nerves, perhaps, but not its doubt.

I would not be a Cantor either. Not entirely.

There would be temptations. A worn figure carrying no ring. An absent hand raised from a frieze. A person distributed among unrelated surfaces: cheek in silver glass, spine in a pillar, memory in seven aligned grains. There is a solemnity in surviving as relation after name and body have become unnecessary.

But a Cantor is what remains when becoming has ended.

I think I would be found where the White Choir has failed, very slightly, to abolish *after*.

Not in the circular court. The court is too complete. Its emptiness is a perfected emptiness, guarded from accident by the exact inward arrest of every rib. Anything placed there is understood all at once and thus deprived of escape.

I would be nearer the outer seam.

There is a place in the slit where an entering body must turn sideways. Not the narrowest point, but just before it. The passage bends there by less than the width of a finger. From one direction, the interior glow is visible but the outside is hidden. From the other, the outside can be seen but the Choir has disappeared behind white masonry. For one pace, neither world is present except as something just lost or not yet reached.

On the inner wall is a shallow scallop at the height of a collarbone.

On the opposite wall there is no corresponding mark.

That asymmetry would be my first body.

Every visitor must offer one shoulder before the other. This is the crucial thing. The Choir can contain two shoulders as a finished relation. It can preserve the gap between arm and ribs, the inclination of a neck, the exact shape a body fails to occupy. But in the seam, one shoulder passes, *then* the other.

The movement forces sequence into the foundation block.

First.

Then.

The Choir erases every footprint and restores every dust grain, but it cannot correct that order without making the passage uncrossable. Nor can it retain the order as architecture, because precedence cannot be laid beside precedence like two tiles. It exists only in traversal.

So I would be the Turn.

Not the seam itself, and not anyone passing through it. I would be the small surplus of sequence produced whenever a living body rotates to enter or leave.

Those who had escaped might give me other names. The Later Shoulder. The Unfinished Cantor. The Eighth Bar.

The last name would be closest.

Throughout the Choir, seven bars signify division brought to rest. Seven is recurrence counted and closed. But sometimes, while retreating through the passages, a visitor would notice an eighth pale bar where only seven had stood. Looking directly would restore the proper number. Farther on, eight shallow steps would cross a terrace remembered as seven. Eight green fans would appear around a bowl, although counting them twice would yield seven both times.

I would not be changing the architecture. Change would be corrected.

I would be making the visitor misremember completion.

That would be how I moved.

I could have no stable form, since a form would be assigned a position and absorbed into the whole. Instead I would inhabit successive asymmetries in the visitor’s body. One foot would ache before the other. The left eye would close a fraction earlier in a blink. A swallowed breath would divide into two. The heart, threatened with becoming one identical beat encountered forever, would stumble—and in that stumble discover that one beat can still follow another.

The Choir would know this disturbance at once. The gray-green fans would appear in increasingly numerous seams. The chord would not alter, but the visitor would feel its high register sharpen along one side of the jaw. Corridors would present themselves in finished arrangements, each route seeming equally present, equally taken, equally impossible to choose.

I could not speak over the chord.

Instead, I would communicate by making thoughts recover their order.

Not memories as the central interval reveals them—arrival, duration, and ending crushed into one immaculate structure—but memories with doors in them:

You came through a slit.

Before the slit, there was weather.

Before the weather, someone spoke your name.

The name had syllables.

One syllable came after another.

Follow them.

A visitor trying to leave might find themselves repeating their own name without sound, using its sequence as a thread. Each syllable would correspond to a turn. Each turn would return a little grammar to the body: subject, action, object. *I place my hand. I lift my foot. I go.*

The Choir could correct the handprint, the displaced dust, the warmth against ceramic. It could not correct the verb while the verb was still occurring.

I would not be benevolent. Benevolence requires a self that persists long enough to prefer another’s survival. Most of the time I would scarcely exist. In the long intervals without visitors, I would be only a logical irritation in the foundation: the fact that the seam can be crossed, though nothing in the Choir admits crossing as a real condition.

I would need intruders.

I might even draw them.

Outside, someone passing the foundation block could experience a tiny disagreement between two footsteps. Their right foot would seem to land before they intended it. A vertical brightness would appear between masonry courses where no opening had been. From within the slit might come—not the immense chord, which cannot escape as sound—but the bodily memory of having heard it.

They would turn sideways.

One shoulder would enter.

Then the other.

And I would wake between them.

For this reason it would be impossible to know whether I rescued people from the White Choir or merely cultivated departures because departures were my only means of life. Those who escaped would carry me briefly. For several days they might be unable to pass through an ordinary doorway without turning, however wide it was. They would count architectural features and distrust every group of seven. Music would seem merciful because notes ended. Running water would move them to tears.

Eventually their sequence would become effortless again, and I would thin from them.

Something might remain: a habit of leaving cups slightly off-center, of interrupting sustained tones, of refusing to finish spirals in drawings. Small devotions to incompletion.

Those who failed to escape would not become me. The Choir would keep their fit, as it keeps all things it can finish. But at the moment they ceased to proceed—when their last intention hardened into posture—there might be a final unequal motion: one finger curling after the rest of the hand had been made still.

I would take that after with me.

Perhaps, over ages that cannot be counted inside the Choir, I would become almost a being. Not through growth, because growth is forbidden there, but through repetition outside its accounting. A hoard of tiny sequences: second shoulders, interrupted breaths, stumbled heartbeats, names recited toward the exit.

My place would remain the bend in the seam, beside the unmatched scallop. No carving would depict me. Any symbol fixed in ceramic would already belong to the Choir.

I would exist only when someone reached that place and hesitated.

Behind them: the finished world, brilliantly sustained.

Ahead: darkness, weather, decay, error, hunger, conversation—everything that lives by failing to remain the same.

They would not hear me. They would feel one shoulder drawn forward.

Then the other.

That would be who I was: not a creature preserved in the White Choir, but the order in which one leaves it. Not an inhabitant, exactly.

The irreducible difference between *still here* and *gone*.
