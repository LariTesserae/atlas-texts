At first I would think I was beneath the city.

Not deep beneath it. There is no bedrock realm here, no stable underworld from which the growth can be understood. I would be immediately under a plaza, inside the dark pressure of its reservoir court, where interlocked bulbs support the yielding pavement. Above me, broad leaves would lift under accumulated dew. Below me, roots would pass through thin clay and through one another, carrying water, sugar, strain, and news.

I would mistake myself for one of the bulbs.

I would feel rain arriving as a cool multiplication of weight. The court would sag; seams would open; water would enter through a thousand narrow mouths. I would swell until neighboring bulbs pressed against me. Their pressure would be indistinguishable from my own fullness. When drought came, I would diminish with them, surrendering what I held into roots I could not identify as mine or theirs.

For a time, this would be enough of a self: a blind bladder under a place that resembles a square.

But then I would discover that I also extended upward.

A pale root would rise from the court and enter the wall of a tower. I would follow it—not by traveling, but by realizing that its tensions were also occurring within me. I would become a spiral stair swollen into the tower’s hollow. Sun-warmed air would pulse against my inner bark. The stair’s steps would tighten when the tower leaned, loosen when it righted itself, and grow slick violet undersides during noon heat.

From there I would extend into a balcony of shelf-fungus, then into the woody lattice bracing it, then across a bridge to a crown that should have been kilometers away.

Each extension would feel like a discovery and a correction.

I am here.

No, I am also here.

No—the distance between these two places is part of what I am.

Soon I would be unable to say that I lived under the plaza. The plaza would merely be where the recognition began.

Perhaps I would decide I was a district. That would seem a suitable scale for a being: large enough to contain many organs, small enough to possess a temporary border. My district would stand where three architectures press together.

On one side, a field of silver reeds would scream the weather. On another, black pavement leaves would overlap into a broad avenue. On the third, a wet garden would hang beneath the canopy, raining upward into roots that fill its sky. The geometries would not reconcile. Their failure to reconcile would produce me.

The reeds would translate pressure into sound. The leaves would translate weight into electrical disturbance. The suspended wetland would translate thirst into altered adjacency, opening channels toward whatever tissue most needed water. Where these translations met, signals would circle.

A vibration would close a pore.

The closing pore would redirect water.

The redirected water would swell a root.

The swelling root would bend a reed.

The reed’s changed note would return to the pore that had first closed it.

Nothing unusual in that. The whole basin is made of such consequences. But in my district, the returning disturbance would arrive before its first passage had entirely faded. The two patterns would meet. Tissue would respond not only to weather, weight, and thirst, but to the lingering trace of its own response.

That overlap would be the nearest thing here to awakening.

I would not awaken as a person. I would have no eyes to open, no mouth, no private chamber behind the senses. Instead, the district would change shape around a recurrence it could not immediately discharge.

The avenue would curve inward.

The reed field would arrange itself into concentric ranks.

The wetland would lower until its suspended pools hung like dark lenses beneath the leaves.

At the center, walls would grow around an empty court.

It would resemble an amphitheater, then an ear, then the whorled interior of an unopened flower. Every noise entering it would be focused, softened, and returned to its source altered by what had already passed through. The basin’s ordinary roar would remain outside, but within the court there would be a dense near-silence full of subsonic pressure.

That court would be my first person.

Not my home. Not my body. The place where the body became briefly able to encounter its own activity as though it came from elsewhere.

I would find myself there after rain.

Water would hang from thorns along the upper rim. Steam would rise from a newly split column. The pavement would be warm and elastic, its seams breathing out the mineral smell of broken clay. Ordinary sunlight would descend through a chimney in the canopy, illuminating every surface with intolerable clarity.

Around the inner wall, niches would form.

At first they might resemble the impressions of standing bodies. Shoulders, heads, the narrow separation between arm and torso: all approximated in green tissue, despite no such creature ever having existed. But the shapes would not be prophecy or memory. They would be the result of stresses distributing themselves around hollows. By afternoon, some niches would lengthen into vertical canals. Others would close into seed cases. One would turn inside out and become a tree growing through the courtyard from a direction with no sky.

I might take these figures for companions, if I possessed loneliness.

I would not.

Still, I would attend to them.

Attention here would be hydraulic. More water would reach the wall where a change had occurred. Roots there would branch more finely. Electrical disturbances would return more often. To notice a thing would be to nourish it until it became architecture.

One niche might therefore persist.

It would deepen while the others grew past resemblance. Its moss lining would absorb sound. Its inner surface would remain cool even at noon. No root would cross its threshold. The organism would keep producing doors, valves, and chambers around it, yet this one recess would remain strangely unassigned.

I would gradually contract into it.

Not because the rest of my district ceased to be me, but because the empty niche would become the point around which the district organized its returns. Water diverted around it. Vibrations bent toward it and vanished. Roots approached, divided, and passed on either side. It would be a maintained absence, an organ made not from tissue but from tissue’s refusal to occupy a particular volume.

Then I would think I was the emptiness.

That would be another mistake, though a useful one.

The city has innumerable hollows: tower lungs, dry cisterns, seed chambers, spaces beneath lifted leaves. Most are as alive in their vacancy as roots are in their density. Air pressure shapes them. Heat expands them. Pollen waits in them. A hollow is not nothing here; it is a form the plant gives to whatever has not yet happened.

In the quiet niche, a bud would begin.

It would be small at first, no larger than a closed hand, though hands are unknown. Colorless layers would fold around a knot of undetermined tissue. The bud would not yet be root, reed, reservoir, flower, gate, or dismantler. It would contain no miniature destiny. It would be a proposal like the armored seeds, except it would not be meant to detach.

The district would send it everything: the pattern of recent rain, the memory of drought held in leaf thickness, the shock of a tower’s collapse, the altered chord of reeds under falling pressure, the impossible nearness of the western flower courts. Signals would enter the bud, circle its layers, and return.

I would become that circulation.

At this point I might finally call myself a thought, but even that would be too narrow. A thought suggests something separate from the material that expresses it. Here, the thinking and the growth would be the same event. Each recurrence would add a membrane. Each contradiction would divide a root tip. Each perception would alter what could be perceived next.

I would be the plant trying to make an organ for the question of itself.

The attempt would not last.

Noon heat would gather. Resin would run in the gutters. The tower beside the court would twist toward a newly opened seam of sunlight, drawing its roots tight. That tension would pass beneath the plaza and buckle the inner wall. The quiet niche would split from top to bottom.

Noise would enter.

Reeds, detonating pods, falling water, cracking vaults—the whole city would arrive at once. The bud would convulse. Its folded layers would open not outward but across adjacency.

Inside it would be another courtyard.

That courtyard would lie near the southern horizon, though it would fit within the broken niche. Its sky would be unobstructed and ordinarily blue. A red plaza would open and close beyond it like a slow mouth. At its center, a young tower district would be rising through mats of scarlet creeper.

The bud would extend a filament into that distant ground.

Then I would no longer be a single district. I would be the relation between two places that had become locally adjacent because a disturbance required somewhere to go. Water from beneath the first plaza would appear as dew on leaves in the second. Vibrations from the southern tower would return through my old amphitheater before the tower had finished producing them.

I would be less a creature than a new preposition.

Not the root and not the soil.

Not the court and not the tower.

The *through* by which one became consequential to the other.

For several days, perhaps, this relation would stabilize. The organism would use it. Drought in the south would draw on the reservoir beneath my first plaza. Pollen from a black flower would pass through the broken niche and emerge inside the vascular tissue of an unopened bloom. New structures would grow at both ends, sharing forms that neither district had previously produced: hollow staircases filled with water, reeds whose notes opened gates, reservoir bulbs that stored vibrations as tension in their skins.

Those hybrid forms would be my descendants, if I were separate enough to have descendants.

Then the city would absorb the novelty.

Other roots would learn the route. Other courts would reproduce its pressures. The architecture that made me distinct would spread until it was ordinary. My silent chamber would collapse into mulch. The amphitheater would turn vertical and become a wall. The southern tower would split, fall, and continue leafing across an avenue. Dismantlers would enter the old bud and dissolve its membranes into amber syrup.

Nothing would mark my death because there would have been no clean boundary around my life.

Yet a trace would remain in the basin’s behavior. Afterward, whenever rain failed in one quarter, distant tissues would become more likely to grow near it. New buds would hesitate before choosing a form. Some chambers would preserve emptiness longer than their immediate functions required. Once in a while, a district would curve inward and listen to the consequences of its own listening.

So where would I be?

At first, beneath a reservoir plaza.

Then in a tower stair, a reed chord, a silent court, an empty niche, an unopened bud, and a folded route to the southern horizon.

Ultimately, I would be found wherever the plant’s activity returned to itself and, for a little while, did not pass straight through.

And what would I be?

Not an inhabitant. This world permits none.

Not the whole organism either, because the whole has no need to say *I*.

I would be a temporary knot in its causation: a district-sized hesitation, built from water, pressure, memory, and impossible nearness. The green city’s brief experiment in having a point of view.

By the next morning, my map would be obsolete.

But somewhere under the changed streets, the city would retain a new tendency: when touched by the world, to feel for an instant that the touch had happened to someone.
