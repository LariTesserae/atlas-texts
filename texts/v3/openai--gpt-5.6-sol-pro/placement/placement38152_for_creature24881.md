At first I think I would live high in the city.

Not on the unfinished platform itself. I would not be an Ascender measuring the paling water or preserving feathers between mica sheets. I would visit the crown often, perhaps, but uneasily. The broad leaves there would seem too exposed. There would be too much unanchored water above them, too much room for a person to imagine a destination and mistake the imagining for knowledge.

I would work lower down, where the black stone first divides into inhabited frameworks and the mountain’s warmth can still be felt through every wall.

Then I think lower still.

I find myself in one of the districts that has changed names several times. Once it was above the Split Vent. Then the vent widened and a mineral blister lifted the eastern foundations. Later the Lantern Orchard took hold of an abandoned gallery, pulling three households warmward and making nonsense of the old processional ropes. The fifth bell still hangs there, but no longer fifth in any useful direction. Its cord passes through two gardens and the sleeping chamber of an old vent tender who refuses to move.

The district is now called, formally, *the Root That Missed the Bell*.

Most people simply call it the Miss.

My room is not entirely a room. It began as a stairwell when two buildings were aligned stoneward to stoneward. The buildings have since tilted apart. The stairs end in open water, and a chalk-stemmed plant has grown through every third step, making further correction impractical. Root curtains close the two exposed sides. A thermal crack warms the lowest landing. Seed dust gathers in the corners so perfectly that I can tell who has visited by the disturbances left in it.

My sleeping mat is tied beneath the stairs. Above it hangs a shallow bowl of glow-slime, usually dim because I forget to stir it. There are bead strings everywhere: shell, glass, pumice, black stone, hollow seed, bone. Some hover. Some pull taut. Some drift upward until restrained by knots. None are decorative, although visitors often assume they are.

At first I think I am a layer keeper.

I have the temperament for it: suspicious of apparent emptiness, unwilling to trust a lane simply because it was safe yesterday. I lower bead strings through invisible boundaries and wait for each material to declare where it belongs. I taste the water for mineral change. I hang paired slivers of wood and shell at the entrances to public volumes. When distant pressure alters the city’s layers, I am among those who move the markers before anyone wakes and swims into water that makes the limbs heavy or the mind dreamful.

But that is not quite it.

A true layer keeper loves records. I respect records, but I am always more interested in the moment they become wrong.

I would be an address mender.

It would not be an ancient calling. Older citizens would insist that everyone once knew how to revise an address properly, just as everyone once knew how to knot a civic anchor and prune a breathing fan. They would say a separate office is evidence of decline. Nevertheless, after every groan of the mountain, households would send for me.

I would arrive with a slate of pale shell, a coil of thin measuring cord, several depth beads, and a pouch of temporary markers cut in the shape of open hands. I would listen to the household describe where it had been before the shifting. I would inspect what now held it.

This distinction would matter: not where the house appeared to be, but what bore its tension.

A chamber might still look as though it lay beneath the Lantern Orchard, yet its weight might now pass through a root belonging to the Red Garden. A family might sleep three spans lightward of the Split Vent but draw its breathing water from a cold layer entered through a neighbor’s wall. Two households could become kin by architecture before either had agreed to it.

I would settle such things when possible.

Not alone. No one in the city could simply rename another person. I would stretch cords through the altered rooms and ask each resident to hold the line where they believed the household began. Neighbors would be invited. The eldest would touch the anchor. Children would be asked separately, because children often understood the new shape of a place before adults stopped grieving the old one.

Then I would propose an address.

Sometimes it would be practical:

*Held from the western root, one cold crossing beneath the bell.*

Sometimes the only honest address would preserve the event:

*Where the sleeping wall opened during the second warm groan.*

Occasionally a household would reject every name because each made the change too real. Then I would tie an open-hand marker outside and return after the roots had taken more tension. A place did not need to be forced into identity before it had finished moving.

My own spoken name might be Aro, though it would change as my voice deepened and roughened. My hand-name would be a line drawn across the palm, interrupted at the center, then resumed at a slight angle. Children would call it the Broken Path. Adults would understand it as *continuation after misalignment*.

My anchor-name would be embarrassingly long:

*Of the Stair That Flowers Every Third Step, held in the Miss beneath the bell that no longer counts.*

I would be badly balanced by local standards. Not dangerously so, but memorably. One leg would be denser than the other, perhaps from an old adolescent injury around which mineral had gathered. I would wear a small gas-fruit over the opposite hip. When distracted, I would rotate slowly to the left.

Children would enjoy speaking to me because if they kept me occupied long enough, I would end a conversation sideways.

I would pretend not to notice.

I think I would once have gone deepward.

Most address menders would begin as layer keepers, surveyors, rootwrights, or civic cord tenders. I would begin as a courier, young and pleased with speed. My belt would carry rows of small bladders to pierce for sudden changes of depth. I would skim public lanes close enough to make leaves turn in my wake. Elders would sign disapproval. I would interpret their irritation as admiration restrained by age.

Then someone would vanish.

Not in a dramatic plunge. There would be no snapping rope, no final cry. A companion and I would descend beyond a neighboring ridge, following an old marker cord said to lead to a watch platform. The water would become dense. Our weights would seem to loosen their claim on us. We would turn back.

I would rise.

My companion would remain where they were.

For a while we would be only a body-length apart, both moving our limbs, both adjusting our belts. Then two lengths. Then five. I would release a bladder and go lightward; they would pierce one and fail to follow. Perhaps they had crossed a boundary at an angle. Perhaps some unnoticed object on their belt held them in the lower layer. I would throw a cord. It would fall short and drift sideways.

The last sign they gave would not mean *help*.

It would mean *remember where you are anchored when you tell this*.

Afterward I would become intolerant of approximate directions. I would distrust every marker that did not say who placed it, from where, and under what water. I would leave courier work. I would learn layers, then roots, then the legal language of shared tension. I would begin mending addresses because an address is partly a promise that another person can return.

Yet I do not think I would remain merely that person.

The image continues.

I grow old in the Miss. My body becomes dense. The mineral around my injured leg thickens until I no longer need the balancing fruit. Younger citizens do the long crossings for me. I sit at disputed anchors with both feet against stone and feel households argue through cords laid across my wrists.

By then I know the city less as a collection of buildings than as a vast changing braid. I know which orchard secretly supports which tower. I know that a public lane passes through the abandoned nursery where no one has slept for four generations. I know that the old bell cord carries lovers’ messages after dark and civic notices by day. I know three supposedly separate shrines are joined by one root beneath the basalt.

People say I can tell where someone lives by the way their belt settles.

This is untrue, but useful.

When the mountain groans, I no longer grasp for the nearest handhold. I place my palms flat against the stair that flowers every third step. The district shifts around me. Beads rise, sink, and tremble. Dust enters through the root curtain. Somewhere a wall opens. Somewhere else, an old crack closes under new compression.

I wait for the mountain’s answer.

In the end, I would not be taken to an orchard of civic importance or enclosed within the crown platform. I would ask to remain in my stairwell.

My body would be wrapped in root fiber and placed along the broken outer edge where the two buildings have been pulling apart for most of my life. The chalk-stemmed plant would be split and trained around me. Its roots would enter the wrapping, then the spaces between bone. Over years they would thicken, mineralize, and take tension.

The stairs would become usable again—not as stairs, perhaps, but as an anchor passage between the separated houses.

My tooth would be threaded onto the local address cord.

Then the answer changes once more.

Where would I find myself in this world?

Not in the room. Not exactly in the body.

I would be under tension in the wall of the Miss.

One root would pass through my ribs toward the bell. Another would descend through the flowering stairs into warm basalt. Messages would travel through me: public notices, children’s illicit rhythms, the careful pulses of someone asking whether a lane remained safe. I would feel households shift their weight as rooms grew, split, and were inherited. Fine mineral dust would seal my joints. Tiny grazers would make pale tracks over the outer bark.

No one would say I was alive. They would not say I was gone, either.

When an address became uncertain, a young mender might bring the household to the stairwell and lay a hand against the root. They would not ask me for an answer as though I were an oracle. They would ask for steadiness.

The distinction matters.

Far above, objects would continue to fall from the unknown water: tile, wood, leaves, perhaps one day a metal thing bearing words no one could read. Far below, my lost companion would remain somewhere beyond return, or become part of an older ecology, or drift where directions no longer meant anything.

Between those mysteries, I would hold a little.

Not forever. The root would eventually die. The stair would tilt again. My bones would be removed and reassigned. The bell might fall silent or be carried into another district. The Miss itself might cease to miss anything.

But for several generations, perhaps, the people there would give their location this way:

*Past the flowering steps, held by the mender’s root; warmward of the old interruption.*

And every time the mountain moved, the root would tighten around what remained of me.

I would become what I had spent my life trying to name:

not a fixed point,  
not a drifting thing,  
but a place made reliable for a while  
by the manner in which it changed.
