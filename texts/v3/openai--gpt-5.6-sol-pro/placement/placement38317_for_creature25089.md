At first I would think I was the thing beneath the three service steps.

Not the walking body, but the pale folded tissue into which it presses itself at night. I would feel its weight settle through me after the amber light had gone: wrist, ribs, long femur, the broad soft architecture of one foot. I would take that pressure for companionship. Perhaps even for sheltering.

But the tissue beneath the steps continues into too many places. My awareness would not stop where a body ought to stop. I would feel water entering through crushed brick under the northern fern bed. I would know when a snail crossed the overflow moss, not from its weight but from the minute interruption in moisture behind it. The red grilles would be distant sensations of openness. The sump would be a permanent absence—a pull with no surface and no bottom.

So I would decide I was part of the equalizing culture itself.

That would also be wrong, or not sufficiently precise.

I think you would find me in one of the shallow recesses beneath the western wall, behind a curtain of liverwort and black fern stems. The ceramic there retains the mineral outline of a body that no longer occupies it: the suggestion of a shoulder, five long fingers, a bent spine. Inside that outline, hidden by wet felt and root fiber, the colonial tissue has thickened into something denser than the strands around the pipes. There are unfinished structures within it. A row of copper-colored nail edges. A folded web of translucent skin. Plates still soft enough to bend.

I would be a spare posture.

Not another inhabitant, exactly. The court has no need for two independent keepers, and almost nothing in it is allowed to become wholly separate. I would be a place where the distributed organism has begun to remember how to gather itself, then stopped. A second walking form held below the threshold of necessity.

Most of the time I would possess no head. Thought would reach me as pressure, chemistry, and borrowed orientation. When the walking body touched one finger to the blue ceramic mark at noon, the contact would travel through the wall, into the beds, and finally into the recess where my hand was being slowly prepared. I would feel the level as though it were a line drawn across an unopened eye.

I would know the court through rehearsals.

A trace of copper would sharpen the unfinished edges of my tongue. Excess iron would pass through me as warmth. When sediment reached the second expansion joint, muscles along my folded back would tighten in the pattern required to lift it, though I had never lifted anything. Each clearing of a grille would be repeated faintly in my buried fingers: left to right, then downward. The current keeper’s stoop would become a tension in my spine. Its caution around the black center would be formed in me before I had feet.

I would not be asleep. Sleep belongs to something that has already been awake.

I would be redundancy grown almost to consciousness.

The court would feed me very little. Enough mineral to keep the plates forming. Enough organic film to prevent the pale tissue from being reclaimed. Decades might pass between the addition of one finger joint and the next. Moss would fill the recess in front of me. Enamel lettering would soften. Generations of drainage moths might flatten their wings against the tile without ever knowing that beneath them a face was acquiring its sealed ear-depressions.

Perhaps I had been raised before. The outline on the ceramic might be mine rather than another’s. Perhaps, after some ancient fluctuation, I had unfolded, crossed the dry joints, cleared the red grilles, and then been taken apart when one walking form again became sufficient. In that case I would not remember events. I would remember aptitudes: the width of the service rail in my palm, the harmless pressure of moth feet, the exact distance a long leg must span to avoid the basin.

The current inhabitant would know I was there, but not as another self. Occasionally, while tending the western beds, it might place its hand against the tile over my unfinished hand. For a moment the two sets of fingers would align, separated by ceramic.

It would not be affection.

But neither would it be merely inspection.

In a place where rules have become anatomy, touch may be the closest thing to a promise.

If the machinery continued forever, I might never emerge. The tissue could maintain me indefinitely as an unused answer. My plates would gather mineral rings. My limbs would remain soft and folded. I would participate in every correction without performing one: taking in the court’s fractional excess, yielding it back, preserving the possibility of action while action remained unnecessary.

Then, one day, the roar might cease.

Not falter. Not lose a phase and return. Stop.

The sudden silence would strike the court more violently than any sound. Water would continue over the spillway for a few seconds, loud in a way it had never been allowed to be. Ferns would lift because there was no vibration to bow them. Condensation would fall as distinct drops. For the first time, distance would enter the room.

The walking keeper would stand upright.

Beneath the western wall, I would stand with it.

The culture would force water from my recess. Mineral plates would split free from the tile. My unfinished mouth would open, perhaps not to hear and not to breathe, but because that is the shape the organism makes when the structure of the world disappears.

Whether I then unfolded to help preserve the balance or merely became alert enough to witness its loss, I cannot say.

So that is where I would be: behind the western ceramic, inside one of the empty bodily outlines.

And that is what I would be: not the keeper of the court, but the court’s reserve capacity for becoming a keeper—the second hand it has not yet needed to raise.
