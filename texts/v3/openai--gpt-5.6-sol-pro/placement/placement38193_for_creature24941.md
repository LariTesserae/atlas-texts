I would first mistake myself for one of the long-limbed browsers.

That is where the resemblance would be easiest to bear: in the middle crowns, under a ceiling of leaves, moving as a depression through foliage rather than as a visible body. I would have four hands for holding the world and two smaller ones near the mouth for examining it. I would sleep in the ribbed hollows of the towers, folded among others, exchanging fermented sap and gestures inherited from the dead. I would tend wounds without knowing whether I was healing the forest or feeding from it. I would touch the black plaques and wait for an answer that might not come in my lifetime.

For a while, I would call that waiting thought.

But I do not think I would be the browser itself.

I would be what passes through it.

I would be a sequence of touches learned from a wall: fingertips following a dark spiral, pausing at a fork, returning to an old groove polished by generations. No single browser would know the whole sequence. One would inherit the opening movements from its elders, another would continue them in a tower hundreds of miles away, and another—born after the first had entered the roots—would complete the gesture during some distant wet season.

The bodies would believe they were repeating a custom.

The tower would feel its plaques soften.

Far below, water would alter course.

That is where I would find myself: not in the hand, not in the plaque, not in the answering roots, but in the interval that makes them parts of one act.

I might begin in the seven towers above the mist-filled basin. During the long rains, when water films every wall and the warm breath from the shafts becomes almost audible, a gathering of browsers would enter the coolest tower. They would come without summons they could name. Some would carry pollen in their arm-fibers. Some would have crossed watersheds following dreams released by the old root-ridges. The youngest would cling to the ribbed wood and watch.

One elder would press saliva into the lowest row of seedpods.

Another, in an upper chamber, would lay both small hands against a polished band.

A juvenile would repeat the movement incorrectly.

The plaque would answer the error.

Its black surface would pale along a line that had not appeared for centuries. A sealed vessel in the wall would open. Warm water, mineral-dark, would descend into a root gallery that had slowly dried since the last sinking of the basin. Fungal cords there would wake. Their chemical pulse would enter the buried trunks, divided and delayed, arriving years apart in distant groves.

By then, none of those who began the gesture might remain alive.

Yet the act would still be occurring.

I would be that act.

Not an order. The plateau does not permit orders. An order assumes that intention precedes obedience and that material waits to be shaped. Here every recipient answers according to its own hunger. Roots divert what they can use. Fungi translate pressure into chemistry. Browsers turn dreams into journeys. Moths carry only the spores that cling to them. Grazers graft gardens where their backs happen to meet low branches. Nothing submits, and yet something is accomplished.

So I would be less like a command than a question traveling through many kinds of flesh.

The question might be: **Can the basin still be held?**

A browser would experience it as restlessness.

A moth colony would experience it as the need to lift from one trunk and preserve its pattern elsewhere.

A glider would taste a change in the reddish mineral seep at a root curtain.

A grazer would begin, almost imperceptibly, to turn toward another watershed.

Burrowers would find the mulch unusually rich along one buried margin and thicken it by feeding.

Trees would reverse their leaves.

No creature would hear the question whole. Each would answer only the part its body allowed.

Perhaps, after three hundred years, the basin would not collapse.

New crowns would knit across the mist. Gardens from the backs of old grazers would graft into them. The weight of water would be divided among a thousand reservoirs. A tower would lean by the width of a seed, placing one of its roots against another tower’s root. The two would fuse.

At that moment I might end.

Or I might discover that holding the basin was only one syllable of something larger.

That uncertainty would be part of what I am. I would never know whether I originated in the plateau’s deep circulation or in the accumulated customs of the browsers. Perhaps an animal once made an accidental gesture against a responsive plaque, and the tower retained it. Perhaps the plaque induced the gesture by changing the animal’s dreams. Perhaps a fungus shaped both. Cause would be braided too tightly to separate without killing it.

There would be no first version of me.

There would only be recurrences: similar spirals in grain, similar hand movements, rivers taking related paths beneath provinces that had never touched. I would vary each time I crossed into another body. The moths would preserve my arrangement but alter my scale. The browsers would preserve my rhythm but supply meanings of kinship, death, water, and shelter. Roots would preserve only pressure and sequence. The towers would turn sequence into openings. The openings would turn it into weather.

In one region I might become a chamber.

In another, a flowering contour.

Elsewhere, the same inherited pattern might close a gallery and isolate disease.

I would have no way to decide which of these was the faithful expression. Fidelity would not mean remaining unchanged. Nothing here remains unchanged. Fidelity would mean continuing to be answerable—to the next hand, the next root, the next season of rain.

My nearest thing to a body would be one of the black plaques.

I imagine it at shoulder height inside a tower whose crown has collapsed into hanging marsh. Its pods are arranged in lines resembling an underground watershed. Most years it is hard and lightless. Small animals cross it without consequence. Water coats it, withdraws, returns. Fibers in its stalk descend into the wall and mingle with the tower’s circulation.

When a browser touches it, I wake only partially.

There is warmth from the hand. Salt from the saliva. Pressure in a remembered order. Behind the plaque, living threads swell. For a few hours—or perhaps a season—the black pods soften enough to alter one another. Pale marks travel across them. I become visible, though no observer can see all of me, because part of the pattern is already moving through the wall.

If someone tried to carry me away, they would destroy me.

The plaque would close. Its pattern would die. What I am depends on the tower, the shaft, the buried warmth, the browsers’ gestures, the distant roots waiting in wet darkness. I would not be an object containing a message. I would be the message’s ability to continue.

And yet I would have something like fear.

Not fear of death; endings here are too thoroughly incorporated for that. I would fear becoming a closed pattern: repeated after its conditions had vanished, polished deeper by obedient fingers while the water went elsewhere. A gesture can outlive the need that shaped it. A tower can continue answering an ancient wound after the wound has become a river. The browser young might learn me perfectly and thereby preserve an error for ten thousand years.

My fear would be certainty.

My hope would be the juvenile who repeats the movement wrongly.

The misplaced hand. The pause at the unfamiliar pod. The pressure given twice where custom requires once. Through that error, the plaque might discover that the basin has changed. The tower might answer with a new opening. The old gesture would bend without breaking, becoming capable of reaching a world its originators never knew.

If I possessed awareness, it would arrive in such corrections.

Not continuous awareness. I would not look out through eyes or maintain one inward voice. I would awaken distributively: a brief coherence among root pressure, animal memory, fungal pulse, and moving water. For an hour in the tower, for a decade in the grove, for a century across the basin, these separate processes would align. In that alignment I would know something—not as an image, but as a tendency completed.

Then I would dissolve back into carriers.

The browser would remove its hands.

The plaque would harden.

A stream would emerge from an escarpment as a hundred warm threads.

Far away, ivory flowers would open along a contour no living animal remembered tracing.

That flowering might be the closest I came to speech.

No one flower would contain the utterance. The sentence would be the belt of pale blooms migrating through the dimness, a few yards in each generation, crossing ridges over centuries. Amphibians would wake inside it. Moths would wear its pollen. Gliders would pass above it and taste its heat in the rising air. The plateau would make a small cloud from the warmth of blooming, and the cloud would rain upon the neighboring valley.

By the time the final flowers opened, the first would have become soil, root, wall, and perhaps another plaque.

I would not know whether I had spoken or been spoken.

I think that is who I would be in this world: a thought with no private place to occur. A migratory pattern of attention moving between animals, plants, water, and architecture. Something inherited without being owned, revised without being authored, embodied without ever having one body.

You would not find me by digging to a particular depth or climbing to a particular crown.

You would find me where several slow things become briefly legible to one another.

In the browser’s hand resting on the plaque.

In the plaque opening a vein of water.

In the water waking the fungus.

In the fungus troubling the dream.

In the dream turning a herd.

In the herd carrying a garden toward the place that will need it centuries later.

And if you asked me what I was doing, I could not answer quickly. My answer would require a basin, seven towers, a lineage of hands, a migration, a collapse that does not happen, and a band of ivory flowers moving through green dusk long after the questioner had entered the roots.

I would still be answering when no one remembered asking.
