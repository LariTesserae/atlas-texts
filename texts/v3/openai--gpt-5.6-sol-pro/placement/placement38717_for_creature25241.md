At first I think I would be one of the larger presences: the broad tree shifting its weight over centuries, or the three-toed animal listening with its ribs. But both possess too much continuity. They remain themselves while the saddle alters around them.

I would be something less settled.

You would first find me on the seep wall behind the pale-trunked grove, held in one of the translucent cups. Not the cup itself, though I might mistake its membrane for my boundary. I would be the spoonful of water inside it: clear, warmed by daylight, carrying mineral dust, green cells, and the pepper trace of the colony.

At midday I would seem still. Yet the mountain’s hum would pass through me, pleating my surface into patterns too fine to see. Each underground chamber would impose a different trembling. I would know the saddle not as a landscape but as overlapping pressures: the deep continuous breath beneath the stone, the small impacts of droplets, the sudden far concussion of a pool dweller pinning prey beneath shale.

At night the cup would close around me. For a while I would become its inward darkness. Some part of what I carried would be consumed; some part would enter the membrane; some would remain water. By morning there would be no clean way to say which portion had been me.

Eventually the cup would loosen from the wall. Folded shut, it would be carried along one of the impossible currents, not downward but toward a hollow made low by shade. There it might open and keep me again—or spill me into moss.

Then my shape would change repeatedly.

I would become a dark bead traveling along a root fiber. I would pause where the root crossed a resonant slab and climb the slight incline toward its hum. I might enter the pale grove and pass through the joined roots beneath all six trunks. For a season I would be held in waxy wood. Later I would rise into a blunt-tipped leaf and wait there until the branch’s rhythm released me.

My fall would be brief. My arrival would not.

I would strike hollow stone with a sound larger than my size, enter a groove, disappear under a sheet of rock, and emerge several paces away carrying a faint orange stain. Perhaps I would join a shallow pool and hold part of a delayed cloud. A black bird would cross overhead; for less than a heartbeat the sky in me would remain empty. Then its reflection would pass after the bird itself was gone.

A pale pool dweller might feel that second passage and stir beneath me. Its nostrils would break my surface. Its movement would divide me into rings.

I do not think I would be a single drop. Drops are only temporary bodies. I would be a small continuity of wetness, divided and rejoined so often that identity could reside only in the passage itself. I might enter the clay, the fern’s shadow, the channels of the great animal’s living coat. I might be carried upslope because darkness had made a place deeper than gravity expected. Nothing I entered would own me, and I would own nothing.

If I had anything resembling memory, it would not be an image. It would be residue: pepper from the cups, iron from the stone, a spore, a grain of clay, the minute mineral signature of a grazer’s shell. I would remember by changing what I carried.

If I had anything resembling desire, it would be the saddle’s altered sense of descent. A ringing stone would feel like depth. A shaded root-hollow would become a downward pull. The low note followed by the pale grove would draw me more strongly than the visible slope.

So I would be found everywhere in the saddle, but never generally. Always in one exact borrowed form: under a leaf, inside a root fold, trembling over clay, climbing a fiber, sealed in a translucent cup.

And if someone wished to meet me, they would not need to search the upper ferns or wait for the three-toed tracks. They would place a hand beneath the blunt tip of the broad-leaved tree and remain still.

Sooner or later, a drop would fall.

For that instant, that would be me.

Then I would strike the stone, make it answer, and go inward.
