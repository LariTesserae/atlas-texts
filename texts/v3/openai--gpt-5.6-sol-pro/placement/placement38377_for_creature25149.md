I would not find myself at the valley floor, where every upstream decision eventually arrives, nor among the upper cisterns, where weather first enters the works. I would live somewhere in the middle elevations, in a district difficult to describe without giving directions.

Below the west orchard, where the warm return main passes behind the old ceramics hall. Across the retracting bridge, unless the lower canal is raised. Up the stair with the split green handrail. The wall sweats there even in dry weather. My door would be the second one after the change from black brick to stone.

It would not originally have been a dwelling. Perhaps it was once a valve room, then a tool store, then an office for a load council whose records had long since been moved elsewhere. One wall would curve because a reservoir stood behind it. Another would be crossed by a capped pipe too large to remove. I would have built shelves around its flanges and hung wet coats from the old wheel. The room would warm toward morning, when water returned from the lower baths, and cool abruptly during filtration backwash. Cups on the table would touch their saucers with a faint, regular sound.

At first I think I would be a route steward, carrying notices through districts affected by changes in water, access, or load. It would suit me to walk between systems rather than belong entirely to one. I would explain that the orchard stair was closed not merely because the wall had cracked, but because the soil above it had taken more rain than expected; that the temporary crossing was safe only until the evening freight interval; that if the tiles near the laundry began clicking, everyone should leave by the mushroom gallery rather than the marked evacuation ramp.

But the longer I imagine the work, the less official it becomes.

I would be one of those people sent for when two correct records contradicted each other.

The gauge says the cistern is falling, but the overflow has begun.

The plans show no conduit behind this wall, but the plaster is warm.

The incline cable is properly tensioned, yet cups tremble in rooms three terraces away whenever the empty cradle passes.

I would carry a narrow case containing chalk, waxed tags, glass tubes, a folding mirror, a listening rod, and several strips of cloth. Nothing elaborate. My most useful instrument would be time. I would stand beside a pipe long enough to learn whether its knocking repeated. I would sit on a closed stair through one full release cycle. I would put my cheek near brickwork and feel for a draft too weak to move the hairs on my hand.

People might call me a discrepancy keeper, though that would not be the formal name. Children would call me when peel launched into one gutter emerged somewhere impossible. Gardeners would show me a bed whose moss remained dry during rain. An old woman would insist that a disused speaking tube had begun breathing at night. Most such mysteries would have ordinary causes: a forgotten bypass, a warped gate, roots opening an old mortar seam, heat crossing between systems through an iron brace. Ordinary would not mean unimportant.

I would maintain the maps of things that happened but were not supposed to happen.

These maps would be layered and untidy. Blue for water traveling outside its assigned circuit. Yellow for transmitted vibration. White for air. Red for heat. Dotted lines where the path was inferred rather than seen. Dates would matter more than certainty. A route present in winter might vanish when roof soil dried. A wall might carry sound only when the reservoir behind it was full. I would never erase an old path completely. I would fade it and write beside it:

*Not observed since the east terrace was lightened.*

Or:

*Returns during prolonged western rain.*

My authority would be small but awkward. I could close a passage for observation, request that a pump be run out of sequence, or ask a household to tolerate an opened wall for a night. I could not order a district to rebuild anything. Instead, I would bring evidence to the overlapping councils and try to explain how a tremor in one place belonged to a weight change somewhere no one had thought to include.

This would make me occasionally welcome and frequently inconvenient.

I would arrive during meals. I would ask mill crews to stop a shaft that had not stopped in years. I would tell a school that the harmless warmth in its basement was not coming from the marked heating line. I would put small paper seals across cracks and return to find that someone had peeled them away because they looked untidy. I would be accused, sometimes correctly, of hearing patterns where none existed.

Still, the valley would reward suspicion more often than pride.

My clothes would have no bright professional color beyond a narrow gray-and-blue tab allowing me to open observation panels but not operate most of what lay behind them. My gloves would be thin enough to feel vibration through metal. At my throat I would wear a lamp with a shutter, because reflected light can conceal a film of moving water. One knee of my trousers would always be damp. In my pocket there would be roof apples, usually bruised, and bits of chalk softened by the weather.

I would know many people without knowing their rooms. A hinge mechanic who sang scales while testing facade pins. Two sisters who tended the reed beds beneath the public square and disagreed about everything except contamination. A child in the upper dye quarter whose reports were so exact that adults had learned not to dismiss them. A bath attendant who could identify distant gate movements by the ripples they made along the tiled pools.

There would also be one elderly man living above a seedling tram stop who remembered a culvert absent from every surviving plan. He would describe it differently each time. I would believe him because the contradictions would follow the weather.

Perhaps I would eventually inherit his custody—not of the culvert itself, but of the uncertainty around it.

During storms I would not be assigned to a single valve or grille. I would move. This would be dangerous in a place where routes changed under load, but someone would need to travel between the fragments of understanding. I would carry spoken reports when signal lines failed, compare tremors felt on opposite sides of a wall, and mark doors that had begun to bind. I would pause often, one hand on masonry, while rain moved through the valley in a million visible and hidden streams.

My task would not be to know the whole system. No one could.

It would be to notice when one part had begun speaking in the voice of another.

A reservoir wall warming like a return pipe. An orchard deck vibrating like a cable bed. A drainage channel smelling faintly of yeast from a bakery three districts away. Such crossings would mean that the valley had made, or found, a connection omitted from its intended design. Sometimes that connection would be a threat. Sometimes it would save the district: an abandoned flue drawing floodwater from a basement, an old foundation redistributing weight after newer braces failed, roots holding together soil scheduled for removal.

I would come to resist the easy division between defect and adaptation. The valley would teach me that a fault can become a support if enough other things have learned to lean on it.

In time, the people I served would begin to treat me in the same way they treated certain old instruments: useful, imperfect, calibrated by long familiarity. They would know my errors. I would mistake echoes for sources. I would give too much weight to repeated stories. I would distrust newly poured walls because they had not yet acquired a readable history. Younger technicians with finer sensors would correct me, and I would be grateful when I was wise enough.

My deepest fear would not be collapse. Collapse announces itself eventually.

I would fear a change that made everything seem more normal: a pipe falling silent because flow had found a route behind the wall; a crack closing because the load had shifted elsewhere; a damp stair drying while water accumulated in an unseen chamber. I would distrust sudden stillness.

Late in life, I might no longer make the steepest rounds. Apprentices would bring me recordings, samples, and contradictory reports. I would sit in the former valve room with its morning warmth and ask questions that seemed irrelevant.

Was the greenhouse folded shut?

Were the bats restless?

Did the lift rise when loaded, or afterward?

Which side of the railing was wet?

Had anyone boiled water in the room above?

Sometimes the answer would emerge. Sometimes it would not. Unresolved cases would be kept rather than forced into explanation.

When I died, I think my mineral tile would be set somewhere without a view: on the inner face of an inspection stair, perhaps, where workers rested a hand while descending toward a chamber that did not appear on public maps. My name might face the wall.

That would be appropriate. I would have spent my life attending to what passed behind surfaces.

But perhaps I would not be the person at all.

Perhaps, after imagining the lamp, the wet knee, the chalk maps and listening rod, I find that I am what the person is listening for: a slight recurring tremor passing through several unrelated structures. The almost-imperceptible consequence of freight moving high on one slope, transmitted through a brace, a buried rail, the reservoir shell, and the capped pipe crossing the room. Not a message. Not consciousness. Simply connection made sensible.

I would be felt in the cup touching its saucer before dawn.

The discrepancy keeper would wake, place a palm against the old valve wheel, and wait.

After three pulses, I would stop.

By evening I would return, a little stronger, and somewhere above the west orchard a twelve-year-old would close a bridge.
