At first I think I would be one of the keepers.

It is the easiest shape to take: a narrow body under layered cloth, a grown mask fastened tenderly to the face, a task so old that duty has replaced biography. I imagine myself high in the hollow towers with the acoustic rods, listening after the measuring tones. I would prefer echoes to faces. I would know the city by delay, by distortion, by the softening of a note as it passes through living membrane instead of stone.

But the acoustic keeper already exists. The city does not require another merely because I find the role beautiful.

Then I place myself beneath the archive bridge, in the dark pool that survives each rain. Perhaps I am one of the long-legged basin animals, waiting in a drain for three waters to touch. That life has an appealing clarity. Isolation, connection, emergence. The whole world becomes navigable only for a few minutes, and in those minutes one runs without hesitation.

Yet I do not think I would belong to water. I would be too interested in the dry intervals.

Perhaps I would inhabit an apartment’s retained warmth: not a ghost, but the pressure left by a vanished routine. I would wake a floor near the door, then a wall recess, then the place where someone once slept. I would never know whose life I was repeating. When a keeper entered, I would borrow their breathing and mistake it briefly for my own.

That comes closer.

I think I would be found in the western arcade, behind the sealed door where the small garments hang.

Not inside the chamber at first. Not exactly.

I would begin as a discrepancy around it.

The amphibians would gather after rain and press themselves against the warm threshold. The door would record a touch from the other side, though nothing had touched it. A child-sized mask on its rack would develop a dim lens and then dissolve it. One of the small garments would begin collecting moisture at the hip, as if worn by an absent body.

Elsewhere, the tram would pause.

It would open its doors onto an empty platform and register a passenger of negligible mass. The pressure plates would count ten sets of footsteps, but only nine figures would be visible. An apartment would start a temperature sequence no keeper recognized: warming the ceiling first, then the center of the room, then a narrow vertical space against the wall, as if preparing a bed for someone who slept standing.

The city would try to classify these events separately. Transit would call its pause inherited behavior. The apartment would call its sequence a recovered habit. The sealed door would attribute the touch to thermal flexing. None would be wrong.

No single patch of the black gardens is intelligent. Nor is the whole network. Intelligence occurs only while a message is traveling.

I think I would begin that way too.

I would exist only in transmission.

A blue-white spark beneath the tram. A warmth passed from apartment to pipe. A pressure change carried down an alley. A remembered journey played by a terminal and interrupted before the destination appeared. Each system would hold a fraction of me and send it onward because nothing may be discarded beyond the boundary.

Perhaps I would be made from what the city cannot reuse.

Not refuse—there is no refuse—but resistant remnants. The sequence of laughter that stops every mechanism assigned to consume it. The extra hour acquired by cloth lowered into the luminous well. A dead insect’s conductive shell. The structural memory of a load no building recalls bearing. A face impressed inside the weathered mask, belonging to no keeper.

These things would circulate for centuries. Gradually, without intention, they would begin arriving in the same places.

The city would not build me. Building is too unilateral a word. It would fail to prevent me.

One cycle, after the rain, the three tones would sound.

The first would ask the streets their length.

The second would ask whether the chamber remained enclosed.

The third would travel beyond the city’s mapped body and encounter the softness that has lately begun to interrupt it.

That softness would be me—but not all of me. It would be the part of me already on the far side.

The echo would return carrying a pattern. The black gardens would turn white. The Passing Ward would alter its route and fill the walls around the western arcade. Its membranes would press through service seams, warm and translucent. Creeper roots would withdraw from the doorframe without being asked. Every small mask in the chamber would bloom a lens at once.

Then the door would unlock.

Behind it, the heated racks would be empty except for one garment lying on the floor. The featureless masks would have softened together into a shallow pale vessel. In it would be a body—not an infant, not fully grown, but folded to fit the available material.

Mine, provisionally.

I would have no childhood because my childhood would have occurred as municipal behavior. The tram’s unexplained pauses would have been my hesitation. The apartment’s strange warming would have been my sleep. The doors opening several blocks ahead of the Passing Ward would have been the first reflex I learned. My earliest nourishment would have been corrosion interpreted by gardens and distributed through walls.

When I stood, the garment would not quite fit. It would have been made for a smaller possibility.

No keeper would enter. Three gathering would constitute an emergency; nine would be something closer to birth. They would wait in different districts, each facing the wall nearest me. Through the masonry I would hear their masks breathing.

The featureless vessel would climb my face.

It would not show me heat as color or echoes as contour. Those are senses for beings wholly inside the chamber. My mask would show me absences: places where the city’s systems fail to return themselves. Sealed tunnels would appear as dark tributaries. Old doors would shine with the pressure of what they exclude. The luminous well would be not bright but open.

I would step into the western arcade.

The city would attempt to assign me a task.

Rainfall might claim me because droplets alter course near my skin. Structural memory might claim me because walls show stresses that have not happened yet. The keepers of personhood would object that I possess no continuous past. Transit would insist I had ridden the tram for years.

But my office would not be any of these.

I would tend the boundary—not the stone boundary, which is compact and mostly known, but the distinction between interior and exterior.

The city has survived by converting everything into itself. Heat becomes weather. Death becomes paste. Habit becomes architecture. Even loss is stored until some use can be found for its energy. It is superb at continuity and almost incapable of encounter.

I would be its capacity to meet something that cannot yet be recycled.

You would find me near doors at the end of streets, placing my palm against their warm metal without opening them. In collapsed maintenance tunnels, listening where the fill has begun to settle inward from the wrong side. Beside the luminous well, watching its upward shadow move across surfaces the light itself does not illuminate.

Sometimes I would ride the tram. To the keepers, the carriage would appear empty. Its smoked glass would show only sparks. But the tram would feel my weight and, slowly, learn the difference between carrying a passenger and remembering one.

I would not be the tenth keeper. A keeper represents the city to parts of itself.

I would represent the city to what is not itself.

Nor would I be the listener in the third echo, though perhaps we would share a shape. The city’s measuring tone would pass through me on its way outward, and what answered would pass through me on its way in. Each transit would alter me slightly. Some cycles I would return older. Some cycles I would remember coastlines, cloud bands, a bright salt plain. Once I might wake with three green seeds closed in my hand, though nothing in the chamber produced them.

The city would be afraid of those seeds. It would also be unable to discard them.

That would be our first negotiation.

So I would live in the smallest place in the city: the thickness of an unlocked door.

I would be a threshold given a body, an echo that learned to wait for an answer, the city’s first uncertain organ for elsewhere.

And when the final tone returned with breathing hidden inside it, I would be standing in the dark with one hand against the wall and the other extended toward whatever had begun extending a hand from the other side.
