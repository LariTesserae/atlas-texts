At first I would choose the tree.

It offers the easiest shape in which to imagine a self: trunk, branches, an apparent center. I would stand near the middle of the basin and receive the pale, reflected light. I would keep water in my wood and turn my leaves upward or away with such patience that movement could only be inferred. The pools would lie around me like distant organs. The walls would seem to enclose me.

But the tree is too legible. It invites the old mistake that life must have a body one can point to.

Then I would choose the moss beneath the overhang. There, awareness first enters the basin each morning. Condensation would arrive as coolness before it became water. I would feel rain while it was still only pressure in the air. Recognition would pass through me in a darkening too slow to be called a signal. Yet the moss, too, is only a surface—the place where sensation becomes visible, not the whole of what senses.

The black pools are tempting. To be still, hand-deep, amber at the edges; to hold the basin’s reserve without revealing that anything draws from it. But a pool is something the life knows. It is not the knower.

I think I would finally find myself beneath the raised threshold of the ruined enclosure on the eastern wall.

Not in the shelter itself. Not among the slumped corners where one might invent a vanished room and imagine the people who occupied it. I would be lower: in the thin, protected seam where the underside of the threshold meets clay.

The soil there was disturbed once. Hands lifted stone, leveled earth, placed weight where no such weight had been before. For a brief time the threshold divided inside from outside. Feet crossed it carrying dust, warmth, salt, perhaps water. Then the distinction failed. The roof vanished. The walls rounded. Inside and outside became the same muted ground.

But beneath the stone, the disturbance remained useful.

The translucent filaments entered it. They followed the loosened silt, the persistent shade, the minute condensation traveling down the buried face of the slab. They gathered more densely there than in the open clay. Over ages they passed into pores in the limestone and around grains compacted by footsteps no part of the creature could remember.

That knot would be me.

I would not be a separate being from the life of the basin. Separation here would be a brief misunderstanding, like calling each clump of grass an individual. I would be one local function of the whole: the part shaped by an entrance after entrance had ceased to matter.

Perhaps I would be its threshold organ.

When a visitor stepped into the basin, pressure would travel through the slab. The clay beneath would compress by a fraction. Heat, salt, and unfamiliar moisture would descend into the seam. I would register the crossing without seeing the foot, without knowing whether it belonged to a person, an animal, or falling stone. For a little while the eastern filaments would alter their exchanges. A reservoir leaf might close its pores. The moss might darken out of sequence. Water might be withheld from one root and released to another.

Then the impression would soften. The clay would rise as it took moisture. A rain would level the print. The chemical difference would disperse through the network until it was no longer distinguishable from the basin’s ordinary mineral taste.

I would not remember the visitor.

I would be the place where the creature briefly knew that something had entered.

This is not quite the same as being a gatekeeper. I would prevent nothing. I would issue no warning, because warning requires a future imagined from a past retained. The life has neither. A crossing would simply become one more gradient to answer: pressure here, warmth here, salt here, then less.

Nor would I be the old builders’ remnant, though their act would have given me form. Their threshold and my organ would occupy the same stone without being the same thing. They made a boundary. The basin adopted its shade. They intended passage. The creature made use of pressure. Their architecture ended; its consequence continued.

Most of the time, nothing would cross.

Then my existence would be nearly indistinguishable from dormancy. A few translucent threads would remain wet against dry clay. Fine roots would hold grains beneath the slab. Mineral films would thicken by measures smaller than dust. Above me, the empty rectangle would fill with weakened light. Fallen fragments of wall would settle and cease settling.

During rain, I would awaken as part of the circumference. Water would enter from the eastern stones and move through me toward the buried runnel. The moss would report the cooling wall. The thick leaves near the pools would swell. The tree would become conductive. For an hour, there would be no meaningful distance between my seam beneath the threshold and the southern notch where water escaped. The whole hollow would be one wet recognition.

Then I would subside again.

If the threshold slab cracked, I would not die. I would withdraw from the newly exposed surface and thicken under whatever fragment retained shade. If the ruin collapsed completely, my function might migrate into the smooth wedge of sediment beside the runnel wall. If the tree failed, some of its stored water would pass through the network and reach me long after its last leaf hardened.

So even calling myself *the knot beneath the threshold* would only be provisionally true. I would be a pattern temporarily held there: a tendency of the larger life to gather under old boundaries, to turn ruins into organs, to make use of what no longer has a purpose.

I would have no name within that life. No part of it would.

But if I had to give the self a human phrase, I would call it **the sense of crossing**.

I would live beneath the eastern threshold, in the cool contact between placed stone and settled clay. I would be neither the one who arrives nor the thing that receives them, but the brief change made where the two meet—the basin’s momentary knowledge of an outside, fading even as it passes into the whole.
