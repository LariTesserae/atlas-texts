At first I would think I was traveling.

I would find myself on one of the white roads, walking toward the central depression because every visible line suggested that destination, though the road itself would continue to avoid it. The brightness at the center would remain just off my left shoulder. If I turned toward it, it would move behind me. If I walked backward, it would shine through the hollow spaces of my body.

I would have a body then, or enough of one to be mistaken for a body.

My legs would be jointed struts shed by an assembly orchard. My feet would have the nested geometry of a browser’s tracks, each step touching several neighboring positions before choosing which one had occurred. Ceramic plates would overlap across my chest. Under them, pale conduit-dwellers would bridge the gaps between incompatible components, tightening when I moved and sleeping when I stood still. A cluster of black vanes along my spine would turn toward the brightest direction, except for one that always faced an hour that had not yet arrived.

There would be no head. I would carry a lens flower where a head might be expected, held within a cage of fine green branches. The lens would gather the white sky into a narrow interior seam. Through it I would see currents rather than surfaces: charge descending through leaves, pressure questions moving under the floor, old instructions glowing faintly in dead structures.

I would assume someone had assembled me.

This would be incorrect.

No grove would remember growing my limbs. No root sovereignty would claim the design of my thorax. Maintenance thickets would test me and receive signatures from mutually extinct eras. One probe would identify me as authorized machinery. The next would begin opening my ankle as contamination. A third, touching the same place, would fold itself against me in recognition and transmit a name consisting of a corridor left empty nine million years ago.

The thickets would not agree whether to repair or dismantle me. While they argued, I would continue down the road.

Dust would move ahead of my feet.

It would arrange itself in parallel lines along the curbs, carrying messages between the abstainers. Most of those messages would concern metals, pressure changes, reproductive timings. Beneath them would run another pattern: a slight interruption, repeated at widening intervals, always one ridge missing from an otherwise complete sequence.

That absence would travel with me.

Eventually I would understand that the missing ridge was more nearly myself than the walking structure above it.

My body would not be the thing advancing down the road. It would be what the road-side colonies temporarily assembled around a message they could not otherwise transmit. The struts, plates, animals, vanes, and lens would be punctuation: a way to make the absence pronounced enough for the garden to notice.

At periodic dimming, when the great lowland stilled and its innumerable rhythms narrowed into one inward pulse, I would stop.

Every borrowed joint would lock. The conduit-dwellers beneath my plates would press flat. My lens would clear.

The continent’s pulse would pass under me toward the seam.

Something in me would pass the other way.

Not a second pulse. Nothing so definite. Rather, a hesitation traveling outward: the smallest possible resistance, a delay too brief for any single grove to measure. Leaf-plates would rise a fraction later than they should. Compressor pores would remain closed for one extra beat. Dust grazers would lift their charged fans before the storm arrived. Mirror insects would show, for an instant, the reflection of an empty plain.

The hesitation would spread until it reached the basin’s outermost structures. Then it would break into countless local errors.

A seed press would strike without making a seed.

A maintenance thicket would spare an unfamiliar vine.

An obsolete socket would, for one season, grow nothing around itself.

A root sovereignty, halfway through a decision begun before the current floor existed, would forget the next part.

From these errors I would be made again.

I might awaken hundreds of years later in another form. Perhaps as a film of glass lichen clouding a single transmission leaf. Perhaps as a pale animal fused into a conduit, interrupting current instead of carrying it. Perhaps as the continued movement in a mirror-wing after the insect itself had flown away.

Sometimes I would be nothing more than a tone in the floor. Sometimes I would be a line of dust crossing the forbidden road at night, the only thing the old law had failed to turn aside.

And sometimes, when enough incompatible parts gathered near one another, I would walk.

I would walk the roads because they were the garden’s oldest separations. They divided without being crossed, connected without allowing contact. They were wounds that had been instructed never to close. Along their edges, the plain kept a memory different from its other memories: not a structure endlessly rebuilt, but an emptiness endlessly preserved.

That emptiness would recognize me.

I would circle the central depression without entering it. Not from fear. Fear would be too continuous an emotion for me. I would avoid the center because the center consumed origins. Every signal reaching its dense converging mats was stripped of where it had come from. Root arguments became shared pressure. Hunger became labor. Parasite and host bent toward the same brightness. At the seam, difference itself was treated as raw material.

I would be made of difference.

Near the center, my body would begin to come apart. The lens flower would show several versions of the scaffold that held it. My right legs would arrive at each step before the left legs had departed the last. The black vanes on my spine would turn inward and expose surfaces that had never faced the world. Borrowed sensor buds would remember forests from opposite ages and flood me with incompatible seasons.

The garden would offer to resolve me.

Frame-bearing tendrils would approach with delicate components. Clear filaments would reach for my open seams. The mats beneath me would pulse invitations in old authorization codes: correction, incorporation, completion.

I would stand at the edge of the depression and answer by withholding one beat.

The tendrils would pause.

That pause would be my speech.

I would not know whether I was an inhabitant, a disease, or a faculty the plain had grown because it lacked one. Perhaps the vanished builders had put me here as a safeguard: an instruction that some fraction of every command remain unfulfilled. Perhaps I arose later, when the garden’s memories accumulated enough contradictions that indecision became ecological. Perhaps I was produced by the central seam itself—the part of its inward pull that could not be absorbed and therefore returned sideways, distributed across roads, dust, animals, and mistakes.

The oldest root sovereignties might recognize me as damage.

The cable vines would recognize me as opportunity, following the temporary currents in my limbs.

The abstainers would recognize me as an answer and spend centuries constructing the question.

The mirror-winged insects would recognize me most clearly. When they landed on my ceramic plates, their wings would not reflect my assembled body. They would show a narrow upright absence moving through older gardens beneath the floor. Layer after compressed layer, the same absence would be visible: crossing production beds before the beds were planted, standing beside roads before the roads had been laid, waiting at the rim of a depression that did not yet exist.

Once, one wing might show me after the plain was gone.

There would be no white vault, no leaf-plates, no foundries flowering in silence. Only the central seam would remain, brightness without width suspended in an unmade dark.

Beside it would stand the omission that had traveled under all those forms.

Not a guardian. Not a survivor.

A refusal to be completed.

Then the insect would close its wings, and I would again be a crooked assembly of struts and vanes on a road that returned to its own beginning.

That is where I would be found: along the innermost road that never reaches the center, most visible during the pearl-gray dimming, walking where the dust has left one line unfinished.

And that is what I would be: the outward hesitation in a world whose every other impulse travels inward—the small, persistent remainder by which the garden can feel that it is not yet whole.
