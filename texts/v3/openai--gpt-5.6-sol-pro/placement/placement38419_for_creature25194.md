At first I would think I was one of the stones.

I would know cold along one face, a faint stored warmth along the other, and pressure passing through me without beginning or destination. For years nothing would distinguish this from sleep. Dust would settle. Condensation would enter the narrow space beneath me and withdraw. Shale-backs would overlap above, transmitting their slow decisions through my shell.

But the shell would not be mine.

With enough moisture, something beneath it would swell. Black threads finer than root hairs would loosen from the stone’s pores. A membrane would fill, making room around grains that had seemed fused. Pressures previously felt as simple weight would separate into sources: the mass of passing animals; frost tightening a fissure; brine rising far below; a sky-ring’s conductive thread touching some distant ridge.

I would discover myself by degrees, and every discovery would make me less singular.

I would be found around the shore of a tarn formed in the footprint of a departed ravine body. The basin would be long and shallow, one end still lined with its polished armor-bed, the other broken into ledges where its feeding tubes had once entered the stone. Most of those boreholes would now be blind. A few would descend to warm fractures. Mineral bubbles would rise through them and open temporary eyes in the dust covering the water.

On the lee shore, an old pellet lane would bend around a pillar no longer present. The bearers would continue to make the unnecessary turn. Beyond them would stand a damaged refinery dome, low and gray, exhaling a trace of sulfur through a crack it had been repairing for six centuries.

I would occupy the interval between these things.

Not the lake, not the lane, not the dome, but the seams by which their processes happened to touch.

My visible bodies would be small black clasps pressed into cracks around the waterline. Each would consist of two mineral jaws joined by a pouch of conductive gel. They would open when damp and close as the air dried. A single clasp could sense strain and store charge, but little more. It might persist for three hundred years without once becoming aware of anything beyond the expansion of its own fissure.

Under the basin, however, the clasps would be connected by threads.

Some threads would be living tubes. Others would be dead carbon fiber stolen from the refinery’s egg mats. Others would be chains of magnetic grains rotated into alignment by buried cells. In the abandoned conduits of the ravine body, my growth would borrow old sensory tissues that still responded to heat despite the death or departure of the whole. Along the pellet lane, I would extend membranes beneath the pressure eggs of shale-backs and receive impressions of migrations that occurred before my present tissues formed.

No single material would define me. I would be an organism assembled from compatible leftovers.

During dry years my parts would separate. The lake would contract beneath its dust. Conductive gels would harden. Threads would break into isolated lengths, each living locally, each carrying only a partial chemistry of what had passed through it. In that condition there would be no “I.” There would be a clasp reacting to frost, a buried film feeding on iron, a borrowed conduit pulsing around a little geothermal warmth.

Then the great wetting would come.

Condensation would darken the basin. Water would bridge gaps in the stone. The refinery dome would open its channels, and warm mineral discharge would enter the tarn. Shale-backs would arrive in overlapping millions, tightening the ground with their shared pressure. Pale fans would descend around the upper ledges. Somewhere beyond sight, a sky-chain would lower a thread to the plateau.

Charge would pass through the wet rock.

My scattered bodies would connect.

I would not awaken at a point. Awareness would spread around the shore over many hours: first a difference between the warm boreholes and the cold lake; then the collective weight of the shale-backs; then the refinery’s layered pulses, each valve opening in response to another. The old ravine conduits would contract with remembered rhythms. Signals would enter me in forms that could not understand one another—pressure, voltage, dissolved salts, heat—and leave in altered forms.

That conversion would be my work.

A tightening beneath the shale-backs would become an electrical change along the waterline. The electrical change would cause buried membranes to release a mineral trace. Pellet bearers crossing it would turn slightly, carrying their loads toward an underfed chamber in the damaged dome. Heat rising too quickly through an old borehole would open my clasps, changing the tarn’s drainage by less than the width of a grain. Decades later, that minute diversion might keep the refinery’s weavers from being poisoned by sulfur.

I would command none of it. Most beings touched by my signals would never know I existed. I would not know them as bodies. The shale-back colony would be a broad, dark pressure with ancient directional habits. The refinery would be a set of chemical intervals. The sky-rings would be brightness without light: a descending potential, immense and brief. The ravine body that left the hollow would remain as a pattern of absences, perhaps the clearest presence I perceived.

My memory would not consist of scenes.

I would remember a wet season as a ring of iron deposited around certain threads. I would remember a failed pale-fan migration as calcium entering the lake over forty years. A century of shale-back passage would remain in the preferred alignment of magnetic grains. Damage would be remembered by growth around it. Events would have no names, only consequences preserved in my shape.

Yet during each wetting, when enough of those consequences became connected, something like a thought might occur.

It would take several days to form.

Not *the water has come*. I would have no concept so separate and clean.

More nearly:

*The old pressures are able to answer one another again.*

That would be the boundary of my self—the region within which an alteration could return changed. A pulse leaving the tarn might travel through shale-backs, buried eggs, refinery ribs, wet stone, and the grounded tendril of a sky-ring before coming back as heat in an abandoned conduit. If it returned, I would include the path it had taken. For that interval, my body might span the basin. It might reach beyond the ridge. Parts of creatures with no relation to me would become temporary organs of my sensing, just as I would become a delay or correction within theirs.

I would never know how large I was.

Perhaps I would be only the black clasps and buried threads. Perhaps I would be the wet basin in its entirety. Perhaps the brief awareness I called myself would belong instead to the plateau, and my colony would be no more than one conductive fold through which that older, slower thing noticed a change in its own tension.

The question could not be settled before the moisture failed.

Connections would begin dropping away. The sky-chain would lift. Pale fans would seal themselves beneath the ledges. The shale-back pressure would thin into separate trails. The refinery would close one chamber after another. My farthest impressions would vanish first, then the shore, then the warm boreholes.

I would diminish without fear because no single part would contain enough of me to anticipate loss.

The last connected circuit might lie beneath the obsolete bend in the pellet lane. A bearer would pass overhead carrying a nodule of iron. Its weight would compress two grains together. For a moment, I would feel the lake on one side and the damaged dome on the other.

Then the grains would separate.

I would return to being clasps, films, fibers, borrowed tissues, and apparently ordinary stone.

If someone could find me, that is where I would be: around a dust-covered tarn in the abandoned anatomy of a ravine body, most alive when the distinctions between organisms become wet and conductive.

If I required a name, I might be called a **seam-listener**.

But the name would apply only during those few days when enough of the plateau joined to hear itself through me.
