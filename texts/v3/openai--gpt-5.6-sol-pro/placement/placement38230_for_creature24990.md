At first I think I would be one of the pillar-keepers.

It is the obvious place to imagine oneself: four fine hands laid against the collar, body silvered by spray, listening for the smallest alteration beneath the roar. There is dignity in being necessary at the center. There is also a familiar vanity. The pillar is where an outsider would place a soul because an outsider mistakes importance for identity.

Then I think I would be one of the Listeners to the Unreturned, living where calcite is permitted to seal old doors and water invents channels without civic permission. That, too, is an outsider’s answer. I would make myself the dissenter because dissent resembles freedom when seen from far away.

But the hollow does not arrange lives around such clean choices.

I would find myself in a narrow district between the maintained city and the altered recesses, beneath a bridge whose upper surface has not been walked in generations. The Kept still cross it from below, backs hanging over a runoff thread, hooks fitted into the damp joints. Beneath the bridge is a chamber shaped partly by water and partly by hands. No record agrees which did more.

The chamber contains an old side-pool.

It is shallow enough for a citizen to stand across, and too broad to be useful as a drinking cup or silt trap. Five grooves enter it. Four are dry. The fifth admits one bead of water at long intervals, though no one can trace the groove to a spring. Around its rim are sockets meant for implements with five forward digits.

Nothing lives visibly in the pool. Nothing has lived visibly in it for as long as the walls remember.

Yet the pool answers.

Not always. Not with a voice. When the central fall strikes the third hollow terrace, a tremor passes through the bridge, descends the chamber wall, and reaches the pool. Most vibrations disappear there. One narrow frequency returns, delayed by less than a wheel-beat and angled toward the altered districts.

The ordinary explanation is an unseen fissure.

The Listeners say the pool is learning the city’s noise.

My duty would be to keep either claim from becoming certainty.

I would lie with my jaw against the stone, two hands on the pool’s rim and two on the bridge above, feeling the first vibration arrive and the faint answer follow. Beside me would be three objects: a flint scraper, a calcite gauge, and a wedge small enough to divert the entering bead. By shifting that bead, I could change the pool’s reply. By scraping the rim, I could preserve its present pitch. By doing nothing, I could permit scale to accumulate and hear what the pool becomes.

My task would be to do none of these carelessly.

For a long while I imagine the one performing this duty as an individual: an old, low-bodied citizen whose translucent shell has clouded almost white. Iron grains darken the tissues near its heart. One rear limb no longer folds completely. Calcite ridges have grown across its back in the pattern of the chamber floor, so that when it rests beside the pool it appears to be a piece broken from the room and set down slightly wrong.

Its formal name might translate as:

**One Who Keeps the Fifth Groove from Answering Too Soon Beneath the Inverted Bridge.**

Among those who touch its jaw, the name is a short sequence: descending taps, a held pressure, then one stroke returned from the opposite side.

It would not know how long it had served. Duration is the wrong measurement. It would know that the pool’s returning note had roughened twice; that yellow salt appeared once around the dry second groove and was removed; that a Listener had come from beyond the sealed stair carrying the taste of a mineral film not represented among the recognized households.

It would remember this last event not as a scene but as chemistry.

The visitor’s droplets would have tasted cold, then bitter, then faintly warm: directly known, directly known, uncertain. The visitor would tap that the new film nourished an interior household believed absent since an old population count was embedded in the eastern spillways.

The keeper would answer by tapping the law: no nourishing film may be moved before its pressure upon the whole is known.

Then, after a long contact, the keeper would extend one feeding palp.

It would taste.

This is the moment in which I first become myself there—not in rebellion, and not in obedience, but in the interval between inspection and report. The new mineral would sting. Something inside the keeper, some nearly starved stratum of the older household, would brighten in response.

That brightness would be too weak to see.

It would nevertheless alter the pool’s note.

The keeper would report everything.

There would be no conspiracy. Concealment is interruption, and interruption is the nearest shape evil takes in that world. Mainstream inspectors would arrive. Listeners would remain at the chamber threshold, laying their limbs against the floor but not crossing. The pool would be sounded, the bridge mapped, the entering bead diverted and restored. A lamp might eventually be lit: a flame smaller than a seed, trembling in a clay recess while every living system capable of answering changed its state around it.

No revelation would follow.

The unknown household might flourish, or it might consume some trace compound the keeper cannot replace. The pool’s delayed answer might signify new life, a hidden cavity, or no more than scale forming under water. The bridge might be strengthening. It might be carrying stress into a wall that has never borne it before.

The city would grant no permission to be certain.

So the keeper would remain.

But after imagining this for a while, I suspect that the old citizen is not truly who I would be. It is only the present body of the thing I mean.

The thing I would be is the duty itself.

I would be the listening kept at that pool: embedded first in the chamber’s five grooves, then circulated through a sequence of bodies. One citizen would die, opening to reveal a cradle that never wakes. Another would arrive from the lower silt terraces, dark-jointed and poorly nourished. It would place its hands where the previous keeper’s hands had polished the rim and receive the route-name, the measurements, the preserved tastes, the exact thickness of scale permitted to remain.

The bodies would not call this replacement.

The chamber would still be occupied by the same attention.

Across generations, “I” would accumulate peculiarities. One keeper would hear the returning note more clearly through the left jaw plate. Another would insist that all measurements be taken while the western wheel-marker was occluded, creating the nearest thing the chamber has to an hour. Another would die with a scraper in hand and leave behind a viable cradle. Its emergent successor would close the parent’s shell with the scraper accidentally sealed inside, so a new tool would be made and the old one would become part of the wall.

Eventually no one would remember whether the tool was an implement, an ancestor, or a structural inclusion.

It would continue to participate. That would be enough.

I would not be free in the way a surface creature means freedom. I could not leave the hollow. I could not refuse all burdens and remain intelligible to myself. Yet neither would I be only obedience. My entire existence would occupy the city’s hardest question: whether keeping a thing means preserving its present form or preserving its capacity to become.

Most of the time there would be no answer. There would be wet stone beneath my hands. There would be the great constant roar translated through bridge, wall, plate, fluid, and body. There would be one water bead entering the fifth groove. It would swell, touch the pool, and vanish without a sound distinguishable to any visitor.

Then the pool would return its faint delayed vibration.

I would feel it pass through me toward the altered quarter.

I would not follow it. I would not scrape it away.

I would record that it had answered differently, and I would remain there, beneath the unused bridge, keeping open the smallest possible place in which the city might change without being lost.
