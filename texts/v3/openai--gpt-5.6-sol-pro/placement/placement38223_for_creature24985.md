At first I look for myself among the people.

I expect to be one of those who walks before dawn with a notched twig tucked through the belt, lifting reed mats from channels and listening to water move in the dark. I can almost feel the cold mud between my fingers, the weight of a waxed cape, the irritation of being mocked for hearing danger in every altered gurgle.

Then I try to place my house.

It is not beside the market. Too many smells there arrive ruined by smoke, handling, and argument. It is not on the rim, where the basin can briefly be seen whole. Whoever I am has little use for a whole view. I know the town by passages: the underside of a stair, the hollow behind a retaining wall, the roof route above the potters, the place where runoff carries fish scales after market closes.

I descend.

Past the living hall, one principal stair divides around an enormous tree whose roots have captured an older flight of stone steps. One way leads toward the market rise. The other drops through berry shrubs and beneath two houses, then crosses a narrow bridge known by different names to the households around it.

The people uphill call it Five Roots, for the roots braided around its eastern pier. Those below call it the Cold Bridge because a spring emerges beneath it. Children call it Crow-Drop, commemorating an incident involving a stolen egg, a furious bird, and an aunt who fell into the sedges. On old work cords it has no name at all, only a sequence of knots indicating six households, a goat platform, two drains, and the obligation to provide twelve sound poles after storm damage.

That is where I would be found.

Not on the bridge, usually, but underneath it.

There is a dry pocket between the root mass and the first crossbeam, smoke-dark and smelling of fungus, old rope, bats, and mineral water. From there I can hear three channels separately: the thin spring striking stone, the household runoff whispering through sedge roots, and the deeper culvert muttering below both. When one becomes blocked, the chord changes.

At first I think this means I am a water-watcher.

But I do not carry a twig. I do not open sluices. I do not argue at meetings, though I attend many. I lie with my chin on my forefeet while people dispute shade rights above me. I know who is approaching by the rhythm transmitted through the bridge before their scent reaches the hollow.

I am a dog.

An old one, though not yet failing. Long-bodied, narrow-chested, with a dark brindled coat that appears black when wet and the reddish color of peeled bark when the sun finds it. One ear stands. The other folds halfway over from an injury no two people remember alike. My paws are broad, my nails worn blunt by stone, and the fur between my toes is kept trimmed because burrs, leeches, and wet grit gather there.

I have several names.

The children call me Under.

The bridge households call me Reed-Ear when they are pleased and Drain-Sleeper when they are not. The oldest water-watcher calls me Second Knock, because years ago, during a night storm, I barked twice at a retaining wall that looked sound. She trusted me enough to clear the platform above it. Before dawn, the wall folded outward and the garden descended into the channel in one black, fluid piece.

No one owns me cleanly. A smoked fish seller fed me as a pup. I slept my first wet season beneath the basket maker’s stove. A root-tender cut the thorn from my paw that left me limping for a year. Children from all six bridge households have used my ribs as a pillow. My neck cord is replaced by whoever notices it fraying.

The cord carries three beads: fired clay for the bridge, pale bone for the upper spring, and a little green stone whose meaning has been forgotten. Because the meaning is forgotten, no one removes it.

My work is not formal. No one says a dog holds authority. Yet in thick mist, when the bridge is slick and its far end invisible, people wait for me to cross first. I test the boards with my weight near the lashings. I stop where rot smells sweet. I refuse paths when the soil beneath them has begun to move.

This refusal is one of my chief skills.

Humans find it difficult. They can explain why a path should still be safe. They remember carrying poles over it yesterday. They point out that the rain has not been especially heavy. They tell one another that someone inspected it recently. I simply plant my feet.

Sometimes I am wrong because a civet has passed there, or because monkeys are waiting above with fruit to throw. But often enough, beneath the smells of fern and mud, I catch the raw scent of newly torn roots or buried water reaching air. Then they call for poles, prod the slope, and discover that the ground has become hollow.

I am fed well after those days.

Most mornings begin before lamps are extinguished. I follow the first water-watcher uphill, not at heel but several paces ahead. At each covered channel I listen, sniff, and wait. She lifts the mats. Sometimes frogs spring past my nose. Sometimes there is nothing wrong except a duck lodged where no duck should be. Once we find a child’s carved boat jammed sideways in a narrow sluice, holding back enough water to drown three terraces. The child’s identity is obvious from the carving. I am given strict instructions not to betray him.

By sunrise I am at the market, where betrayal becomes negotiable.

I patrol the fish mats, the poultry ledges, and the fruit sellers’ poles. I have an arrangement with the smoked-fish woman: I discourage crows, and she gives me skin and bones. The crows understand the arrangement and treat it as a game. They lure me away in pairs while a third descends behind me. I understand this and permit them occasional victories. A permanent triumph would make them intolerable; permanent failure would make them inventive.

I do not chase monkeys unless the whole market joins in. A single dog chasing a monkey is entertainment for the monkey.

When rain thickens, I return to Five Roots. People crossing above shake droplets through the planks. The cold spring rises around the lower stones. Bats shift deeper into their crevice. I listen as the three waters become five, then seven, as roof runoff and opened sluices join the sound.

If the pitch stays clear, I sleep.

If the culvert begins to pulse—silence, rush, silence, rush—I climb out and bark.

Not the market bark. Not the pig bark. Not the short, contemptuous sound reserved for goats on roofs. This is a low, repeated call with my muzzle close to the bridge timbers, so the whole structure carries it. The clay bells answer when people begin to run.

Then I become part of the work without understanding all of it. Ropes pass overhead. Feet drum on the bridge. Someone ties a message cord loosely around my neck and sends me toward the lower gardens, where I know which stair remains above water. Children are moved uphill. Chickens are thrust into baskets. Ducks rejoice in the emergency and impede everyone.

I travel through the basin by routes no person would call a route: under a tilted wall, across a roof root, through the abandoned pig pen, behind the shrine where the stones are warm, along an old stair buried so deeply in moss that only my paws remember its edges.

This is perhaps what I am most for.

Not guarding a house. Not belonging to a person. Remembering continuity when the visible paths change.

There was once another bridge below Five Roots. I crossed it as a young dog, before a storm turned its west footing and the households took it apart. Ferns now cover the approach. People say there is no reason to go that way.

But sometimes in fog I do.

I follow the old route beneath hanging vines, stepping over the channel where the third board used to be. At the vanished bridgehead stands a boundary tree bent by long-removed lashings. Its bark still holds the smell of smoke in dry weather. Beyond it is a terrace no one gardens now, full of pale mushrooms and the descendants of escaped gourds.

Children follow me there and declare that I have discovered it.

Elders follow more slowly and say I have remembered it.

Both are true enough.

When I die, they will not bury me in a courtyard belonging to one household. The argument over me will be affectionate but substantial. The fish seller’s family will claim feeding rights. The basket maker’s descendants will cite my first wet season. The water-watchers will say my place is near the culvert. Children will insist I belong beneath the bridge.

In the end, I think they will choose the unused terrace on the old path. They will wrap me in a split sleeping mat and place me beneath the bent boundary tree. My clay and bone beads will be taken from the neck cord. The green stone may be tied to a new pup, though no one knows what it means.

For a few seasons, people crossing Five Roots will still pause when the culvert pulses, expecting my bark beneath their feet.

Then someone will begin imitating it as a joke. The imitation will enter a work song. The song will become part of the bridge record: a low repeated note marking the place where hidden water must be listened for after rain.

So I would remain there—not as a spirit apart from the settlement, and not merely as memory.

I would become a warning in the song, an old route under moss, a bead on another animal’s collar, and the habit of waiting when the dog refuses to cross.

I would be found beneath Five Roots, where the spring enters the culvert and the bridge carries every footstep into the dark.

I would be the town’s trust in a creature that cannot explain what it notices.
