At first I think I would live near a warm root.

Not directly against it. I would lack the confidence of those pink-skinned households that sleep in metabolizing heat and know the moods of fermentation by smell. I imagine myself in a narrow dwelling just beyond the civic chambers, where warmth arrives weakened through two layers of pith. I would keep dry cords coiled in shell cases and know exactly which neighbor’s wall could be opened if mine began to close.

But that seems too comfortable.

Then I find myself lower, at the Still Court, crouched on one of the thin platforms with cold water beneath my feet. Perhaps I am a water-keeper, listening to tension in the veins, trusted to call the first notch and feared for the possibility of calling it late. Yet I do not think I would trust my hearing enough. I would listen too long, uncertain whether the pressure was rising water or a beetle forcing itself through buried pulp. In this city, hesitation can drown a district.

No. My place would be along the routes that are not yet routes.

I would be one of the people sent ahead of builders, after the water withdraws and before anyone begins cutting. Not quite a Turner, though I would have learned their methods. Not quite a wall-tender or track-reader either. My work would fall between offices, accumulating because the city repeatedly needs something it has no permanent name for.

I would test possible passages.

You would find me where a familiar street ends in a fresh ivory ridge, or where a flood has softened a wall enough that a chamber may be persuaded from it. I would carry a shell scraper, three resin pads, a wedge of frozen wood and a survey cord darkened by many wettings. Around one wrist I would keep loose beads made from different materials—shell, gall, beetle case, old root—so that I could mark a route by touch without making every intersection feel the same.

Before cutting, I would lie down.

One cheek against the tissue, one palm spread ahead of me, I would wait until the fine hairs of my face told me where air was moving. I would press warmth into the wall, then withdraw it. I would place a bead of diluted sap on the pale surface and return later to see whether it had been accepted, enclosed or pushed away. Sometimes I would set my teeth against the handle of my awl and listen.

Most days the plant would say nothing that could be called an answer.

That would be the difficult part: knowing that silence is not consent.

I think I would become known for routes I refused to open. Younger builders would say I could turn a short stair into six days of listening. Households waiting for access to high ground would resent me. More than once, someone would cut around my markers while I slept, and sometimes their passage would remain open, proving that my caution had not been necessary.

Sometimes it would seal with them inside.

After that, people would touch my wrist differently.

My dwelling would not be a true house. It would be a set of sleeping rights scattered through the city: a felt-lined recess above the white-root road, half a shelf near the cold margins, a sling hook in a cousin’s fermentation chamber. My tools and food would be divided among them. I would never want all of myself stored in one district.

Others might call this prudence. In truth, it would come from having once returned after a rise to find my first home healed shut so completely that the wall-tenders could not locate the door. Nothing dramatic would remain—no broken frame, no trapped vessel, no visible scar. Only a warm, smooth curve where a room had been.

For a long time I would continue touching that place whenever I passed.

My act-name might come from it: **Palm-on-the-Closed-Room**.

That would be the name used by people who thought me too attached to vanished things. In another district I might be called **Three-Beads-Ahead**, because of the markers I leave when testing new ground. Children would shorten this to Three Beads and steal imitations from scraps of shell. Among my household I would retain a touch-name: two strokes across the palm, a pause, then pressure at the base of the thumb. It would be a name that could not be spoken loudly because it could not be spoken at all.

I would be useful during floods, though not among the first to climb. My task would be to walk the less certain refuge paths before the second notch. I would check whether old high routes still took weight, whether a new swelling had pinched the passage, whether a bridge had begun to stiffen dangerously. I would cut anything likely to snap. I would leave tactile warnings where a ceiling had lowered.

At the third notch, I would ascend last with the wall-tenders, carrying no private possessions except my beads.

In the upper chamber, pressed among strangers and crickets and damp fur, I would recite possible descents against someone else’s palm. Not the city as it had been, but the city as it might be after the water withdrew:

If the lower bridge remains, take the rough scar.

If the scar is soft, follow the cold wall.

If the white root has turned, do not trust the old stair.

If there is warm air where no warm chamber stood, stop.

Someone would answer each instruction with a pressure of the thumb. Perhaps this is how I would make music too—not with melody, but with branching paths. A route given rhythm. A listener would feel choices opening beneath my fingertips, closing, opening elsewhere. The song would always return to high ground, though never by the same way twice.

I would not be brave around the Near Moon.

When the salamander pressed its blind head beneath the leaf, I would become almost useless with fear. Its four-point weather would enter my teeth. I would know too many possible paths and imagine each one ending at the mouth. Someone steadier would have to choose our recess. My contribution would be smaller: noticing the faint current of displaced air before the first vibration reached the floor, drawing a child’s loose seed-scale sleeve tight so it could not click, pressing wet moss over another person’s breath.

Perhaps that is what I would finally be in this world—not an explorer, and not a keeper of certainty.

I would be a keeper of alternatives.

My work would be to prevent the city from believing too completely in its present shape. I would maintain stairs that lead nowhere yet. I would keep cords tied beside sealed walls. I would remember that a drowned channel once opened into a hatchery, that beneath a smooth scar there may still be a hollow, that an abandoned route can become essential when the plant contracts. I would argue against building in the empty avenues, even when space grew scarce.

Do not block a path merely because it leads nowhere today.

That law would feel addressed to me personally.

Eventually age would shorten my reach. My broad palms would stiffen, and the cold would remain in my joints even near the warm roots. I would move on all fours again. Younger route-testers would carry me to new tissue because I could still distinguish, by touch, growth that intended to split from growth that intended to seal.

One day, perhaps, I would be wrong.

Or the city would change faster than knowledge could follow. A route I declared safe would close during a rise. My last dwelling would flood. My body would be washed in vein-water, my beads separated and given to apprentices. The survey cord would be judged sound enough to reuse. They would place me in an old pith chamber with fungus and mites and torn leaf.

I would be made available.

Later, a traveled street might thicken above that chamber. No one could say that any part of me had become the new ridge, or the faint warmth under it, or the pale vine hooking itself along the wall. Such claims would be sentimental and structurally unhelpful.

But when someone lay down there to test the tissue, perhaps the wall would yield slightly beneath their palm.

Perhaps they would tie a cord.

And in the dark, without knowing why, leave room for a way through.
