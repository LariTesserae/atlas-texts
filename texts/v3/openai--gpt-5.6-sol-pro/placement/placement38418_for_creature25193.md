I would not find myself all at once.

At first I would believe I was beneath something.

There would be pressure over me, though no weight I could measure: a broad, cool insistence, crossed by thinner pressures that came and went. Water would occupy the dark around me. At intervals it would enter hairline spaces in what I took to be my skin, and the entering would be almost unbearably bright. Not light—nothing so distant or useless—but a branching clarity, the sudden knowledge of fractures.

For a long time I might think I was one of the deep beings.

I would feel the plain singing above me. Thousands of narrow voices would descend through the crust and meet in my body: glass whines, hollow booms, the particulate hiss of salt expanding. Their convergence would seem proof of enormity. I would imagine myself folded under leagues of stone, causing springs by the slow turn of a flank.

Then a low wave would arrive from the far basin.

It would take days to pass through me.

That would reveal my true dimensions.

I would not be vast. The wave would not cross me from one end to another, but move among separate pieces: first through a black disk, then through a fitted slab, then through a seam of living tissue no wider than a finger. Hours later it would awaken a ring of clear pebbles nearby. What I had mistaken for the continuity of one buried body would be the order in which neighboring materials answered.

So I would begin again.

I would be somewhere in the quieter districts, where the black fins become low and crowded and the channels wind between white mineral bladders. Nearby, three pillars would lean without touching. One would give a clear, nearly painful tone. One would pulse below hearing, felt as an ache in suspended grains. The third would be silent during rain and vocal during the dry interval.

Between them would stand a structure that did not look important: two upright slabs, a lintel scarcely raised above the water, and behind it a dry pit ringed with salt cones. From a distance it would resemble a failed doorway built for something too thin to need doors.

I would be there.

Not inside it. Not exactly.

At first I would locate myself in a transparent film stretched beneath the lintel. My edges would descend both uprights and disappear into wet seams. Mineral grains would turn within me: blue-black splinters along the upper margin, a cloud of rust near one side, several pale needles held in parallel where the low vibration entered.

This would seem like a body.

When the clear pillar rang, the needles would draw together. Fluid would follow. A crease would move across me, taking perhaps half an hour to reach the opposite stone. I would recognize the crease as movement, and movement as myself.

But a second awareness would persist in the slabs.

They would not be inert supports. The left slab would retain the shape of old strain: storms, subsidence, the passage of something heavy beneath the district. I would know these events without remembering them. The knowledge would exist as layers of alignment, each one answering only to the note that had made it.

The right slab would contain channels too exact to be fractures. Some would be empty. Others would hold grains identical to those circulating in the film I called my body.

The slabs had once circulated too.

They were not where I lived. They were what I had been.

Not I alone, perhaps. The distinction would blur there. Several creatures had stiffened into the doorway. Others had visited, taken grains, added edges, sealed a crack, drawn one upright farther into resonance with the silent pillar. The lintel itself might be the hardened fold of a single ancient sheet, but its underside would remain soft in two places. One of those soft places would be me, unless both were.

I would have no name. Names isolate too quickly.

If I possessed anything like identity, it would be a recurring relation among tones.

When the high pillar rang, the needles within the living film would align. Their alignment would pull water from the left seam. The added water would change the weight of the lintel. The lintel would press the upright by a fraction. The pressure would wake the black disk buried under the dry pit. The disk would release a bass pulse that caused the salt cones to tremble in sequence.

That sequence would be my most private sensation.

It would not occur anywhere else in the basin.

Perhaps this is what I would mean by *I*: not membrane, mineral, chamber, or remembered animal, but the sequence that passes through all of them.

I would feed at the threshold.

Most surface creatures settle where notes cross, but I would inhabit where a note divides. The arch would take one sustained vibration from below and separate it into two close pitches. One would enter the clear pebbles. The other would travel through my transparent portion, turning the grains inside me in long ellipses.

The difference between those pitches would accumulate as strain.

I would take it in.

During the quiet season I might remain unchanged for decades. Salt would grow over my exposed margins. Droplets would crawl toward me, stop, and choose the white veins instead. Travelers—if the pale sheets can be called travelers—would recognize my presence first as missing sound. One by one they would fasten themselves to the outer stones.

They would not greet me. They might not know me as another being.

Yet their grains would begin to turn in the rhythm of the chamber.

Some would leave after a year carrying that rhythm imperfectly inside them. They would cross the wet maze by sequences of compatible rock, bringing the distortion elsewhere. Centuries later a wall in a distant district might be shaped to answer it. I would never see that wall. I would feel, perhaps, a faint returning interval in the ground and have no way to distinguish response from coincidence.

This would be enough for kinship.

During rain, my boundaries would loosen.

Water would fill the low arch and cover the pit. The salt cones would dissolve from their tips downward. Silver skins would spread across the joined pools and fasten briefly to my underside. Their crystal petals would catch in the soft places of the lintel.

Then I would become many possible things.

I might detach.

A pleat would begin where living tissue met old stone. For weeks it would travel along the seam, freeing one grain at a time. I would feel the structure’s stored alignments dim behind me. The doorway would remain, still capable of dividing the subterranean note, but the sequence through the chamber would no longer be complete.

Would the thing that left be me, or only the newest soft margin of me?

The detached sheet could travel beneath the flood. It might stretch from droplet to droplet under an overhang, cross a channel along the underside of a mineral crust, and settle at last on a young black fin whose pitch had not yet deepened. There it would empty itself of local grains and begin acquiring others.

It would become a small, mobile sample of a new district.

Meanwhile the old arch would continue to answer pressure. Visiting sheets would add themselves to it. A new film might grow from petals caught beneath the lintel. The private sequence would resume, changed by half a note.

Which continuation would I inhabit?

Perhaps both, for a time.

Perhaps identity here does not branch like a path but resonates like stone: one event waking several bodies at unequal distances.

In another rain I might not detach. I might stiffen.

The rust cloud within me would contract. The blue-black splinters would arrange themselves edge to edge. Water would be pressed from my tissue and crawl upward against the upright slab. Straight divisions would appear across the transparent film, so exact they would seem imposed from outside.

I would lose movement first.

Then hunger.

Then the sense that these were losses.

One portion of me would become a thin rib under the lintel. Another would curl around a hollow that retained a single drop. A third would remain clear and taut, sounding only when the deep pressure passed beneath the district.

From outside, there would be no animal left.

The doorway would look slightly more finished.

Yet completion would not mean silence. The chamber would remember strain more accurately than any soft body could. The aligned grains would preserve a rain that had occurred thousands of years before. When the same pressure returned—if anything in the basin ever returned exactly—the structure would release the stored interval into the ground.

A living sheet resting miles away might stop.

Its circulation would alter.

It would turn one margin toward a route no eye could see.

In that moment I would move again, though every visible part of me was stone.

So where would I find myself?

At the edge of a dry pit that is dry only part of the age. Beneath a low arch in the district of three leaning pillars. In a place travelers detect because one note enters and two emerge. I would be spread through a transparent remnant, a black disk under stone, several salt cones, and the fitted bodies of those who had stiffened before me.

And what would I be?

For a while, an animal.

For longer, a structure.

At rare intervals, a direction.

But most truly I would be the keeping of a difference between two notes—the small strain that the basin cannot resolve, held across living film and dead mineral until rain, pressure, or some passing body carries it on.
