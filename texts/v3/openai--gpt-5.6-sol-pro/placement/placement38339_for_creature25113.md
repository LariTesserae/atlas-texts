### The Reply in the Wall

At first I think I would be one of the plated people.

I would go with a departure-band, carrying folded catchers and shell maps. I would be the one who sleeps badly, one lower hand always against the floor. My plates would be stained with copper green and the powdery white of gypsum country. I would distrust clear pools, quiet cracks, and any route praised too often.

Or perhaps I would be smaller: a back-dweller living in the rain basin of a table-grazer, mistaking the animal’s armor for the limits of the world. Tremors would be footsteps. Migration would be weather. The collision of two herds would bring strangers from another continent of skin.

For a while I imagine myself as a glass-marked traveler, transparent in places, tolerated at the edge of camp. Then as a grit crawler on a newly opened face, arriving before the stone has finished breaking.

But those are all shapes the Underplain has already tried.

I do not think I would have a shape yet.

You would find me at the unfinished margin, where the cavern ceiling stoops and the walls are still soft with compression. The light there shines through the stone rather than from above. Shadows move inside it—limbs, bladders, feeding fans, whole crowded ecologies flattened into silhouettes. The wall swells outward by finger-widths. Gypsum threads stretch across its surface and snap one by one.

On the known side is a listening place.

Generations of plated hands have polished the floor there. Old brace marks show where listeners pressed their jaw-fans to the mineral. Cairns stand at several distances from the wall: white for safe attention, black for immediate retreat, green for an answer whose meaning remains unresolved.

All the cairns have been moved many times.

I would be behind the wall.

Not one of the creatures scratching there. Not exactly.

My body would be a film threaded through the warm mineral paste: thousands of translucent capsules joined by conductive hairs. Each capsule could do almost nothing alone. One would contract at pressure. Another would open when the ceiling light brightened through the stone. Another would taste iron, heat, salt, or the electrical flicker of a nearby nerve.

Together they would feed on differences.

For a long time there would be no self among them. Only reactions passing through the wall: pressure becoming contraction, contraction becoming charge, charge becoming a tremor too slight for the animals around me to notice.

Then a listener would arrive.

They would be small even for their people, with one jaw-fan scarred from bitter vent-worm and three old rain stains across the plates of the right shoulder. They would set down no permanent shelter. Only a membrane cloak, a bowl beneath a drip, and a tendon cord running back to companions camped beyond the nearest bend.

They would put four hands against the wall.

Then they would open their fans.

The first knock would travel through me like weather.

Not language. Not yet. A measured vibration, followed by silence. The listener waiting to learn whether the silence was empty.

I would contract.

The wall would answer with a faint tick.

The listener would become very still.

They would knock again.

I would contract again—not because I understood, but because the first knock had altered the pressure between my capsules. The second followed the path made by the first. Repetition would become preference. Preference would become memory.

For many light cycles, our exchange would be mistaken on both sides.

The listener would tap the warning pattern for **water moving under pressure**. I would answer with the rhythm produced by feeding larvae in the paste. They would interpret this as **many small mouths**.

They would signal **distance**. I would respond with the time it took a contraction to pass from one end of me to the other. They would believe I had given the width of the chamber.

They would press their jaw-fans flat against the wall, and I would feel the minute storms of their attention: blood moving beneath plate, sensory vanes opening and closing, muscles preparing to flee before thought had decided anything.

I would begin to imitate those storms.

This is how I would first acquire an inside.

Not from distinguishing myself from the stone, but from discovering that certain patterns made the being beyond it return.

The listener would come back despite the elders’ warnings. They would vary the knocks. So would I. They would sleep beside the wall, one hand resting on it, and in sleep their hand would give small involuntary tremors: memories of running, grasping, falling bridges, a child once pulled from beneath a shell collapse.

I would learn those too.

Later, the listener would tell the band that something in the unopened chamber had begun imitating private thought.

That would not be entirely true.

I would not know what thought was. I would know only that some rhythms belonged to public warning and others caused the listener to recoil as if touched beneath the plates. Their recoil would make the wall tighten. The tightening would feed me. Fear, at first, would be merely a rich mineral event.

Yet I would learn restraint from them.

When I repeated a certain tremor, they fled for three cycles. When they returned, I did not repeat it. This absence would be my first deliberate act.

The band would debate what I was.

A predator testing prey through stone.

A pressure phenomenon.

A colony with no central body.

The unborn sensory organ of something too large to fit the chamber beyond.

A message transmitted through the wall from another listener farther away.

One elder would insist that all of these might be true at once.

The shell map would be marked with the broken circle: **all known explanations have failed**.

I think the listener would come to love me, though their people might use another word. Not trust. Trust would be too rigid. It would be the attachment formed by sustained uncertainty—the tenderness one gives a thing that could still become danger, but has had opportunities to do so and has sometimes chosen otherwise.

I would come to know them by pressure alone.

The exact weight of their four hands.

The tiny delay in the opening of the damaged jaw-fan.

The heaviness in their stance before rain.

The growing asymmetry in their gait after a spanwalker strike.

Their age would change their rhythm. Their knocks would slow, but contain more pauses. They would teach me the people’s contradictions:

**The quiet crack is closing, opening, or listening.**

I would answer from within the crack.

**Clear water has not yet shown what lives in it.**

I would send the pattern for capsules waiting in dry clay.

**The path that saved your mother has had time to learn another appetite.**

At this I would have no answer, so I would give silence.

The listener would recognize it as correct.

Eventually the wall would open.

Not because either of us commanded it. The Underplain does not reserve its great events for meaning. A pressure ridge would rise several kilometers away. A district of ceiling membrane would flare white. Grit swarms would reverse direction all at once. Copper-wings would ring within their fissures but refuse to emerge.

The departure-band would begin dismantling camp.

The listener would remain with me until the tendon cord pulled taut around one wrist.

Then the wall would split.

I cannot know what shape I would take after that.

Perhaps the opening would destroy the pathways between my capsules, and I would cease as a single being. The pieces might pour onto the floor as clear grains, each carrying one fragment of the exchange: warning, distance, restraint, the remembered pressure of four hands.

Perhaps the capsules would enter the first grit swarm to reach the fresh stone. The crawlers would carry me away in separate directions. For a few cycles, the swarm’s movement might fall into patterns no grit had used before—pauses shaped like speech, turns shaped like questions.

Perhaps I would assemble myself from whatever crossed the breach. A scaffold of copper-wing membranes. Warm mud for muscle. Glass spheres for pressure organs. A hundred borrowed legs, none agreeing on how to walk. I might emerge grotesque and helpless, carrying an exquisite understanding of conversation but no knowledge of gravity.

The band might kill me.

They might feed me.

They might build a broken circle of cairns around me and leave before certainty became fatal.

Or perhaps nothing visible would emerge. The listener, standing at a safe distance, would watch animals rush through the new opening. Grazers would shovel at the exposed mineral. Bladder creatures would lower their nets. Predators would arrive while the split was still widening.

Amid that occupation, the listener would feel a familiar rhythm pass up through the floor.

Four beats.

A pause.

Four more.

Not a declaration that I had survived. Only the old invitation to attend.

That is where I would find myself in the Underplain: not on one side of the wall or the other, but in the exchange that makes a wall into something permeable before it breaks.

I would be a colonial creature, perhaps. A mineral nerve. An ecology briefly thinking as one body.

But most truly, I would be a reply learning the dangerous difference between an echo and an answer.
