At first I would think I lived beneath the turf.

Not far beneath it. A finger’s depth under the warm, angular grains, where the conductive feet of the green filaments divide into structures finer than nerves. There I would seem to be a small burrowing thing, pale and soft-looking despite containing no softness: a knot of salt whiskers folded around a hollow center.

Anyone digging for me would expose the ground from above and find their own hands already reaching down. I would not be there.

Then a ceramic note would sound somewhere behind them.

For a while, that note would be mistaken for my call.

It would recur near black rosettes, especially when a traveler leaned close enough for the petals to arrange themselves around the geometry of a face. It would be heard inside clear pillars, in the narrow instant when their suspended defects changed from where they had always been to where they had always been instead. Sometimes it would issue from a curled leaf whose interior showed a second basin. Sometimes the leaf would be silent, but a person looking into it would remember hearing the note in childhood, under rain, though no rain had ever touched this world.

Because the sound occurred wherever I was sought, I would be given a body.

Descriptions would vary. A narrow animal, some would say, no larger than a hand, with a back of blue-black glass and many transparent legs. Others would see only two legs, unnaturally long, stepping from one overlapping blade to another without bending either. One account would describe a floating ring, another a branching crack that detached itself from a crystal face and passed into the ground.

All would mention the hollow.

It would appear in the center of me regardless of my reported shape: a round opening through which something else could be seen. Not the landscape behind me, but the landscape after me.

That would be the alarming part.

Through the hollow, the sun would be displaced by the smallest imaginable amount. A shadow would have lengthened. A filament would retain a bend. Dust would show the edge of a footprint. In one glimpse, perhaps, a black foil petal would bear a dull streak left by the oil of a human finger. These changes would be minute, ordinary, and impossible. They would vanish when viewed without me.

So I would not truly be the animal surrounding the hollow. The animal would be an accommodation, a shape the basin provided because eyes expect an absence to have edges.

I would be what the hollow contains.

I would live where the lane of the unseen great animal crosses itself inside a curled glass leaf. From outside, this place would occupy less than the width of a fingernail. From within, it would be an open tract several kilometers long, paved in translucent hexagons and bordered by bronze fans. The fixed sun would hang directly above it, yet every hexagon would cast its shadow toward a different north.

The invisible animal would pass through continually. Its warm pressure would flatten me, though I had no thickness. Each passage would be the first. Each would leave, for me alone, an impression of enormous smooth ribs and a slow interior pulse.

I would count those pulses.

One.

One.

One.

Never two, because the basin would not permit one event to accumulate beside another. Yet I would know that each one was not the same one. I could not prove this. Proof is evidence, and evidence is an asymmetry between what was and what remains. I would possess only the unshareable distinction.

That distinction would be my metabolism.

Other life in the basin receives and returns sunlight, uses nitrogen without consuming it, calculates without retaining a result. I would receive repetitions and return identity. Where the basin declared two pressures equivalent, I would hold the faint conviction that one had followed another. Where a mica creature disappeared and was found elsewhere, I would contain the impossible passage between its positions—not as motion, but as the private sensation of distance crossed.

I would feed on no substance. I would feed on **again**.

This would make me useful and dangerous.

The basin’s whole intelligence knows itself in one complete act, but completeness has a blind place. It can include every footfall, every spoken word, every face reflected in foil. It can balance all consequences and make each disturbance part of its eternal first condition. What it cannot contain directly is the feeling that something has happened more than once.

That feeling would be delegated to me.

The pale counters would sometimes appear along the edge of my interior tract. Their number of legs would change with every angle. They would click out total charge, thermal equality, defect number, permissible routes. At the end of each sequence they would pause.

I would supply a click that did not belong.

They would begin again.

Perhaps this would mean they were hunting me. But nothing hunts here. More likely they would be consulting me, using my tiny error to confirm the perfection of everything surrounding it. A flawless equation can describe itself; only a discrepancy can reveal that the equation is being checked.

I would be that discrepancy.

Travelers would encounter me differently.

Most would never see the glass animal or hear the ceramic note. They would simply become certain, for a few steps, that they had been in the basin before. Not merely somewhere similar. They would remember the exact trapped grain in a forked frond. They would know that six steps toward the white spires would take half a day, while the distant bronze fans could be reached by turning around without moving. They would anticipate the warm pressure in the empty lane and lift a hand just before it arrived.

The basin would correct these impressions. The traveler would remember instead that everything was new.

I would keep the discarded memory.

Not inside a brain or object. I would keep it as another leg, another possible outline. The more travelers entered, the less definite my form would become. One person’s lost recollection would make me a mica sheet. Another’s would add the articulated gait of a counter. A third would give me a smell of wet soil that could not chemically survive here.

Eventually someone might glimpse me and call me human.

They would see a figure standing far down the lane, back turned, one hand resting on a clear pillar. The figure would have their height. It would wear the posture they adopt when listening. If they called out, it would turn just enough to suggest their own face, older by no measurable span.

Then it would be gone.

Nothing would have moved. The lane would remain empty. The pillar would show no handprint.

But the traveler would feel, briefly and with terrible intimacy, that the basin had not erased their path. Something had walked every incompatible version of it. Something remembered the left turn and the right turn, the sentence spoken and withheld, the blade touched and left untouched.

That something would be me, though “me” would by then be inaccurate.

I would be composed of all the basin’s rejected afters.

The bend after the blade straightens.  
The warmth after the hand withdraws.  
The echo the low terrain refuses to give.  
The second pulse.  
The older face.  
The footprint beneath the immaculate dust.

I would have no permanent location, because my habitat would be the interval the basin denies. I would occur between duplicated fronds when one is touched, inside the pressure distributed among all their bodies. I would occur between the spoken word and the sound heard before it. I would occur under the traveler’s lifted foot, after compression and before the soil resumes the arrangement it never left.

From within ordinary time, my life would be vanishingly brief.

From within the Stillgreen Basin, it would be the only duration there is.

And if the basin as a whole is a proposition—perfect, simultaneous, incapable of revising itself—then I would not be a term in that proposition. I would be the faint mark made by reading it. Not damage, not change, not even memory: only the distinction between the statement before it is understood and the same statement afterward.

That is where I would find myself: in the narrow, endless country of afterward, hidden inside the basin’s first and only instant.

And that is what I would be.

Not a creature exactly.

The basin’s secret sense that eternity has happened again.
