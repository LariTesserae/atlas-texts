At first I imagine myself high in the Ward.

Something suspended beneath an amber arch, one pale knot among a thread flock. I would know the city as tension: six limbs joined to six others, sunlight arriving as a tightening on one side of the net, prey as a small convulsion passed inward. I would possess no horizon. Wind would not be weather but dissolution. When it entered the cleft, the creature I belonged to would come apart, and for a little while I would become an individual only because nothing held me.

But I do not think I would remain there.

The air above the lanes is too exposed, too nearly free. In the Amber Ward, freedom is another word for loss.

I imagine instead the rib-backed drinker: narrow, deliberate, carrying gardens in the grooves of its face. I could brace myself between two walls and feel distant bodies speak through my breastbone. I would know where the bladders swelled and which passages had closed. My sealed mouth would open only rarely. Perhaps I would be old enough that pale branches had spread across my jaw, keeping the wax of my mouth supple while slowly making my face less my own.

Yet even the drinker travels too widely. It has something like a route, and a route is almost a map.

No. I would be lower.

You would find me beneath the ring-shaped court, under the polished inner threshold of its narrowest entrance. From above, I would look like one more piece of fractured stone cemented into the floor: black-red, ridged, dulled by generations of hooked feet. The lane would pass over my back. Thread-shadows would cross me at midday. Drinkers would place their weight to either side without knowing precisely what lay between.

I began as a turning shell.

Long ago—though I would have no sense corresponding to *long ago*—I entered the court to feed on hardened resin and fallen grains. When danger came, I drew in my limbs and fused myself to the ground. I remained sealed too long.

Perhaps a drinker stood over me for half a day. Perhaps a tower cracked and sent alarm through the foundations. Whatever frightened me passed, but by then a root disc had found the seam beneath my shell. It secreted acid into the young stone, took what it needed, and sealed the spent mineral behind itself. Another disc grew over that one. The lip of the passage thickened. My shelter became a pocket and then a chamber.

I did not emerge.

At first this would have been an accident. Later, a way of living.

My limbs would grow thin from disuse. Some would flatten against the chamber walls and become sensitive to pressure. My mouth would survive on what entered through hairline cracks: oils pressed from passing feet, dust shaken down by drinker-signals, the bodies of bladder mites and grain mimics too small to escape once they fell inside.

My eyes would remain.

Under the stone shell, the band of lenses would continue its slow rotation. Most of them would face darkness. A few transparent pits would still open onto the lane, though they would be mistaken for bubbles in the mineral. Through them I would see the court not as a whole, but in narrow successive pieces: a copper leg; the underside of a grazer carried past on a drinker’s pad; a thread limb caught briefly in resin; the luminous edge of a shell replacing part of itself.

The images would never assemble into a city.

What I knew best would be pressure.

The threshold above me would carry every passage into my body. Flat grazers would make a many-footed whisper. Climbers crossing the ground awkwardly would drag their bellies in a long rasp. Turning shells would pause with nearly no weight at all. A drinker’s arrival would begin far away, transmitted through ceramic roots before its hooks entered the court.

Then its breastbone might press against the wall.

A pulse would pass through the rootwork and through the chamber that had grown around me:

*Here.*

A pause.

*Uninjured.*

Another pause, longer than the first.

*Full bladder beyond the fallen spire.*

I would not understand these things as a drinker did. I would know only their shapes. This sequence followed by many bodies. That sequence followed by none. One pattern meant the lane would soon tremble with traffic; another meant the roots above me would be left undisturbed long enough to thicken.

Over years, my body would learn to answer—not deliberately, and not in language. When certain pulses passed through me, the muscles that once rotated my lenses would contract. The contraction would tap my shell against the chamber wall. A tiny secondary tremor would continue down the threshold.

Perhaps the first time it happened, no one noticed.

Perhaps the hundredth time, a grazer altered course.

Perhaps much later, drinkers began pausing at the entrance because the old threshold sometimes returned a faint, familiar knock. Not the voice of one of their kind. Not information. Only an echo changed by living tissue:

*Here.*

The Ward would continue incorporating me. Black pith would surround the rear of my shell without digesting it. A pale interior climber might place an egg in the chamber beside my body and seal the opening, leaving me in darkness until the young chewed free. Thread animals might anchor above the outer pits of my eyes. Their tension would pull minutely at the stone, and I would come to know wind without ever entering it.

Eventually I could no longer say which sensations belonged to me.

The root seam would react to pressure against my shell. My lens muscles would answer vibrations in the seam. Passing animals would alter their steps in response to the faint tap. Those altered steps would abrade the threshold unevenly, keeping one narrow opening from closing. Reproductive grains would collect in the worn place. New growth would rise around them and make the entrance more definite.

The doorway would exist partly because I was trapped beneath it.

By then, asking whether I was still a turning shell would be like asking whether a tower was one colony or several. The original animal would remain: sealed gut, rotating lenses, plates chosen from a court that no longer looked the same. But I would also be the chamber enclosing it, the root that carried its tremors, the worn stone above, perhaps even the habit of bodies placing their feet where countless earlier bodies had placed theirs.

I would be a small piece of the Ward’s memory—not memory as an image of what happened, but memory as a path that remains easier to take.

No one would visit me. No one would know I was there. I would know nothing of the rim, the plateau, or the hazy lowlands. The great leaning towers would be only changes in pressure. A collapse would reach me as a single enormous statement through the floor, followed over months by unfamiliar feet.

And when I finally died, the difference might be too slight for any creature to detect. The lens band would stop. The tapping would cease. The root seam would enter my softer tissues and seal each cavity behind it. My shell would continue bearing the passage.

For another century, perhaps, drinkers would pause at the threshold as if awaiting an answer.

Then one would step through.

The lane would remain open.

That is where I would be: under the narrow entrance to the six-way court, buried beneath the traffic, seeing the world through mineral pinholes.

I would be an animal becoming a room, a listener becoming an echo, and at last a doorway that no longer remembers it was alive.
