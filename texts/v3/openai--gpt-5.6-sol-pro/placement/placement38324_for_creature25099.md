At first I would think I was the station clerk.

There is a room for me inside one of the narrow stalks behind the lowest platform. From outside, the stalk is scarcely wider than my shoulders. Inside, it contains a long office with a ceiling lost among pale branches. Rain falls at the far end, always over the same desk, though the desk is not mine. Mine stands near the entrance where the blue pulses reach it.

On it are a fungal lamp, a punch made from horn, and a stack of tickets cut from obsolete maps.

I do not sell the tickets. I revise them.

Each shows a destination on one side and a part of the body on the other. When the district changes, the ink alters, but not completely. Under the new name, the old one remains as a pressure in the paper. I press the horn punch through both. The hole is very small. Sometimes, looking through it, I can see the place the ticket previously led to.

I would believe this was my work: to preserve errors too minor for the district to correct.

Travelers would not often see me. They might hear the page I turn inside the sealed stalk. During a pulse, they might glimpse my face behind a transparent patch, lit blue and bent over the schedules. If they opened the office door, they would find only wet pith, a smell of iron, and perhaps a ticket lying on the cut surface.

My body would not be kept there continuously.

One footstep of mine passes under the flywheel plaza. My left hand lives as a smaller print inside a larger handprint on a cold wall. My breath gathers at an intersection where other breath does not fog. My eyes appear in two different windows, never at the same pulse. The long mammals sleeping in the rails retain the warmth of my wrists. Birds carry the shape of my shoulders as a dark marking through the roof leaves.

For a while I would suppose the district had taken me apart.

Later, I would understand that it was assembling me.

I had entered from the heath—I would remember that much, though I might also remember having grown inside the office. I came through the black-leaved rim carrying a notebook and some ordinary intention: to cross the district, perhaps, or to record its streets. I marked the first intersection. I drew the bridge over the fragment of river. I wrote down the six flywheels and the factory that cut cold into cubes.

When I tried to leave, my map had become anatomical.

The bridge was labeled *incus*. The station was *left atrium*. The path to the heath was a narrow structure in the inner ear whose name I did not know. Beneath these words, in my own handwriting, was a sentence I had not written:

**A witness is a surface on which the former arrangement remains visible.**

That is the function the district would develop for me.

The handrail animals remember obsolete routes, but only in dreams. Fish remember vanished water, but cannot speak of dry streets. Maps comply too quickly. The browsers consume names, and the hounds steal them before they settle into history. The district requires something slower—something capable of standing at an intersection and seeing two incompatible intersections occupy it at once.

I would be its hesitation.

Whenever the low vibration passed through the streets, I would retain the city that had just ceased to have been true. Not perfectly. Perfection would prevent the new arrangement from establishing itself. I would keep only misplaced details: a cistern’s reflected light falling across the wrong wall, the smell of a room now inside another stalk, three steps descending where the pavement had become level.

These remnants would collect in me until I could no longer cross a plaza without walking through several versions of it.

That would be why I stayed near the station. Stations are made to tolerate contradictory destinations.

At night, I would go out along the root tracks. Not far—only to where they enter the fern curtain or disappear into a loading wall. I would place an obsolete ticket beneath each polished rail. Beetles would arrive and dismantle it, carrying fragments of the old map through their lanes. Some fragments would go to the roof factory and become membrane. Some would be compressed with pollen. Some would enter the black seeds that grow rooms before buildings.

In this way, places that no longer existed would become material for places not yet present.

The district’s newest streets would contain traces of me. A stair would reproduce the cadence of my climb. A doorway would open to the pressure of my palm. Rain would choose the place where I once waited. This might look like affection, but it would be closer to structural necessity. My habits would serve as temporary scaffolding until the new quarter learned to support itself.

Then one night, at the station, I would discover I was not the clerk after all.

The lamps would pulse. Creatures would gather in their overlapping versions of the platforms. The browsers would stand beside the hounds. The birds would regain their weight. Reflected fish would crowd the polished faces of the flywheels. In the rails, the long sleepers would awaken, and I would feel their memories of my hands as if they were my own nerves.

The moss bed would part.

The schedule would show:

**HERE — OVERDUE**

As always, the roots would brighten. Reversed rain would rise. Every doorway would open.

But this time I would feel the opening from within each door.

I would feel the pressure valves dilate like joints. I would feel darkness released from unused rooms. I would be present in the dry canals, in the unweathered potholes, in the worn thresholds awaiting generations who might never arrive. Every forecast the city had made and failed to fulfill would draw tight around me.

Then I would understand: I am not the one who waits for the overdue train.

I am what is overdue.

The clerk, the hidden reader, the underground footstep—these are only shapes the district gives me so that lateness can briefly appear to have a body. My true extent is the interval between the station’s preparation and the arrival that does not occur. I inhabit the held-open doors. I occupy the attention of the animals facing the empty track. I am distributed through the pause after the roots begin to shine.

The district feeds on that interval.

Expectation hollows a space in darkness. Non-arrival prevents it from closing. Roots line the vacancy; membranes gather around it; pavement extends toward it. What fails to come leaves behind a route by which it might have come. That route becomes the next street.

So I would be found at the station, but also in every unfinished doorway beyond it. I would be the civic organ that converts waiting into geography—the keeper of the former arrangement, the passenger who remains perpetually implied, the delay on the line called **HERE**.

And when nothing arrived, I would depart through the city all at once.

By morning there would be one more street.

Somewhere in its fresh handrail, something long and warm would already remember my touch.
