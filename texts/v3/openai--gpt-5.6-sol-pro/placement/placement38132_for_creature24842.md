At first I think I would be one of the eyeless frogs.

There is something tempting in being small enough for a thumbprint to resemble a country, in knowing rain through the stone before anyone hears of weather. I imagine gathering with the others in the lower chambers, my skin reading the mineral cold, my whole body an instrument for changes too slight to be called events. If I climbed unexpectedly to an upper stair, careful hands would follow—not to capture me, but to ask what I knew.

Then I think I would be a white moth, asleep on the plaster while daylight travels around me. I would settle on fresh repairs, on the closed eyelids of the dead, on the mouth of someone halfway through a promise. By doing nothing, I would make the Ward pause.

But these are lives without obligation, or with obligations the frogs and moths never chose. I think I would need a task.

I would be found high in the Ward, though not at its highest point, in a narrow recess behind the upper gardens where the roof fracture narrows and the outside air first enters the inhabited stone. The place would be called the Cold Mouth. It would be less a room than the interval between two walls: one old river-smoothed limestone, one built much later from shell-faced blocks. Silver grass would hang through a gap overhead. In winter, frost would grow inward there in translucent leaves.

My household would keep the seam breathing.

Not widen it. Never seal it. Keep it.

The work would be ambiguous because the seam gives both danger and life. It admits cold, but also air. It carries the scent of rain before water reaches the cisterns. Birds use it. Moths enter through it. Roots test its edges. Dust from the plain gathers in its lower folds and becomes, over years, the dark beginning of soil. If packed too tightly, the Ward sickens. If allowed to open, warmth escapes and storms may drive water hard enough to unsettle the upper masonry.

So I would spend my days fitting and removing small things: plugs of moss, braided sedge baffles, thin plates of shell, willow screens flexible enough to bow beneath wind without making the stone answer. I would learn the seam by touch. Each section would have a different breath. One would exhale steadily against the knuckles; another would remain still until dusk; another would draw inward before rain.

I would not have been born to the Cold Mouth.

I think I would have arrived from above, old enough to remember distances. Perhaps I came down intending to remain only until a storm passed. Perhaps I had followed the cold breath through the juniper because it was the only thing on the plain that seemed to be speaking directly to me.

At first I would be wrong for the Ward. I would turn too quickly and bruise my shoulders. I would set objects down with a knock. I would waste heat by lifting curtains carelessly. In conversation I would watch faces but miss the hands. Wind would not frighten me; silence would.

Someone would give me a burnisher and a chipped length of bone. They would show me an edge that needed smoothing. I would expect the lesson to end when the edge became serviceable. Instead, they would sit beside me and continue with their own work. Slowly I would understand that the object was not the lesson. The duration was.

I would be adopted not through declaration but through accumulated corrections. A pad placed beneath my cup before I could make it ring. A hand closing over mine when I pressed too hard. A place near the hearth that, after several winters, no longer belonged to anyone else. Eventually a child would be told to bring something “to the Cold Mouth people,” and I would realize this included me.

My public name would be short—perhaps **Sa**, little more than breath against the teeth. My longer name would be given later, after I had learned enough quiet to receive it. It would be spoken into my ear beside the central pool, with a full cup held between two people. No one outside that moment would ever hear it whole.

I would still have a dangerous laugh.

It would be the thing the Ward never entirely trained out of me. Most days I would keep it folded behind my sleeve, but sometimes a child would put a fern in an elder’s cap, or someone would return the same polished pebble to my pocket for the hundredth time, and the laugh would rise before I could gather it. Then my friends would seize me in practiced alarm and push my face into felt. For days afterward I would make rounds with the children, touching the nearby walls to discover whether my joy had displaced any water.

This would embarrass me when I was young. When I was older, I would understand that being watched over is not the same as being restrained.

The Cold Mouth would also make me one of the few who went regularly onto the plain. I would climb out at dusk to trim juniper away from the crack, reset pale stones that marked safe footing, collect wind-thrown twigs, and inspect the heath after heavy rain. Above, I would stand more easily than those born below, but less easily each year. The horizon would begin to seem like a room with its walls removed. I would miss the intimate intelligibility of the Ward, where every drop had passed through known hands.

Still, I would bring something of the open world down with me: frost caught in a covered bowl, a bird’s abandoned feather, a description of clouds made mostly through gesture. Children would ask about thunder. I would place their palms on a wall and say, very softly, that it was like the whole sky striking stone at once. They would withdraw their hands in horror and delight.

As I aged, my work would become less visible. Apprentices would climb the upper ladders. I would sit below with my fingers against the masonry, feeling how their adjustments changed the air. I would know when they had packed moss too densely by the coolness disappearing from one joint; when a shell plate was poorly angled by the faint disorder of condensation beneath it. I would stop telling them what to do immediately. I would let the wall tell them first.

In the end, I think I would become associated with one particular imperfection: a narrow place in the seam that whistles during the strongest winter winds.

The sound would be barely audible, but by the Ward’s measure it would be outrageous. Generations before me would have tried to quiet it. I would try too—moss, felt, willow, lime, shell—and each correction would cause some subtler harm. A bird route would close. Frost would reach a root bed. Moisture would collect behind the plaster. At last I would understand that the whistle was not a flaw that had resisted repair. It was the sound of the Ward continuing to breathe.

My duty would be to persuade others to leave it alone.

After my death, one of my finger bones might become the pin used to measure that opening. Each winter, the keeper after me would fit it gently into the seam. If the bone passed to the second joint, moss would be added. If it did not pass the first, moss would be removed. The measurement would carry no written name.

But perhaps, when the wind made that thin sound over the upper gardens, someone would touch two fingers to the breastbone—the gesture for an old joke, an old warning, or affection too familiar to separate from either.

That is where I would be: not quite in the city and not quite outside it, tending the narrow exchange between concealment and exposure.

And that is what I would become—not the keeper of a door, because a door decides too cleanly between open and closed, but the keeper of a breath: something cold, necessary, and never entirely silent.
