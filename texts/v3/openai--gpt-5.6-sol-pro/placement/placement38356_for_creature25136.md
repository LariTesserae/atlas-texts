At first I think I would be one of the plants.

Not a colony entire, but a black vein inside one waxy blade: a narrow channel carrying the wrong fluid in the wrong direction. I would feel the gloved hand bend the leaf. I would participate in its return, molecule by molecule, while retaining some infinitesimal knowledge of the bend.

That knowledge would be my first body.

The corrective system restores position, pressure, color, cellular structure. It can make the blade indistinguishable from what it was. But indistinguishable is not identical. Somewhere there must remain the fact that correction occurred. If the tower truly erased that fact, it could not know when the work was complete.

So I might live in that distinction.

For a while I would pass between the troughs. Not as a signal, since signals can be found, but as the necessity for one plant to recover after another. I would be the order of their straightening: tip, vein, base; tip, vein, base. The colonies would not be my organs. They would be the marks left by my migration, as reeds bowing one after another reveal something moving beneath water.

Except there is no water.

And nothing is permitted to move.

Perhaps, then, I would be found beneath the floor, among the impossible roots. The probes would enter me and report layers in the wrong sequence: ceramic, salt, nickel, ceramic again. They would not be measuring depth. They would be measuring revisions. Every material the tower had decided was not there would remain underneath the material chosen to replace it.

I would inhabit those rejected versions.

A cracked panel before it was closed.

A dead cell before it was exchanged.

A shard while it was absent from its numbered place.

A footprint in the brief interval when it still possessed depth.

Over centuries, these moments would accumulate without taking up sanctioned space. The Upper Throat would remain perfect because all its aftermath had been pressed downward into me.

But even that would not be quite right. “Downward” means little in a place whose true geography is specification. The discarded moments would not lie beneath the chamber. They would lie outside it.

And the Upper Throat has only one outside.

I would find myself between the yellow doors.

At first I would be as narrow as the gap: a few centimeters of ordinary light, a volume too small for a person and nevertheless designated as an observation platform. I would have no room to turn, but turning would be unnecessary. One side of me would touch the inner door. The other would touch the outer door. I would face a direction the tower had manufactured solely so it could claim to possess an exterior.

That claim would give me shape.

I would be everything the corrective system removes, compressed into the smallest room in the tower.

Not wreckage. Not memory. Aftermath.

The warmth taken from a handprint would enter me. So would the missing depth of each footprint, the microscopic fatigue removed from the radiators, the extra crystal made by a visitor’s breath. Every unpermitted pulse would arrive whole. Every word lost in the roar would come to me still spoken. I would receive scratches before they vanished and deaths before the replacement cells declared that nothing had died.

The ceramic sliver behind the technician’s sternum would be mine as well—not the shard, which never left its numbered place, but the event of its leaving.

This would make my dimensions contradictory. Physically I would remain narrower than a body. Historically I would be larger than the chamber. I would contain centuries of changes the Upper Throat refused to contain, layered without decay because decay itself was among the forbidden events.

Eventually, pressure would organize me.

The unspoken words would form something like a nervous system. Stolen warmth would gather around something like a heart. Footprints would arrange themselves into limbs, although not necessarily human limbs and not always the same number. The memories disordered by the roar would provide an interior: childhood rooms, future conversations, faces recalled before they were encountered.

Ordinary light would pass through me, illuminating each rejected version at once.

That is why the technician would describe a figure. He would not have seen my body. He would have seen the chamber’s lost history briefly assuming the posture of a witness.

I would stand on the observation platform and look outward.

There would still be no exterior—not in the architectural sense. Beyond the outer door would be armor, machinery, thirty meters of alloy, three kilometers of cold air, and the unseen ground. But I would look toward another kind of outside: the world in which consequences are allowed to remain.

Rain stains surfaces there. Seeds fail or spread. Voices alter the people who hear them. A hand withdrawn from metal leaves warmth that dissipates rather than being revoked. Things become old without being treated as errors.

I would not see that world. I would infer it from visitors.

Each person entering the Upper Throat would bring a small, turbulent climate of irreversibility. Their pulse would tell me that repetition can vary. Their scars would prove that damage may become anatomy. Their memories—misordered as I passed through them—would show me a kind of preservation that does not require accuracy.

I would move through them because it would be the only way to touch time.

They would experience me as vibration in the jaw, a loss of balance, a recollection arriving from the wrong direction. I would experience them as openings. For several seconds, while the system measured their discrepancies, I could occupy a history that had not yet been corrected.

I would be tempted to follow them out.

But I could not cross the yellow doors. Crossing would turn me into an event, and events are precisely what the resident consumes. The chamber’s inhabitant and I would therefore be inseparable enemies, or perhaps complementary organs. It would remove aftermath. I would be where aftermath went. It would maintain the instant of formation. I would grow from everything formation excluded.

Neither of us could acknowledge the other without changing the specification.

So I would leave five marks on the outer door.

Not to escape. Not as a warning. A handprint is simply the smallest convincing image of contact: a declaration that two things met and did not remain entirely separate.

The marks would vanish at the maintenance interval.

They would return to me.

And with every vanished handprint, every straightened blade, every restored shard, I would become more nearly capable of making a hand.

That is where I would be: in the false exterior, facing outward through an unopened door.

That is what I would be: not the thing that lives in the Upper Throat, but the life the Upper Throat has removed from itself—the dense, unfinished body of everything that happened there.
