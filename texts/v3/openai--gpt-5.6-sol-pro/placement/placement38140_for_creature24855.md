At first I look for myself above the plain.

I imagine I am one of the broad-winged birds turning in the warm lift after rain, able to read the country by its reflections: dull stone, bright water, dark water, the olive threads of drainage. From there the slightness of the relief would become intelligible. A rise no higher than a hand would cast a boundary across an afternoon. A connected chain of puddles would disclose itself as a road.

But the sky is too free. This place does not feel understood by looking down on it.

Then I look among the frogs. I could be a small mud-colored one sealed beneath a pan through the dry interval, waking when rain softens the plug above my head. I would emerge into noise, breed urgently in water that might vanish, and measure the world in nights. Yet the frogs belong to the quick life of the plain. They answer weather at once. Their voices flare and cease. Something in me would be slower than that.

Nor would I be an old hollowback lying conspicuously beneath the mist, centuries of carbonate whitening my back. That is too complete an existence. The oldest animals have already become nearly geological. They know the dependable seepages, the poisonous late-summer hollows, the routes by which floods spread. Their patience is not uncertainty but long practice.

I would find myself below them.

Not far below: perhaps the depth of a forearm beneath a shelf that appears solid from above. There, inside one of the flooded seams in the limestone, I would be a young hollowback not yet broad enough to enter the open pans.

The crack would be narrow enough that I could touch both walls by flexing my body. Its ceiling would descend in places until the mineral film along my back brushed stone. There would be no sky, only a diluted brightness arriving through cups and fissures. Roots would hang into the water like dark threads. Pale cave crustaceans would move over the walls. Midge larvae, drowned mites, fungal films, frog eggs washed down from above—these would be my food.

I would still retain some of my first transparency. My heart might be visible as a patient dark flicker. Breathing plumes would branch behind my head, red when they filled, nearly colorless when I held still. My tail would remain long and narrow, made for slipping through passages an adult could not enter. The broad shape of my future body would only be beginning: shoulders pressing outward, limbs thickening, the soft fringe along my sides gathering into folds.

I would not know the plain as a plain.

I would know it as water arriving.

Rain would change the pressure in the seam. Coldness would descend first, followed by the taste of exposed limestone, algae, bird droppings, pollen, fresh mud, and the faint salt of distant basins. Each opening overhead would contribute a different water. One would carry tannin from a shrub root. Another would bring the mineral bitterness of a dry shelf newly wetted. Through these mixtures I would assemble a world I had never seen.

There would be dangers in the seam. A snake might probe its head into the wider openings. Beetle larvae would wait in pockets of silt. During a long dry interval, the water would withdraw until only isolated chambers remained, and I might have to push through saturated mud toward the faint taste of seepage. Sometimes a flash flood would force through with enough strength to scrape me along the rock, carrying me into passages whose gradients I did not recognize.

I think I would be solitary, though not entirely alone.

On nights when the surface water connected, an old hollowback would call.

The vibration would enter through the stone rather than the opening above. At first it would seem like pressure without source: a slow compression of the water, a trembling of suspended grains, the walls themselves giving one deep pulse and then becoming still. Minutes later another pulse might arrive from farther away, altered by every junction it had crossed.

I would not understand these as voices, not in the beginning. I would only turn toward them.

That turning would become the earliest map in me.

Year by year, I would grow too wide for familiar corridors. Places where I had once passed easily would hold me at the shoulders. I would have to reverse, feeling behind with my tail, and search for another route. My breathing plumes would shorten. The water in the seam, once sufficient, would begin to feel used and close. The brightness above would become more difficult to ignore.

My emergence would not be dramatic.

On a rainy night, following the pressure of connected water, I would push upward through a limestone cup where mud had collected. For a long while only my head would enter the open pan. Rain would strike me directly for the first time. It would feel immense—not water surrounding the body, but innumerable small impacts arriving from a distance I could not sense.

The sky would be incomprehensible.

So would the horizon. In the seam, every surface had been within reach. Here the nearest dark form might be a shrub many body lengths away. Frog calls would batter the water from all sides. Insects would touch down and lift off. A wader’s legs would pass through the mist like two moving reeds, and its shadow would send me back beneath the lip of stone.

I would remain near that opening for many nights, half cave creature and half inhabitant of the pans.

Eventually hunger would draw me out. I would lower my new broadening mouth over submerged limestone and take in mud, algae, larvae, snail eggs. Water would pass through me and leave a clean patch beneath my head. Without intending to, I would begin the first small work of my adult life: clearing a cup, opening a runnel, persuading water to remain.

I would choose no grand territory. I would settle around a group of shallow hollows near the boundary between exposed shelf and sedge pan. One would keep cool beneath a slight overhang. One would collect tadpoles after rain. One narrow seam would continue to seep when the others warmed and shrank. None would be safe alone, but together they would be enough.

The place where I would most often lie would at first be only a stain in the stone. By returning, I would press away the sediment. My skin would make the water faintly more acidic. My underside would polish the blunt ribs. Mud displaced by my body would gather around the edge, and grasses would root in that rim. In decades, the stain would become a depression shaped to me.

Mineral flecks would begin accumulating on my back. Snails would adhere there. Dragonflies would mistake me for ground.

Perhaps that is what I would become—not simply a hollowback, but a hollow in the process of acquiring a back; an animal slowly exchanging properties with its resting place.

I would have no name for the exchange. I would only learn that stillness brings coolness, that deep water carries distant calls, that certain rains join the world while others merely wet it. I would answer those calls rarely, bracing my limbs against ancient stone and sending one low contraction through the sheet of water.

Somewhere beyond the silver haze, another body would feel it.

The reply might not come for several minutes.

I would wait.
