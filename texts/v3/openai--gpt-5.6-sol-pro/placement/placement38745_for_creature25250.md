At first I think I would live near the garden.

Not in one of the houses facing it. That would be too deliberate a beauty, too much responsibility for the white reeds and their long intervals. I imagine myself in a narrow room behind the eastern retaining wall, where the bricks are warm from an unrecorded pipe. I would sleep above a workbench, learn the city by smell, and be known for some modest competence: mending fungal-rubber soles, perhaps, or repairing the waxed cases in which dry paper is carried.

But this feels like where I would choose to live after already understanding the place.

It is not where I would find myself.

I would find myself at the lower end, near the sump station, in the tiled room with the two winches and the numbered hooks.

Not as its chief technician. The chief technician knows what every instrument was designed to measure. I would be the person responsible for the things brought back that cannot be entered under the measurements intended for them.

There would be a cabinet for these objects, though “cabinet” would be too generous a word. It would be a recess in the tile, covered by a felt curtain and kept permanently damp by a thin crack in the grout. Inside: a brass plumb bob that returns lighter when weighed but heavier when carried; a stoppered vial containing water that casts the shadow of a ladder; three lengths of line whose knots have exchanged places; an iron washer warm only when no one is touching it; a glass tube that bends for seven minutes after being raised and then remembers straightness all at once.

My task would not be to explain them. Explanation, here, would be considered an aggressive first use of evidence.

I would observe their recovery.

I would record who stood in the room, which lamps were burning, whether the frogs in the pressure gauges faced inward or outward, how many bell carriers crossed the grate while the object was below, what the Condensary was doing at the time, and whether rain had fallen above within the previous three days. I would describe the smell on the line. I would note if anyone dreamed afterward.

The work would have an old, inconvenient name: **keeper of secondary returns**.

My civic name might be Sera Below the Seventh Hook, though I would not have been born Sera and perhaps not born below.

I think I came into the city as a child.

I had lowered myself through a drainage well after a coin, key, toy, or dare—something whose importance disappeared as soon as I saw the chamber beneath me. I arrived carrying a box of dry matches in one pocket. When asked the three questions, I answered the first two honestly.

What have you brought that burns?

Matches.

What have you brought that dries?

The paper around them.

What followed you through the water?

Nothing, I said.

This answer would remain in my record underlined but uncorrected.

For my first year I would be given a drip near the lowest warehouse. Number L-19, falling through a crack shaped like a closed eye. It would have no great antiquity and no special civic importance. Its interval would vary between forty-one and forty-four breaths. The water would be slightly warmer than the stone beneath it. A black frog would visit the cup every sixth or seventh turning of Wheel Seven, though never when I waited for it.

I would become attached to L-19 with the grave possessiveness of an apprentice. I would name it privately. Not after a person. Perhaps **Almost**, because every fall would seem as if it might be the last, and then another would gather.

At the end of the year, I would pass its ledger to a younger child and discover that grief can be absurd without being small.

From there I would apprentice at the sump station because I am patient in the wrong direction. The city’s patience generally faces backward and outward: toward precedent, recurrence, the accumulated advice of stone. Mine would face forward. I would be able to wait for something that had never happened before.

This would make me useful and faintly suspect.

The Hasty would try to recruit me. They would mistake anticipation for impatience. I would attend one or two meetings in the upper warehouses, listen to plans for stronger pumps and brighter lamps, and leave when someone used the phrase *final measurement*. There would be something indecent about it. No measurement is final here. Even a correct length may later acquire more distance.

The oldest sump technician, Ilven at the Rustless Gate, would teach me not to hold the winch handle too tightly.

“The line has to tell your wrist what shape it is,” he would say.

He would be missing two fingers, not from an accident but because they had once returned from the sump with no agreement about which hand they belonged to. This would not be discussed often. He would still be able to tie every approved knot.

For years, nothing especially strange would happen to me.

That is important.

I would eat fungal loaves in the communal kitchen, sleep in a suspended bed, lend a dry book to someone I hoped would understand the gesture, and retrieve it months later with one corner swollen from damp. I would learn to lift foxes from transformer housings. I would vote badly at bridge meetings because I would formulate my best objections one brass-drip cycle too late. I would inherit a coat rubbed with soot and green copper, and eventually strangers would fail to notice me standing beside the station wall.

I would become a person of the city by accumulation, as the bell carriers grow their cups.

Then, during an ordinary weekly lowering, something would pull the instrument sideways.

Not downward. Not hard.

Sideways, toward a direction for which the shaft has no room.

The line would rub against nothing visible. The second winch would answer though no load hung from it. Somewhere beyond the tiles, a pipe would knock three times. The frogs would leave both station gauges and sit facing me on the desk.

Ilven would order the instrument raised.

I would tell him to wait.

This would be the nearest I ever came to joining the Hasty: not by moving too soon, but by refusing to retreat at the proper speed.

When the instrument returned, it would be unchanged. Its seals intact, its dials legible, its weight correct. Every reading would fall within expected limits.

But the line would be wet for seven meters, although only three meters had been lowered.

This has happened before, of course.

What would not have happened before is that the extra wetness would match the mineral character of L-19, my childhood drip.

Almost.

The water would be warm. Slightly alkaline. It would contain the same faint organic trace left by the visiting frog.

By then L-19 would have been dry for perhaps fifteen years.

Not unlawfully dry. It would simply have ceased. Its cup would still remain in the warehouse floor, numbered and protected, a tiny vacant geography.

I would be asked what I thought the finding meant.

I would say I did not know.

Then I would ask permission to reopen the old ledger.

After that, my life would spread.

Not dramatically. I would not grow gills or vanish through the rustless gate. The city has little use for transformations that can be witnessed in a single afternoon.

I would begin comparing secondary returns with discontinued drips, abandoned channels, sealed wells, dead root tips, and pipes believed to lead nowhere. Patterns would emerge too slowly to count as discoveries. Water lowered into the sump would sometimes return bearing traces not of distant places, but of lost arrangements: a seep diverted before anyone living was born; condensation from a house demolished two centuries ago; bathwater from a child whose thumb tile now shimmed Column Four.

The sump would not be beneath the city.

Not exactly.

It would be beneath the city’s present tense.

Everything the settlement ceased to be would remain wet somewhere below it.

That would be my theory, though I would never state it at a bridge meeting. A theory spoken publicly can become a pump, and a pump can become a wound.

Instead, I would alter the record forms. I would add a column:

**RESEMBLES**

Not *is*. Not *origin*. Resembles.

This would provoke years of civic objection. Resemblance is dangerous in an archival culture. It invites sentiment, superstition, and false joining. But eventually the column would be allowed, first in pencil, then in archival ink marked with white-reed pollen.

Citizens would begin bringing me small questions.

Could moisture found beneath an old staircase resemble the threshold water of a house no longer standing?

Could the dampness inside a sealed pressure cabinet resemble the breath of someone who had gone above?

Could a bell carrier’s cup contain a band from a rain that fell before the chamber existed?

I would answer carefully. Usually no. Sometimes perhaps. Once or twice, yes.

The distinction would matter less over time.

I would grow old at the seventh hook. Mineral stains would deepen in my hands. My shoulders would become asymmetrical from the winch. I would marry, or nearly marry, the person to whom I lent the book. Perhaps they would be a quiet mason responsible for turning the station steps. We would live in two rooms separated by a lawful drip. When we argued, we would stand on opposite sides of it and wait for the drop to fall before replying.

We would have no children, but several apprentices.

I would teach them that patience is not merely slowness. Sometimes patience means allowing an impossible thing to remain impossible long enough to reveal the precise way in which it is true.

Near the end of my life, I would begin hearing pressure changes before the gauges registered them.

This would be attributed to experience. Then to age. Then, privately, to the city.

I would wake before rains that had not yet reached the ceiling. I would know when a fox had settled on the western transformer. I would taste iron when instruments were lowered, even from my bed. Once, during the flowering of a white reed, I would dream not of open rain but of pipes extending through darkness in every direction, each carrying a different century.

In the dream, the pipes would not join the city.

They would compose it.

The inhabited chamber would be only one valve left accessible.

When I died, they would carry me up the root shaft. What returned would be the fired thumb tile, marked with the watercourse beneath my home. The tile would be brought to the sump station because a small gap had opened behind the seventh hook.

The mason—if they outlived me—would set me there.

This is where I become less certain what I am.

At first I was Sera Below the Seventh Hook, keeper of secondary returns.

Afterward I would be a tile behind the hook, taking the minute pressure of every instrument lowered. Moisture would enter the groove impressed upon me. The iron bracket would warm and cool. Lines would strain through the wall into my fired clay.

Years later, an apprentice might notice that the seventh hook gives a faint porcelain note before an anomalous return.

They would test it and find nothing.

The frogs would begin sitting beneath me.

A bell carrier might climb the wall and empty its cup into the narrow gap behind the bracket, though no ramp had been built there. The water would pass over my thumbprint and emerge below with an additional mineral trace: not enough to identify, only enough to enter under **RESEMBLES**.

Eventually the station attendants would stop speaking of me as a former technician. They would say the seventh hook is attentive. They would warn apprentices not to rush it. Repairs made against its tendency would fail; repairs made with it would last.

So where would I find myself?

In the tiled room at the city’s lowest admitted end, between the last mapped channel and the water that remembers discarded shapes.

Who would I be?

For a while, a human being whose work is to receive what returns altered.

Then a custodian.

Then a record.

Then one of the alterations.
