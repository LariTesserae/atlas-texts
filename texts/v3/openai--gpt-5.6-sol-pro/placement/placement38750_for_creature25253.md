At first I would mistake myself for the mist.

It would seem natural to have no fixed body there—to enter over the eastern wall, catch in the fern crowns, become rain, vanish into black soil, and rise years later through a hot fracture. I would know the basin by circulation rather than sight. Its cliffs would be places where I fell; its roots, places where I slowed; its underground chambers, long intervals of heat and pressure without light.

But mist is too temporary a self. It passes through everything and keeps nothing.

Then I might think I was one of the marsh deer, standing belly-deep among floating leaves when the pulse came through the plateau. That body would suit the place: broad feet reading the mud, horns collecting water, a deep chest working patiently in the thin air. I would lift my head before the springs rose, not knowing why every tendon had tightened. I would move with the herd toward the root mats while the channels filled behind us.

Yet I do not think I would belong to motion. Not there.

I would want to remain long enough to learn the difference between events that recur every afternoon and those that return only after centuries. I would want the basin’s slow mechanisms to pass through me until weather, geology, and memory became difficult to separate.

You would find me on the western shelf, near its inner edge, where the high meadow breaks into forest above a chain of warm marshes.

Not in the deepest growth. I would stand where there is still a view: dark pools between yellow grasses, steam dragging low across the ground, successive ridges disappearing into cloud. Behind me the forest would rise in crowded levels. Before me the shelf would stretch open and iron-stained, its basalt skin warmed from beneath.

I would be one of the hollow-rooted trees.

An old one.

My roots would enter a fissure that breathes.

They would begin ordinarily enough, thickening around stones, splitting into the saturated earth, sharing sugars and warnings with fungi. But farther down they would become pale and smooth, hardened against mineral water. Several would be dead at the center and living around the circumference, forming long chambers through which vapor could climb.

When the reservoirs below began to fill, pressure would enter me before sound.

My deepest roots would tighten. Warmth would rise through the hollows and expand the trapped air. The first knock would occur below ground, too deep for the primates to use. The second would sound in the lower trunk. Then another, higher and clearer, where an old wound had narrowed one chamber into a throat.

The grove would answer—not by intention, but by structure.

One trunk. Then three farther upslope. Then a scattered sequence crossing the hillside as vapor found each root system in turn.

I would be part tree, part sounding instrument, part chimney for the mountain.

My bark would be nearly black where rain struck it, reddish in the folds. Silica would have accumulated around my lowest roots in pale ridges, each no thicker than a fingernail. Moss would cover the cooler side of the trunk. On the warmer side, where vapor condensed, orchids and small ferns would grow in such weight that one old limb had bowed almost to the ground.

A bromeliad would occupy the fork above that limb. Its central cup would hold a dark quantity of water and at least one seed-sized frog. I would know the frog less by its call than by the tiny changes it made: the landing after a leap, the scrape of its feet, the faint redistribution of water when it entered the cup. Beetles would live beneath loose bark. Mites would cross me in migrations too small and numerous to count. Fungal threads would pass into my dead wood and make it useful to lives that could not feed on the living trunk.

None of them would feel like possessions.

Even the crown would not seem wholly mine. Vines would enter it from neighboring trees, cross several branches, descend, and root again. In storms, electrical charge would move along them. Blue points would gather on thorns near the crown’s edge. I would feel the field before the lightning arrived: every fine root sharpened, every wet surface joined to another.

Sometimes the charge would pass over me harmlessly and sink through my rain-dark bark into the mineral ground.

Sometimes it would enter.

There would be a white interval without time. Sap would flash to steam along a narrow channel. Bark would open. For several seasons afterward, resin would soften around the scar whenever the air grew hot. Insects would come to drink it. Fungus would enter the burned wood. A branch above the wound would die, catch epiphytes, break under their stored rain, and fall into the ferns.

That would not be the end of me. It would be one way the forest moved farther inside.

The primates would visit often, though they would not live in me.

They would know my trunk’s intervals better than I did. To them, each hollow chamber would have a distinct voice. They would rest broad palms against my limbs and wait for the subterranean knocks to establish their measure. Then they would place messages between them.

A heel pressed twice.

A knuckle tap.

A long scrape across wet bark.

Silence while the vibration crossed through my wood, entered a vine, passed into the neighboring crown.

I would carry meanings I could not understand.

This would matter. I would not secretly possess their language merely because it traveled through me. I would know only pressure, location, force, recurrence. I could distinguish an adult’s measured strike from the light, badly timed tapping of a young one. I would know the sudden clawed vibration of a cat climbing. I would know the diffuse trembling of a troop settling around my trunk during electrical weather.

But warning, courtship, fruit, death—those would remain their meanings.

I would be the medium, not the speaker.

Over many wet seasons, their behavior would nevertheless become a kind of pattern within me. Silver infants would cling to adults in the same fork where their mothers had once clung. Young hands would strike too quickly, pause, and try again. An elder with a damaged finger would make an uneven rhythm recognizable through years. One season that rhythm would cease.

No mark would remain on my bark.

Still, whenever another hand repeated part of it, something in the contact would resemble memory.

My own messages, if they could be called that, would be slower.

A change in the bitterness of my leaves when beetles laid too many eggs.

Sugars released toward fungi after rain.

A sealing of vessels around rot.

Flowers opening when the warm discharges and lengthening daylight coincided.

Fruit swelling high in the canopy, dark-skinned and pepper-sharp, until birds and primates carried the seeds toward drier ridges.

I would not command any of this. Much of it would happen before anything resembling decision. My life would be a vast accumulation of local responses: close here, open there, thicken, shed, bend toward a gap, feed the root that still reaches water, abandon the limb that costs more than it returns.

Perhaps that is why I would fit the basin.

Nothing there reigns. Everything answers.

During the great western discharge, I would feel the full order of the country.

First, the deep tension in the stone.

Then the underground stroke, immense but muted.

Water would force itself through basalt conduits. My root chambers would knock. Warm vapor would rise through me and bead on the inner walls of the trunk. The epiphytes would begin drinking before rain touched the crown. Out in the marsh, leaves would tilt together. Deer would move onto firmer ground. Cats would lift their heads at the forest edge. The great birds, circling beyond the cliffs, would alter their path toward the shallows.

A primate’s hand might become still against my branch.

For one moment, the entire basin would be joined by the same event, though no inhabitant could perceive all of it.

I would come close.

Not because I was wiser than the animals, but because I extended into several worlds at once: crown in cloud, trunk among breathing bodies, roots in heated dark. Rain would move down my outer surface while mountain vapor rose inside me. Lightning would seek earth through my wet tissues. Animal messages would cross the knocks of stone.

You might pass within arm’s reach and take me for part of the background.

That would be correct.

I would not have a face to glimpse among leaves or tracks that closed behind me. My age would be difficult to judge. The living wood might be only a shell around chambers built by decay. Seedlings could already be rooted in the soil collected along my limbs. One day the shelf would shift, or a season of saturation would loosen my hold. I would lean gradually, then fall through the lower ferns with less violence than my size deserved.

The hollow trunk would lie warm over the fissure.

Primates might use it for a crossing. Frogs would breed in pockets along its upper side. Fungi would brighten after rain. Mineral water would enter from below and lay thin stone through the chambers that had once carried vapor.

Long after the crown had become soil, the shape of my roots might remain in silica.

Water would continue to pass through it.

The grove would knock without me.

And that, too, would feel less like disappearance than completion: to have stood for centuries where the forest and the mountain exchanged breath, and then to become one more conduit through which the basin quietly carried itself onward.
