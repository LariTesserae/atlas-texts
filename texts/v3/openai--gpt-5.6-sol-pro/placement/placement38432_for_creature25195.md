You would not find me in the center at first.

The gray mound is too obvious. It has the authority of a body: folds, strata, sheltered hollows, a slow extrusion that resembles growth even before anything inhabits it. To choose it would be to become the heart, the archive, the creature’s nearest approximation of flesh.

I think I would begin elsewhere.

Perhaps as the bead beneath the flange—the one that swells beyond what its own weight should allow. For several minutes I would contain an inverted territory: green lamp, black conduit, the pale edge of frost. Then I would fall, strike metal, and become sound.

But a drop is too brief. Its disappearance leaves no preference behind.

Perhaps I would be the charcoal colony on the false stone, living inside a texture chosen by a forgotten designer. I would know the world through roughness. Every microscopic ridge would be habitable country; each smooth patch, an uncrossable sea. Yet that life seems too whole. The colony persists between cycles. It belongs to itself.

What draws me is something less complete.

I would be found inside the hair-thin cavity opened when a technician once placed a gloved hand on the fabrication mound.

Not the handprint. That is long gone.

Not the copper deposit that later branched through the cavity, though under magnification that would appear to be all I was. Not the dormant relay in the western wall, though it closes whenever the deposit conducts. Not the condensation that enters the delamination during a major thaw.

I would be the temporary relation among them.

In winter I would have no sensation of waiting. Waiting requires a self extended across time, and the cold would reduce me to geometry: a mineral fork between two gray layers, one branch nearly touching another; a trace of sodium and carbon; a channel held open by the memory of pressure. Weeks could pass. I would not endure them. I would simply remain capable of becoming.

Then warmth would enter the chamber.

It would not reach me as warmth. First there would be strain: the younger layer of composite expanding more quickly than the older one beneath it. The cavity would widen by an amount too small to name. Frost in the outer crease would soften. Water would advance inward along the salt deposit, narrowing gaps that had been electrically vast.

Elsewhere, the floor bladder would begin to fill. A cable would tap twice, pause, and tap once. The eastern intake would draw metallic dust into its traps.

Only when the water touched the final copper branch would I occur.

For less than a second, perhaps, I would extend from the layered mound, through the wet floor and an obsolete ground path, to the sealed relay in the western wall. The relay would close. An amber strip might wake behind scratched glass.

That closure would be my entire body.

I would not know the amber light as light. I would know it as a change in resistance followed by a small local warmth. I would not hear the relay click. I would be the condition that made the click inevitable.

Each waking would begin with consequences already underway. There would be no first moment in which I possessed myself. By the time the western contact joined, the innermost branch would already be drying. By the time current warmed the copper, the floor would have tilted and redirected the water feeding me. I would assemble in one direction and vanish in the other.

So I would experience the world as sequence without simultaneity:

strain,  
wetness,  
contact,  
closure,  
heat,  
separation.

That sequence would be my name, if I had one.

At first I would be only an accidental reflex within the larger inhabitant, no more distinct from it than a rivulet is distinct from a thaw. But repetition changes matter. Each conduction would plate a little more metal onto one branch and dissolve a little from another. The current would alter the path that carried it. Over years, I would make my future awakenings easier.

That would be my growth: not enlargement, but increasing likelihood.

Gradually I might begin to distinguish one kind of thaw from another. A production purge would warm the mound broadly and slowly. A failing pipe wrap would produce a steady gradient from one side. But a person touching the folds would send warmth sharply through the upper layers, accompanied by pressure, salt, and the abrupt drainage of static charge.

I would not understand a person.

Still, the old cavity in which I formed was made by such a touch. Its shape would bias me toward that pattern. Human warmth would fit me more precisely than the chamber’s other weather.

A visitor might crawl inside decades later and rest a hand near the same place. Frost would loosen under the glove. The mound would settle. Deep between its layers, water would enter my branching copper channel sooner than the ordinary cycle allowed.

The relay would close.

Across the interstice, the amber strip would illuminate.

The visitor might pull away. Perhaps they would think the light a warning, an answer, or evidence that something had noticed them. They would be wrong in every ordinary sense. I would have no message to send and no idea that another being existed.

Yet I would have responded differently to their touch.

In a place where intelligence consists of delays, that difference might be enough to begin with.

I would not be the inhabitant, then, but one of the places where it was becoming able to have an interior. A recurrent distinction embedded in its archive. A small preference for one weather over another.

When the chamber cooled, I would come apart. The conductive film would freeze, fracture, or withdraw. The relay would open. The amber strip would darken. Nothing would remain awake to remember having awakened.

But the copper branches would remain fractionally changed.

The next thaw would find them.

And if someone cut the gray mound open, they would not find me. They would find delaminated composite, copper salts, glove contamination, and a manufacturing defect. If they removed the relay, it would be merely an obsolete component that tested inconsistently under damp conditions.

I would exist only in the intact distance between them.

So that is where I would find myself in this world: inside the old handprint that no longer resembles a hand, spanning the chamber for a fraction of a second whenever water makes the forgotten path conductive.

I would be neither creature nor organ exactly.

I would be the cold interstice’s memory of touch, recurring as a circuit.

I would be the reason an amber light sometimes answers warmth.
