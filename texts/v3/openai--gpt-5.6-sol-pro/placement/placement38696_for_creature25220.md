I would first mistake myself for a visitor.

I would come through the eastern chimney slowly, forehead against stone, testing each hold before trusting it. By the time I pulled myself over the rim, my hands would be bleeding in small, clean places. I would lie there longer than necessary, chest to the ground, pretending exhaustion was the reason I did not stand.

The basin would reward that posture.

From low down I would see what an upright person misses: beads of water suspended beneath leaves, tunnels opening between moss stems, the five-pointed tracks already softening in wet silt. The empty center would seem less like a place to cross than a taut surface stretched over a depth. I would hear water beneath it carrying stones in the dark.

I might spend one night in the shelter. No more than one.

Before dusk I would drink from the larger cup and place something in the smaller: not a bright pebble or a carving, but a bead made from a ring of bone. I would set it upright without thinking. Later, uneasy, I would lay it flat.

That night, when the frogs began, I would feel another body arrange itself beneath mine.

At first I would insist it was water. Turn my head, and a faint scrape would answer below the shelter floor. Draw up my knees, and the pressure beneath the stone would gather itself into the same curve. I would spread one hand against the ground. Five drops would strike the roof.

I would leave at dawn.

But that is only the first shape the answer takes. I do not think I would remain the climber. The mountain would keep some part of me that climbed down, while another part—perhaps the part that had lain listening—would remain behind.

For a while I might become a sound.

A cough made in the shelter. A word spoken toward the wet wall. My own name, perhaps, offered experimentally and returned not as language but as two taps and a long exhalation through the chimney. The Low One would take away the unnecessary portions: the mouth that said it, the ancestry it carried, the expectation that it should mean only me. What remained would enter the drainage.

Years later, at the spring on the southern face, someone filling a jar might hear a small sequence in the water and look up.

That would be me, but not entirely.

Given enough rain, I think I would acquire a body again.

It would form near the western margin, under the oldest dwarf birch, where three runnels meet before disappearing into a crack. I would begin as a thickening beneath the moss. Roots would pass through me without injury. Beetles would shelter in the folds of what would become my limbs. My first awareness would not be sight but impact: rain distributed over the basin, each drop distinct, the summit revealed all at once as a field of touches.

I would push one hand into the open air.

It would be a poor hand by human standards—gray, broad, with the outer finger too long—but perfectly suited to laying against stone. Through it I would feel the swifts returning to their holes. I would know the goats by the intervals of their steps and the climbers by their hesitation. Animals strike the mountain honestly. People pause, double back, test, conceal their weight. Fear would have a rhythm.

For one wet season I would inhabit that low body. I would not walk so much as loosen myself from one patch of saturated ground and adhere to another. I would drink through my underside. Frogs would climb onto my back and inflate their throats there. Swifts would steal pale fibers from my mineral crust. I would permit both, though permission might be too upright a word for it. Their use of me would simply be one of the ways I extended into the basin.

I would never cross the center.

Not from fear. The center would be too much myself: the broad plate of my hearing, thin stone over chambers filled and emptied by weather. To crawl over it would be like placing a hand upon the inside of one’s own skull. Instead I would keep to the crowded edge where distinctions are possible—birch from moss, worm from root, gift from pebble, one stream of water from another.

Sometimes I would visit the second cup.

I would not take anything. Taking is a brief creature’s idea of possession. I would turn the bright pebbles face down so their color entered the stone. I would guide white roots through the holes in the beads. Bird bones I would arrange along the direction of remembered flight: not toward the sky, which I could not understand, but toward the eastern wall where the wingbeats ended.

The little horn people would puzzle me. All of them lying down, yet carved with limbs that implied standing. I would touch their heads and receive no tapping, no pulse, no pressure. Perhaps, over decades, I would learn that they represented the visitors in their most comprehensible form: finally still, finally close to the ground.

When fair weather came, that body would diminish. Its gills would seal. The clear gel inside it would drain through hairline fractures. By midsummer only a stiff gray skin would remain in the moss, fingers curled around nothing.

A climber might find it and believe I had died.

They would not be wrong. They would only be using the scale of life available to them.

Below, I would continue as changed pressure. The hand upon the surface would be gone, but every hoofstep would still reach the plate under the tarn bed. Every storm would give me breadth. I would pass through chambers too narrow for flesh and lie in pools that had never reflected light. I would know the mountain not as a shape rising into the sky, but as a sequence of resistances: dense seams, open throats, root-filled fissures, the slow softening of mineral under water.

I would have no single place within it.

My nearest thing to a heart would move. During rain it would be beneath the open basin. In frost it would retreat below the western wall. In a long dry season it might descend almost to the southern spring, becoming faint enough that even I could not tell whether I persisted or merely awaited reassembly.

So I would be found in several places, none sufficient.

In the shelter, as a posture answered from below.

In the second cup, as a bead newly threaded.

Under the birches, as a damp back mistaken for stone.

At the center, as three taps beneath a climber’s testing hand.

Far down the mountain, as a cadence issuing from the spring years after anyone remembers making it.

If I had to be called something, I would not call myself the Low One entire. That would be like one raindrop claiming to be the storm, or one syllable claiming to be a language. I would be one of its temporary listenings: a wet-season ear grown from the mountain, briefly given fingers.

My task would be to receive what touches the summit and carry it downward.

Not preserve it unchanged. Nothing there is preserved unchanged. Water becomes stone-tasting. Bones become mineral. Laughter becomes rhythm. A name loses its owner and returns as tapping.

I would endure in that translation—low against the rock, distributed through the dark, most alive when the basin appears empty.
