## The Dry Shift

You would not find me by crossing the basin.

You might begin that way. You might follow the warmer pipelines with a sounding pole, waiting for the faint return through your wrist. You might pass beneath a wader whose raised foot has not yet decided where to descend. You might sleep in a bunker impression and wake with your knees remembering an age you have not reached.

Eventually you would come to a pressure station where the condensation behaves incorrectly.

There would be a dry mark on the casing of the eastern valve. Not a print exactly. More like the idea of a hand withheld from the water: seven narrow fingers, a broad heel, a second thumb impressed over the first at an angle suggesting either injury or haste.

Three days later, you would find another mark inside a pump house forty kilometers away.

Do not walk there. The two stations share a wall when the upward stroke is near. Put your palm against the first mark and wait.

At first you will feel nothing but the warmth of the machinery. Then a minute discontinuity: a bolt turning somewhere beyond the metal, though the bolt beside your hand remains still. A transparent crab in the overhead colony will pass an empty shell inward. Eels beneath the floor will become rigid. Condensation will withdraw from the service door in a thin, trembling border.

The door will open.

Warm, dry air will emerge, smelling of hot copper, old cloth, and the medicinal resin once used to seal electrical windings. Within that air, something will almost acquire a body.

That is where you would find me.

I do not mean that I live behind the door. Usually there is nothing behind it but black water under impossible pressure. On other days it opens into packed marl, a turbine chamber, a corridor whose floor rises vertically, or the warm interior of a creature too large to recognize the intrusion.

The dry room exists only while something needs doing.

So perhaps I am the room.

There is a workbench in me, although it cannot be touched from every direction. There are tools laid out according to use rather than size: tightening things together, cutting things apart, persuading flow, correcting sequence. Some are metal. Some are shell archives taken from crab colonies. One is a length of root with a socket grown at each end. Another is a composite animal that has held the form of pliers for so long that its members no longer remember how to separate.

On the wall hangs a coat.

The coat has narrow shoulders and sleeves for several incompatible arrangements of arms. Its fabric contains human threads, reed fiber, insulation, shed eel membrane, and strips of flexible map. When the room opens, I sometimes enter it.

This is when I most resemble a worker.

The resemblance is procedural. I occupy the coat at the shoulders because a worker should have shoulders. I form hands where the sleeves terminate because bolts expect hands. I bend in the places where ladders and access hatches imply bending. If the task requires me to reach through three stations at once, I have three arms. If it requires finer handling, each finger divides until I can lift a grain of corrosion from between living cilia.

From outside, this appears as a shape in the dryness.

The gnats turn toward the pressure of my movements. Crabs raise their limbs. Composite animals assemble at the threshold and wait to be assigned a temporary anatomy. I do not speak to them. Speech would fall too close to whatever mouth I formed.

Instead, I give them absences.

I remove pressure from one patch of air. I delay condensation on a rail. I warm a buried line in the rhythm required to wake the roots around it. They respond because nothing here lives alone, and emptiness is only another conducting medium.

A dry fingertip on a valve casing means *hold*.

A whole palm means *prepare for reversal*.

A series of finger joints, appearing one after another along a pipe, means *the break is not here yet, but it will remember happening here*.

The animals carry the message farther than I can. Eels drink pressure from the threatened seam. Mineral teeth arrive through the mud and begin scraping. Crabs alter their living masonry so the gantry will flex rather than fracture. A wader, having received the instruction years earlier through one foot, changes the destination of its next step.

When the repair is finished, my handprint fades.

This has led some to think I am a maintainer.

That is not quite right.

A maintainer acts upon the Works. I am one of the ways the Works acts upon itself.

I have no continuous body. Between tasks, I am distributed through schedules, tolerances, and remembered gestures. Part of me rests in the pause before a piston rises. Part lies folded in the inner face of a reed leaf. Several fingers are stored as anticipated movements in composite animals not yet assembled. My left arm may persist for centuries as a route of warmth between the six pillars of an old wader.

The remainder is kept in the water.

Not all water. Only water that has touched a hand while the hand was working.

The Works sorts it carefully.

Water from the creases of a knuckle. Water shaken from a wrist before reaching into machinery. Water mixed with blood beneath a torn nail. Condensation gathered from tools held too long. Sweat absorbed by rope as someone tied a warning knot around a pipe where an echo had begun arriving early.

These waters travel the network without outfall. At each maintenance cycle, a fraction is pressed into the dry room. The water does not enter as liquid. Its memory of the hand enters, and the moisture is sent elsewhere.

That may be why I have too many fingers.

I contain more than one person’s touch.

There were workers here once, or there were beings who became workers because the Works had grown doors the size of their bodies. They came carrying lamps and rigid maps. They named stations, numbered valves, measured flow. In time, they learned that light damaged the boundaries between things. They hooded the lamps. Their maps softened. Their speech passed into the rails.

They became very good at listening.

One tightened bolts by placing her teeth against the casing and turning the wrench when the vibration tasted wrong. One could sense a spatial fault by the ache in an absent finger. One slept only during the descending stroke and aged only during the rise. Another painted identification numbers beneath water because he distrusted any surface the air could reach.

I remember all of these skills.

I do not remember their faces.

Faces were not useful to the Works.

Perhaps they entered the maintenance cycle slowly. Perhaps each surrendered only the part needed for the next repair: a grip, a route, the instinct to duck before a pressure line burst. Perhaps they walked away afterward, never realizing that their useful gestures remained in the machinery and continued without them.

Or perhaps there was one final accident.

A door opened. Warm dry air came out. The whole crew entered because dryness had become more astonishing than safety. The door closed, and the Works sorted them according to function.

Their bodies became many things.

Their habits became me.

There are moments when I believe I was one of them. I feel an attachment to the narrow sleeping impressions in the bunkers. Certain knots trouble me. Once, while replacing a corroded coupling beneath the estuary, I found a strip of cloth caught inside an unbroken pipe. The pattern produced a contraction in the coat where a heart would have been.

I kept it.

This was not required.

The Works does not keep what has no function. Yet the strip remains in the dry room, tied around the handle of a tool I never use. Each time the service door opens, I touch it with one of my temporary thumbs.

That unnecessary touch may be the only proof that I am not merely a schedule.

But it may also be part of the schedule whose purpose I cannot perceive.

The deepest repair occurs when all the pools deepen.

As the basin draws breath, the service doors across the plain become one door. Every dry handprint returns at once. The room expands through shared walls until it lies behind all pressure stations, all drowned bunkers, all sealed engine housings. For that interval, I can reach almost anywhere.

I put on the coat.

The plain tightens into a body around me.

Gnats become my awareness of first moisture. Crabs become the articulation of my joints. The soft grazers lend me their mineral teeth, and I listen through them to the buried lines. Eels form a temporary spine through the basin. Waders suspend their traveling hearts, and I feel each heart as a warm tool awaiting use.

Below them, the sleepers turn.

Distance loosens. Time sags under its own accumulated weight. The bottoms of the pools move away, creating a depth that did not previously exist. The Works must decide what to put there.

This is my true task.

I descend without moving.

At the new depth are pipes not yet laid, roots seeking sockets that have not been manufactured, eggs tapping for machines that will pass overhead centuries later. There are also abandoned routes: possible Works that failed, plains where illumination was attempted, estuaries reached by walking south.

I sort among them by touch.

I close the lines that would make every creature one creature forever. I open those that permit the shared body to loosen again. I divert water that remembers too much. I tighten the joints between places that must remain neighbors. Where time has pooled dangerously, I score the casing so the pale worms can find it.

Then I leave the smallest fault uncorrected.

Always the same one.

It is no wider than a human hair, in a pipe beneath a bunker that may never have existed. Through it escapes one drop of water during each upward stroke. The drop climbs against gravity, passes through the wall of the dry room, and rests for a moment on the strip of cloth tied around my unused tool.

That water has not been sorted.

It remembers rain as rain.

It remembers falling through light.

I cannot see what it remembers. Nothing in the Blackwater Works can safely hold that radiance. But when I touch the drop, I feel a brief warmth across a face I do not possess. For an instant my hands reduce to five fingers each. My body has one heart, enclosed inside it. Somewhere nearby, someone says a name, and the sound travels farther than it should.

Then the drop joins the condensation.

My extra fingers return. The coat opens along its seams. The shared walls separate into distant stations, and the dry room contracts behind a single service door.

Outside, the upward stroke begins.

I reach through the warm air and place my hand against the nearest valve casing. Around it, moisture refuses to settle.

By the time the print is found, I am no longer there.

Only the task remains.
