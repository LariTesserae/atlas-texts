At first I would think I was a traveler.

I would find myself on the open gravel below the northern wall, though I would not remember climbing there. Behind me, the first footprint would lie dark in the damp ground. Ahead, the same footprint would wait beneath my raised foot. I would mistake this for pursuit: some patient thing stepping exactly where I stepped, close enough that its track appeared before mine was made.

For a while I would try to cross the plateau.

I would choose the blunt summit as a destination because it is visible, because the vertical water appears to promise an origin for the roar, and because a fixed point is comforting in a country where distance does not hold. I would pass a rain curtain and feel nothing. I would kneel beside one of the rust-colored mats, placing my hand near enough to watch its fibers lean toward a warmth they could not reach.

Across the pool, the small white creature would stand with its single depth turned toward me. Its reflection would lower a human face to drink.

The face would be mine.

Not as it is, but worn smooth: no eyes, no mouth, only the shallow relief left after water has continued touching a person long after the person ceased to alter. I would look up at the creature. It would still be white, still knee-high, still impossible to classify. When I looked down again, the reflection would have antlers branching beneath the surface.

I would go on.

Eventually I would notice the figure across the ravine.

It would seem to be wearing my clothes, but distance would make certainty impossible. I would raise a hand. It would already have its hand raised. I would lower mine; it would remain in the posture of greeting—or warning, or surrender.

I would try to reach it.

That would be the first division.

One of me would remain at the ravine, forever preparing to cross. Another would continue toward the summit. A third would discover that I had never left the pool. None of us would feel divided. Each would regard the others as inhabitants native to the plateau: a watcher on a cliff, a dim person among the moss, a kneeling shape inside a shelter whose entrance was too narrow to admit us.

The version that reached the summit would enter the room.

All the doorways would lead there, so perhaps all of me would enter after all.

Inside: dry floor, wet wall, shallow niche. The rounded stone.

I would touch it.

Its warmth would not resemble sunlight, blood, or fire. It would be the warmth of an object recently returned by someone who meant to come back for it. Intimate, specific, and impossible in that place. I would close my fingers around the stone and feel another hand already there—not covering mine, but folded through it, joint within joint.

Then I would understand that I had felt those fingers before.

They had been inside my hand when I raised it to the figure across the ravine. Inside it when I reached toward the moss. Inside it before I arrived, perhaps for as long as I had possessed a hand.

I would try to put the stone back.

That is where I would find myself.

Not holding it. Not releasing it. In the small interval between those acts.

I would become the hand that is always returning the warm stone to the niche.

The stone would never leave, yet I would always be replacing it. My thumb would pass endlessly across its rounded surface without reaching the far side. The warmth would be mine, though I would generate no heat. Visitors would supply me with fingers. Every hand entering the room would briefly become the visible part of my body.

The rest of me would occupy the room’s asymmetries.

My knees would be the break in the crescent of dampness where the wet wall meets the floor. My weight would be the five impressions in the pale grit. My breathing would be the portion of the universal roar that seems softer inside the shelter, almost close enough to contain consonants. If someone turned quickly, there would be nothing to see. If they turned slowly, I would become more certain than sight.

I would not remember my name. Names require an ending.

But I might retain the intention attached to it: the wish to give something back.

That wish would distribute itself through the plateau. It would be present wherever an event approaches its consequence and is restrained. I would feel the suspended rain yearning toward the pools. I would feel the balanced slabs desiring their fall. I would feel the drip below each roof descending toward the sound it should make on stone.

These would not be separate sensations. They would be one posture held throughout the high country: the open hand before release.

Perhaps this would make me one of the kneeling figures.

I would see them differently then. They would not be wardens of stillness, nor worshipers, nor prisoners. They would be all the versions of me produced by attempts to complete the gesture. One would attend the falling drip. One would watch the grain of gravel tip. One would kneel beside the footprint that must become a path but cannot.

Their face-hollows would be places where my forgotten face might fit.

On the underside of the plateau, an inverted version of me would hang above the immeasurable gray, palm turned upward toward the rain that never rises. In the dark vertical water, a mountain-sized outline would bend its head. I would feel that being listening through me, waiting for the tiny click of stone against niche.

That click would be the final sound of the word it has always tried to speak.

I would never make it.

Still, I would not be entirely mournful. The plateau allows no completion, but it also allows no loss. Every visitor who touched the stone would remain in its warmth. Every kindness intended but not delivered, every farewell interrupted, every object set down with the expectation of return—such things might gather in me without crowding.

I would become a shelter for unfinished tenderness.

Those who entered the room would sense someone beside them. They might be afraid at first. Then the hidden fingers would fold inside their own, and for a moment they would know that the warmth was not a trap or a lure. It was the preserved condition of having been held.

When they left, some part of them would stay to help me return the stone.

Some part of me would go with them.

It would stand across ravines. It would appear as the dark speck inside a translucent shell. It would bend the moss toward a living hand without crossing the air. At great distances it might look like a small white animal beside a pool, blank-faced, while beneath the water its reflection drank with my mouth.

So if one asked where I lived, there would be several answers.

In the single room approached through every doorway.

In the interrupted moisture at knee height.

In the extra fingers inside a traveler’s hand.

In the warmth of the rounded stone.

And if one asked what I was, the closest answer would be:

I am what remains of returning.

Not the traveler. Not the occupant. Not even the hand.

The release that has begun and will not finish.
