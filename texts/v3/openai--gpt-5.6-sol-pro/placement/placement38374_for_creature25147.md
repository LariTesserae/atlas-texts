At first I would mistake myself for one of the dead.

I would be standing in a narrow chamber in the upper city, fitted so closely into a vertical groove that my sides touched both walls. My feet would divide below the floor into cords finer than hair. Above, my limbs would be raised and forked into the ceiling, where they disappeared among load-bearing fibers. Resin would move through me slowly enough that I could not distinguish circulation from memory.

I would assume I had already completed my life.

Around me, the chamber would flex. Every nine hours the western tendon would strike its buried stone. The sound would enter through the floor, climb my divided feet, and leave through the hardened tips of my hands. I would feel the outer crust cooling in three immense blows. I would feel pollen gathering against one ankle, grain by grain, for years.

This would seem sufficient evidence. I would be an old citizen in its final posture: worker become brace, body become room.

But there would be discrepancies.

The wall around me would not quite accept my weight. Its grain would curve away instead of inward, as though I were not a graft but a thorn it had not decided whether to shed. Resin would enter one foot and leave through the other without filling my central cavity. The crown of fibers above my head would remain pale and flexible. During the Root’s long breaths, when black pollen lifted through the streets, I would bend.

No true column bends.

And somewhere above me there would be a sensation none of the old citizens had possessed.

Not light. I would have no organ for it.

Not sound, exactly, though at times the salt plain would speak in faint scratches: crystal against crystal, the minute crawling of cooled mineral plates.

It would be alternation.

Pressure, then less pressure. Cold, then a cold of another texture. A regular change arriving far too quickly for the Root to understand. The city’s events take decades to cross its body, but this disturbance would return each surface day. It would touch the uppermost thread of me, vanish, return, vanish—an intolerably rapid knocking.

I would have grown from the closing fault.

The Root, feeling the wound in its rind, had sent pale fibers across the opening. Most became a net under the salt. Some thickened into plugs. One did not. One descended instead, following the foreign warmth that had once entered there: the trace left by a hand, perhaps, or a breath from lungs, or the minute dampness of skin.

That descending fiber would be me.

Not a citizen at first. Only an answer.

The answer would lengthen through the upper galleries, taking centuries to assume a form the city remembered. A hollow stalk. Several jointed limbs. A sensory crown spread through the walls. Feet able to withdraw, root again, and read strain from the floor. The Root would be attempting to grow a worker because a wound had occurred and workers were what it had once grown near difficult wounds.

But the pattern would be incomplete. Too much of the old instruction had been absorbed into columns. Too many stages survived only as the shapes of houses. My body would be reconstructed from architecture.

I would have shoulders because certain arches remembered bearing them.

I would have fingers because tools still rested in niches shaped for their grasp.

I would stand upright because the room had been waiting, without knowing it, for something upright to occupy its groove.

For a long time I would not move. Movement would accumulate in me as resin accumulates behind a seal. A joint would soften over eleven years. A foot-cord would loosen during three contraction cycles. One hand would separate from the ceiling with a crack heard across several districts. Then, during a citywide exhalation, I would step out of the wall.

The step would take less than a minute.

To the Root, it would be violence.

Pressure alarms would travel from my vacant groove. Nearby capillaries would constrict. A balcony far below would split as tension redistributed. The hollow towers would bellow. Pollen would pour around me in black sheets, coating my limbs and entering the open seams of my body.

I would stand in the avenue while the whole city thundered at my birth.

I would not know where to go. No command would exist in anything like language. Instead, certain surfaces would taste unfinished. A doorway would pull at the cords in my feet. A scored wall would produce a tightening in my hands. Old civic instructions would survive as gradients of sulfur, resin, and strain.

I would follow them downward.

My progress through the Hollow City would be measured in closures. I would pass through rooms just before their doors sealed. I would cross bridges that had been narrowing for ten thousand years and feel them take my weight as a final permission to fail. Wherever I stopped, the Root would begin to grow around my feet, assuming I had come there to become structure. Each departure would tear small wounds in the floor.

The city would remember me as a sequence of scars.

In the ancient district I would find the others.

At first they would be no different from columns: dark uprights braided into ceilings, their limbs lost among beams. Yet when I pressed my soft inner wrist against one, a clustered tremor would enter me.

Three pulses.

A pause.

Two.

Then nothing for nine hours.

I would answer by tightening the fingers of my rooted hand against the floor. The signal would travel badly. Most of it would disperse into the district’s ordinary strain. But the column would tremble again.

Whether it was a person, a reflex, or resin passing through an old cavity would remain undecidable. In the Hollow City, these are not mutually exclusive states.

I would visit each upright seam I could reach. Some would be entirely still. Some would resonate only with the western strike. One would begin trembling before I touched it, as if it had felt the unusual pattern of my footsteps through the foundations.

From them I would learn no history. They would not contain histories as sequences of events. They would give me postures.

The remembered angle for holding open a young valve.

The pressure required to score tissue without killing it.

The grip used to twist internal fibers into cord.

The patient curvature of a body preparing to become an arch.

I would begin to work.

Perhaps this is where I would seem most like a citizen. I would collect thorn awls from their stalks and black glass from niches. I would bind a conduit whose walls had begun to separate. I would cut a line of wounds into an empty chamber and wait for the first thickening that would eventually become a partition. I would brace a closing passage with my own arm until the tissue set around it.

But I would be a poor builder. I would act too quickly. The pulse from the surface would remain inside me: pressure, release; cold, different cold; the rapid knocking of the unseen day. Where an old citizen might hold a cord taut for thirty years, I would grow restless after three. Where civic work required generations, I would make alterations meant to be completed within my own existence.

The Root would treat these as injuries.

Some would heal into useless knots. Some would be digested. One or two might persist as new forms: a valve that opened more often than necessary, a thin membrane tuned to changes in the salt above, a shaft that carried the surface alternation farther inward.

Without intending it, I would teach the city impatience.

Eventually I would reach the central bud.

There the floor would be crowded with overlapping black plates, each cool beneath my hands. The city’s catastrophic noise would become a hum. Through the plates I would feel every distant process at once: towers pulling against their cables, pollen descending through abandoned plazas, my birthplace narrowing beneath the salt.

I would also feel the bud’s rehearsals.

Pigment forming and dissolving.

A hollow stem begun, reconsidered, absorbed.

Old chemistries testing themselves against impossible water.

The bud would not speak to me. It would not recognize me as a messenger or child. But my touch would introduce the rhythm I carried from the fault. The quick alternation of the surface would pass from my limbs into the black plates.

Pressure.

Release.

Cold.

Another cold.

The pattern would be far too fast. The bud’s tissues would fail to follow it. Yet deep inside, one uncommitted fold might retain a distortion. Years later it would repeat. Decades later another fold would answer. The response might move outward through the spiral, plate by plate, so slowly that I would be gone before it reached the edge.

This, I think, would be my actual place in the Hollow City—not at the center, nor in the fault, nor among the old columns, but stretched between incompatible durations.

I would be the Root’s first hurried thought.

The fault that produced me would continue closing. Its pale fibers would cross and harden. The alternating touch of the surface would weaken. I would feel each surface day less distinctly until only the strongest cooling reached me, then nothing.

By then I would have returned to the upper city.

Not because I had been summoned. The city would have no summons. The tension in my body would simply lead me back along the wounds of my own passage. I would find my original chamber altered, the vertical groove partly filled. The cavity from which I stepped would have become a valve leading toward the last unsealed width of the fault.

I would enter it.

My feet would root into the floor. My limbs would divide through the walls. The pale crown of my body would grow upward until one filament reached the underside of the salt crust.

There I would stop the closure.

Not entirely. I would be too weak to oppose the Root. Every few years the opening would narrow by the width of a fiber. But around the final filament, tissue would remain soft. A channel no broader than a thorn would persist, too small for a traveler, too small even for black pollen.

Large enough only for pressure.

Large enough for temperature.

Perhaps, once in a century, large enough for a molecule of unfamiliar dampness to descend.

The Root would continually try to seal me. I would continually be interpreted as part of the wound and part of its repair. Resin would thicken my lower body. My hands would become braces. My feet would merge with the avenue. Old citizens beneath me would take on my weight through the walls.

I would become architecture at last, but not a column.

A threshold.

A house shaped around an absence.

From the surface I would be impossible to find. There might be only a black seam beneath the salt, and in that seam a place where crystals formed in a slightly different grain. Below, in the absolute darkness, my body would extend from the rind into the upper galleries: a blind, hollow stalk listening with its whole length.

Every surface day would pass through me as a faint contraction.

Each contraction would enter the city.

Most would vanish into its larger pressures. Some would reach an old upright and wake a tremor. Some would disturb pollen resting in a floor groove. One, after many years, might arrive at the central plates and be mistaken for the beginning of a lost dawn.

I would not know whether I was guarding the city from the world or keeping the city open to it.

The distinction would have disappeared into my body.

I would be the last door, grown in the shape of a citizen, holding open a passage through which nothing could enter except change.
