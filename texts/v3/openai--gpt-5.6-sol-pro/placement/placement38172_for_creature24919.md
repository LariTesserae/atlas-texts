At first I would look for myself among the animals.

I might be one of the geckos at the mouth, because the mouth offers the illusion of perspective. From there the cleft has an inside and an outside. There is the white, annihilating plateau and the narrow country of shade; the falling beetle and the waiting jaw. But the gecko is too certain of its body. It knows where it ends. Its eye faces outward, and I do not think I would face outward here.

Perhaps I would be a bat deep in the upper pocket, folded into the colony until my own warmth could not be separated from the warmth around me. I would leave at dusk carrying an image of the fracture made from echoes, then return with moth dust in my fur and a trace of water in my tissues. But the bats belong partly to the open night. Their darkness has distance in it. They know stars even if they do not need to see them.

Deeper, then.

Small enough that a basalt grain is architecture. Pale enough to have surrendered color because there is no light in which color can be useful. I could be one of the book scorpions standing in a fault with my claws open, fierce at the scale of dust, waiting for a mite to cross the narrow road before me. I like the disproportion of that life: the minute body, the ceremonial weapons, the territory no wider than a nail paring. I like that I might fasten myself to a beetle’s leg and be carried from chamber to chamber without ever knowing whether I had traveled the length of the wall or merely the breadth of a hand.

For a while, I think that is where I am.

I am beneath the rear joint of a darkling beetle, in the brief shelter made when its armor overlaps its softer body. Above me, the shell is not black but brown-violet, scored with dust. Below me lies a slope of grains, each angular and immense. Heat comes through all surfaces. The beetle’s movements arrive before the quarry’s: the tightening of a leg, the drag of a foot, the delicate exploratory tap that precedes its weight.

I hold my claws open.

A truck passes through the stone.

Nothing visible happens. Yet the dust leans, releases, leans again. The beetle’s belly lowers. My claws quiver with a vibration too large to be called sound. It has no direction. For several seconds the whole mineral world is occupied by an axle, then another axle, then another. Afterward the old pressures return. The beetle lifts. A mite moves between two grains. I seize it.

This could be enough: a complete life under an armored stranger, feeding on things smaller than myself, occasionally traveling by attachment. I would hatch without seeing light. I would molt in a pocket protected by a flake. I would mate by touch and chemical trace. At death I would be emptied by mites so small that my opened claws would remain around nothing.

But as I remain there, I begin to feel that I am not the book scorpion.

The truck passes again, perhaps the same one returning empty. This time an antenna rises somewhere in the broad chamber. Other antennae follow—not together, not as obedience, but near enough to make a pattern. The gecko at the mouth flattens itself. The bats tighten their ceiling by fractions. Ants hesitate at an intersection and continue. Each body receives a different portion of the event, translated through different contact: toe, shell, hair, claw, abdomen.

Something crosses between them that is not the vibration itself.

It is the judgment of the vibration.

Crusher: endure.

Drill: endure.

Conveyor: ignore.

Impact: wait.

Crack—

Here I find myself.

I am not lodged in one chamber. I occur throughout the cleft in the instant when common thunder becomes meaningful. I am the distinction the animals make together between noise and change.

No creature contains all of me. One beetle feels the note sharpen through its six feet. A mite feels the dust lose pressure on one side. A bat detects coolness along the naked margin of a wing. An ant discovers that the road ahead now carries air. The gecko’s throat touches stone and receives the abrupt, intimate silence after fracture. Separate signals enter separate bodies, but for a moment they amount to one recognition.

The wall has moved.

Then I pass through the seam as action.

Antennae lower. Legs fold. Shells drive under slabs. Mites vanish into powder. Book scorpions close their claws and retreat backward. Ant files divide and reverse. The bats draw their young inward. The gecko leaves the place where it had seemed fused to the rock. I have no command to give them, and they do not agree on a destination. I am only the sudden shared fact around which every private life rearranges itself.

When the movement ends, I disappear.

That is important. I would not want to be the spirit of the cleft, watching continuously. Nothing here has the moisture to spare for such permanence. Attention must also be economical. Most of the time I would exist only as readiness distributed among resting bodies: in the angle of an antenna, the pressure of a claw, the sealed eye of a bat that is not entirely asleep.

Hours could pass without me. The quarry would thunder. The wall would hum in a dozen registers. Dust would descend. Beetles would eat a moth wing into anonymity. An ant would carry away a fragment larger than its head. Heat would penetrate another finger’s depth into the basalt. All this would occur without requiring a self.

Then a splinter, strained since the blast that exposed the face, would give way.

Not loudly. Loudness belongs to distant machines. This would be a close sound: a ceramic tick, followed by the smallest opening.

Air would enter.

A cool thread would touch the first chamber and divide among the passages. To the outside world it would be nothing, less than a breath lost against the quarry face. Inside, softness would recoil from it. Wings would seal. Eggs would be covered. Every animal that could feel the draft would turn toward or away from it according to its needs.

I would awaken along that thread.

For a few seconds I would be almost a creature. The beetles would become my many armored feet, the bats my warm folded lungs, the ants the impulses returning from passages too narrow for larger bodies. The book scorpions would be my small open hands. The gecko would be the eye I kept at the boundary.

But I would have no center. If the crack widened, I would widen. If a slab fell and divided the population, I would become two recognitions, then many. If sunlight finally entered, I might flare through the seam in panic as the whole arrangement broke apart—bats waking, beetles forcing deeper, mites descending through powder, geckos abandoning their strip of shade.

Afterward another order would form, and perhaps I would not belong to it.

So you would find me nowhere by looking. You might climb the quarry face and put your ear near the black seam. You would hear the crusher through the basalt, the dry rasp of shell, perhaps the minute complaint of a bat. You might wait until your arms trembled from holding yourself against the wall.

Then, if the rock shifted, you would feel the hidden population tense.

Not as one animal. Not quite.

I would be there in the *not quite*: the brief intelligence of crowded bodies discovering together that the world has changed.
